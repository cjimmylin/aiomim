WRITING KNOWLEDGE-BASE ENTRIES FROM CITED
PAPERS, SCORED ON CURATORS’ PER-PAPER FACTS
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
ABSTRACT
Designs that turn papers into cited text are rarely compared with model, sources
and scoring fixed. We compare strategies for writing knowledge-base entries from
exactly the papers curators cited: 100 phenotype entries of OMIM, a catalogue of
genetic disorders, with 2–12 references (19% of 515 papers readable in full). The
primary endpoint scores what a draft says about each cited paper against 6,652
facts split once from curator-written, variant-centred per-paper text. Hiding the
full text of the same papers lowers mean coverage from 79.8% to 36.1% (46 en-
tries; 43.7 points, 95% CI 38.3 to 49.1). For entries with 2–12 references read
in one prompt (direct reading), no agent, evidence graph or case table conveyed
more at the main settings (−0.5 to −1.9 points; their order shifts with entries and
settings). On 38 test entries with a complete draft from every arm, a complete-
ness pass adds 2.6 points (1.7 to 3.6) and lengthens drafts from 10,802 to 14,412
characters; on all 41, it requires keeping the unrevised draft where the model’s
safety filter stopped the revision. A claim-level audit catches swapped citations,
not added citations or omissions. One model family wrote, audited and judged
everything; no person validated any judgement.

INTRODUCTION
Curated knowledge bases are written by experts who read papers and record, entry by entry, what
each paper showed. Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), the catalogue of human genes
and genetic phenotypes, is built by expert review of the literature and held over 25,000 entries by
2021 (Amberger et al., 2014; 2019; Hamosh et al., 2021); ClinVar archives variant interpretations
(Landrum et al., 2017; 2025), and ClinGen experts classify gene–disease validity (Strande et al.,
2017). Publication outpaces curation (Caufield et al., 2024a; Green et al., 2023).
Language models can now read the papers, and many designs turn documents into cited, structured
text: retrieval (Lewis et al., 2020) or long-context reading (Li et al., 2024), extraction into a graph
(Edge et al., 2024) or an evidence table (Joshi et al., 2026), agents that choose what to read (L´ala
et al., 2023; Skarlinski et al., 2024), drafting then verifying (Dhuliawala et al., 2024), and prompts
optimised from examples (Agrawal et al., 2026). A review of 19 evaluations found a median of 14%
incorrect extractions and no support for use without human oversight (Clark et al., 2025). What can
be read is a further limit. Literature annotation covers about 36 million abstracts but 6 million open-
access full texts (Wei et al., 2024a); full texts yield more to text mining than abstracts (Westergaard
et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2019), which differ in structure and content (Cohen et al., 2010); and open
access most often lacks an open licence (Piwowar et al., 2018).
These designs were evaluated on different tasks, models, sources and scoring. Comparisons that
hold the model and settings fixed address question answering and query-based summarisation (Li
et al., 2024; 2025a; Han et al., 2025), and the closest work in genetics searches a corpus for its
sources, measures memory, or compares models rather than strategies (Section 2). Three questions
are open. With the model, sources, guards and scoring fixed, how much does the strategy matter?
What limits agreement with what experts wrote: the strategy, or what it was given to read? Which
errors does a claim-level audit against the cited source catch, and which can it not see?
We answer them with a benchmark in which only the generation strategy varies (Figure 1). Each of

Access to the source bounds agreement in the regime tested. This is expected, as curators read
full papers; we measure its size. Hiding the full text of papers that have one lowers coverage of the
same facts from 79.8% to 36.1% (means over 46 entries, all before the audit’s repair round; +43.7
points, 95% CI +38.3 to +49.1). No design beat direct reading in the regime tested. For entries
with 2–12 references read in one prompt, at the main settings and on the 93 entries every strategy
finished, no design conveyed more than direct reading: the agent and the evidence graph were 0.6
and 0.5 points behind (intervals −1.3 to +0.0 and −1.5 to +0.5; no equivalence is claimed) and our
case-table implementation 1.9 points (−3.1 to −0.8), a suggestive deficit. The graph was ahead only
on subsets of entries (the 30 replicate entries; the 38 of a post hoc ablation), and matching settings
narrowed the case table’s gap by 2.1 points; each difference is small against the effect of full text.
Reading more completely recovers a few points and writes more. On 38 entries not used in its
design, each with a complete draft from every arm, a completeness pass adds 2.6 points (1.7 to 3.6),
with drafts of 14,412 instead of 10,802 characters; on all 41 (post hoc) the gain holds only if the
unrevised draft is kept where the model’s safety filter stopped the revision (+2.4 points; −3.5 if
such entries count as zero). The audit checks fidelity, not completeness or correctness. It caught
every planted citation swap but no added citation and, by construction, no omission.

RELATED WORK
Language models for curation in genetics and biology.
Language models assist the curation
of knowledge bases, ontologies, gene and gene-set annotations and literature summaries (Caufield
et al., 2024b;a; Toro et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025c; Hu et al., 2025; Niyonkuru et al., 2025; Green
et al., 2026; Murphy et al., 2024; Green et al., 2023), and extract variant, gene–disease and gene–
phenotype evidence from papers (Li et al., 2025b; Furlan et al., 2025; Wang & Wang, 2026; Yin
et al., 2026; Twede et al., 2026). Model recall of curated rare-disease knowledge is generally low,
better for genes than phenotypes (Groza et al., 2026), and GEN-KnowRD builds a knowledge base
from model-generated profiles, evaluated by disease ranking (Yan et al., 2026). Closest to ours,
FlyAOC compares memorisation, pipeline and agent harnesses that recover FlyBase curators’ an-
notations given a 16,898-paper corpus (Zhang et al., 2026). Two benchmarks built from ClinGen
judge model explanations or summaries against human-written ones, comparing models, not strate-
gies (Queen et al., 2025; Saadat & Fellay, 2026); PaperQA2’s cited Wikipedia-style summaries are
compared with Wikipedia articles (Skarlinski et al., 2024); and agents given a source publication
annotate phenotypes within inter-curator variability (Balhoff & Lapp, 2026). Here the papers are
fixed to the curators’ reference list, the reference is the curators’ own per-paper text and fields, ac-
cess is manipulated within the same papers, and the strategy is the only variable; the contribution is
this controlled measurement, not a new strategy.
Intermediate representations versus long-context reading.
Whether long-context reading beats
retrieval is contested (Li et al., 2025d; Yu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al.,
2025a). Graphs serve query-focused summarisation (Edge et al., 2024), and comparisons ask when
they help (Han et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025); retrieval-augmented systems write cited answers
(L´ala et al., 2023; Asai et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2025), and STORM writes Wikipedia-like articles
(Shao et al., 2024).
Memory, verification and contamination.
Verification methods plan verification questions (Dhu-
liawala et al., 2024), post-edit unsupported content (Gao et al., 2023a) or consult domain databases
(Wang et al., 2025). As curated knowledge bases are public, memory is a confound; contamination
is detected from black-box access (Shi et al., 2024; Oren et al., 2024; Golchin & Surdeanu, 2024;
2025) or with training cut-offs and new test items (Roberts et al., 2023; Jain et al., 2025; White et al.,
2024). We read a memory-only control by creation date, including entries created in 2026.
Improving a fixed reader.
Prompts are optimised by models (Zhou et al., 2022; Pryzant et al.,
2023; Yang et al., 2023), evolved (Guo et al., 2023; Fernando et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026)
or compiled (Khattab et al., 2023; Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024); self-refinement helps (Madaan et al.,
2023) but can fail without feedback (Huang et al., 2023), and the most consistent sample helps
(Wang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023). Our guide and reading-better arms are small prompt-level
instances.
Evaluating cited long-form text.
Factuality metrics check atomic claims against a knowledge
source (Min et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024b; Song et al., 2024), and human evaluation checks attribu-
tion to identified sources (Rashkin et al., 2023). Citation support is often incomplete in generative
search, cited generation and medical answers (Liu et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023b; Wu et al., 2025),
and about a quarter of quotations in medical articles are erroneous (Jergas & Baethge, 2015). Model
judges can match human preferences (Zheng et al., 2023) but need validation (Shankar et al., 2024)
and may favour their own outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024); a model reviewing extracted data caught
2 of 39 deliberate errors (Stewart-Evans et al., 2025). Our judges share the writer’s model family.

3.1
TASK, ENTRIES AND REFERENCE
An entry is e

(h, R) with header h (number, title, alternative titles) and references
R = {d1, . . . , dn}, the papers its curators cited.
Each paper has an access level a(d) ∈
{full text, abstract, title} set by open access. A strategy S maps (h, R) to a draft D: paragraphs,
each citing a non-empty subset of R, and sets of genes, inheritance modes, ontology terms and
allelic variants. The causal gene is withheld, as naming it is part of the curator’s task.
Entries. We drew 100 OMIM phenotype entries at random with a fixed seed, 20 from each of five
bands of entry number that track creation date (1xxxxx–5xxxxx, 600000–609999, 610000–615999,
616000–619999, 620000 onward). Eligible entries have 2–12 cited references, a causal gene in the
gene map, at least five phenotype annotations and at least one curator paragraph in scope; this selects
well-annotated, molecularly characterised entries. Of the 515 cited papers, 96 (19%) are readable in
full through PubMed Central or Europe PMC (Rosonovski et al., 2024), 345 as abstract only and 74
as title only; supplements were not retrieved. Appendix Table 2 lists the entries of every experiment.
Reference. The curator-written per-paper text is OMIM’s allelic-variant descriptions as deposited
by OMIM in ClinVar (Landrum et al., 2025): variant-centred prose from OMIM’s gene entries, each
paragraph about one or more cited papers. A paragraph is in scope when it is about a paper its
entry cites; 476 are. Of the 515 cited papers, 327 have an in-scope paragraph (81 of 96 readable in
full, 221 of 345 abstract only, 25 of 74 title only); what a draft says about the other papers is not
scored. Narrative sections of phenotype entries are not public and are not compared. The clinical-
synopsis reference is the HPO disease annotations (Gargano et al., 2024): 2,311, of which 1,835 are
the HPO team’s own curation from papers and 476 are taken from OMIM. The causal gene comes
from OMIM’s gene map via MedGen (Louden, 2020) and mim2gene; inheritance from HPO and
MedGen modes, compared as coarse classes that must be equal as sets; the variants are the 427
in-scope OMIM-submitted ClinVar records.
Reuse. The fixed fact lists contain curator wording and are not redistributed, so scoring a new
strategy against the same denominators requires rebuilding the reference under the rights holder’s
terms and splitting it again. How much a fresh split by the same procedure moves levels was not
measured; splitting afresh for each draft, a different procedure, ran 2–5 points higher with the same
order of strategies (Appendix Table 6). Differences within one split are the comparable quantity.
3.2
STRATEGIES AND SETTINGS
The strategies form three paradigms, a control and three add-ons, each add-on tested against its
parent (Figure 1; Appendix Table 4). Read and write: direct reading, the baseline, puts all papers
in one prompt; the agentic curator works in a folder of the papers with read and search tools and
must give a verbatim quote for every paragraph. Extract, then write: the evidence graph extracts
quote-backed findings per paper into a graph of papers, genes, variants, phenotypes and inheritance;
case table first extracts one record per reported patient or family per paper and pools them; in both,
the writer sees only the intermediate. Memory first: recall, then verify writes from memory, audits
the draft against the papers and keeps, restates, corrects or deletes claims; as its writer adds nothing
from the papers, it is no competitor on coverage but shows how much of what memory supplies
survives verification. The memory-only control is the memory draft with no papers, audited but
not repaired. The four strategies that read the papers are the reading strategies. The add-ons are
second-hand evidence on direct reading (passages from later open-access papers that describe a
paper not readable in full), a reconciled case database on case table first and a learned curation
guide on direct reading (two reflection rounds on 27 training entries).
Settings and guards. One model, Claude Opus 5.5, writes, extracts, audits and judges through a
command-line interface with schema-constrained output and no temperature or seed. Writers run
at high reasoning effort. In the main comparison, per-paper extraction in the evidence graph and
the case table runs at medium effort, and their synopsis terms (and the case table’s inheritance) are
assigned by rule; the audit and all judging also run at medium effort. A matched ablation raises
extraction to high effort and lets the writer choose synopsis terms (Section 4.2). For every strat-
egy, ontology identifiers come only from ontology-search candidates, gene symbols are normalised

3.3
DEFINITIONS, AUDIT, SCORING AND STATISTICS
Definitions. Each in-scope curator paragraph g was split once, without sight of any draft, into
a fixed list Φ(g) of atomic facts, reused for every strategy.
A judge marks each fact present
(conveyed), partly present (conveyed less precisely), absent or contradicted in what draft D says
in its paragraphs citing g’s papers. Over an entry’s facts, coverage is cov(D) = (#present +
1
2 #partly present)/#facts judged, and OMIM facts conveyed, the primary endpoint, is cov(D) av-
eraged over entries. A level is a mean over entries unless marked pooled (one ratio over all facts of
a group); paired differences are always means over entries. A fact is stated in the supplied text when
the judge gives a quote for it that is found verbatim in the text available for its paper, and otherwise
partly stated or not stated; this is a property of the text, the same for every strategy. A stopped reply
is one the model’s safety filter ended: it comes back empty, is recorded and is never retried with
a changed prompt, and its reasons are not visible. A strategy completes an entry when it writes a
draft with every paper processed. A stopped reply costs a one-call strategy the whole entry but a
per-paper strategy one paper, so under the symmetric rule strategies are compared only on entries
every compared strategy completed, and completion is reported as an outcome. A draft is repaired
after the audit’s one repair round and unrepaired before it; a comparison is like for like when its
arms share the same entries and the same repair state. Held out: the entries created in 2026 from all
development; the reading-better test split from the design of its prompts and from guide learning,
not from the development of the main strategies.
Audit. Each paragraph is split into atomic claims c attributed to the papers R(c) ⊆R it cites. Each
cited paper gives a verdict v(c, d) ∈{supported, partly supported, unsupported, contradicted} with a
quote; a “supported” verdict whose quote is not verbatim in the paper is downgraded. A claim takes
the best verdict over R(c) (Section 4.5 shows what this hides), and flagged claims (unsupported
or contradicted) get one repair round. Judged supported means a final verdict of “supported”, a
judgement of fidelity to the cited text. Unsupported is not wrong, so unsupported and contradicted
are reported separately.
Secondary measures (Appendix B): synopsis HPO terms (F1; exact, hierarchical, Lin similarity;
K¨ohler et al., 2009; Resnik, 1995), allelic variants (recall; precision, counting a variant OMIM does
not list as a miss), causal-gene F1 and inheritance agreement; predicting nothing where the reference
is non-empty scores zero. The primary endpoint has no length or precision counterpart, so it rewards
recall; we report draft length beside coverage gains.
Statistics. Paired differences from direct reading come with a percentile bootstrap 95% interval over
entries (10,000 resamples; Efron, 1979), entries better/worse/tied and an exact two-sided sign test
with ties dropped (Dixon & Mood, 1946). Sign tests on the eight secondary headline measures are
Holm-adjusted within each strategy (Holm, 1979); the primary endpoint is not adjusted across the
five comparisons with direct reading. The interval over entries is the test of a difference; run-to-run
noise, from three replicates of generation, audit and scoring on 30 entries, is a yardstick, not a test.
“Points” are percentage points.
Status of each analysis (Appendix Table 2). Pre-specified: the main comparison on the primary
endpoint over the symmetric set, the hidden-full-text experiment, and the first three reading-better
arms against their baseline. Choices made after results were seen: (a) fixed fact lists replaced
splitting afresh for each draft; (b) the symmetric rule, adopted after a first analysis had put the
evidence graph 2.1 points below direct reading (Appendix M.4); (c) the matched ablation; (d) the
restrictions by what the supplied text states, and the provenance of conveyed facts; (e) scoring the
hidden-full-text arm before repair; (f) re-auditing planted-test paragraphs unaltered; (g) a claim-
selection rule for deleted qualifiers widened after the run; (h) the fourth reading-better arm, designed
after the other arms’ held-out results were seen; (i) the reading-better analysis with stopped entries
put back. (a) and (b) apply to every result; (c)–(i) are marked post hoc. Exploratory: groupings
across entries, runs on 10 or fewer entries, and planted errors at 30 items per class. The rest is
secondary.

EXPERIMENTS

4.1
ACCESS TO THE SOURCE BOUNDS AGREEMENT
Hiding the full text of the same papers. The experiment used the 49 entries with a full-text paper
that an in-scope curator paragraph is about and hid all 91 of their full-text papers (the other 5 have
no in-scope paragraph, so hiding them could not change the score). Each entry was rewritten by
direct reading from the abstracts, with and without second-hand passages; 46 have a full-text draft
(the writer was stopped on 3). The facts scored are those of every in-scope paragraph about a
hidden paper: 136 paragraphs, 2,120 facts. The by-access tables class a paragraph about several
papers by its least readable paper, so 283 of these facts count as abstract or title only there. Like for
like, with every arm unrepaired (a post hoc rescoring of the full-text drafts before repair), full text
conveys 43.7 points more than the abstracts alone (+38.3 to +49.1; Figure 2a), better on 45 entries
and tied on one. Repairing the full-text arm, as first designed, moves it by 0.2 points (Appendix
Table 24). Coverage by access level across different papers (Appendix Table 13) is observational
and confounded with creation date and journal.
Second-hand passages substitute only in part. For the same hidden papers, passages from later
open-access papers that describe them add 4.9 points to the abstracts (+2.8 to +7.1); as an add-on on
96 entries they add 1.8 points (+1.0 to +2.8) but raise the share of claims unsupported before repair,
and of 112 claims about hidden papers resting on a passage, the full text leaves 11 unsupported (an
upper bound, as supplements were excluded) and contradicts 3 (Appendix G).
What the supplied text states (post hoc). The supplied text states three quarters of the facts about
full-text papers but only a quarter of those about abstract-only papers (pooled; Figure 2b). The
reading strategies convey 91.0–94.4% of the facts the text states and 5.5–6.9% of those it does not
(Figure 2c); on the latter they do no better than the memory-only control (8.2%). This contrast
agrees with the hidden-full-text result but holds partly by construction: “stated” needs a quote the
model found, so facts that need inference are more likely classed as not stated. On facts stated in
abstract-only papers most entries tie, so this restriction has a ceiling of its own (Appendix Table 19).

4.3
READING BETTER WITHIN WHAT ACCESS ALLOWS
Half of what direct reading misses in full-text papers is stated in their text, and the audit cannot see
omissions. Of ten approaches reviewed, we tested three variants of direct reading. Their prompts
were written on three training entries and frozen before the 41 held-out entries with a paper readable
in full were run. A checklist lists what a curator records about each paper. In a completeness pass,
a per-paper check lists findings the paper states but the draft omits, each with a quote verified in
the paper, and one revision adds only those. A three-draft merge keeps every distinct statement.
A fourth arm, the checklist with the completeness pass, was designed after the other arms’ held-
out results were seen and run on the same entries (post hoc). No arm is repaired; the baseline is
direct reading’s unrepaired draft. The first three arms are compared on the 38 entries all completed
(Holm-adjusted across the three), the fourth on the 35 all five completed (Appendix I).

to +3.6; 28/4/6; Holm-adjusted p < 0.001), and its drafts are longer: 14,412 characters per entry

the supplied text states, most of it on full-text papers (+4.5, +2.9 to +6.3; Figure 3b); coverage
of facts the text does not state does not change. The only precision-side check, the share of claims
judged supported, did not fall on the 20 audited entries (97.2% against 97.0%; no interval; Appendix
Table 33).

4.4
EXPLORATORY RUNS ON 10 OR FEWER ENTRIES
Released pipelines. PaperQA2’s document-query pipeline without its agent (Skarlinski et al., 2024),
GraphRAG with local search (Edge et al., 2024) and STORM (Shao et al., 2024), which produced
no article, ran at one setting each on the same papers and model (Appendix J; 10 and 4 entries).
Input size. Six entries were padded with unrelated full-text papers to about 500,000 and 1,200,000
characters; no draft cited an added paper, so this tests size, not crowding (Appendix K). On the 2
entries every strategy completed at every size, direct reading changed by +4.7 and +3.5 points, the
others by −3.0 to +2.1. On 6 entries allowing stopped calls on added papers, direct reading rose
3.6 points (+1.0 to +5.9) at about 500,000 characters and the others moved at most 1.0, leaving
them 2.8–4.6 points further behind (interaction intervals exclude zero, sign tests do not reach 0.05;
not at 1,200,000). Unpadded drafts are an earlier generation, so changes mix size with run-to-run
variation. Large entries. On four new entries with 76–148 references, the safety filter stopped at
least one strategy on every entry (Appendix K.2). 30–75 references. Ten compared entries, mostly
abstracts, give no findings (Appendix M.1). Each run shows directions and large effects only.

5
DISCUSSION, LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION
For one model, entries with 2–12 references whose papers fit one prompt, and 19% of papers read-
able in full, access to the source limits agreement with curator-written variant facts far more than
how a strategy reads. The regime favours direct reading (most papers are abstracts; 2,669 of 6,239
compared facts are not stated in the supplied text; stated full-text facts span 40 entries), yet the
same measurement resolved gains of a few points from reading better. Measurement lessons:
treat stopped replies symmetrically; fix fact lists before scoring; split coverage by what the text
states; report run-to-run noise and draft length beside intervals; compare designs on the same entries
and settings before crediting the representation; keep per-paper audit verdicts; check that released
pipelines run with the intended model.
Limitations, and what each missing check would establish. (i) No person has validated any judge-
ment, all made by one model family. Expert labels on a stratified sample, with chance-corrected
agreement, would establish whether the levels, strategy differences, stated/not-stated contrast and
audit catches hold (95.7% agreement between two judgings shows only self-consistency); differ-
ences of a few points are the most exposed to judge error, and a manual check of facts classed as not
stated would show how much of that contrast is mechanical. (ii) No second model family was run:
a judge from another family would test self-preference and correlated errors between the “stated”
and “conveyed” judgements, and a second writer whether the access effect and the order of strate-
gies transfer, notably to smaller-context models. The same model’s safety filter removed entries
unevenly across strategies for reasons not visible, so comparisons on completed entries condition
on an outcome of the strategy. (iii) Entries with few references dominate: input size was tested
only by padding with unrelated text, and large sets of relevant papers, where designs might separate,
could not be compared (stopped replies); matched settings ran on 38 entries, and each paradigm
has one implementation. With no margin set in advance, parity is untested; an equivalence analysis
against a pre-set margin would test it. (iv) The endpoint rewards recall, with no length or precision
counterpart; a length-matched baseline or length-controlled endpoint would show how much of the
reading-better gains exceeds writing more. (v) The reference is indirect and narrow (variant-centred
paragraphs, ontology annotations from two sources, gene and inheritance tables): about 24% of
a direct-reading draft’s statements have a counterpart in it (Appendix H), so most draft content is
checked only by the audit; the fact lists are not redistributed, and a fresh split’s stability is unmea-
sured. (vi) Every strategy is given the curators’ reference list; finding the literature was tested on
ten entries (Appendix M.3). (vii) Only open-access text without supplements was used, where much
variant and patient data lives. (viii) Variant names are not validated against reference sequences; in-
heritance is compared as coarse classes. (ix) The main strategies were developed on their evaluation
entries; held-out evidence is limited to the 2026 entries and the reading-better arms. (x) Each entry
was run once, without temperature or seed control; cost is at list price. (xi) The audit misses over-
citation, most broadened claims and omissions; a larger stress test would give per-class sensitivity,
and an any-cited-paper-fails rule applied to stored verdicts its flag rate. (xii) Released systems ran
at one setting each, PaperQA2 without its agent, STORM not at all; intended runs would test them
as released, and direct reading through the six questions would separate protocol from design.
Conclusion. Hiding the full text of the same papers lowers coverage of curator-written variant
facts from 79.8% to 36.1%. For entries with 2–12 references read in one prompt, no other design
conveyed more than direct reading at the main settings; which comes closest depends on entries and
settings. The audit checks fidelity, not completeness; no person validated any judgement.

ETHICS STATEMENT
The benchmark is built from public resources (NCBI, ClinVar, HPO, MedGen, Europe PMC,
HGNC). Curator-written text is used for scoring and is not redistributed; released score files carry
fact identifiers and statuses, not wording. Whether others can rebuild the reference and score against
the same fact lists depends on the rights holders’ terms of use for curated text, which apply to anyone
who does so. No human subjects were involved. Generated entries are drafts for expert review and
are not clinical guidance; they must be marked as machine generated and not as OMIM content. A
passed audit is not a check of correctness or completeness: it passes a draft that faithfully copies
a source’s error and does not detect omissions or over-citation, so an expert who trusts an audited
draft may miss both. No curator has checked any draft, and disagreements between drafts and the
reference reported here are unconfirmed.
REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT
Section 3 specifies the entry sampling (fixed seed, five creation-era bands, eligibility rules), the refer-
ence sources, the strategies, settings, guards, audit, scoring, statistics and the status of each analysis;
Appendix Table 2 gives the entry set and status of every experiment, Appendix B the model settings,
and Appendix J the released systems’ versions, entry points and every setting changed from their
defaults. One model (Claude Opus 5.5) was used through a command-line interface that exposes no
temperature or seed, so exact regeneration is not guaranteed; we report replicate and judge variation
instead (Appendix E). Replies are cached, so each call was made once; all experiments, pilots and
re-scoring used 45,291 cached model calls, 338 million input and 82 million output tokens, about
$3,587 at list price. The curator-written text and the fixed fact lists are not redistributed; every part
of the reference can be rebuilt from public resources under the rights holders’ terms, but a rebuilt
reference must be split again, and differences are comparable within one split. The harness and the
per-entry drafts, verdicts and scores are released as supplementary material. After the evaluation, the
released harness writes entries with the checklist and completeness pass by default where a paper is
readable in full, keeping the unrevised draft when the revision is stopped; this is a project decision,
not a finding of the study.

B
STRATEGIES, MODEL SETTINGS AND GUARDS
Table 4 describes each strategy.
Settings:
one model for everything, Claude Opus 5.5
(claude-opus-5-5), called through the Claude Code command-line interface in headless mode
with schema-constrained output; no temperature or seed is exposed. Reasoning effort is high for
writers (direct reading, the graph and table writers, the agent, the memory draft) and medium for
per-paper extraction in the evidence graph and case table first, for the audit and for all judging; the
matched ablation raises per-paper extraction to high. The agentic strategy has read and search tools
over a folder of the papers and nothing else. The agentic curator reports which papers it opened
(self-reported; tool calls are not logged): in the 98 entries it wrote it reported opening every paper,
and all 2,600 quotes it gave were found verbatim in the papers. The memory drafts that recall, then
verify starts from are the memory-only control’s drafts. The evidence graph discards any finding
whose quote is not in the paper (none was). Synopsis terms are assigned by a lexical search over the
ontology’s labels and synonyms that returns candidates, from which the model chooses. Secondary
measures: synopsis terms are compared with the HPO annotations exactly, hierarchically (both sets
expanded with all ancestors) and by Lin semantic similarity, an ontology-aware comparison in the
spirit of K¨ohler et al. (2009); Lin similarity uses information content computed from the ontology’s
disease annotations, averaged in each direction to give precision and recall. Allelic variants are
matched on normalised protein or nucleotide change, with a model judge for names that do not nor-
malise; precision counts a variant OMIM does not list as a miss whether or not the paper reports it.
No fact went unjudged in any run.
Table 4: The strategies: three paradigms, a control, three add-ons each tested against its parent, and
the four ways of reading better (variants of direct reading, Section 4.3). Guards for every strategy:
ontology identifiers chosen only from ontology-search candidates; gene symbols normalised against
HGNC; entry cross-reference numbers recalled from memory removed; every paragraph must cite a
supplied paper.

MEASUREMENT STABILITY
Judge variation: the same drafts and fact lists judged three times (top). Evidence graph minus direct
reading in the three judgings: +0.7/+0.5/+1.3 points; per-fact agreement between two judgings of
the same draft: 3,498 of 3,654 statuses (95.7%). Run-to-run variation: generation, audit and scoring
repeated three times on 30 entries (bottom). Evidence graph minus direct reading in replicates 1/2/3:
+0.7/+1.9/+0.5 points; within-entry SD of that paired difference 5.0 points, giving a replicate-noise
95% half-width of 1.0 point over 93 entries and 1.8 points over 30. One replicate is one sample; the
main comparison is a single run per entry. The half-width is a yardstick for how much a difference of
this kind moves when generation, audit and scoring are repeated; the bootstrap interval over entries
is the test of a difference. On these 30 entries the evidence graph is ahead of direct reading in all three
replicates and all three judgings, whereas on the 93 entries of the main comparison its difference is
−0.5 (−1.5 to +0.5). Replicate 1 reproduces the main run on these entries, so the difference in sign
comes from which entries are averaged, not from replicate noise; the replicates themselves range
from +0.5 to +1.9. Crediting a fact anywhere in the draft rather than only in text citing its paper
(same 30 entries): direct reading 46% against 47%, evidence graph 46% against 46%, case table
first 45% against 45%, so strategies that pool across papers do not lose credit under another citation.

ABLATION OF THE EXTRACT-THEN-WRITE STRATEGIES
The 40-entry subset contains every entry with at least half its papers readable in full that all reading
strategies completed, filled to 40 with entries drawn at random to bring each creation-era band to
8 entries; 22 of the 40 have at least half their papers readable in full and 29 have at least one, so
absolute levels are higher than in the main comparison. 38 were completed by all seven strategies.
The two changes in matched (high-effort extraction; writer-chosen synopsis terms and, for the ta-
ble, inheritance) were made together; since choosing synopsis terms cannot change which facts or
variants are extracted, attributing the change in facts, claims and variants to extraction effort is an
inference. Also giving the writer the papers’ text (plus text) raises claims judged supported, not facts
(Table 23).
The ablation set and the main comparison. The ablation reuses the main run’s drafts for direct
reading, the evidence graph and case table first: on all 38 entries the drafts and fact marks are
identical, so these rows give the main settings on the ablation’s entries, and they differ from the
main comparison’s estimates only in which entries are averaged. By the main comparison’s access
groups the 38 entries comprise 11 with no full text, 7 with under half, 19 with half or more and 1
with all papers in full text; all are among the main comparison’s 93 entries. At the main settings, the
evidence graph is +1.0 against direct reading on these 38 entries and case table first −2.3 (Table 21),
against −0.5 and −1.9 on all 93 (Table 1). Of the main comparison’s 21 entries with half or more full
text (Table 14), 19 are in the ablation set (the other two are among the entries a matched or plus-text
variant did not complete); on those 19 the evidence graph is at −1.2 (−3.8 to +1.3; better/worse/tied
7/10/2) and case table first at −6.2 (−8.6 to −3.5; 3/16/0). On the ablation’s other 19 entries (no
full text, under half, or all full text): evidence graph +3.1 (+1.0 to +5.6; 12/5/2), case table first
+1.5 (−1.1 to +4.7; 10/8/1). The matched evidence graph’s +1.8 against direct reading is therefore
mostly the +1.0 these entries already show at the main settings, with a settings step of +0.8 whose
interval includes zero; the clear settings effect is the case table’s step of +2.1.
Completion: direct reading, the evidence graph and case table first completed all 40; the matched
and plus-text graph variants each lost one entry and the matched and plus-text table variants two,
because the safety filter stopped a high-effort extraction call for a paper that had gone through at
medium effort. Generation cost per entry at list price: direct reading $0.32, evidence graph $0.65,
matched $0.72, plus text $0.88, case table first $0.72, matched $0.84, plus text $0.97; with audit
and scoring: $1.59, $2.00, $2.14, $2.25, $2.07, $2.26, $2.29. Variant precision (share of a draft’s
variants that are OMIM’s) in this ablation: direct reading 87.8%, evidence graph 81.4%, graph plus
text 84.4%, case table first matched 76.5%, case table first plus text 85.0%.

Open-access papers that cite and describe each target paper were searched for all 515 papers through
Europe PMC; passages (paragraphs or table rows whose citation points at the target paper) were
found for 473 papers (326 of 345 abstract-only, 53 of 74 title-only, 94 of 96 full-text): 2,430 pas-
sages. The hidden-full-text experiment used the 49 entries with a full-text paper that an in-scope
curator paragraph is about and hid all 91 of their full-text papers (81 of the 96 full-text papers have
an in-scope paragraph); the other 5 full-text papers, in 5 entries, have no in-scope paragraph, so hid-
ing them could not change the score. All 91 hidden papers have citing papers, 89 have open-access
citing papers and at least one passage, and they received 523 passages. Over all 49 entries the two
new conditions convey 35.3% (abstract only) and 41.1% (abstract plus second-hand) under the ear-
lier scoring, pooled over facts. Under the earlier scoring facts are split separately for each condition,
so the fact counts differ slightly.

RECONCILED CASE DATABASE AND LEARNED CURATION GUIDE
Reconciliation. Over the 100 entries, reconciliation proposed 57 merges of patient or family records
and accepted 40 (17 rejected by the verifier) in 27 entries; it merged 11 variant names and found 57
conflicts between papers. Counted individuals went from 2,580 to 2,413 (net; counts rose in a few
entries where a re-report gave a larger family size) and families from 1,126 to 1,092. A planted test
on six entries merged 10 of 10 copied reports with their originals and merged no unrelated records;
this easy case shows only that a stated re-report is caught. The accepted merges were not checked
by a person. Cost: one or two extra calls per entry. None of the entry-level measures uses patient
counts, so no change in them is expected. Agreement of the table’s patient-count fractions with
those in the HPO annotations (about 560 terms) was 48% before and after, with a mean absolute
difference of 0.26; each reference fraction comes from one source while the table pools all papers.
Learned guide. Training (27 of 30 training entries had a direct-reading draft; scored without the
audit, with the earlier per-draft fact splitting): baseline OMIM facts conveyed 53.8% and synopsis
exact F1 36.6%. Round 1 changed facts by +0.14 points and synopsis F1 by +1.29; round 2 by
−1.41 and +3.57. The text rules of round 1 and the synopsis rules of round 2 were selected by a rule
fixed in advance. The final guide, run once more on the training entries: facts +0.06 points, synopsis
F1 +1.28 points. A first guide, learned before stopped replies were recorded, had treated three empty
drafts as omissions and was discarded. On the held-out entries, a draft makes about twice as many
statements about a paper as the OMIM paragraph has facts (direct reading 24.0 statements per paper,
with the guide 22.2, against 11.9 facts), and 24% (direct) and 25% (guide) of a draft’s statements
have a counterpart in that paragraph; the paragraph is variant-centred, so this is a lower bound on
agreement with the entry.

READING BETTER: FULL RESULTS
Design. Ten approaches were reviewed; the three judged most likely to help at feasible cost (check-
list, completeness pass, three-draft merge) were implemented, piloted on three training entries with
the prompts then frozen, and evaluated on the 41 entries of the held-out test split that have at least
one paper readable in full and a direct-reading draft. That split was held out from the design of these
prompts and from guide learning, not from the development of the main strategies. The checklist
with the completeness pass starts from the checklist draft and applies the completeness pass; it was
designed after the other arms’ held-out results were seen and evaluated on the same entries (post
hoc), and the other arms were not re-run. No arm has a repair round, and the baseline is direct
reading’s draft before repair. The first three arms are compared with the baseline on the 38 entries
complete for every arm (Table 30; sign tests Holm-adjusted across the three arms); the combined
arm on the 35 entries complete for all five (Table 31; against the baseline as its first comparison,
against the completeness pass and the checklist as secondary ones, unadjusted). Whether the sup-
plied text states a fact is the stored availability judgement (a model judgement with verified quotes,
not checked by a curator). Claims were audited without repair on a seeded sample of 20 of the 41
entries (Table 33); a stopped audit call leaves its paper’s claims not checkable and does not affect
completion. The approach with the largest expected effect, reading papers that are free to read but
not openly licensed (about a third of all curator facts concern such papers), could not be tested, as
only openly licensed text is used.
What the arms did. The completeness pass, on 38 entries, listed 768 omissions and kept 767
whose quote was verified; the revision ran on all 38 and added 959 sentences, none citing only
papers without listed omissions; no carried-over field was changed and no citation was lost. The
merge, on 41 entries, merged 2,486 draft paragraphs into 852 (807 from more than one draft); none
was rejected as longer than its sources, no unknown identifier or unsourced paragraph occurred,
nothing was left out, and no citation was lost; roughly, 3,454 of 3,550, 3,401 of 3,556 and 3,324 of
3,479 sentences of the three drafts have at least 85% of their content words in the merged draft. On
the 37 entries where both completeness arms wrote a draft, the combined arm listed 600 omissions
against 736 for the completeness pass alone (kept 600 against 735; fewer on 27 entries, more on
6, equal on 4), added 730 sentences against 908 and completed 118 existing sentences against 126;
it added no sentence citing only papers without listed omissions, changed no carried-over field and
lost no citation.

pleted. Table 32 keeps all 41 entries and puts back the entries the completeness arms lost to a
stopped revision in two ways. If the draft the revision would have revised is scored in their place,

RELEASED SYSTEMS: VERSIONS, SETTINGS AND FULL RESULTS
(EXPLORATORY)
These runs, like those of Appendices K and L, were made on 20–21 November 2026 with the same
model, reply cache and scoring as the rest of the study; a reply stopped by the model’s safety filter
was recorded and never retried with a changed prompt. Their sizes were reduced from the design
before any run finished (for example 10 instead of 24 entries for PaperQA2 and STORM), and some
ended below even the reduced size because entries cost more model calls than projected. Every result
here is exploratory and shows directions and large effects only. No person checked any judgement.
How the systems were run. Each system (Table 37) ran on exactly the entry’s supplied papers, at the
access level each paper has, through a local adapter in front of the one model, each at one setting with
its own code, prompts and defaults, changed only where integration required (Tables 38 and 39). All
three used one small local embedding model (Xiao et al., 2023); the interface ignores temperature
and length limits; and PaperQA2’s agent needs function calling, which the adapter does not provide.
PaperQA2 and GraphRAG, both question-answering systems, were asked six fixed section questions
per entry and their answers were concatenated; the strategies write an OMIM-style entry in one
pass. GraphRAG’s answers include headings and framing lines as separate blocks; all text counts
for crediting anywhere in the draft and for the share without a mapped citation. Citations were
mapped back to the supplied papers without editing the text: PaperQA2 names papers; GraphRAG’s
local search cites text units, entities and relationships (a block’s papers are the union over every
record it cites, counting only records the query showed the model); global search cites community
reports only, each mapped to the papers of the text units its community’s entities came from, a
looser mapping. Facts are credited to paragraphs citing the paper, as in the main comparison, and,
as a check that does not depend on the mapping, anywhere in the draft (Tables 36–40). The two are
separate judge passes, so the second can fall below the first; it did on 5 system entries, by differences
within the judge’s noise. The claim-level audit without repair was run on every paragraph with
mapped citations of the 4 entries both systems completed; the strategies’ figures there are their
stored audits before repair (Table 43). Table 35 summarises the comparison with direct reading.
What the comparison shows. PaperQA2 and STORM ran on the first 10 entries of a seeded order
and GraphRAG on the first 4 that fitted its call budget; only the strategies’ drafts had been through
the repair round. On these entries and settings, PaperQA2 conveyed 14.5 points fewer curator-
written facts than direct reading (−21.8 to −7.0; worse on 9 of 10 entries, sign test p = 0.021) and
GraphRAG with local search 13.4 fewer (−25.3 to −1.5; with 4 entries the sign test cannot reach
0.05), mostly on facts the supplied text states (Table 35). Both wrote more text at several to many
times the generation calls and cost, and fewer of their claims were judged supported (91.4% and
89.8% against 98.3%; pooled, without repair, 4 entries). So on these 10 and 4 entries, at one setting
each, neither released pipeline conveyed more curator-written facts than direct reading. With so
few entries, one setting per system, PaperQA2 without its agent, six concatenated answers against
one-pass writing, and unrepaired against repaired drafts, this supports no ranking of the systems
or of their designs, and says nothing about their published results. STORM produced no article:
the model’s safety filter stopped every chain-of-thought prompt of its research stage while its plain
prompts were answered, and nothing was reworded or retried. Released pipelines thus depend on
the model behind them in ways a model-agnostic description hides.
Deviations from the design. (1) Sizes: PaperQA2 and STORM on the first 10 entries of the seeded
order instead of 24; GraphRAG within 1,000 model calls (floor 6 entries) instead of 3,000 (floor
12). GraphRAG stopped at 4 entries when the fifth did not fit what was left of the cap (698 calls
were made in all, including the global-search queries and a test run). (2) PaperQA2 ran without
its agent, as the document-query pipeline (the package’s manual, no-agent usage): its default agent
selects tools by function calling, and its alternative agent ends with a function-calling step, which
the adapter cannot serve. Retrieval, contextual summaries and the answer step are PaperQA2’s
own; the agent’s iterative search and its decision to stop are not exercised. (3) The shared local
embedding model truncates its input at 512 tokens, so the first PaperQA2 run embedded only the
first about 510 tokens of each about 5,000-character chunk. PaperQA2 was run again with whole-
chunk embeddings (windows of 510 tokens, averaged), which is the main result; the first run is kept
as a sensitivity result. Requests identical between the two runs were served from the cache in the
second, so its fresh calls understate a stand-alone run; calls, tokens and cost count each distinct
request once, with the figures it had when made. (4) GraphRAG’s global search was added on the

INPUT PADDING AND LARGE ENTRIES (EXPLORATORY)
K.1
CONTROLLED PADDING OF THE INPUT
Design. The entries are the ablation entries that every ablation variant completed and that have at
least half their papers readable in full, taken in entry-number order; all four strategies had completed
them in the main run. The added papers are full-text papers already fetched for other entries, exclud-
ing the entry’s own papers, papers cited by any entry sharing a causal gene with it, papers whose
text names one of its genes, papers whose citing entry has no reference record (gene unknown), and
papers over 180,000 characters (the extraction cap). They are drawn in a seeded order per entry; the
additions of level P1 (about 500,000 characters) are a prefix of those of P2 (about 1,200,000), and
each level stops at the paper that brings the total nearest its target (sizes in Table 44). The supplied
order is shuffled with the entry’s seed, but direct reading and the agent’s index sort papers by year
and the extract-then-write strategies read each paper separately, so order has little effect. The task
prompt is unchanged. Level P0 (no padding) uses the main run’s drafts before repair, rebuilt from
the model cache without new generation (replaying the cached audit and repair on them reproduces
the stored final drafts exactly); P1 and P2 are fresh generations. No level was audited or repaired.
Coverage is that of the entry’s own fixed facts, credited to paragraphs citing the paper (present plus
half of partly present, over judged facts). Completion follows the main rule, so a per-paper extrac-
tion failure makes a draft incomplete; every such failure here was a safety-filter stop on an added
paper. The primary analysis applies the symmetric rule, under which 2 entries remain (Table 46); a
secondary analysis allows failures on added papers and keeps 6 (Table 47; completion in Table 45).
The unpadded drafts are an earlier generation with no replicate, so a change from P0 mixes input size
with run-to-run variation (within-entry standard deviation of single-entry coverage in the replicate
runs: 2.5 points for direct reading, 4.0 for the evidence graph; Table 20).
What the strategies did with the added papers. No paragraph of any padded draft cited an added
paper (0 of 115–140 paragraphs per strategy and level), and none attributed a statement about the
disorder to one. The agentic curator opened every one of the entry’s own papers at every level, 5 of
63 added papers at P1 and none of 174 at P2. The extract-then-write strategies marked 59 papers
not relevant at P1 and 159 (evidence graph) and 163 (case table first) at P2. Direct reading’s prompt
capped each paper at 75,000–300,000 characters at P0 (one own paper cut) and at 33,333–66,666
at P1 (two own papers, both in one entry, and 24 added papers cut); at P2 it was split into three
prompts and a merge, with no paper cut and none lost in the merge. Extraction calls were stopped
for 1 (evidence graph) and 3 (case table first) papers at P1 and for 3 and 5 at P2.
Result, symmetric rule (primary; 2 entries). On the 2 entries every strategy completed at every
level, direct reading’s coverage rose by 4.7 points at about 500,000 characters and 3.5 at about
1,200,000; the agentic curator changed by +0.4 and +2.1, the evidence graph by +0.8 and −1.2,
case table first by −3.0 at both, and direct reading stayed first at every level (Table 46). With 2
entries no interval is computed and no sign test can reach 0.05.
Result, failures on added papers allowed (secondary; 6 entries). Direct reading rose by 3.6 points
(+1.0 to +5.9) at about 500,000 characters and by 3.2 (−0.7 to +6.8) at about 1,200,000; the other
strategies moved by at most 1.0 point at either size (Table 47). So at the first size they fell behind
direct reading by a further 2.8 (agentic curator), 3.8 (evidence graph) and 4.6 points (case table first),
with interaction intervals excluding zero but no sign test below 0.05 (p = 0.2188, 0.375, 0.2188);
at the second size every interaction interval includes zero. With 6 entries these results are indicative

LARGE ENTRIES
Selection and deviations. New eligible phenotype entries with many references were selected by
a fixed rule with a seed; the design called for a sample of 400 candidates and 16 entries, while the
sample was up to 200 and 8 entries were selected. Only the first 4 in rule order were run: an entry
cost about 250–330 new model calls, most of them per-paper extraction for the evidence graph and
the case table, and the cap of 1,500 new calls allowed no more (1,338 were made). The selection
was resolved in three passes across machine restarts, and the first three entries were run once their
place among the 8 was certain. The comparison on facts the supplied text states was not made. Direct
reading holds 600,000 characters in one prompt, cutting each paper to the larger of 20,000 characters
and 600,000 divided by the number of papers; above that, papers are split into batches of that size
and the partial drafts merged, and only a paper longer than a whole batch is cut. Each per-paper
extraction reads at most 180,000 characters; no paper of these entries exceeded it. The new entries
were generated and scored without audit or repair, whereas the 12 earlier entries (Appendix M.1)
were scored after repair.
Outcome. No new entry was completed by every strategy, so the tier’s comparison rests on the 10
earlier entries, with the figures of Table 54. Table 49 gives the new entries; its coverage figures
describe drafts written without the papers whose extraction was stopped and are not a comparison.
On the one new entry where every strategy wrote a draft, direct reading conveyed 52.3%, the agentic
curator 51.5%, case table first 49.6% (2 papers lost) and the evidence graph 39.8% (3 papers lost):
one entry, not a result.

AUDIT COVERAGE: FULL RESULTS
Single values and the unaltered re-audit. In 60 paragraphs the audit had passed, one fact was
altered by rule (a count, a zygosity, a residue or a mode of inheritance). The audit flagged all 60,
each on a claim containing the altered value; in 6 of the 60, other claims were also flagged. The
test measures sensitivity to blunt single-fact substitutions only. The 60 paragraphs were then audited
again as written (post hoc), with fresh model calls and the procedure of the planted test: none had a
claim flagged; of 361 claims none was flagged (0 unsupported, 0 contradicted), 6 came back partly
supported and 3 could not be audited; no reply was stopped. The paragraphs were selected for
having passed the audit once, so this is the rate at which a re-audit flags a paragraph that passed
before, not the false-flag rate on arbitrary paragraphs.

by all classes (Table 50). Citation swaps and added citations change the in-text author–year label
as well as the cited paper; the new paper is a random readable paper of another entry (swap to
another entry, added citation) or another readable paper of the same entry (swap within the entry).
A moved sentence names a variant, carries no author citation and is inserted at a seeded sentence
boundary after the first sentence of the target paragraph, whose paper must not already name that
variant. Truth is decided mechanically from the text the auditor reads (title, abstract and full text
cut at 180,000 characters): a variant occurs in a paper if any of its protein or nucleotide forms, its
residue or nucleotide position, or its raw name does. A same-entry swap is truth-known when the
paragraph names at least one variant absent from the new paper, a moved sentence when none of
its variants occurs in the target paper. Qualifiers are deleted by five rules (count, hedge, negation,
single family or patient, zygosity; Table 51). All 30 qualifier edits were read before the run: 5 of 8
hedge edits change the claim, 2 are nearly meaning-preserving and 1 is degenerate; all 8 negation
edits reverse the claim (so they plant contradictions, not broadening); about 3 of 8 zygosity edits
are nearly meaning-preserving where de novo or dominant inheritance implies heterozygosity; 2 of
4 count edits are clean, 1 ungrammatical and 1 awkward; both single-family edits are clean. No
rule mostly produced ungrammatical or meaning-preserving edits, so all five were kept. The planted
claim is every claim for swaps to another entry and added citations, claims naming the absent variant
for same-entry swaps and moved sentences, and claims taken from the altered sentence for deleted
qualifiers; an item counts as flagged when any planted claim is unsupported or contradicted. The
unaltered paragraphs of all classes (113) were audited once, fresh, as the shared control. The audit
used its own prompts and best-verdict rule, on items in chunks of 5 paragraphs (the single-value test
audited its 60 together).
Completeness check. A separate check lists findings a cited paper states that the draft’s text about
that paper omits, each with a quote that is verified against the paper. It was run on 30 planted
deletions (a sentence other than the first, carrying a variant or a number, years and entry numbers
aside, that appears nowhere else in the draft’s text about that paper), on the same texts unaltered,
and on natural omissions: 60 curator facts (a seeded sample, facts of papers readable in full first,
of 130 eligible) that direct-reading drafts omitted although the text the writer had states them, on
28 papers. A listed omission matches a deleted sentence mechanically when it names one of the
sentence’s variants or carries all its numbers, and by judgement when the model judge says it does
(Table 52).
Citation precision. The main run’s entry files keep only each claim’s best verdict. Verdicts per cited
paper were recovered without new calls by replaying the stored audit from the model-reply cache,
which is possible only for entries whose paragraphs the repair round left unchanged (about 80–88%
of entries per strategy, and every memory-only entry, which has no repair); a paragraph was kept
only if the replayed best verdict of every claim equals the stored one (Table 53).
Deviations and cost. 30 items per class instead of about 60, and at most 600 new model calls
instead of 1,200, pilot included; natural omissions capped at 60 facts; the audit run on chunks of
5 paragraphs rather than on all items together; one control audit for all classes. The qualifier rules
were tightened once before the pilot after reading the edits they produced, and after the main run
the rule that picks the planted claims for deleted qualifiers was widened (a claim also counts when it
contains two of the content words next to the deletion point), which recovered two flagged negation
items, with no new calls. The mechanical matching of the completeness check was corrected after
the pilot (number words and thousands separators read as digits), with no new calls. The run made
540 new model calls (cap 600) at a list price of $43.7849; 2 replies were stopped by the safety filter,
both on the completeness check of one item (its altered and unaltered text), so the completeness
figures are over 29 items.

Of the 178 claims with an added citation, the claim-splitting step attributed 176 to the added paper,
and that paper’s own verdict was “unsupported” on all 176 (2 not attributed): the per-paper verdict
sees the unrelated paper, but the claim’s best verdict hides it. The shared control: 113 unaltered
paragraphs, none flagged; 733 claims, none flagged, 15 partly supported.

references and one in-scope paragraph, and the 30 newest were run, chosen by creation date alone
(newest qualifying entries by creation month, then entry number). They were used in no other exper-

were generated: rerun with the final implementation, the five strategies issue only the original model
calls and reproduce the stored drafts exactly. They are therefore a sample not used in developing

ANALYSES THAT WERE CORRECTED
Before fixed fact lists were introduced, the judge split each OMIM paragraph afresh for each draft;
that scoring is kept as a secondary row (“facts split afresh for each draft”, Table 6), runs 2–5 points
higher and gives the same ordering. The first analysis excluded entries whose writer was stopped
but scored entries in which a per-paper call was stopped; under it the evidence graph appeared 2.1
points below direct reading on OMIM facts conveyed, and with completion treated the same way
for every strategy the difference is not distinguishable from zero. A bug in variant naming wrote
