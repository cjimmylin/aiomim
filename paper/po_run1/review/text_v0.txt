ABSTRACT
Designs that turn papers into structured, cited text have been evaluated with dif-
ferent models, sources and scoring, so their results cannot be compared. We build
a controlled benchmark in which the generation strategy is the only variable: each
of 100 OMIM phenotype entries is written from exactly the papers its curators
cited, causal gene withheld, by one model under the same guards, claim-level au-
dit and scoring. Three paradigms (read and write; extract, then write; memory
first), a memory-only control and three add-ons are scored on coverage of fixed
fact lists from curator-written per-paper text (OMIM facts conveyed). Hiding the
full text of the same papers lowers coverage from 77.4% to 31.2% (46 entries;
paired difference +43.9 points, 95% interval +38.5 to +49.3). On the 93 entries
every strategy completed, the reading strategies are close (47.2–49.1%; replicate
noise ±1.0 point) and memory-first strategies convey 15.1% and 14.6%; differ-
ences grow where more full text is readable. The audit flagged all 60 planted
single-fact errors but checks fidelity to the cited text, not correctness. One model
family wrote, audited and judged everything, and no human validated any judge-
ment.

INTRODUCTION
Curated knowledge bases are written by experts who read papers and record, entry by entry, what
each paper showed. Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), the reference catalogue of
human genes and genetic disorders (Amberger et al., 2014; 2019; Hamosh et al., 2021), is built
this way, and so, in part, are the resources that record variant interpretations (Landrum et al., 2017;
2025), phenotype annotations (Robinson et al., 2008; Gargano et al., 2024) and the validity of gene–
disease associations (Strande et al., 2017). Automated text mining already annotates genes, variants
and diseases across PubMed abstracts and open-access full text (Wei et al., 2019; 2024a; Allot et al.,
2023), and language models can now read the papers themselves. Many system designs have been
proposed for turning a set of documents into structured, cited text: retrieval-augmented or long-
context reading (Lewis et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024b), extraction into a graph or a table before writing
(Edge et al., 2024; Shankar et al., 2025; Joshi et al., 2026), agents that decide what to read (Nakano
et al., 2021; L´ala et al., 2023; Skarlinski et al., 2024), drafting first and verifying afterwards (Gao
et al., 2022; Dhuliawala et al., 2024), and prompts optimised from examples (Khattab et al., 2023;
Agrawal et al., 2026); reviews of generative models for evidence synthesis and literature review
document the spread of such tools (Clark et al., 2025; Scherbakov et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2025a).
These designs have been evaluated on different tasks, with different models, retrieval stacks and
scoring, so their results cannot be compared, and the closest work in genetics and biology varies
several of these at once or scores against something other than what curators wrote about each paper
(Section 2; Zhang et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026; Skarlinski et al., 2024; Groza et al., 2026). Three
questions therefore remain open for evidence-grounded knowledge-base writing. With the model,
the sources, the guards and the scoring held fixed, how much does the generation strategy matter?

What limits agreement with what experts wrote: the strategy, or what the strategy was given to read?
And which errors does a claim-level audit against the cited source catch, and which can it not see?
We answer them with a controlled benchmark in which the generation strategy is the only variable
(Figure 1). Each of 100 OMIM phenotype entries with 2–12 references is written from its header
and exactly the papers its curators cited, with the causal gene withheld; only 19% of the cited papers
are readable in full through open access. One model writes every draft under the same guards, audit
and scoring. The strategies form three paradigms (read and write; extract, then write; memory first),
one control (a memory-only draft with no papers) and three add-ons, each tested against its parent.
The primary endpoint is OMIM facts conveyed: coverage of the facts in OMIM’s allelic-variant
descriptions as deposited in ClinVar, curator-written, variant-centred paragraphs about individual
cited papers, each split once, without sight of any draft, into a fixed list of facts (6,652 in all over
476 paragraphs) reused for every strategy.
At current open access, the reading strategies are close, and access to the source bounds agree-
ment. When the full text of papers that have one is hidden and the same entries are rewritten
from their abstracts, coverage of the same fixed facts falls from 77.4% to 31.2% (paired difference
+43.9 points, +38.5 to +49.3). On the 93 entries every strategy completed, only the case table’s
difference from direct reading (−1.9, −3.1 to −0.8) exceeds the replicate-noise half-width of 1.0
point; differences grow where more full text is readable, and in a matched ablation the gap of our
extract-then-write implementations depends on extraction effort.
Our contributions are: (1) a benchmark and harness for evidence-grounded knowledge-base writing
against curator-written references, built only from public resources (Sayers et al., 2026; Louden,
2020; Rosonovski et al., 2024; Seal et al., 2023), with a claim-level audit, fixed fact lists and per-
entry release of every draft, verdict and score; (2) a controlled comparison under one model that
reports completion (replies stopped by the model’s safety filter are an outcome, not an exclusion),
variance between repeated runs and matched ablations; (3) evidence, led by a within-paper experi-
ment, that what the strategy can read bounds agreement with curators; (4) a characterisation of what
the audit can and cannot see; and (5) a memory-only control by creation date as a contamination
check, with negative results, at this scale, for recall-then-verify, a small learned guide and reconcili-
ation. The study is scoped to what was run: one model family wrote, audited and judged everything;
no curator has read any draft, verdict or fact judgement; only open-access text without supplemen-
tary material was available; and every strategy was given the curators’ reference list. The generated
entries are drafts for expert review, not clinical guidance.

RELATED WORK
We organise prior work along the axes our strategies vary. Published systems were not run here; the
strategies we compare are our own implementations of the ideas below.
Language models for literature curation in genetics and biology.
Concept recognition against
the Human Phenotype Ontology has moved from dedicated taggers (Luo et al., 2021; Groza et al.,
2024b) to language models (Groza et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2024; Garcia et al., 2025). For populat-
ing and maintaining knowledge bases, structured prompting and assistant tools have been proposed
(Caufield et al., 2024b;a; Toro et al., 2024; Niyonkuru et al., 2025), as have an agent that assists
manual gene function annotation (Li et al., 2025c), generative annotation of the severity of ontology
phenotypes (Murphy et al., 2024), and agents for ontology curation of natural phenotypes (Balhoff
& Lapp, 2026). Closer to clinical genetics, language models have been applied to extracting variant,
gene–disease and gene–phenotype evidence from papers (Li et al., 2025b; Furlan et al., 2025; Wang
& Wang, 2026; Yin et al., 2026), benchmarked on clinical-genetics reasoning (Queen et al., 2025),
given database tools for genomics questions (Jin et al., 2023), and evaluated for gene-set function
(Hu et al., 2025), gene prioritisation (Kim et al., 2024) and rare-disease diagnostics (Twede et al.,
2026). The four closest studies differ from ours in what is held fixed and what serves as the refer-
ence. FlyAOC scores memorisation, pipeline and agent harnesses against FlyBase curators (Zhang
et al., 2026); GEN-KnowRD generates schema-guided rare-disease profiles, assesses their quality
against curated knowledge and builds a knowledge base from them (Yan et al., 2026); WikiCrow
writes gene articles compared against Wikipedia (Skarlinski et al., 2024); and Groza et al. (2026)
measure model memory of rare-disease genes and phenotypes. In our benchmark the cited papers

Intermediate representations versus long-context reading.
Retrieval augmentation supplies a
model with selected passages (Lewis et al., 2020), possibly retrieving on demand and critiquing its
own output (Asai et al., 2023). Whether this beats placing all documents in a long context is con-
tested: one re-evaluation finds long context generally ahead on question answering, especially for
Wikipedia-based questions (Li et al., 2024a); others argue that very long contexts dilute focus on
relevant information (Yu et al., 2024), propose hybrids (Li et al., 2024b), or report no single win-
ner (Li et al., 2025a). Graph intermediates are used for query-focused summarisation (Edge et al.,
2024) and retrieval inspired by hippocampal indexing (Guti´errez et al., 2024), and systematic com-
parisons ask when graphs help (Han et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025). Table- and database-centred
pipelines rewrite document-processing operations agentically (Shankar et al., 2025), reconcile ex-
tracted databases for systematic reviews (Joshi et al., 2026) or carry out deep research over large
databases (Liu et al., 2026). Agentic and retrieval-augmented systems write cited answers or articles
from the scientific literature (L´ala et al., 2023; Asai et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2025;
Skarlinski et al., 2024). These comparisons are on question answering, summarisation or open re-
trieval, where the model, the retrieval and the scoring change together; we hold the papers fixed and
report a matched ablation that separates the intermediate representation from extraction effort.
Memory, verification and contamination.
Draft-then-verify methods plan verification questions
(Dhuliawala et al., 2024), find attribution and post-edit unsupported content (Gao et al., 2022), detect
hallucination from the consistency of sampled outputs (Manakul et al., 2023), or check gene-set
analyses against domain databases (Wang et al., 2025b). Because curated knowledge bases are
public, memory is also a confound. Surveys catalogue contamination and its detection (Xu et al.,
2024; Deng et al., 2024; Ravaut et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025); methods detect
pretraining data or contamination from black-box access (Shi et al., 2023; Oren et al., 2024; Golchin
& Surdeanu, 2024; 2025), exploit training cut-offs as a natural experiment (Roberts et al., 2023), or
collect new test items over time (Jain et al., 2024; White et al., 2024). We adopt the time-based view
within the task: a memory-only control analysed by entry creation date, and entries created in 2026.
Supervision by prompt optimisation.
Instructions can be searched or optimised by language
models (Zhou et al., 2022; Pryzant et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023), evolved (Guo et al., 2023; Fer-
nando et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2026), compiled with demonstrations in multi-stage programs
(Khattab et al., 2023; Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024), or updated from textual feedback (Yuksekgonul
et al., 2024). Our learned curation guide is a deliberately small instance of this idea; its result says
nothing about better-resourced prompt optimisation.
Evaluating cited long-form text.
Factuality metrics decompose long-form text into atomic claims
and verify each against a knowledge source (Min et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024b), and VeriScore
restricts this to verifiable claims (Song et al., 2024). Citation quality has been measured for models
that generate text with citations (Gao et al., 2023), in human audits of generative search engines
that check whether statements are supported by their citations (Liu et al., 2023), and for medical
references (Wu et al., 2025); the accuracy with which medical articles quote their sources has itself
been reviewed systematically (Jergas & Baethge, 2015), which bears on our second-hand evidence.
Work studies model judges against human preferences (Zheng et al., 2023; Shankar et al., 2024) and
reports that evaluators recognise and favour their own generations (Panickssery et al., 2024). Our
audit and fact judgements use the same model family that wrote the drafts, with no human validation;
we therefore report judge and replicate variation, fixed fact lists shared by all strategies and planted-
error tests, and we describe audit verdicts as judged supported. Text mining yields more from full
text than from abstracts, whose structure and content differ (Cohen et al., 2010; Westergaard et al.,
2018); our access experiments measure this effect for knowledge-base writing.

BENCHMARK AND STRATEGIES
3.1
TASK, ENTRIES AND REFERENCE
An entry is e = (h, R) with header h (number, title and alternative titles) and references
R = {d1, . . . , dn}, the papers its curators cited.
Each paper has an access level a(d) ∈
{full text, abstract, title} determined by open access. A strategy S maps (h, R) to a draft D: para-
graphs, each citing a non-empty subset of R; a set of genes; a set of inheritance modes; a set of
ontology terms; and a set of allelic variants. The causal gene is withheld, because naming it from
the papers is part of the curator’s task.
Entries and papers. We draw 100 OMIM phenotype entries at random with a fixed seed, 20 from
each of five bands of entry number that track when the entry was created (1xxxxx–5xxxxx, 600000–
609999, 610000–615999, 616000–619999, 620000 onward). Eligible entries have 2–12 cited ref-
erences, a causal gene in the gene map, at least five phenotype annotations and at least one curator
paragraph in scope, so the sample is stratified and selects well-annotated, molecularly characterised
entries. Of the 515 cited papers, 96 (19%) are readable in full through PubMed Central or Europe
PMC (Rosonovski et al., 2024), 345 as an abstract only and 74 as a title only; supplementary material
was not retrieved.
Reference. All parts come from public resources. The curator-written per-paper text is OMIM’s
allelic-variant descriptions as deposited by OMIM in ClinVar (Landrum et al., 2025): variant-centred
prose from OMIM’s gene entries about one or more cited papers. A paragraph is in scope for
an entry when it is about a paper the entry cites; 476 are in scope. The narrative sections of the
phenotype entries are not available publicly and are not compared. The clinical-synopsis reference
is the Human Phenotype Ontology’s disease annotations (Gargano et al., 2024): 2,311 annotations,
of which 1,835 are the HPO team’s own curation from papers and 476 are taken from OMIM. The
causal gene comes from OMIM’s gene map via MedGen (Louden, 2020) and mim2gene; inheritance
comes from HPO and MedGen modes, compared as coarse classes (autosomal dominant, autosomal
recessive, X-linked, mitochondrial and others), with the sets required to be equal. The allelic-variant
reference is the entry’s 427 in-scope OMIM-submitted ClinVar records.

3.2
STRATEGIES
The strategies are three paradigms, one control and three add-ons (Figure 1; Appendix Table 3).
Read and write: direct reading puts all papers in one prompt and is the baseline; the agentic
curator works in a folder of the papers with read and search tools and must give a verbatim quote
for every paragraph. Extract, then write: the evidence graph extracts quote-backed findings per
paper, discards findings whose quote is not in the paper (none was discarded in this run), and joins
them into a graph of papers, genes, variants, phenotypes and inheritance that is the writer’s only
input, with synopsis terms computed from the graph by rule; case table first extracts one record per
reported patient or family per paper and pools them, computes synopsis terms and inheritance by
rule, and has the writer confirm candidate genes from the table alone. Memory first: recall, then
verify writes from memory, audits the draft against the papers, and rewrites it keeping supported
claims, restating partly supported ones, correcting contradicted ones and deleting the rest; it may
not add content from the papers. The memory-only control is the memory draft with no papers,
audited but not repaired. The add-ons are second-hand evidence on direct reading (for a paper not
readable in full, passages from later open-access papers that cite and describe it, marked as second-
hand), the reconciled case database on the case table (a propose, verify, apply step that merges the
same patient, family or variant across papers and settles conflicts), and a learned curation guide
on direct reading (general rules learned in two reflection rounds from the omissions and extras of
27 training entries, tested on held-out entries). These are our own implementations of the ideas in
Section 2; published systems were not run.
Guards and model. For every strategy, ontology identifiers are chosen by the model only from
candidates returned by an ontology search, gene symbols are normalised against HGNC (Seal et al.,
2023), entry cross-reference numbers recalled from memory are removed, and every paragraph must
cite a supplied paper. One model, Claude Opus 5.5, does everything, called through a command-line
interface in headless mode with schema-constrained output; the interface exposes no temperature or
seed. Reasoning effort is high for writers and medium otherwise (Appendix A); no strategy has web
access. Replies are cached, so each call is made once. When the model’s safety filter stops a reply,
it comes back empty, is recorded as stopped by the model’s safety filter and is never retried with a
changed prompt; the reasons are not visible.
3.3
AUDIT, SCORING AND STATISTICS
Completion. S completes e when its draft exists and no per-paper call failed. A one-call strategy
whose reply is stopped loses the entry, whereas a per-paper strategy loses a paper; scoring the second
and excluding the first would bias the comparison. Strategies are therefore compared only on entries
every compared strategy completed, completion is reported as an outcome, and a sensitivity analysis
counts a missing draft as zero over all entries.
Audit. Each paragraph is split into atomic claims c attributed to the papers R(c) ⊆R it cites. Each
paper receives its claims and returns a verdict v(c, d) ∈{supported, partly supported, unsupported,
contradicted} with a quote; a “supported” verdict whose quote is not found verbatim in the paper
is downgraded. A claim’s verdict is the best over R(c). We report the share of claims judged
supported and, separately, the shares unsupported and contradicted, before and after one repair
round for flagged claims. All verdicts are model judgements.
Primary endpoint. For each in-scope curator paragraph g, a fact list Φ(g) was produced once,
without sight of any draft, and is reused for every strategy; splitting afresh for each draft lets the
split adapt to the draft and gives strategies different denominators. Each fact is marked present,
partly present, absent or contradicted in what D says about the paragraph’s papers, and
coverage(D) = #present + 1

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
ACCESS TO THE SOURCE BOUNDS AGREEMENT WITH CURATORS
Hiding the full text of the same papers. In 49 entries, the 91 papers readable in full were hidden
and each entry was rewritten by direct reading from their abstracts, with and without second-hand
passages (89 hidden papers received at least one; 523 passages). Coverage was scored against the
same fixed fact lists as the main comparison on the 46 entries whose full-text direct-reading draft
exists (three were stopped by the model’s safety filter): 136 paragraphs about the hidden papers
and the same 2,120 facts in every condition. Coverage is 77.4% with the full text, 31.2% from the
abstracts and 36.7% from the abstracts plus second-hand passages (Figure 2). The paired difference
between full text and abstract is +43.9 points (+38.5 to +49.3), with 45 entries higher and one tied;
second-hand passages add +4.9 points (+2.8 to +7.1; 33 better, 9 worse, 4 tied). The full-text draft
is the main run’s and went through the audit’s repair round; the two new drafts did not.
Where the missed facts went. Each fact that direct reading did not fully convey was checked
against the text available for its paper (Figure 3). For full-text papers, 245 of 501 missed facts (49%)
were stated in the text the model read; for abstract-only papers 46 of 2,837 (2%), and for title-only
papers none of 341. Over all 6,498 facts, 43% were conveyed, 4% were omitted although stated in
the available text, 13% were omitted and partly stated, and 40% were not in the available text. Most
of the shortfall is information the model was not given.

over 30). Judge variation is smaller (Appendix D). The differences of the evidence graph (−0.5)
and the agentic curator (−0.6) are thus within replicate noise; only the case table’s −1.9 (−3.1 to
−0.8) exceeds it. An earlier analysis that excluded entries whose writer was stopped but scored
entries with a stopped per-paper call placed the evidence graph 2.1 points below direct reading; with
completion treated the same way for every strategy, that difference is not distinguishable from zero.
Counting missing drafts as zero over all 100 entries gives the same picture: evidence graph −0.2
(−3.0 to +3.1), agentic curator +0.4 (−1.0 to +2.7), case table −1.4 (−4.2 to +1.4; Appendix
Table 7).
Secondary measures. Extract-then-write raises synopsis recall (37.4% graph, 37.7% table against
36.2%) at lower precision (40.6%, 40.7% against 44.1%), with more terms per entry (19.2 and 21.0
against 15.4), and produces more variants that OMIM does not list (111 and 130 against 36; pooled
precision 69.3% and 65.6% against 87.5%), whether or not the paper reports them. Recall, then
verify keeps 94.2% of claims judged supported but conveys 14.6% of facts and states no inheritance
in 22 of 93 entries; since it may not add content from the papers, its low coverage is partly by
construction. At list price for this one model, everything per entry (generation, audit and scoring)
costs $1.18 for direct reading, $1.15 for the agentic curator, $1.72 for the evidence graph and $1.78
for the case table (Appendix Table 10).

What it cannot see. In the hidden-full-text experiment, the second-hand draft made 2,308 claims
about the hidden papers, each audited against what the writer saw and against the paper’s full text.
Of the 112 claims whose supporting quote came from a later paper’s passage, the full text supports

worse (9 of 21 unsupported) than those resting on paragraphs (2 of 91). Because our full texts
exclude supplementary material, “unsupported by the full text” is an upper bound on error. An audit
against the text the writer saw passes such second-hand support, and in general it checks fidelity
to the cited text rather than correctness: a draft that faithfully copies a source’s error passes. Re-
examining by the same model the 37 facts on which a direct-reading draft contradicted the reference
paragraph, the paper was judged to support the draft in 22, the reference in 1 and both in 1, with 13

already passed the same claims.
4.5
THE MEMORY-ONLY CONTROL AS A CONTAMINATION CHECK
From the oldest to the newest band, the memory-only control conveys 14.5%, 24.1%, 17.5%, 12.6%
and 5.7% of facts, against 34.9–53.9% for direct reading, and its causal-gene F1 falls to 41.2% for
entries from 620000 onward (Figure 5). On 20 of 30 entries created June to November 2026 that
every strategy completed, memory alone conveys 7.0% of facts (paired difference −45.2, −52.4 to
−38.0) against 52.2% for direct reading and 49.4–51.5% for the other reading strategies, names the
causal gene with an F1 of 30.0% against 100.0%, and has 26.2% of claims judged supported (Ap-
pendix H.2). Memory thus contributes little for the newest entries. The control does not show that
the reading results are free of memorisation: within abstract-only papers, direct-reading coverage
declines from 49.8% for the 600000–609999 band to 37.9% for entries from 620000 onward (33.0%
for the classic band).
4.6
MATCHED ABLATION OF THE EXTRACT-THEN-WRITE IMPLEMENTATIONS
On 40 entries weighted towards full text (25 with at least half their papers readable in full), we varied
our extract-then-write implementations. Matched raises per-paper extraction to high effort and lets
the writer choose synopsis terms (and, for the case table, inheritance) instead of fixed rules; the two
changes were made together. Plus text also gives the writer the papers’ text. Thirty-eight entries
were completed by all seven strategies; the safety filter stopped some high-effort extraction calls.
Matching moves the case table from −2.3 to −0.2 points against direct reading (matched minus
original +2.1, +1.1 to +3.2; on full-text papers +3.3, +1.6 to +5.1) and the evidence graph from
+1.0 to +1.8 (+0.4 to +3.3; Table 2). Choosing synopsis terms cannot change which facts or
variants are extracted, so we attribute the gain in facts to extraction effort; this attribution is an
inference. Adding the papers’ text raises claims judged supported (+1.5, +0.9 to +2.1, for the
graph) but not facts (+0.0). The point estimates of synopsis precision stay below direct reading for
every extract-then-write variant (−2.6 to −5.7 points), and generation costs $0.65–$0.97 per entry
against $0.32. At least part of the main-comparison deficit of our extract-then-write implementations
thus reflects implementation choices rather than the intermediate representation itself (Appendix E).

4.7
ADD-ONS: ONE SMALL GAIN WITH COSTS, AND TWO NULLS
Second-hand evidence (96 entries; 361 of the 401 papers not readable in full received passages)
raises OMIM facts conveyed by +1.8 points (+1.0 to +2.8; 49/31/16) and variants recovered by
+5.0 (+1.3 to +9.2); pooled coverage of title-only papers rises from 17.5% to 26.7%. It also raises
unsupported claims before repair from 0.4% to 3.9% (+3.6, +2.2 to +5.1) and facts contradicted
(+0.2, +0.0 to +0.5), and lowers claims judged supported by −1.8 (−2.9 to −0.8). The learned
curation guide did not improve OMIM facts conveyed on its own training entries (final guide +0.06
points); on 68 of the 70 held-out entries it changed facts by +1.0 (+0.0 to +2.0; 30/25/13) and
variants recovered by −1.6 (−3.7 to +0.0). With two reflection rounds on 27 entries, this null says
nothing about better-resourced prompt optimisation. Reconciliation accepted 40 of 57 proposed
merges of patient or family records in 27 entries and reduced counted individuals from 2,580 to
2,413 and families from 1,126 to 1,092; it changed OMIM facts conveyed by +0.1 (−0.1 to +0.4;
97 entries) and no other score. A planted test merged 10 of 10 copied reports and no unrelated
records, which shows only that a stated re-report is caught; the case database was not validated
against external per-patient ground truth (Appendix G).
5
DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
The closeness of the reading strategies is a statement about this benchmark at current open access,
not a claim that the method matters little in general: where more full text is readable the differ-
ences grow, and the deficit of our extract-then-write implementations depends on extraction effort.
The negative results for recall-then-verify (which, as implemented, cannot add content), the learned
guide and reconciliation hold for the implementations and entry sizes tested.
Limitations. (i) One model family wrote, audited and judged everything; no human has read any
draft, verdict or fact judgement, so no judge is validated, and no second model was tested. (ii) The
reference is indirect and narrow: variant-centred curator paragraphs, an ontology annotation set from
two sources, and gene and inheritance tables; narrative sections of entries are not compared, and a
draft makes about twice as many statements about a paper as the paragraph has facts (24.0 against
11.9 for direct reading), so coverage is a lower bound on agreement with the entry. (iii) Every
strategy is given the curators’ reference list; finding the literature was tested only on ten entries,
where a capped search selected 56 of the 85 references OMIM cites (66%). (iv) Only open-access
text without supplementary material was available, where much variant and patient data in genetics
papers lives. (v) Variant names are not validated against reference sequences, and inheritance is
compared as coarse classes. (vi) Strategies were developed on the same entries they are evaluated
on; there is no separate development set. (vii) Stopped replies remove entries, unevenly across
strategies. (viii) Entries with few references dominate and inputs too large for one prompt were not
tested; the 30–75-reference tier has about ten comparable entries, mostly abstracts, and its fact score
covers a minority of each entry’s references, so it is exploratory (Appendix H.1). (ix) Cost is at list
price for one model.
6
CONCLUSION
We asked how much the generation strategy matters when everything else is fixed, what limits
agreement with curators, and what a claim-level audit can see. On 100 OMIM entries written by one
model from exactly the cited papers, the reading strategies are close at current open access, within
or near a measured noise floor; access to the source bounds agreement, as hiding the full text of the
same papers lowers coverage of curator-written facts from 77.4% to 31.2%; and the audit checks
fidelity to the cited text, not correctness. The benchmark, built only from public sources with fixed
fact lists and per-entry release of drafts, verdicts and scores, makes these comparisons repeatable for
other strategies.

STRATEGIES AND SETTINGS
Table 3 describes each strategy.
Settings:
one model for everything, Claude Opus 5.5
(claude-opus-5-5), called through the Claude Code command-line interface in headless mode
with schema-constrained output; no temperature or seed is exposed. Reasoning effort is high for
writers (direct reading, the graph and table writers, the agent, the memory draft) and medium for
per-paper extraction in the evidence graph and the case table, for the audit and for all judging; the
matched ablation raises per-paper extraction to high. The agentic strategy has read and search tools
over a folder of the papers and nothing else. The agentic curator reports which papers it opened (self-
reported; tool calls are not logged): in the 98 entries it wrote it reported opening every paper, and all
2,600 quotes it gave were found verbatim in the papers. The memory drafts that recall, then verify
starts from are the memory-only control’s drafts. Synopsis terms are assigned by a lexical search
over the ontology’s labels and synonyms that returns candidates, from which the model chooses; Lin
similarity uses information content computed from the ontology’s disease annotations, averaged in
each direction to give precision and recall.

RECONCILED CASE DATABASE AND LEARNED CURATION GUIDE
Reconciliation. Over the 100 entries, reconciliation proposed 57 merges of patient or family records
and accepted 40 (17 rejected by the verifier) in 27 entries; it merged 11 variant names and found
57 conflicts between papers. Counted individuals went from 2,580 to 2,413 (net; counts rose in a
few entries where a re-report gave a larger family size) and families from 1,126 to 1,092. A planted
test on six entries merged 10 of 10 copied reports with their originals and no unrelated records. The
accepted merges were not checked by a person. Cost: one or two extra calls per entry. None of the
entry-level measures uses patient counts, so no change in them is expected. Agreement of the table’s
patient-count fractions with those in the HPO annotations (about 560 terms) was 48% before and
after, with a mean absolute difference of 0.26; each reference fraction comes from one source while
the table pools all papers.
Learned guide. Training (27 of 30 training entries had a direct-reading draft; scored without the
audit, with the earlier per-draft fact splitting): baseline OMIM facts conveyed 53.8% and synopsis
exact F1 36.6%. Round 1 changed facts by +0.14 points and synopsis F1 by +1.29; round 2 by
−1.41 and +3.57. The text rules of round 1 and the synopsis rules of round 2 were selected by a rule
fixed in advance. The final guide, run once more on the training entries: facts +0.06 points, synopsis
F1 +1.28 points. A first guide, learned before stopped replies were recorded, had treated three empty
drafts as omissions and was discarded. On the held-out entries, a draft makes about twice as many
statements about a paper as the OMIM paragraph has facts (direct reading 24.0 statements per paper,
with the guide 22.2, against 11.9 facts), and 24% (direct) and 25% (guide) of a draft’s statements
have a counterpart in that paragraph; the paragraph is variant-centred, so this is a lower bound on
agreement with the entry.
