### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene (612659) on chromosome 6q22.1.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder of neonatal diabetes and small bowel atresia, often associated with intestinal malabsorption (summary by Piccand et al., 2014). Other features include hypoplastic or annular pancreas, duodenal and jejunal atresia, and gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia (Mitchell et al., 2004). Several patients have had clusters of chromogranin A-positive, hormone-negative cells in the pancreas (summary by Piccand et al., 2014). Diabetes may also begin in childhood rather than in the neonatal period (Sansbury et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Smith et al. (2010) noted that OMIM listed their patients under Martinez-Frias syndrome. They considered the cases distinct: the original Martinez-Frias patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias without diabetes, and no RFX6 mutation was found in the parents of 1 Martinez-Frias case. They proposed the name Mitchell-Riley syndrome, after the 2 clinicians who first described the condition.

### Clinical Features

Mitchell et al. (2004) described 5 infants from 3 families (2 sib pairs and an isolated case) with neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia. The parents of one sib pair were consanguineous. Four children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. One patient had a milder form and was surviving without insulin. Pancreatic immunohistochemistry in 3 of the infants showed a few scattered chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but complete absence of insulin, glucagon, and somatostatin. Exocrine histology was variable.

Ashraf et al. (2005) reported an infant with neonatal diabetes due to pancreatic agenesis. The infant also had cardiac septal defects, gallbladder agenesis, and duodenal malrotation. The infant survived after treatment with insulin, exocrine pancreatic supplements, and surgical repair of the heart and intestinal anomalies.

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient born to consanguineous parents with neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis, as a further example of a recently described phenotype. They stated that this was the first such case with neonatal diabetes but no demonstrable structural pancreatic abnormality, showing that a deficit in pancreatic function is involved.

Smith et al. (2010) described patients with neonatal diabetes and small bowel obstruction due to bowel atresia. Pancreas size was somewhat reduced, but exocrine pancreatic enzymes were not deficient. Autopsy in 2 cases showed normal-appearing exocrine pancreas with clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. The syndrome also included a hypoplastic gallbladder and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement.

Spiegel et al. (2011) reviewed the clinical and molecular features of all patients with RFX6 mutations, 6 of whom had been reported before their study. They suggested that, despite a fulminant course early after birth, patients who survive may have a relatively favorable prognosis.

Concepcion et al. (2014) described a male infant born at 34 weeks' gestation to nonconsanguineous Vietnamese parents. Prenatally he had intrauterine growth restriction and duodenal atresia. He was small for gestational age (birth weight 1375 g) and had severe hyperglycemia (446 mg/dL) within 24 hours of birth, which needed an insulin infusion. He had low C-peptide, low amylase and lipase, and low stool pancreatic elastase. Laparotomy on day 5 confirmed duodenal atresia, intestinal malrotation, and jejunal perforation, and ultrasound suggested an annular pancreas. His course included cholestatic jaundice, recurrent infections, feeding intolerance, and severe chronic diarrhea that needed intermittent parenteral nutrition and did not improve with pancreatic enzymes. Laparotomy at 3 months showed gallbladder agenesis. A hepatobiliary scan did not visualize the gallbladder or common bile duct, and liver biopsy showed bile ductular proliferation. He developed liver failure and died of sepsis at 5 months of age while awaiting liver transplantation.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome whose diabetes was diagnosed at ages 3 and 6 years rather than in the neonatal period. The first, a girl born at 32 weeks' gestation, had duodenal atresia, jejunal web, and Meckel diverticulum. She had hyperglycemia at age 2 and presented with diabetic ketoacidosis at age 3, with very low insulin and C-peptide and negative autoantibodies. She also had an absent gallbladder and gastrointestinal bleeding due to gastric heterotopia in the small bowel. Her cousin, a boy born at 34 weeks with duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation, had asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia at age 5 and was diagnosed with diabetes at age 6. He also had chronic iron deficiency anemia. Neither child had liver disease, and both were alive at age 9 years.

Sansbury et al. (2015) also reviewed 8 previously reported cases of Mitchell-Riley syndrome confirmed by biallelic RFX6 mutations. All had duodenal atresia, 7 of 8 had gallbladder agenesis, and all had liver involvement, with intrahepatic cholestasis in 7. Diabetes had begun within the first 2 weeks of life in all 8, and 5 had died within the first year of life.

Zegre Amorim et al. (2015) reported a girl born to first-cousin Gipsy parents. Prenatal ultrasound showed intrauterine growth restriction and a 'double bubble.' She was born at 35 weeks with weight, length, and head circumference below the 3rd centile. She developed hyperglycemia on day 2 and needed continuous insulin. On day 3, insulin and C-peptide levels were very low, and GAD, islet cell, and IA-2 autoantibodies were negative. Laparotomy on day 4 confirmed type II duodenal atresia with annular pancreas, malrotation, and gallbladder agenesis. Cholestasis began on day 6, with conjugated bilirubin of 3.07 mg/dL and GGT of 458 IU/L, and resolved without treatment by 2 months of age. Ultrasound showed biliary duct dilation, and the head of the pancreas was disproportionately large with an unidentifiable tail. At surgery, perijejunal submucosal tissue that looked like ectopic pancreatic tissue was seen. The authors described this as a novel feature, but the tissue was not biopsied. The patient had chronic diarrhea and malabsorption that did not respond to exocrine pancreatic supplementation. At 7 months she had failure to thrive and still depended on insulin and partial parenteral nutrition.

### Inheritance

Mitchell et al. (2004) concluded that the combination of anomalies probably represented a new autosomal recessive syndrome. They based this on the affected sib pairs and on parental consanguinity in one family, and suggested a genetic abnormality that interferes with normal islet development. Because their patient was born to consanguineous parents, Chappell et al. (2008) also concluded that the disorder is likely inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. ⚠︎

Smith et al. (2010) found that all RFX6 mutations in their patients were inherited from carrier parents. Concepcion et al. (2014) and Zegre Amorim et al. (2015) found that both parents of their homozygous probands were heterozygous carriers. The mother of the proband reported by Zegre Amorim et al. (2015) had previously had 3 first-trimester spontaneous abortions and a child with isolated congenital anorectal malformation.

In the family reported by Sansbury et al. (2015), several heterozygous relatives had adult-onset diabetes, but C-peptide levels did not differ between heterozygotes with and without diabetes. HbA1c also did not differ between nondiabetic heterozygotes and relatives without the familial mutation. The authors concluded that the data did not support heterozygosity for either RFX6 mutation as a risk factor for diabetes, although carriers of 5-prime mutations might still have an increased risk.

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) performed high-resolution homozygosity mapping in 2 probands whose parents were first and second cousins, respectively. They found 10 homozygosity-by-descent (HBD) segments larger than 500 kb in proband 1 and 25 in proband 2. Only 3 HBD regions, totaling 24 Mb, were shared (2p15-16, 6q21-22, and 6q23). Of the 194 RefSeq genes in these regions, only RFX6, in the largest segment at 6q21-22, had pancreas-enriched expression.

### Heterogeneity

Chappell et al. (2008) proposed that cases reported elsewhere with overlapping features, including malrotation, biliary atresia, and pancreatic hypoplasia (e.g., those described by Martinez-Frias), may represent variations of the same syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) found no RFX6 mutation in 1 of their 6 probands (proband 6). DNA from the proband was unavailable. Neither parent carried a point mutation in RFX6 or NEUROG3, and long-range PCR showed no RFX6 deletion. The authors could not rule out a de novo mutation but considered the case most likely a phenocopy. They also found no RFX6 mutation in a case of Martinez-Frias syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

In one patient from the consanguineous family, Mitchell et al. (2004) found no duplication or uniparental isodisomy of PLAGL1 at 6q24, no contiguous gene deletion involving the glucokinase gene, and no mutation in the coding sequence or splice sites of PDX1.

In their infant with pancreatic agenesis, Ashraf et al. (2005) sequenced both coding exons of IPF1 and found no mutation. They suggested that the disturbance happened before the dorsal pancreatic bud appears, around embryonic day 25, which is before IPF1 is expressed. ⚠︎

In their patient, Chappell et al. (2008) sequenced genes with a known role in monogenic diabetes (KCNJ11, ABCC8, GCK, IPF1, HNF1-beta, NeuroD1, and TCF7L2). They also sequenced a new candidate gene, HNF6, which is involved in hepatobiliary and pancreatic development. They found no mutations in any of these genes.

In proband 2 (see Mapping), the child of consanguineous parents, Smith et al. (2010) used array capture and deep sequencing of the exons within the 3 shared HBD regions. Of 30 novel variants, the only nonsynonymous change was a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) substitution in RFX6, which direct sequencing confirmed. Smith et al. (2010) then sequenced the 19 exons and splice junctions of RFX6 directly and found missense, splicing, or frameshift mutations in 5 of 6 available probands with the syndrome. Proband 1, the child of first cousins, was homozygous for a splice donor mutation in intron 2 (IVS2+2T-C). Proband 3 was compound heterozygous for 2 splice mutations (IVS6+2T-G and IVS1-12A-G). Proband 4 was homozygous for an out-of-frame deletion in exon 7, and proband 5 was homozygous for an arg181-to-gln (R181Q) missense mutation. All mutations were inherited from carrier parents. In EMSA, the R181Q mutation completely abolished DNA binding by RFX6, whereas S217P only modestly reduced DNA binding.

Spiegel et al. (2011) reported a new patient with the syndrome, including intestinal atresia and hepatobiliary abnormalities, who carried a novel homozygous splice site mutation in the RFX6 gene. They also gave an update on the clinical outcome of a previously reported patient. ⚠︎

In a Vietnamese boy with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, Concepcion et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous missense mutation in RFX6 (K260T). Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. Runs of homozygosity suggested that the parents might be distantly related.

Chandra et al. (2014) identified a previously unreported homozygous missense mutation in RFX6 (V506G) associated with neonatal diabetes. The mutant protein could not activate the insulin promoter or increase calcium-channel expression.

In 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes, Sansbury et al. (2015) used targeted next-generation sequencing of neonatal diabetes genes, with Sanger confirmation. They identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in RFX6: R726X, inherited from the fathers, and R866X, inherited from the mothers. The mutations were traced back to the grandmothers. These were the first RFX6 mutations reported in exons 9 to 19. Sansbury et al. (2015) concluded that biallelic mutations in RFX6 can cause diabetes beginning in childhood as well as in the neonatal period.

In a girl with Mitchell-Riley syndrome born to consanguineous Gipsy parents, Zegre Amorim et al. (2015) used Sanger sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation in exon 4 of the RFX6 gene (R181W). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) reported a genotype-phenotype correlation. The 3 probands homozygous for a splice donor loss, a frameshift deletion, or the R181Q mutation (which abolished DNA binding) all died in the first few months of life. The 2 longer survivors were the patient homozygous for S217P, which only modestly reduced DNA binding (alive at 4.5 years), and the compound heterozygote for 2 splice site mutations (alive at 9 years and intermittently off insulin).

In a review of 8 patients with RFX6 mutations, Concepcion et al. (2014) noted that the 2 longest survivors were a compound heterozygote with 2 splice site mutations and a patient with a homozygous missense mutation that reduced but did not abolish DNA binding. These 2 patients also had the highest birth weights. In contrast, the authors' patient, who was homozygous for K260T, had disease as severe as that of patients with homozygous splice site, frameshift, or DNA-binding-abolishing mutations. They noted that chronic diarrhea had been reported in most patients with RFX6 mutations.

Sansbury et al. (2015) noted that the 8 previously reported molecularly confirmed cases had splicing or frameshift mutations in exons 2 to 8, or missense mutations in or near the DNA-binding domain. They suggested a possible genotype-phenotype correlation in which escape from nonsense-mediated decay of the 3-prime R866X allele might preserve some RFX6 function, leading to later-onset diabetes and an otherwise milder phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Smith et al. (2010) found that Rfx6 is expressed broadly in the definitive endoderm and later becomes restricted to the gut and pancreatic bud. It acts downstream of neurogenin-3 (Neurog3) and upstream of many islet transcription factors, and is required for the differentiation of 4 of the 5 islet cell types. Mutations in both RFX6 and NEUROG3 cause diarrhea and diabetes. The authors attributed the small bowel atresia and biliary abnormalities, which are seen only with RFX6 mutation, to Rfx6 expression in the early gut endoderm, where neurogenin-3 is not expressed.

Chandra et al. (2014) showed that RFX6 is present in adult human pancreatic endocrine cells. In the human beta-cell line EndoC-betaH2, RFX6 regulated insulin gene transcription, insulin content, and insulin secretion. Knocking down RFX6 lowered expression of calcium-channel genes and reduced L-type calcium-channel activity, which suppressed depolarization-evoked insulin exocytosis.

Piccand et al. (2014) showed that islets of mice with Rfx6 deleted in adult beta cells (Rfx6-deltaBeta mice) had strongly reduced Gck (glucokinase) and Abcc8/SUR1 transcripts, together with reduced Ucn3, NeuroD1, and Pax6. Slc2a2/Glut2, Pcsk1, and Kcnj11/Kir6.2 were unaltered. ChIP-seq and quantitative ChIP in Min6B1 beta cells showed that endogenous Rfx6 binds X-boxes in the Gck promoter and in intron 10 of Abcc8, suggesting that Rfx6 directly and positively regulates transcription of these genes. Multicellular imaging of intact islets showed that glucose-induced intracellular calcium responses were reduced by 50 to 60% and glucose-induced cytosolic ATP/ADP increases by about 60% in Rfx6-deleted beta cells. RNA sequencing showed downregulation of 8 voltage-dependent calcium channel transcripts (including Cacna1a, Cacnb2, Cacna1d, and Cacna1c), and ChIP studies showed Rfx6 binding to X-boxes in intron 1 of Cacna1c and Cacnb2. In addition, 54 of the 68 known 'disallowed' genes, which are normally selectively repressed in mature mouse beta cells, were upregulated in Rfx6-deltaBeta islets, including Slc16a1, Ldha, Pdgfra, and Igfbp4. Rfx6 bound a conserved region about 10 kb upstream of Ldha. The authors proposed that Rfx6 acts as a master repressor that helps maintain beta cell maturity and functional identity.

### Diagnosis

Sansbury et al. (2015) recommended considering genetic testing for RFX6 mutations in patients who present with intestinal atresias even when neonatal diabetes is absent, especially when there is evidence suggesting autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Clinical Management

On the basis of their mouse findings, Piccand et al. (2014) suggested that patients with neonatal diabetes carrying RFX6 mutations are likely to have functional defects in their remaining beta cells, in addition to having fewer beta cells. They suggested that it might be worth investigating whether such individuals respond to pharmacologic treatments, including sulfonylureas or GLP-1 analogs, that could prompt insulin release from the remaining beta cells.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) generated Rfx6-null mice by replacing the first 5 exons, which include the DNA-binding domain, with an eGFP-cre cassette. Homozygous mice were born at the expected mendelian ratio but fed poorly, had gross bowel distension from small bowel obstruction, and died within 2 days of birth. Some had a reduced pancreas size. At embryonic day 17.5, mutant pancreata contained many chromogranin A-positive endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin. Pancreatic polypeptide-expressing cells were increased.

Piccand et al. (2014) generated a floxed Rfx6 allele and deleted it in endocrine progenitors with Ngn3-Cre (Rfx6-deltaEndo mice). Like the full-body knockout, mutant pups were diabetic and died between 2 and 3 days after birth. Insulin-, glucagon-, somatostatin-, and ghrelin-expressing cells were nearly absent, PP cells were spared, and chromogranin A-positive, hormone-negative cells were present in the pancreas. The authors concluded that Rfx6 function downstream of Ngn3 is essential for beta cell generation and that impaired beta cell development does not result from defective endoderm.

Piccand et al. (2014) also inactivated Rfx6 in the adult beta cells of 8- to 10-week-old mice (Rfx6fl/fl; Ins1-CreERT2, Rfx6-deltaBeta) by tamoxifen treatment, achieving 95% deletion within 5 days. The mutant mice did not develop overt diabetes, and insulin, C-peptide 1, and C-peptide 2 remained detectable. Ins1 transcripts were reduced by approximately 54 to 65%, while Ins2 was unchanged. Rfx6-deltaBeta mice had normal fasting and fed blood glucose but significant glucose intolerance on both intraperitoneal and oral glucose tolerance testing. First- and second-phase glucose-induced insulin secretion was markedly reduced in vivo, and glucose- and KCl-stimulated insulin secretion was reduced in isolated islets. Beta cell mass, alpha cell mass, and beta cell proliferation were unaffected. The authors concluded that induced inactivation of Rfx6 in a mature beta cell does not preclude insulin production.

### History

Martinez-Frias et al. (1992) described 2 Spanish sibs born to consanguineous parents with a multiple congenital anomaly pattern that included low birth weight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. They suggested that the constellation represented a previously unreported autosomal recessive syndrome. A computerized search of the Spanish Collaborative Study of Congenital Malformations (ECEMC) found 3 other unrelated infants with intestinal atresias, hypospadias, and low birth weight. The authors suggested that these infants might represent a milder expression of the same syndrome. Smith et al. (2010) considered Martinez-Frias syndrome distinct from Mitchell-Riley syndrome (see Nomenclature).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [p.S217P]

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 2, the child of consanguineous parents. Using array capture and deep sequencing of the exons within 3 shared homozygous regions, they found a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) substitution in the RFX6 gene. It was the only nonsynonymous change among 30 novel variants, and direct sequencing confirmed it. The variant lies between the DNA-binding and dimerization domains. In EMSA it only modestly reduced DNA binding, and it did not affect dimer formation. The patient was alive at age 4.5 years.

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous splice donor mutation in intron 2 of the RFX6 gene (IVS2+2T-C) in proband 1, the child of first cousins, who died in the first few months of life. RT-PCR of RNA from the patient's autopsy pancreas detected no properly spliced RFX6 transcript. No RNA was detected from exons 1 and 2, which the authors attributed probably to nonsense-mediated decay.

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, FRAMESHIFT DEL, EX7

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7 of the RFX6 gene in proband 4, who died in the first few months of life. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents.

**.0004 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181GLN [p.R181Q]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous arg181-to-gln (R181Q) missense mutation in the RFX6 gene in proband 5, who died in the first few months of life. The mutation affects a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain. In EMSA, the mutation completely abolished DNA binding by RFX6.

**.0005 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2

Smith et al. (2010) found that proband 3 was compound heterozygous for 2 splice mutations in the RFX6 gene: loss of the donor site in intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G) and disruption of the acceptor site in intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G). The mutations were inherited from carrier parents. The patient was alive at age 9 years and was intermittently off insulin.

**.0006 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS1AS, A-G, -12

In proband 3, who was alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin, Smith et al. (2010) found compound heterozygosity for a mutation disrupting the acceptor site in intron 1 of the RFX6 gene (IVS1-12A-G) and a splice donor site mutation in intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G). The mutations were inherited from carrier parents.

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SPLICE SITE MUTATION

Spiegel et al. (2011) reported a patient with the neonatal diabetes syndrome, including intestinal atresia and hepatobiliary abnormalities, who carried a novel homozygous splice site mutation in the RFX6 gene.

**.0008 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, LYS260THR [c.779A>C; p.K260T]

In a Vietnamese boy with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, Concepcion et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous c.779A-C transversion in the RFX6 gene, resulting in a lys260-to-thr (K260T) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from the dbSNP and 1000 Genomes databases. It affects a highly conserved lysine and was predicted to be damaging. It also changes the consensus 5-prime splice donor site of intron 7 and was predicted to disrupt splicing. The patient died at 5 months of age.

**.0009 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, VAL506GLY [p.V506G]

Chandra et al. (2014) identified a previously unreported homozygous val506-to-gly (V506G) missense mutation in the RFX6 gene associated with neonatal diabetes. The mutant protein could not activate the insulin promoter or increase calcium-channel expression.

**.0010 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.R726*]

In 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes, Sansbury et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene. One was a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution. The other was R866X. The mutations were found by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The authors predicted that the R726X mutation would be subject to nonsense-mediated decay. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X, and the mutation was traced back to a grandmother.

**.0011 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG866TER [c.2596C>T; p.R866*]

In 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes, Sansbury et al. (2015) found compound heterozygosity for the R726X mutation and a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution. The R866X mutation was inherited from the mothers. Because it lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary, the authors predicted that it escapes nonsense-mediated decay and yields a partially truncated protein that keeps the entire DNA-binding domain and may retain some function. They suggested that this residual function might explain the later onset of diabetes and the otherwise milder phenotype.

**.0012 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181TRP [c.541C>T; p.R181W]

In a girl with Mitchell-Riley syndrome born to consanguineous Gipsy parents, Zegre Amorim et al. (2015) used Sanger sequencing to identify a homozygous c.541C-T transition in exon 4 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg181-to-trp (R181W) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The arginine at codon 181 lies in the DNA-binding domain and is highly conserved across species. The authors noted that a different mutation at the same residue (R181Q) had been reported previously.
