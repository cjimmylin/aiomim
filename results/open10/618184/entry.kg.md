### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy-2 (CHN2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the MPZ gene (159440) on chromosome 1q23.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy is a rare condition. Onset of hypotonia, paresis, and areflexia is prenatal, neonatal, or in early infancy. On histopathology, myelin sheaths are absent or thin. Most of the few reported patients have died within the first years of life (Smit et al., 2008). Smit et al. (2008) noted that mutations had been described in the MPZ, EGR2, PMP22, and MTMR2 genes.

### Clinical Features

Lyon (1969) reported an ultrastructural study of a nerve biopsy from a patient with early infantile chronic neuropathy.

Ono et al. (1982) described a Japanese boy with congenital hypomyelination who was 3 years 11 months old. He had delayed motor development and generalized muscle hypotonia and weakness. Tendon reflexes were absent because of peripheral neuropathy. His mental development was normal. Nerve conduction velocities could not be measured. On right sural nerve biopsy, most myelinated fibers had no myelin at all. Electron microscopy showed onion bulbs around the fibers. These were formed by concentric double-layered sheets of basement membrane with fragments of Schwann cell cytoplasm. The authors noted that these features were similar to those of previously reported cases.

Charnas et al. (1988) described an infant born at 37 weeks' gestation with lethal arthrogryposis multiplex congenita caused by complete absence of myelin in the peripheral nervous system. Supernumerary Schwann cells (onion bulbs) were not found.

Tachi et al. (1998) described a male patient with a Dejerine-Sottas disease phenotype. On sural nerve biopsy, fibers were hypomyelinated without active demyelination, and there were onion-bulb formations made of 2 parallel layers of basement membrane. This pathology was consistent with congenital hypomyelination neuropathy (CHN), but his clinical features were more severe than those of previously reported CHN patients.

Phillips et al. (1999) reported long-term prospective follow-up of 2 unrelated girls with congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy. The first girl presented with neonatal hypotonia and extremely slow nerve conduction velocities. Sural nerve biopsy showed profound hypomyelination without inflammation or myelin breakdown. At age 9 years her motor function was still improving, but follow-up nerve conduction velocities were unchanged.

The second girl reported by Phillips et al. (1999) presented with hypotonia at age 5 months. Her nerve conduction velocities were extremely slow, and sural nerve biopsy showed severe hypomyelination without inflammation or myelin breakdown. By age 5 years her motor function had improved, but repeat nerve conduction velocities were unchanged. Cognitive development was normal in both girls.

Mandich et al. (1999) described the clinical, morphologic, and immunohistochemical features of a patient with congenital hypomyelination (CH). They characterized CH as a hereditary demyelinating peripheral neuropathy with onset in early infancy, distal muscle weakness, hypotonia, areflexia, and severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. The authors noted that the patient's clinical features were quite distinct from those of CMT1B or Dejerine-Sottas syndrome.

Szigeti et al. (2003) reported a patient with congenital hypomyelination associated with cranial nerve dysfunction, respiratory failure, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; the cardiomyopathy confounded the clinical picture. Findings on muscle biopsy suggested that abnormal innervation affected early postnatal muscle fiber differentiation.

Kochanski et al. (2004) described the clinical and morphologic features of a patient with congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy. They characterized it as a severe neuropathy with onset in early infancy, in which sural nerve biopsy typically shows nonmyelinated and poorly myelinated axons, onion bulbs made of basal lamina, and no myelin breakdown products.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy was supported by Smit et al. (2008), who confirmed a heterozygous MPZ mutation in 2 generations of an affected family. In sporadic patients, the MPZ mutation arose de novo: Tachi et al. (1998) did not find the mutation in either parent, and Mandich et al. (1999) and Szigeti et al. (2003) each reported a de novo mutation. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Warner et al. (1996) identified 5 novel mutations in the MPZ gene in patients with Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1B, Dejerine-Sottas syndrome, or congenital hypomyelination. MPZ encodes P0, the major structural protein of peripheral nerve myelin.

Tachi et al. (1998) studied a male patient with a Dejerine-Sottas disease phenotype whose nerve pathology was consistent with CHN. In this patient they identified a GGCA insertion at nucleotide 560 of the MPZ gene. The insertion was a small direct tandem duplication in exon 4, which encodes the transmembrane domain. It shifted the reading frame and created a stop codon. The mutation was not found in either parent. The authors suggested that mutation of the transmembrane domain probably has the largest effect on P0 function.

Phillips et al. (1999) found a point mutation in the MPZ gene in the second of their 2 patients with congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy. The same mutation had previously been reported in 3 other patients diagnosed with Dejerine-Sottas syndrome, but never in a patient with CHN. The authors suggested that CHN, although a distinct clinical entity, may share genetic features with Dejerine-Sottas syndrome.

In a patient with congenital hypomyelination, Mandich et al. (1999) identified a de novo gln215-to-ter (Q215X) mutation in the MPZ gene. The authors concluded that this finding confirmed that CH is allelic with disorders that have a milder phenotype and a different clinical and neuropathologic profile, such as CMT1B and Dejerine-Sottas syndrome.

In a patient with congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy, Szigeti et al. (2003) identified a complex de novo mutation in the MPZ gene. It consisted of a 3-bp deletion of CTA from nucleotides 550 to 552 and an insertion of G at nucleotide 550. Conceptual translation predicted a frameshift.

Nowakowski and Kochański (2004) used SSCP and heteroduplex analysis to screen the MPZ coding and promoter sequences in patients with CMT1, CMT2, and CHN. They found a thr124-to-lys (T124K) substitution in MPZ in 1 patient with CHN.

Kochanski et al. (2004) identified a novel thr124-to-lys (T124K) mutation in the MPZ gene in a patient with congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy.

Smit et al. (2008) described a family with congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy in which a heterozygous MPZ mutation was confirmed in 2 generations.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Warner et al. (1996) suggested that CMT1B, Dejerine-Sottas syndrome, and congenital hypomyelination may not be distinct pathophysiologic entities. Instead, they proposed that these disorders form a spectrum of related 'myelinopathies' caused by an underlying defect in myelination. They hypothesized that the differences in clinical severity among MPZ mutations depend on the type of mutation and its effect on protein function, that is, loss of function versus a dominant-negative effect.

Nowakowski and Kochański (2004) found an MPZ mutation, glu56-to-lys (E56K), in 1 family with axonal CMT (CMT2). They concluded that MPZ screening should be done across a wide range of CMT phenotypes.

### Pathogenesis

In their patient with complete absence of peripheral myelin, Charnas et al. (1988) found that Schwann cells proliferated, migrated, ensheathed axons, and produced basal lamina, and then stopped proliferating normally. However, the Schwann cells failed to lengthen spirally and to grow longitudinally. The distance between Schwann cell nuclei was very short, causing marked Schwann cell hypercellularity, and the Schwann cells took on many morphologic features of unmyelinated nerves. Immunocytochemistry detected none of the peripheral myelin proteins P0, P1, or MAG. The authors concluded that the defect appeared to be an arrest of Schwann cell differentiation at the stages of mesaxon elongation and longitudinal growth.

Wrabetz et al. (2000) studied transgenic mice that overexpressed P0. On Western blot analysis, P0 overexpression dysregulated other myelin proteins even in the absence of dysmyelination. In P28 Tg80.3 nerves with ultrastructurally normal myelin, P0 was overexpressed by about 60%, MBP was reduced by about 50%, PMP22 was reduced by 35%, and tubulin was unchanged. The authors concluded that Schwann cells sense P0 overexpression and respond by dysregulating other myelin proteins before the threshold for dysmyelination is exceeded.

Wrabetz et al. (2000) noted that several features of the P0-overexpressing mice resembled human congenital hypomyelination neuropathy. These included profoundly reduced nerve conduction velocities (1-2 m/s), little active myelin destruction or onion-bulb formation, and redundant basement membrane. Low-level P0 overexpression produced transient hypomyelination, which resembled a subset of reversible CHN. The authors suggested that P0 overexpression represents a new mechanism of hereditary neuropathy. They also suggested that MPZ overexpression might contribute to the pathogenesis of the EGR2 mutation that likely disinhibits Krox 20.

### Animal Model

Wrabetz et al. (2000) generated transgenic mice carrying extra copies of the Mpz gene. Mpz mRNA was overexpressed by 30 to 700%. The mice developed a dose-dependent dysmyelinating neuropathy, which ranged from transient perinatal hypomyelination to arrested myelination with impaired sorting of axons by Schwann cells.

Wrabetz et al. (2000) obtained 10 transgenic founders with Mpz copy numbers ranging from 1 to more than 40. Animals with high copy numbers (more than 20) had gait difficulty, reduced weight, tremor, atrophy of the paraspinal and hindlimb muscles, and a reduced life span. Their nerve conduction velocities were profoundly reduced, to about 2 m/s, with temporal dispersion of the compound motor action potential. Leg muscles showed angulated and atrophic fibers suggestive of denervation, without fiber type grouping.

In P28 sciatic nerve from high-copy Tg80.2 mice, Wrabetz et al. (2000) found marked hypomyelination with an increased number of Schwann cell nuclei. Every transverse section contained bundles of unsorted naked axons of mixed caliber. Schwann cells were surrounded by pockets of redundant basal lamina containing collagen. There were no onion bulbs and little evidence of active demyelination or axonal degeneration. The authors concluded that the dysmyelination resulted from developmental delay rather than myelin destruction. Tg80.2 sciatic nerves contained virtually no myelin at P5 and P14. In the P10 quadriceps nerve, fewer than 1% of axons in a 1:1 relationship with Schwann cells were myelinated, compared with virtually 100% in wildtype mice. The motor branch of the femoral nerve was more dysmyelinated than the sensory branch.

Wrabetz et al. (2000) showed that the severity of dysmyelination paralleled Mpz dosage. Mice with 0 to 10 copies (Tg80.3; 0.3-fold overexpression) behaved normally and had normally myelinated nerves at P28. Mice with 10 to 20 copies (Tg80.4; 0.8-fold) had mild tremor, a nerve conduction velocity of 11 m/s, and about 50% of large axons unmyelinated. Mice with more than 20 copies (Tg80.2; 7.3-fold) had evident tremor, weak grip strength, a conduction velocity of 2 m/s, and severe hypomyelination with delayed axonal sorting. Breeding the mildly affected Tg80.3 line to homozygosity raised overexpression from 30% to 80% and produced tremor and dysmyelination. Crossing Tg80.4 into a homozygous Mpz-null background returned myelination to essentially normal. From these experiments, the authors placed the threshold for dysmyelination between 30 and 80% Mpz overexpression (130 to 180% of total Mpz expression). They concluded that the phenotype depended only on Mpz dosage, not on an ectopic effect or a structural alteration of the transgenic P0.
