### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN; 601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

Congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 is an autosomal recessive disorder of neuromuscular transmission caused by defects in rapsyn, which result in endplate acetylcholine receptor (AChR) deficiency (Ohno et al., 2002). Onset is usually perinatal or in the first years of life. Features include bilateral ptosis, fatigable weakness of facial, bulbar, and limb muscles, and crisis-like exacerbations with respiratory insufficiency provoked by stress, fever, or infection (Muller et al., 2003; Milone et al., 2009). Some patients have arthrogryposis multiplex congenita, and a later-onset form also exists (Burke et al., 2003). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ohno et al. (2002) carried out endplate studies in 4 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and RAPSN mutations. Staining for rapsyn and AChR was decreased, and postsynaptic morphologic development was impaired.

Ohno et al. (2003) reported that 7 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome were of Oriental Jewish stock of Iraqi or Iranian origin and carried a RAPSN promoter mutation. All 7 had facial malformations.

Dunne and Maselli (2003) described 4 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and RAPSN mutations from 4 unrelated families. In 2 cases, microelectrode and electron microscopy studies confirmed the diagnosis. The patient homozygous for the N88K mutation was only mildly affected, whereas the 3 compound heterozygous patients were severely affected.

Muller et al. (2003) described 12 European patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome from 10 families, all carrying the RAPSN N88K mutation. Symptoms usually began perinatally or in the first years of life, although 1 patient had no myasthenic symptoms before the third decade. Typical features were bilateral ptosis and weakness of facial, bulbar, and limb muscles. In early childhood, patients had frequent crisis-like exacerbations with respiratory insufficiency provoked by stress, fever, or infections. Myasthenic symptoms were generally mild to moderate, but severity and age at onset varied greatly.

Burke et al. (2003) described 2 distinct phenotypes among 16 unrelated patients with RAPSN-related congenital myasthenic syndrome. The early-onset form was often associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The late-onset form developed in adolescence or adulthood and was at first mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Yasaki et al. (2004) reported a 28-year-old French man with congenital myasthenic syndrome. He had had mild diplopia and fatigability since age 5 years. Muscle biopsy showed a marked reduction of rapsyn and AChR at neuromuscular junctions and a simplified subneural apparatus.

Muller et al. (2004) described a German patient with sporadic, early-onset congenital myasthenic syndrome. The patient had arthrogryposis multiplex congenita, recurrent episodes of respiratory insufficiency provoked by infections, and moderate general weakness that responded to anticholinesterase treatment.

Milone et al. (2009) reported clinical, electrophysiologic, pathologic, and molecular studies in 39 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome due to rapsyn defects. In all but 1 patient, the disease presented in the first 2 years of life. Nine patients had constant or episodic ophthalmoparesis, and 1 had a pure limb-girdle phenotype. More than half had intermittent exacerbations.

### Inheritance

Ohno et al. (2002) identified recessive RAPSN mutations in patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and endplate AChR deficiency, which indicated autosomal recessive inheritance. Later reports supported this, finding affected patients who were homozygous or compound heterozygous for RAPSN mutations (Dunne and Maselli, 2003; Muller et al., 2003; Yasaki et al., 2004; Muller et al., 2004; Milone et al., 2009).

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes. In these patients, they identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for L14P, in tetratricopeptide repeat 1 (TPR1), and N88K, in TPR3. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. The fourth patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and 553ins5, which causes a frameshift in TPR5. When the mutant proteins were expressed in HEK cells, none of the 3 mutations hindered rapsyn self-association, but all 3 reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified mutations in the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation, which changes an E-box at positions -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8 were all of Oriental Jewish stock of Iraqi or Iranian origin and carried a -38A-G mutation. This mutation changes an E-box at positions -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without affecting the consensus CANNTG sequence. Haplotype analysis indicated that the -38A-G mutation arose from a common founder. Both mutations attenuated reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes, and they altered binding to C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts in electrophoretic mobility shift assays.

Dunne and Maselli (2003) found the RAPSN N88K mutation in all 4 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome from 4 families. One patient was homozygous for N88K. The other 3 were compound heterozygous for N88K and a second RAPSN mutation: L14P, 46insC, or Y269X. N88K lies within the putative leucine zipper motif, which may be important for AChR clustering. The authors predicted that L14P causes a conformational change at the N terminus that may disrupt membrane association, and that 46insC and Y269X truncate the protein.

Muller et al. (2003) screened 120 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome from 110 unrelated families for the RAPSN N88K mutation, using restriction fragment length polymorphism and sequence analysis. They identified N88K in 12 patients from 10 independent families. The mutation was present either in the homozygous state or in the compound heterozygous state with another missense mutation.

Richard et al. (2003) identified novel RAPSN mutations in patients with congenital myasthenic syndromes.

Burke et al. (2003) identified RAPSN mutations in 16 unrelated patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome. All 16 carried the N88K mutation.

Yasaki et al. (2004) studied a French patient with congenital myasthenic syndrome and AChR deficiency. After excluding mutations in the AChR subunit genes, they found that the patient was homozygous for the N88K mutation in the RAPSN gene.

Muller et al. (2004) studied a German patient with sporadic congenital myasthenic syndrome, using RFLP, long-range PCR, and sequence analysis. The patient was compound heterozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation and a deletion of about 4.5 kb that disrupted the RAPSN gene.

Maselli et al. (2007) reported congenital myasthenic syndrome caused by 2 RAPSN mutations other than N88K.

Michalk et al. (2008) identified novel recessive missense mutations in RAPSN in patients with fetal akinesia deformation sequence disorders. These mutations caused a severe, but not necessarily lethal, phenotype.

Milone et al. (2009) studied 39 patients with rapsyn-deficient congenital myasthenic syndrome. Of these, 8 were homozygous and 23 heterozygous for the common N88K mutation in the RAPSN gene. The authors also identified 6 novel RAPSN mutations: 3 missense mutations, an in-frame deletion, a splice site mutation, and a nonsense mutation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dunne and Maselli (2003) observed that their patient homozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation was mildly affected. In contrast, the 3 compound heterozygous patients, who carried N88K with L14P, 46insC, or Y269X, were severely affected. The authors suggested that the predicted effects of the second mutations may explain the severe disease in the compound heterozygotes.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) studied 2 asymptomatic individuals homozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation. These individuals carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination events may have occurred within the rapsyn gene, which may have implications for phenotypic expression of the disease.

Michalk et al. (2008) concluded that complete or severe functional disruption of fetal AChR causes lethal multiple pterygium syndrome. Milder alterations, by contrast, result in fetal hypokinesia with inborn contractures or in a myasthenic syndrome later in life.

Milone et al. (2009) found that homozygosity for N88K was associated with varying grades of severity. They observed no genotype-phenotype correlations, with one exception: 8 Near-Eastern patients homozygous for the RAPSN promoter E-box mutation c.-38A-G had a mild course. This E-box mutation was associated with jaw deformities.

### Pathogenesis

Ohno et al. (2002) expressed mutant rapsyn proteins in HEK cells. None of the L14P, N88K, or 553ins5 mutations hindered rapsyn self-association, but all 3 reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) studied 2 RAPSN promoter mutations. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, the -38A-G mutation gained, and the -27C-G mutation lost, binding affinity for different components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. In luciferase reporter assays, both mutations attenuated reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes. The -27C-G mutation also attenuated expression in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin, and it markedly attenuated the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter. The authors concluded that impaired RAPSN promoter activity predicts reduced rapsyn expression and endplate AChR deficiency.

Yasaki et al. (2004) performed electrophysiologic studies on biopsied intercostal muscle from a patient homozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation. The fatigable weakness reflected both reduced AChR density in the membrane and a decline in endplate potentials evoked at low frequency.

Muller et al. (2004) suggested that the 4.5-kb RAPSN deletion may have arisen through Alu-mediated unequal homologous recombination. They hypothesized that numerous interspersed Alu elements may predispose the RAPSN locus to genetic rearrangements.

Milone et al. (2009) carried out endplate studies in 7 patients. The average AChR count per endplate and the synaptic response to ACh were less reduced than in patients with low-expressor AChR mutations.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) noted that the late-onset phenotype of RAPSN-related myasthenic syndrome can be mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis. They stated that recognizing this phenotype should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy.

### Clinical Management

Muller et al. (2003) found that patients with the RAPSN N88K mutation responded favorably to anticholinesterase treatment.

Milone et al. (2009) followed 25 patients long term after the start of cholinergic therapy. Of these, 21 became stable or improved, including 2 who became asymptomatic; 3 had a progressive course; and 1 died in infancy. The authors concluded that, with early diagnosis and therapy, rapsyn deficiency has a benign course in most patients.

### Population Genetics

Ohno et al. (2003) used haplotype analysis to show that the RAPSN -38A-G promoter mutation in Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin arose from a common founder.

Muller et al. (2003) found that all of their families with the RAPSN N88K mutation originated from Central or Western European countries, and genotype analysis indicated descent from a common founder. They concluded that N88K is a frequent cause of rapsyn-related congenital myasthenic syndrome in European patients.

Richard et al. (2003) suggested a possible founder effect for the RAPSN N88K mutation in patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome.

Yasaki et al. (2004) found the RAPSN N88K mutation in the heterozygous state in 5 of 300 unrelated control subjects. They concluded that the mutation is not infrequent in the healthy population.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) characterized the haplotype associated with the N88K mutation using 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb of the RAPSN gene. In 3 affected N88K homozygotes, they identified a common haplotype that was also present in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, which supported a founder effect.

### Animal Model

Michalk et al. (2008) studied expression of Rapsn and the AChR subunit genes in mouse embryos. These genes were expressed in skeletal muscle and also during early somite development. The authors suggested that, in addition to muscle-specific effects, somite expression might account for early developmental defects such as fetal edema, cystic hygroma, or cardiac defects.
