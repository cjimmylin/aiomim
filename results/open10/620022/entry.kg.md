### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270) on chromosome 20q13.33.

### Description

Faletra et al. (2014) noted that Stickler syndrome is clinically variable and genetically heterogeneous, with ophthalmic, articular, orofacial, and auditory manifestations. It shows both autosomal dominant inheritance (COL2A1, COL11A1, COL11A2) and autosomal recessive inheritance (COL9A1, COL9A2). Stickler syndrome type VI is the autosomal recessive form caused by loss-of-function mutations in the COL9A3 gene. Its consistent features are moderate-to-profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate-to-high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration, and skeletal involvement varies (Rad et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome whose parents, from India, were third cousins. He failed his newborn hearing screen. At 5 months, testing showed moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe loss on the left, and the hearing loss remained stable. Temporal bone CT showed mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months but had high myopia by 2 years (-6.75 OD, -8 OS), which progressed to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS by 12 years. No vitreous abnormalities were noted. He had femoral and tibial bowing at birth. Radiographs at age 6 years showed slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, flattened acetabula, irregular medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly with irregular vertebral endplates. Growth and intelligence were normal, and he had neither cleft palate nor pes planus. He had midfacial hypoplasia, and autoimmune hypothyroidism was diagnosed at 10 years.

Nixon et al. (2019) described 6 patients from 4 families with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to homozygous variants in type IX collagen genes. One of these families had 2 affected patients with COL9A3 variants. All 6 patients were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and all had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy.

Markova et al. (2021) described a Russian boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome whose parents were healthy and nonconsanguineous. At 1 month he had hip dysplasia and bilaterally absent ossification centers. Congenital high bilateral myopia was noted at 3 months, and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss grade 3-4 was diagnosed at 11 months. Ophthalmic findings included peripheral pigment rearrangement, multiple vitreous floaters, a preretinal membrane, and congenital progressive high myopia. The ERG was normal. Radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with platyspondyly, kyphosis, sacral spina bifida occulta, flattened irregular femoral heads, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. When examined at 2 years 5 months, he had valgus knee deformity and waddling gait. From age 3 years he had fatigability and pain in the lower limb joints, which the authors took to indicate early-onset arthropathy. His height was normal. He had a flattened nasal bridge, small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, and high palate, without cleft palate. The authors concluded that his phenotype was more severe than in previously reported COL9A3 patients, with early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration. ⚠︎

Uehara et al. (2022) described a 58-year-old Japanese woman (case 7) whose parents were first cousins. Hearing loss was diagnosed in her twenties. It was severe in the left ear and moderate in the right, with a flat audiometric pattern. Her older sister also had hearing loss. The patient had no arthritis but had a history of cataract and retinal detachment. She had first been diagnosed with nonsyndromic hearing loss, and the cataract and retinal detachment were recognized only on re-evaluation after the genetic diagnosis. The authors classified her as having a very mild type of Stickler syndrome.

Rad et al. (2022) described 5 patients from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families. The proband of family 1 was a 28-year-old woman, the only affected child of first-cousin parents. She had high myopia in both eyes, vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, retinal pigmentary changes, and severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss. Skull x-ray showed unilateral absence of the frontal sinus. Her joints and bones were normal on examination, but she complained of knee pain. She lacked typical Stickler craniofacial features, including midface hypoplasia, cleft palate, micrognathia, depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. Her height was 157 cm (-0.8 SD).

Family 2 of Rad et al. (2022) included 2 affected brothers, aged 65 and 57 years, born to first-cousin parents. Both had undergone multiple vitreoretinal surgeries for recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments caused by advanced vitreoretinal degeneration, and the older brother was blind with no light perception in either eye. Both had severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss (profound in the authors' table), high myopia, cataract, and a herniated cervical disc. The older brother also had muscular atrophy.

Family 3 of Rad et al. (2022) included 2 affected sibs born to first-cousin parents: a boy aged 11.8 years and his sister aged 3.1 years. Both had myopia and congenital moderate to severe progressive sensorineural hearing impairment, and x-rays showed spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia. Both had pes planus, a depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. Midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures were more pronounced in the girl. The boy had knee pain, genu valgum, irregular femoral epiphyses, a flat acetabular roof, flattened femoral heads, and short metacarpals. None of the patients in the 3 families had intellectual disability.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of Stickler syndrome type VI is autosomal recessive. Faletra et al. (2014) described the first family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to a COL9A3 mutation. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) found that both parents and an unaffected sib of their homozygous proband were heterozygous carriers. Markova et al. (2021) showed that the compound heterozygous variants in their patient were in trans. Rad et al. (2022) found homozygous loss-of-function COL9A3 variants in 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome.

### Heterogeneity

Van Camp et al. (2006) reported a consanguineous Moroccan family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome caused by a homozygous R295X mutation in the collagen IX gene COL9A1. This made COL9A1 the fourth gene identified for Stickler syndrome and the first with recessive inheritance. Baker et al. (2011) described a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome in which affected members were homozygous for a loss-of-function mutation in COL9A2 (c.843_c.846+4del8).

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene: a 23-bp deletion (c.1176_1198del) that causes a frameshift (p.Gln393Cysfs*25). They described this as the first autosomal recessive Stickler family due to a COL9A3 mutation.

Using exome sequencing in the proband of consanguineous family R08-308, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) identified a homozygous single-base duplication in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene (c.650dupC; NM_001853.3). The resulting frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination and absence of the gene product. The variant was not present in public SNP databases. Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous.

In 2 affected patients from 1 family with recessive Stickler syndrome, Nixon et al. (2019) identified a homozygous variant in the COL9A3 gene. Across the 4 type IX collagen families they studied (COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3), 3 of the variants were novel.

Markova et al. (2021) used targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes in their Russian patient and identified compound heterozygous nonsense variants in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (R90X) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (R577X) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing of the parents showed that the variants were in trans, and the healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic variants. The authors described this as the first case of COL9A3-related recessive Stickler syndrome caused by compound heterozygous rather than homozygous loss-of-function variants. ⚠︎

In a Japanese woman with mild Stickler syndrome whose parents were first cousins, Uehara et al. (2022) identified a homozygous frameshift variant in the COL9A3 gene (c.1587dupT, p.Gly530TrpfsTer71). The variant was found by massively parallel sequencing of a panel of known deafness genes and confirmed by direct sequencing. It was novel and present at a frequency of less than 1% in public databases and in in-house Japanese controls. The authors classified it as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria (PVS1, PM2).

Rad et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify homozygous COL9A3 mutations in 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. The proband of family 1 was homozygous for a 10-bp deletion in exon 2 (c.107_116del; NM_001853.3), predicted to cause a frameshift (Pro36ArgfsTer49). Segregation of this variant was checked by Sanger sequencing in available family members. It was present in gnomAD at an allele frequency of 0.00001390 (2 carriers) and was not reported in Iranome, GME, or 1000 Genomes. The 2 affected brothers of family 2 were homozygous for a nonsense mutation in exon 23 (c.1204C-T, R402X). The 2 affected sibs of family 3 were homozygous for a 1-bp deletion in exon 7 (c.355delC), predicted to cause a frameshift (Leu119SerfsTer9; given as fs*10 in the authors' table). Neither of the family 2 or family 3 variants had been reported in public databases.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) noted that all Stickler syndrome patients with type IX collagen defects had high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and hearing loss ranging from mild to severe. Vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not with COL9A3 mutations. The authors suggested that loss of the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen causes a phenotype similar to the other autosomal recessive type IX collagen forms.

Nixon et al. (2019) noted that congenital megalophthalmos and myopia occur in both dominant and recessive forms of Stickler syndrome, whereas sensorineural hearing loss is more common and more severe in recessive Stickler syndrome due to type IX collagen variants. ⚠︎

Markova et al. (2021) compared their patient with 6 previously reported patients from 3 families with homozygous COL9A3 variants. All the reported variants caused loss of function. High myopia and sensorineural hearing loss were present in every case, and cleft palate and retinal detachment were absent in every case. The authors suggested that the absence of cleft palate may distinguish recessive COL9A3-related Stickler syndrome from autosomal dominant forms.

Uehara et al. (2022) suggested that COL9A3 variants may cause a broad range of phenotypes, from mild nonsyndromic hearing loss to more severe syndromic forms. They noted that variability between and within families makes clinical diagnosis difficult.

Rad et al. (2022) reviewed their 3 families together with 4 previously reported families with biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants. Moderate-to-profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate-to-high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration were consistent features. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred occasionally, and skeletal involvement was more variable. The older patients in family 2 had milder skeletal findings than the young sibs in family 3. Unlike Nixon et al., the authors saw no mild phenotypes in heterozygous carriers.

### Pathogenesis

Baker et al. (2011) noted that all 3 collagen IX alpha chains, encoded by COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3, are needed to form a functional collagen IX molecule. They suggested that loss-of-function mutations in any of the collagen IX genes can cause autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome.

### Diagnosis

Because early diagnosis matters for children who may develop both visual and hearing impairment, Nixon et al. (2019) suggested adding COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 to genetic screening panels for patients with congenital hearing loss. Uehara et al. (2022) noted that their patient had first been diagnosed with nonsyndromic hearing loss, and her cataract and retinal detachment were recognized only on re-evaluation after the genetic diagnosis.

### Animal Model

Goldstein et al. (2010) studied oculoskeletal dysplasia (OSD), which segregates as an autosomal recessive trait in Labrador retriever dogs (locus drd1) and Samoyed dogs (locus drd2). Affected pups could be recognized from about 4 to 6 weeks of age by excessive doming of the cranium, a pronounced facial stop, exotropic strabismus, and short-limbed dwarfism with forelimb curvature and varus/valgus deformity. Eye examination showed radial cortical equatorial cataracts, vitreal liquefaction, and frequent complete retinal detachment. The authors noted that the phenotype resembles human arthro-ophthalmopathies such as Stickler syndrome. Dogs heterozygous at either locus had a normal skeleton but could show a mild eye phenotype of retinal folds and vitreal strands. Among Labrador-derived litters, 14 of 34 obligate heterozygotes had retinal folds. However, retinal folds were not linked to the COL9A3 locus, and 18 Labradors with retinal folds were wildtype at that locus. The authors suggested a possible semidominant mechanism in which haploinsufficiency affects the vitreous but not cartilage.

In 8 colony families derived from Labrador retrievers, Goldstein et al. (2010) mapped the drd1 locus to the telomeric end of canine chromosome 24 (maximum lod score 3.589 with marker FH2079), making COL9A3 a positional candidate gene. Affected dogs were homozygous for a 1-bp insertion (G) in exon 1 of COL9A3 that causes a frameshift and a premature stop codon after 48 codons, affecting the COL3 domain. The mutation cosegregated with the disease (lod score 25.889 at theta 0.0): all affected dogs were homozygous and all obligate carriers were heterozygous. Among 59 unrelated Labradors and 19 Labradors with retinal folds, 1 dog was homozygous, and that dog also had short-limbed dwarfism. The allele frequency was estimated at about 1%, and 78 dogs from 21 other breeds were wildtype. On Northern blots, COL9A3 mRNA (about 3.0 kb) was not detectable in retina from an affected dog, whereas COL9A2 was still expressed. Because the promoter was not mutated, the authors suggested that nonsense-mediated decay was the likely mechanism. In drd2-affected Samoyeds, the authors identified a homozygous 1,267-bp deletion at the 5-prime end of COL9A2 that removes exon 1. This deletion cosegregated with the disease (lod score 23.179), and COL9A2 mRNA was absent from affected retina. The authors stated that the canine diseases provide valuable models for the recessive form of Stickler syndrome.

Stavinohova et al. (2019) described oculoskeletal dysplasia in 7 Northern Inuit Dogs from 3 related litters in the UK. All affected dogs had short-limbed (disproportionate) dwarfism and angular limb deformities, together with a variable combination of macroglobus, cataracts, lens coloboma, microphakia, and vitreopathy. Five dogs had retinal detachment. In most dogs the ocular lesions progressed to advanced cataract, lens-induced uveitis, posterior lens subluxation, secondary glaucoma, and blindness. Pedigree analysis indicated autosomal recessive inheritance. Histopathology of an enucleated globe showed a hydrophthalmic globe with lens luxation, cortical cataract, vitreal syneresis, and chronic rhegmatogenous retinal detachment without retinal dysplasia (rosettes), and a presumptive diagnosis of vitreoretinopathy was made. Unlike affected Labrador retrievers, affected Northern Inuit Dogs lacked retinal dysplasia, and heterozygous dogs were free of ocular defects. ⚠︎

After excluding the known drd1 and drd2 variants, Stavinohova et al. (2019) performed whole genome sequencing of 2 affected Northern Inuit Dogs and screened 9 candidate genes. They identified a homozygous nonsense variant in exon 14 of COL9A3 (c.700C-T, R234X), predicted to truncate the protein by 451 amino acids. All 7 affected dogs were homozygous. Of 116 unaffected Northern Inuit Dogs, 31 were heterozygous and 85 were homozygous wildtype (p = 1.4 x 10(-11)), consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance with complete penetrance. All 1,109 dogs of other breeds were homozygous wildtype, and the allele frequency in Northern Inuit Dogs was 0.08. Retinal COL9A3 mRNA was not reduced in an affected dog, suggesting that nonsense-mediated decay may not contribute, although the results were inconclusive.
