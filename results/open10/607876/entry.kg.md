### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by an intronic ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat insertion/expansion in the STARD7 gene (616712) on chromosome 2q11.2.

### Description

Familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by shivering-like tremor of cortical origin (cortical myoclonic tremor), myoclonus, and epilepsy (Striano et al., 2004; Madia et al., 2008). Henden et al. (2016) described FAME as a rare disorder with adult onset, involuntary muscle jerks, cortical myoclonus, and occasional seizures, and noted that it is genetically heterogeneous, with more than 70 families reported worldwide and 5 potential disease loci. Corbett et al. (2019) described FAME as characterized by cortical myoclonic tremor and overt myoclonic and later generalized tonic-clonic seizures, with onset in the second to third decade, variable expressivity within and between families, and anticipation in some families; seizures are typically controlled with antiepileptic drugs for generalized epilepsies, although drug resistance occurs rarely.

### Nomenclature

Guerrini et al. (2001) named the disorder in their family autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME). After long-term follow-up of 3 families showing slight age-dependent progression, Coppola et al. (2011) suggested that the name 'familial or autosomal dominant cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy' (FCTME/ADCME) was more appropriate.

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) described a pedigree with 8 affected individuals who had a nonprogressive disorder beginning between ages 12 and 50 years. All patients had predominantly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 had complex partial seizures. Most rarely had seizures and had normal cognition, but 3 with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All affected members had frontotemporal as well as generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Neurophysiologic studies suggested a cortical origin of the myoclonus: back-averaging showed EEG waves whose frequency matched the EMG bursts. In 5 patients, EMG and EEG over the contralateral rolandic area showed significant coherence at 8 to 25 Hz, with EEG activity preceding EMG activity by 8 to 15 ms, and in 2 individuals ipsilateral EEG-EMG coherence was consistent with transcallosal spread of myoclonic activity. Patients had an enhanced C-reflex at rest and high-amplitude somatosensory and visual evoked potentials. Compared with controls, patients had a significantly reduced resting motor threshold to transcranial magnetic stimulation (38%) and a significantly shorter post-motor evoked potential silent period (101 ms vs 137 ms in controls). The authors suggested that these findings reflect diffuse cortical hyperexcitability particularly involving frontotemporal circuits. ⚠︎

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. Eleven members, aged 24 to 56 years (mean 39.2 years), had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 of them also had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study confirmed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected members. Giant somatosensory evoked potential components and an enhanced long loop reflex I were found in 3 presymptomatic family members, who developed hand tremor and upper-limb myoclonus after 1.5 years of follow-up.

Suppa et al. (2009) described an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy (FCMTE). Clinically affected members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neuropsychological testing showed visuospatial impairment, which the authors suggested might be due to temporal lobe dysfunction. Neurophysiologic findings suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex with normal sensorimotor integration.

Coppola et al. (2011) evaluated clinical, electrophysiologic, and treatment data on 14 patients from 3 families with autosomal dominant cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy linked to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2. Mean age at onset was 20.2 years (range 11 to 40 years), and mean follow-up was 14.0 years. Myoclonus worsened gradually in 10 patients (71.4%), and its severity correlated with disease duration and patient age; 2 patients older than 80 years had myoclonus that interfered with walking on their own. Six patients (42.8%) had seizures, usually between the second and sixth decades of life, and more than a third had psychiatric and neuropsychological dysfunction. Over the long term, EEG background activity slowed progressively as myoclonus worsened, whereas paroxysmal activity and photosensitivity were most evident in the intermediate phases of the disease. The authors concluded that the disorder shows slight age-dependent progression.

Crompton et al. (2012) described a 6-generation kindred of European descent with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy, ascertained in New Zealand and Australia. All 55 affected members had fine hand tremor, with onset typically in adolescence (median age 15 years; range 4-60 years). Proximal myoclonus was present in 44 of 55 (80%), arising later than the hand tremor (median 17 years; range 5-60 years), and generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range 18-76 years). Neurophysiologic testing confirmed features of cortical reflex myoclonus. The authors concluded that the most common phenotype in this family was mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor combined with subtle proximal myoclonus; generalized tonic-clonic seizures were uncommon and occurred around sleep onset following severe generalized myoclonus.

Licchetta et al. (2013) described a large Italian family with FCMTE in which 25 of 127 members were affected (9 males, 16 females); 16 of the 19 living affected members were studied (mean age 47.8 years). Cortical myoclonic tremor was associated with generalized seizures in 10 patients (62.5%). Mean age at onset was 28.1 years for cortical myoclonic tremor and 33.8 years for seizures. Four patients (25%) reported slow progression of the tremor, with severe gait impairment in 1, and psychiatric disorders of variable severity occurred in 37.5% of cases. Polygraphic studies in 15 family members recorded rhythmic bursts at 7 to 15 Hz in all 11 affected members tested, and back-averaging and somatosensory evoked potentials with C-reflex studies in 4 showed a cortical origin of myoclonus in all patients tested. Generalized epileptiform discharges were recorded in 25% of cases and a photoparoxysmal response in 31%.

Corbett et al. (2019) summarized clinical data on 22 FAME2 families of Australian/New Zealand, Italian, Iraqi and Israeli Sephardic Jewish, South African, French/Spanish, French, and Syrian origin. Myoclonus/cortical tremor was present in nearly all affected individuals, whereas the frequency of tonic-clonic seizures varied from 15 to 100% between families; focal seizures occurred in a minority. In the large Australian/New Zealand family (family 1), 55 individuals were affected, with mean onset at 18.6 years (range 4-59); all had myoclonus/cortical tremor, 8 of 55 had tonic-clonic seizures, and 2 of 55 had focal seizures.

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) found that the disorder was inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with high penetrance in the family they studied. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also reported by Striano et al. (2004), Madia et al. (2008), Coppola et al. (2011), Crompton et al. (2012), Licchetta et al. (2013), Henden et al. (2016), and Corbett et al. (2019).

Corbett et al. (2019) found evidence of anticipation in the large Australian/New Zealand FAME2 family: median age at onset was 30 years in generation III, 17 years in generation IV, and 12 years in generation V.

### Mapping

By genomewide linkage analysis in the family with ADCME, Guerrini et al. (2001) identified a 12.4-cM critical region on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 between markers D2S2161 and D2S1897. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for marker D2S2175, and the multipoint lod score reached 3.74 around D2S2175.

de Falco et al. (2003) found linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME).

Striano et al. (2004) reported suggestive linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 (lod score of 1.55) in a new family with BAFME.

In the Italian family with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy, Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p, with a maximum lod score of 5.9.

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 locus responsible for cortical tremor associated with epilepsy.

Madia et al. (2008) genotyped 53 affected individuals from 5 BAFME families from southern Italy, 1 of them not previously reported, using microsatellite markers spanning chromosomes 2p11.1-q12.2 and 8q23.1-q24.11. Multipoint linkage analysis found significant linkage to 2p11.1-q12.2, with a maximum cumulative lod score of 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388.

In an Italian FCMTE family, Suppa et al. (2009) found that affected members shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the pericentromeric region of chromosome 2.

In the large Australian/New Zealand kindred, Crompton et al. (2012) narrowed the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

Licchetta et al. (2013) genotyped 30 members of an Italian family with microsatellite markers at the FCMTE1 (8q), FCMTE2 (2p), and FCMTE3 (5p) loci. They established linkage to the FCMTE2 locus on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 and narrowed the critical interval to a 10.4-Mb segment, reducing the number of positional candidate genes to 59.

Henden et al. (2016) performed identity-by-descent (IBD) analysis on high-density SNP array data from a FAME cohort of 10 European families and 1 Australian/New Zealander family, 8 of which had known disease loci. By finding IBD regions shared by several families, they narrowed the FAME2 locus to a 9.78-Mb interval within 2p11.2-q11.2. They also suggested candidate genes by prioritizing genes with gene coexpression analysis.

### Heterogeneity

Guerrini et al. (2001) excluded linkage of their ADCME family to the familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus on chromosome 8q23.3-q24. On the basis of this, the new localization to 2cen, and the different phenotype, they concluded that ADCME is a new epilepsy syndrome.

de Falco et al. (2003) noted that BAFME had been mapped to chromosome 8q24; their finding of linkage to 2p11.1-q12.2 in 2 Italian families supported genetic heterogeneity and the existence of non-Japanese families with BAFME. They suggested that this locus might be allelic with ADCME.

Striano et al. (2004) concluded that BAFME is a worldwide, genetically heterogeneous condition, probably with Japanese families linked to 8q24 and European families linked to 2p11.1-q12.2.

Suppa et al. (2009) excluded the 8q24 locus by linkage analysis in an Italian FCMTE family, which instead shared a haplotype on pericentromeric chromosome 2.

Corbett et al. (2019) found the STARD7 ATTTC repeat in 2 Italian families carrying a previously proposed ADRA2B variant (c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG), strongly suggesting that the ADRA2B allele is not causative.

### Molecular Genetics

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) excluded candidate genes within the refined 2p11.1-q12.2 locus.

In affected members of 3 FAME2 families (Australian/New Zealand, Italian, and French/Spanish), Corbett et al. (2019) used whole-genome sequencing with repeat-expansion detection (ExpansionHunter and exSTRa) to identify an expansion of an ATTTT repeat with insertion of an ATTTC repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene. The ATTTC repeat was absent from 69 control genomes. By repeat-primed PCR, the ATTTC expansion was found in 137 of 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported FAME2 families, in 20 of 20 familial cases from 6 further families, and in 1 of 52 unrelated singletons, for a total of 158 affected individuals from 22 families. Two of 24 unaffected relatives tested positive; both were from younger generations and were likely presymptomatic. The expansion was not amplified in 28 control DNA samples. Long-read sequencing characterized the repeat structure as (AAATG)n[N](AAAAT)n on the forward strand. In 1 patient from family 1, a PacBio read contained 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats and a nanopore read contained 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats, suggesting somatic variability; in a patient from family 5, a read showed 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats (4,645 bp). The endogenous ATTTT repeat was variable in length in the normal population. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In a preliminary genotype-phenotype analysis of 5 southern Italian families, Madia et al. (2008) found only minor clinical differences among patients carrying the founder disease haplotype, and suggested that other factors do not markedly influence the expressivity of the founder mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Corbett et al. (2019) found no change in STARD7 mRNA (by qPCR and RNA-seq) or protein levels (by Western blot) in patient-derived fibroblasts, and no reads containing ATTTC repeats in RNA-seq data. They suggested that the repeat sequence alone is pathogenic, independent of an effect on the encompassing gene, possibly via RNA toxicity as proposed for other ATTTC expansion disorders. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Corbett et al. (2019) reported that the ATTTC repeat-primed PCR assay was diagnostic for FAME with 100% sensitivity in all families with linkage or suggestive linkage to chromosome 2, and noted that FAME2 is the most frequently observed linked locus in Caucasian patients.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found that the haplotype '5332' of the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113, and D2S2175 segregated with the disease in all 5 southern Italian BAFME families studied, and concluded that these families carry the same mutation inherited from a common ancestor.

Licchetta et al. (2013) found that their Italian FCMTE family carried a founder haplotype identical to that previously seen in FCMTE2 families from the same geographic area, confirming a founder effect in Italian families.

Henden et al. (2016) provided additional evidence of a founder effect in 4 Italian families with FAME2. They also found allelic heterogeneity at the FAME2 locus, with at least 4 distinct founders responsible for the disease.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

In affected members of 3 FAME2 families (Australian/New Zealand, Italian, and French/Spanish), Corbett et al. (2019) identified, by whole-genome sequencing with repeat-expansion detection, an expansion of an ATTTT repeat with insertion of an ATTTC repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene. The ATTTC repeat was absent from 69 control genomes. By repeat-primed PCR, the expansion was found in a total of 158 affected individuals from 22 families, as well as in 1 of 52 unrelated singletons, and in 2 of 24 unaffected relatives, both from younger generations and likely presymptomatic; it was not amplified in 28 control DNA samples. Long-read sequencing showed the structure (AAATG)n[N](AAAAT)n on the forward strand, with reads in 1 patient containing 274 to 345 AAATG and 387 to 390 AAAAT repeats, suggesting somatic variability, and a read from another patient containing 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats (4,645 bp). STARD7 mRNA and protein levels were unchanged in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎
