### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder caused by biallelic variants in PNPLA8, which encodes calcium-independent phospholipase A2-gamma (iPLA2-gamma). Patients can have progressive muscle weakness, lactic acidosis, and structurally abnormal mitochondria in muscle (Saunders et al., 2015; Shukla et al., 2018). The phenotype forms a continuum. At the severe end is a congenital-onset form with congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early death. Intermediate forms include infantile- and childhood-onset neurodegeneration with progressive microcephaly. At the mild end is an adolescent- or adult-onset form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy (Abdel-Hamid et al., 2024; Nakamura et al., 2024). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl (CMH193) who was evaluated for suspected mitochondrial myopathy. In her first year she was asymptomatic and met developmental milestones, apart from toe-walking. She had a positive Gowers sign at age 2 years and could no longer walk by age 3 years. Her course was progressive, with proximal muscle weakness, declining fine motor skills, dysarthria, dysmetria, and dystonia. Her hypotonia progressed to spasticity. She had complex partial seizures with left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. Her body habitus was very lean and she did not gain weight. She had significant lactic acidosis that required bicarbonate supplementation. A muscle biopsy at age 4 showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic fibers and no ragged red fibers. On electron microscopy, mitochondria showed marked disarray of cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions. There were subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggesting degenerating mitochondria, and secondary lysosomes were increased. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle increased signal in the medial globus pallidus bilaterally, which was no longer seen 2 years later.

Shukla et al. (2018) described a female infant from India born to consanguineous parents (patient 1). At birth she had congenital microcephaly, with a head circumference of 29 cm (-4 SD). Multifocal clonic seizures began on day 2 of life, and she had spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MRS showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. TORCH testing was negative.

Shukla et al. (2018) also described a girl of India/Fiji origin born to nonconsanguineous parents (patient 2). She developed normally until about 13 months of age and then regressed, with episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures. She remained at an 8-month developmental level, did not speak, and needed a gastrostomy tube. She developed acquired microcephaly, with a head circumference of -4.7 SD at 15 years. Other features were hypotonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent reflexes, choreiform movements, dystonia, and contractures. She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis and obstructive sleep apnea. Visual evoked potentials had severely reduced amplitudes. Serial MRI showed progressive atrophy of the cerebellum, brainstem, and cerebrum, with thinning of the corpus callosum. MRS showed a persistent lipid-lactate peak. Blood lactate was elevated, and GDF15 was elevated at 1293 pg/ml.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset severe and progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) described 2 adult Lithuanian sibs, a brother and a sister. Both had slowly progressive ataxia beginning between ages 10 and 14 years, accompanied by progressive sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, and cognition was preserved in both. The brother's symptoms began at age 14 years with lower limb weakness, distal muscle atrophy, ataxia, and gait disturbance. From age 17 years he could not walk without assistance. He later developed tetraparesis, absent lower limb reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, foot deformities, dysmetria, and distal hypoesthesia. At age 45 years he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, his MMSE score was normal, and brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. The sister developed walking difficulty and weakness in both lower limbs at age 14 years. When examined at age 21 she had distal limb muscle atrophy, absent lower limb reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia, and appendicular dysmetria, and she could not keep an upright posture. Her cranial nerves and cognition were normal. Nerve conduction studies showed an axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, and EMG showed no myopathic changes. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families with PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathy. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease. The authors noted that previously reported patients with congenital onset had congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early death. Patients 2 and 3 had progressive microcephaly, seizures beginning in infancy, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral basal ganglia degeneration, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype of PNPLA8-related disease.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 patients from 12 unrelated families. Common features were central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). The 8 patients in Families 1 through 8 were severely affected. In this group, 5 of 7 had congenital microcephaly, 7 of 7 had global developmental delay with no milestones attained, 7 of 8 had peripheral spasticity, and 8 of 8 had seizure onset within 7 months of age. Neuroimaging in the severe group showed a simplified gyral pattern (6 of 6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7 of 7), and severe progressive cerebral atrophy (5 of 5). The 2 patients in Families 9 and 10 were of intermediate severity. The 4 patients in Families 11 and 12, from Iran and Egypt, had a relatively mild phenotype. All 4 attained walking and all 4 had regression of milestones. Ataxia was present in 3 of 4, and intellectual disability was mild or absent in 3 of 4. Seizures were unique or absent in all 4. Brain MRI in the mild group showed little or no cerebral volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy.

Chen et al. (2024) reported a 27-year-old Chinese woman whose gait became abnormal at age 11 years. At age 17 she still had amenorrhea and an infantile uterus, and at age 21 she developed head and limb tremors. Brain MRI suggested mild cerebellar atrophy. A biceps muscle biopsy showed mild myopathic changes on light microscopy and mitochondrial inclusions on electron microscopy.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015), the anion gap was consistently elevated from age 5 years. At age 7 she had elevated blood lactate (3.8-5.6 mmol/L) and elevated pyruvate, with a lactate:pyruvate ratio of 17.5-21.1. Plasma acylcarnitines and creatine phosphokinase were normal. CSF showed deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. In muscle, SDH and COX activities and electron transport chain function were normal. The authors suggested that the patient had mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect.

In patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018), electron microscopy of the muscle biopsy showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria surrounded by membrane whorls. The mitochondria had disrupted cristae and electron-dense deposits, and lipid droplets and pools of glycogen were also seen. Activities of electron transport chain complexes I to IV were in the normal range. Sequencing of mitochondrial DNA found no variation.

In patient skin fibroblasts, Nakamura et al. (2024) found that cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin levels appeared lower than in controls, with no significant correlation with severity. Fibroblasts from their Patient 1 had normal mitochondrial morphology on electron microscopy but significantly reduced maximal respiration.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MMLA is autosomal recessive. Shukla et al. (2018) noted that biallelic PNPLA8 variants segregated from carrier parents in their 2 families, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Nakamura et al. (2024) reported that the pedigrees of their 12 families were consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

In a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) performed trio exome sequencing and identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsTer29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsTer4). The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Each was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs did not carry the c.334_337delAATT variant. Both variants were absent from the EVS database and from an internal database; for c.334_337delAATT, the internal database comprised 1,913 samples. On Western blot, the patient's muscle showed a dramatic decrease in the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD iPLA2-gamma bands compared with 3 control muscles. A 110-kD band was also absent. Mass spectrometry identified this band as HSPA4 reacting nonspecifically with the antibody, and the authors speculated that loss of this chaperone might contribute to the disease.

In patient 1 of Shukla et al. (2018), the child of consanguineous Indian parents, whole exome sequencing identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in PNPLA8, c.517G-T (p.Glu173Ter; NM_001256007.1). Sanger sequencing showed that both parents were heterozygous. In patient 2, of India/Fiji origin, trio whole exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous PNPLA8 mutations: a 2-bp deletion, c.2275_2276delCT (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), and a nonsense mutation, c.419C-A (p.Ser140Ter). Both were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and segregation analysis showed that each was inherited from a carrier parent. ⚠︎

In a patient with prenatal-onset severe neurodegeneration, Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous missense mutation in the patatin domain of PNPLA8 (c.1874A-G).

In 2 Lithuanian sibs with adolescent-onset ataxia and neuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in exon 11 of PNPLA8. One was a novel 1-bp deletion, c.2269del (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10), which was absent from the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, and gnomAD databases. The other was the previously reported 2-bp deletion c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), which was inherited from the mother. The father's DNA was not available, but the sequencing data showed that the 2 variants were in trans. Sanger sequencing confirmed both variants in the affected sister. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing in 2 families with PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathy. They identified a loss-of-function mutation, c.1231C-T (p.Arg411Ter), in Family I and a missense mutation, c.1559T-A (p.Val520Asp), in Family II.

Nakamura et al. (2024) used exome or genome sequencing to identify biallelic ultra-rare PNPLA8 variants in 14 individuals with neurological manifestations from 12 unrelated families. There were 6 males and 8 females, aged 3 days to 33 years. The 14 variants, 12 of them novel, comprised 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense variants. Each had a population frequency below 0.001%. Patient 1, a Japanese boy, carried c.1625+3_1625+6del. In his lymphoblastoid cells this variant caused skipping of exons 6 to 8, and his skin fibroblasts showed a significant reduction of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, which the authors interpreted as a null effect. Patient 2 carried the splice variant c.2075-2A-G and Patient 5 carried c.1684-2A-G. No patient cells were available for these 2 patients, but SpliceAI predicted loss of the splice acceptor site for each variant (delta scores 0.97 and 0.93, respectively). The authors also studied PNPLA8 protein in patient fibroblasts. The p.Glu296LysfsTer6 variant from severely affected Patient 6 had a null effect. Fibroblasts from mildly affected Patient 12 (p.Leu759AlafsTer4) and from non-neuronal Patient 14 (p.Val7Ter) showed weak expression of the 77-kDa protein, suggesting residual function. Fibroblasts from a previously reported, severely affected patient homozygous for the missense variant p.Val520Asp expressed PNPLA8 at levels similar to controls.

In a non-neurological cohort, Nakamura et al. (2024) also identified 2 individuals from 2 unrelated Iranian families who were homozygous for distinct N-terminal PNPLA8 frameshift variants affecting only the 88-kDa isoform. One individual had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss. In the family with optic atrophy, an affected sib was only heterozygous for the variant. The authors therefore considered the significance of these variants undetermined.

In a 27-year-old Chinese woman with cerebellar ataxia, tremors, and primary ovarian insufficiency, Chen et al. (2024) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous PNPLA8 mutations: a missense mutation, c.1777T-G (p.Tyr593Asp), and a frameshift mutation, c.1515_1516delTT (p.Tyr506SerfsTer27). She had inherited the variants from her parents.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that the 3 patients then known to have PNPLA8 variants had a similar but variable clinical picture. They stated that so few individuals had been reported that a genotype-phenotype correlation could not yet be made.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that c.2275_2276del had previously been reported, together with another truncating variant, in a patient with severe early-childhood neurodegeneration, whereas their sibs had a milder phenotype. Both truncating variants carried by the sibs lie in the last coding exon of PNPLA8. The authors suggested that these transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and may retain partial enzyme activity.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) summarized earlier reports of PNPLA8 variants and noted that only 6 patients with biallelic variants had been reported. These reports described a congenital-onset form, a childhood neurodegeneration form with progressive microcephaly, and an adult-onset form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy. The authors suggested that age at onset correlates strongly with the location of the variant rather than with the variant type. They regarded the congenital, infantile, childhood-onset, and adult-onset forms as overlapping parts of a single spectrum.

Nakamura et al. (2024) combined their patients with 11 previously reported individuals from 8 families, for a total of 25 individuals from 20 families. The PNPLA8-related disorder formed a continuum from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy to childhood-onset neurodegeneration. Biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were found specifically in the severe group with congenital microcephaly. Patients of intermediate severity carried a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent C-terminal frameshift p.Leu759AlafsTer4. All mildly affected patients, who had late-onset movement disorders, carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10 or p.Leu759AlafsTer4).

Chen et al. (2024) reviewed 25 patients from 21 families with PNPLA8 variants. They concluded that age at onset is a very important factor in the clinical phenotype and prognosis. They observed that adult females with PNPLA8 variants may present with primary ovarian dysfunction, and they suggested that mitochondrial inclusion bodies may be a pathologic hallmark of the disorder.

### Pathogenesis

Saunders et al. (2015) suggested that in PNPLA8-related disease, as in PLA2G6-related disease, the primary mechanism may be degeneration of the mitochondrial inner membrane. In their view, electron transport proteins would be affected only secondarily, through altered membrane homeostasis.

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that all PNPLA8 variants reported at that time introduced premature stop codons and were likely to result in truncated protein or nonsense-mediated decay. They also noted that the abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes seen in their patient 2 matched findings in Pnpla8-null mice, which develop a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder.

Nakamura et al. (2024) generated cerebral organoids from CRISPR/Cas9 PNPLA8 knockout iPSCs. At 12 weeks, the knockout organoids had a smaller subventricular zone-like region, fewer PAX6+HOPX+ basal radial glial cells, and fewer SATB2+ upper-layer neurons, whereas basal intermediate progenitors and deep-layer neurons were unchanged. BrdU+ cells were increased, suggesting an altered S phase. Cerebral organoids derived from iPSCs of Patient 1 reproduced these findings, with fewer basal radial glial cells and fewer upper-layer neurons. Spatial transcriptomics showed altered fate specification of apical radial glia and enrichment of lysophospholipid pathway genes. PNPLA8 knockout neural progenitor cells had reduced LPA, LPE, and PA, with normal mitochondrial function. Adding LPA increased the number of basal radial glial cells in patient organoids.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2007) generated mice null for iPLA2-gamma (Pnpla8) by eliminating the enzyme's active site through homologous recombination. The null mice had multiple bioenergetic dysfunctional phenotypes, including growth retardation, cold intolerance, and reduced exercise endurance. After transverse aortic constriction, mortality from cardiac stress was greatly increased. Mitochondrial function was abnormal, with a 65% decrease in ascorbate-induced complex IV-mediated oxygen consumption. Myocardial cardiolipin content was reduced and the mix of cardiolipin molecular species was altered. The authors concluded that iPLA2-gamma is essential for efficient mitochondrial bioenergetic function because it shapes mitochondrial membrane lipid metabolism and composition.

Song et al. (2010) fed a high-fat Western diet to iPLA2-gamma-knockout and wildtype mice. Compared with wildtype mice, the knockout mice were resistant to diet-induced increases in body weight, adiposity, and blood cholesterol, glucose, and insulin. This was despite similar food intake and similar digestion and absorption of dietary fat. The knockout mice were also relatively resistant to diet-induced insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, and changes in the balance of fat versus carbohydrate fuel use. Knockout skeletal muscle had impaired mitochondrial beta-oxidation of fatty acids. On the Western diet, knockout muscle and liver accumulated more long-chain acylcarnitine species than wildtype tissue. The knockout mice also excreted more long-chain acylcarnitine in urine and deposited much less triacylglycerol in the liver.

Yoda et al. (2010) found that iPLA2-gamma (Pnpla8)-knockout mice were born at the expected mendelian ratio and appeared normal and healthy at 1 month of age. Growth retardation began at 2 months, and kyphosis and significant muscle weakness were present by 4 months. On electron microscopy, knockout skeletal muscle showed swollen mitochondria, fewer mitochondria, and atrophy of myofilaments. Knockout muscle also showed increased lipid peroxidation and induction of several oxidative stress-related genes. The composition of cardiolipin and of other phospholipid subclasses was altered, and levels of myoprotective prostanoids were reduced. The authors concluded that loss of iPLA2-gamma causes mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress, leading to loss of skeletal muscle structure and function. They also suggested that iPLA2-gamma may help modulate the production of lipid mediators in vivo.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that their patient's phenotype resembled that of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice show mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, enhanced insulin sensitivity, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance. They also have dysmorphic mitochondria similar to those seen in the patient's muscle.

Hirabayashi et al. (2023) reported that in the liver, PNPLA8 and PNPLA7 form a phospholipase/lysophospholipase pathway that breaks down membrane phosphatidylcholine into water-soluble glycerophosphocholine (GPC). This process makes choline stored in hepatic phosphatidylcholine available for methyl metabolism and for replenishing the methionine cycle. PNPLA8-deficient mice reproduced most of the phenotypes of PNPLA7-deficient mice. These included growth retardation, hypoglycemia, hypolipidemia, increased energy consumption, reduced adiposity, and increased FGF21. They also included an altered histone/DNA methylation landscape, which the authors attributed to methionine insufficiency. Unlike wildtype mice fed a methionine/choline-deficient diet, the knockout mice had decreased hepatic triglyceride. The authors suggested this was likely due to reduced lipogenesis and reduced GPC-derived glycerol flux.

Moon et al. (2023) generated a skeletal muscle-specific iPLA2-gamma knockout mouse (SKMiPLA2gammaKO). They crossed mice carrying loxP sites flanking exon 5, which encodes the enzyme active site, with transgenic mice expressing Cre recombinase from the human alpha-actin promoter. Western blot analysis of mitochondria from white adipose tissue, liver, and skeletal muscle confirmed that ablation was restricted to skeletal muscle. The knockout mice had pronounced muscle weakness, muscle atrophy, and increased blood lactate. These resulted from defects in mitochondrial function that impaired pyruvate processing and caused bioenergetic inefficiency. Tibialis anterior and quadriceps muscle mass was decreased by 30% in males, and females had a greater loss of tibialis anterior mass. Transmission electron microscopy of tibialis anterior muscle from 7-month-old knockout mice showed a prevalence of mitochondria with severe ultrastructural damage, including irregular cristae and electron-dense inclusions, along with disruption of normal striated muscle structure. Histology showed internalized nuclei and smaller myocyte diameter in tibialis anterior. Quadriceps showed irregularly sized myocytes and more markedly small myofibers. Myogenin expression was markedly increased while MyoD was unchanged, indicating more active regenerative myogenesis. The fast-twitch tibialis anterior was further shifted toward a fast-twitch type II fiber phenotype.

In skeletal muscle of SKMiPLA2gammaKO mice, Moon et al. (2023) found that mitochondrial DNA copy number was significantly decreased. Protein levels of the mitochondrial fusion/fission proteins DRP1, OPA1, and MFN1 were dramatically diminished, despite only modest decreases in their mRNA levels. Mitochondrial markers (VDAC, ANT, and citrate synthase) were unchanged. Protein synthesis through the Akt/mTOR/S6 pathway was significantly impaired, whereas autophagic flux was unchanged. The authors concluded that reduced net protein synthesis in the setting of bioenergetic inefficiency likely underlay the muscle atrophy.

In functional tests, Moon et al. (2023) found that 7- to 8-month-old SKMiPLA2gammaKO mice had weaker grip force and fatigued more rapidly during treadmill exercise, regardless of diet or sex. After treadmill exercise, serum lactate rose more in knockout than wildtype mice, even though average exercise duration was shorter. In high-intensity treadmill exercise at 18 m/min for 4 minutes, 3 of 6 knockout females were completely exhausted within 1 to 2.5 minutes. Blood lactate accumulation was markedly increased in knockout mice, with a corresponding decline in blood glucose. Ex vivo, extensor digitorum longus muscle weight and body weight were lower in knockout mice, but specific tetanic peak tension normalized to cross-sectional area did not differ between genotypes. The authors noted that this limited their ability to exclude neurologic or behavioral contributions to the weakness.

Moon et al. (2023) used high-resolution respirometry and found that tibialis anterior mitochondria from male SKMiPLA2gammaKO mice on normal chow consumed 50% less oxygen than wildtype controls with glutamate/pyruvate/malate as substrate. The difference was even larger in females. Protein levels of complex IV and cytochrome c were dramatically reduced. Complex I and II protein levels were unchanged, but their catalytic activities were decreased by about 50% and 25%, respectively. Mitochondrial membrane potential was significantly lower with either complex I or complex II substrates, and hydrogen peroxide production from forward and reverse electron transfer was greatly attenuated. Shotgun lipidomics showed significantly lower levels of tetra-18:2 cardiolipin, the most abundant mature cardiolipin species in skeletal muscle, and decreased 22:6-containing cardiolipin species. The authors suggested that loss of tetra-18:2 cardiolipin contributes to inefficient respiration by disrupting the stability and association of electron transport chain proteins.

On a high-fat diet, SKMiPLA2gammaKO mice were resistant to weight gain, gaining about 8 g on average after 3 months compared with about 16 g in wildtype mice (Moon et al., 2023). They showed improved glucose clearance, with a lower peak glucose (about 240 vs about 320 mg/dL) and a decreased area under the curve, but insulin tolerance and serum insulin levels did not differ significantly. Compared with wildtype mice, the knockout mice had dramatically less hepatic lipid droplet content, significantly lower hepatic triacylglycerol and diacylglycerol species, no increase in liver-to-body weight ratio, diminished diet-induced fibrosis, and higher hepatic glycogen. In contrast, diet-induced increases in white adipose tissue mass were significantly more prominent in the knockout mice. Moon et al. (2023) concluded that iPLA2-gamma supports skeletal muscle bioenergetic function by preserving mitochondrial structural and functional integrity, maintaining an appropriate membrane environment for assembly of protein complexes, and coordinating signaling between skeletal muscle and liver. They concluded that the knockout phenotype reflected multifactorial mitochondrial dysfunction with no single identifiable precipitating cause.
