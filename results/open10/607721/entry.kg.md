### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair (NSLH; Mazzanti syndrome) is a RASopathy. Its features include Noonan syndrome-like craniofacial features, cardiac defects, cognitive deficits and behavioral problems, and reduced growth that is generally associated with growth hormone (GH) deficiency. Patients also have darkly pigmented skin and a distinctive combination of ectodermal anomalies, including loose anagen hair (Garavelli et al., 2015). Couser et al. (2018) noted that the disorder has the typical features of Noonan syndrome plus macrocephaly and loose anagen hair, and that intellectual disability is more common than in Noonan syndrome. The disorder is virtually always caused by the invariant SHOC2 c.4A-G (p.Ser2Gly) mutation (Capalbo et al., 2012; Garavelli et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) found that their 3 patients did not fit any known condition. Their appearance was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, and they also had a behavioral phenotype, GH deficiency, and ectodermal findings. Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed that the patients might represent a distinct, previously unreported syndrome, which they named 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.' Garavelli et al. (2015) referred to the disorder as Mazzanti syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children with short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, and a short neck with redundant skin. The children had severe GH deficiency and mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). They also had a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented, hairless skin and hair defined as loose anagen hair. Two of the 3 children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that 25 patients with a SHOC2 mutation shared a consistent phenotype, previously termed Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. Facial features looked typical of Noonan syndrome. Patients had reduced growth, often with proven GH deficiency, along with cognitive deficits and hyperactive behavior that improved with age. Their hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow-growing, and microscopy confirmed loose anagen hair in 12. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Most also had cardiac anomalies, among which mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were overrepresented. The voice was hypernasal. As infants, several patients had been thought to have features suggestive of Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported that all 8 of their patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that the clinical manifestations partially overlap with typical Noonan or CFC syndrome, and that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair is a distinctive feature of SHOC2 mutation-positive patients.

Capalbo et al. (2012) reported a patient with molecularly confirmed NSLH who had severe short stature associated with GH insensitivity, as well as chronic complex tics. The authors stated that tics had never been described before in this syndrome. The most common causes of secondary tics in childhood were excluded.

Capalbo et al. (2012) described 2 girls with NSLH, each born to nonconsanguineous parents. Patient 1 was born at 34 weeks after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios. She was referred at age 7 years for severe short stature (height -5.7 SDS), and her bone age was delayed by 3 years. She had macrocephaly, high forehead, epicanthic folds, palpebral ptosis, hypertelorism, a high narrow palate, pterygium colli, pectus excavatum with widely spaced nipples, and deep palmar and plantar creases. She had mild psychomotor delay (IQ 52) and a hypoplastic corpus callosum. Over time she developed nail dystrophy, sparse and easily pulled hair, and dark hairless skin. At 9 years of age she developed complex motor and vocal tics, followed by a generalized seizure. MRI also showed a diffuse reduction in white matter.

Patient 2 of Capalbo et al. (2012) was born at term with a head circumference above the 90th centile and a large atrial septal defect. She was referred at age 9 years for short stature (height -3.21 SDS), and her bone age was delayed by 2 years. She had macrocephaly, high forehead, epicanthic folds, a low-set ear, hypertelorism, a short neck with redundant skin, and pectus excavatum. Her hair was fine and sparse, and her skin was darkly pigmented with cafe-au-lait spots. She also had hyperextensible joints, mild scoliosis, and Chiari type I malformation. She had mild psychomotor delay (IQ 65) with behavioral problems that later improved. Puberty was delayed, and she started hormone replacement at age 16.

Gargano et al. (2014) reported severe hydrops fetalis in a preterm newborn who was heterozygous for the SHOC2 c.4A-G mutation. They concluded that hydrops is a clinically relevant complication of NSLH, as it is in other RASopathies such as Noonan, CFC, and Costello syndromes.

Choi et al. (2015) reported a girl who was diagnosed with NSLH at age 6 years on the basis of profound short stature and ectodermal anomalies, including sparse, easily pluckable hair. At age 10 years she had recurrent left hemiplegia from transient ischemic attacks. Cerebrovascular imaging showed occlusion or narrowing of both internal carotid arteries and both middle cerebral arteries, with distal moyamoya-like vessels. The authors stated that this was the first reported case of NSLH with moyamoya syndrome.

Garavelli et al. (2015) reported a child with molecularly confirmed NSLH who developed a neuroblastoma. The tumor was first suspected at 3 months of age on abdominal ultrasound examination. The authors noted that this was the second instance of neuroblastoma in NSLH and suggested that individuals heterozygous for the SHOC2 c.4A-G mutation may be predisposed to this malignancy. ⚠︎

Lo et al. (2015) described 2 individuals with NSLH who also had moyamoya disease, a chronic cerebrovascular condition caused by progressive stenosis of the arteries around the circle of Willis with prominent arterial collateral circulation.

Uehara et al. (2018) reported a 28-year-old man with NSLH1 who had intellectual disability, short stature, dysmorphic facial features, and thin hair. He developed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy at age 7 years and afebrile generalized seizures at age 18 years. At age 24 years he presented with 3 days of intermittent fever, right chest pain, and a malar butterfly rash. He fulfilled both the ACR and SLICC criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). ⚠︎

Couser et al. (2018) reported a female patient with NSLH1 who was born with a posterior cleft palate, micrognathia, and mild hypotonia. In childhood and young adulthood she developed hearing loss and strabismus. She also developed hypopituitarism with deficiencies of GH, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and gonadotropins. The authors noted that true cleft palate did not appear to have been reported before in Noonan syndrome or NSLH, and that endocrine abnormalities other than GH deficiency are rarely documented. They suggested that the SHOC2 mutation may be responsible for the cleft palate and hypopituitarism. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Uehara et al. (2018) noted that SLE had been reported in 5 patients with RASopathies: 3 with SHOC2 variants, 1 with a PTPN11 variant, and 1 with a KRAS variant. They suggested that the association between SHOC2 variants and SLE is more than a chance association. They recommended that SLE be suspected in patients with constitutional SHOC2 pathogenic variants who have fever of unknown origin.

### Biochemical Features

In their patient with NSLH, Capalbo et al. (2012) found only a blunted increase in IGF1 after exogenous GH on an IGF1 generation test. This indicated mild GH insensitivity, with normal STAT5 activation.

In a report of 2 patients, Capalbo et al. (2012) found that patient 1 had low serum IGF1 and a normal GH peak after arginine stimulation. Her IGF1 rose only mildly on an IGF1 generation test, which the authors interpreted as GH insensitivity. Her GHR gene was normal, and GH-induced tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT5 was normal. Patient 2 had a low GH peak after clonidine and arginine stimulation, with IGF1 at the lower limit of normal, indicating partial GH deficiency. The authors noted that GH insensitivity had not previously been described in NSLH.

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that the heterozygous SHOC2 S2G mutation had arisen de novo in all patients whose parents were available for study. Uehara et al. (2018) also identified the mutation de novo in a patient with NSLH1. ✗

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) chose SHOC2 as a candidate gene through an in silico protein-interaction network analysis seeded with known RAS-MAPK disease proteins. They studied 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who had no mutations in the known genes. In 4 unrelated sporadic patients, they identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation was de novo in the 3 patients whose parents were available. It was present in several tissues, pointing to germline origin.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) then screened 410 mutation-negative patients with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype and found the same S2G mutation in 21 of them. The mutation was de novo in the 12 families in which parental DNA was available. In total, 25 patients carried the mutation. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found, which the authors said strongly suggested a specific pathogenic role for S2G. Functional studies showed that the mutation created an N-myristoylation site and caused aberrant targeting of SHOC2 to the plasma membrane (see Pathogenesis).

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They found the previously identified S2G mutation in 8 patients. They found no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 leukemia patients.

Capalbo et al. (2012) found the SHOC2 c.4A-G (p.Ser2Gly) mutation in both of their patients, which confirmed the diagnosis of NSLH. Patient 1 had previously been screened for mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, SOS1, MEK1, and BRAF, with negative results.

Gargano et al. (2014) found that a preterm newborn with severe hydrops fetalis was heterozygous for the recurrent c.4A-G mutation in SHOC2.

Choi et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.4A-G (S2G) mutation in the SHOC2 gene in a girl with NSLH and moyamoya syndrome.

In 2 individuals with NSLH and moyamoya disease, Lo et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous germline mutation in the SHOC2 gene.

After observing neuroblastoma in a child with NSLH, Garavelli et al. (2015) scanned the region of the SHOC2 coding sequence encompassing the c.4A-G change in selected pediatric cancer cohorts. These were malignancies documented to occur in RASopathies: neuroblastoma, brain tumors, rhabdomyosarcoma, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and myeloid leukemia. They found no functionally relevant cancer-associated variant and concluded that their results did not support a major role for somatic SHOC2 mutations in these pediatric cancers.

Using medical exome sequencing, Uehara et al. (2018) identified a de novo SHOC2 c.4A-G (S2G) mutation in a man with NSLH1 and SLE.

Couser et al. (2018) stated that every NSLH1 case reported to date, including their patient, carried the SHOC2 c.4A-G (p.Ser2Gly) mutation. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Capalbo et al. (2012) noted that their 2 patients carried the same SHOC2 mutation but had different phenotypes. Patient 1 had more pronounced features, epilepsy, and chronic tics, none of which had previously been reported with SHOC2 mutations. In contrast, patient 2's mild delay improved over time. The authors suggested that the SHOC2 mutation may alter GHR signaling through RAS-MAPK dysregulation. They speculated that modifier genes and environmental factors could explain the clinical heterogeneity. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that the S2G substitution creates an N-myristoylation site in SHOC2. In Cos-1 cells, SHOC2 S2G took up [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype SHOC2 and the disease-unrelated S2A mutant did not. Wildtype SHOC2 was distributed through the cytoplasm and nucleus and moved to the nucleus after EGF stimulation. In contrast, S2G was targeted to the plasma membrane and did not move to the nucleus. An inhibitor of N-myristoyltransferase reduced or abolished membrane localization of the mutant.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that SHOC2 S2G did not form heterodimers with wildtype SHOC2 and did not block nuclear translocation of the wildtype protein, which ruled out a dominant-negative effect. In Neuro2A neuroblastoma cells, but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells, the mutant increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation compared with wildtype. The authors proposed that constant membrane localization of SHOC2 S2G prolongs PP1C-mediated dephosphorylation of RAF1 at ser259, which would sustain MAPK activation.

Motta et al. (2016) found that leucine-rich repeats 1 to 13 of SHOC2 form a structurally recognizable domain. This domain was required for SHOC2 import into the nucleus and for the constitutive plasma membrane targeting of the S2G mutant. The N-terminal region rich in KEKE motifs was needed for efficient nuclear export. The S2G mutant localized to both raft and non-raft membrane domains and moved to non-raft domains after stimulation. Trapping SHOC2 at different subcellular sites affected the strength and timing of ERK signaling in different ways. The authors suggested that SHOC2 has a dual, counteracting role in controlling ERK signaling from different intracellular compartments.

### Diagnosis

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve analysis to detect the SHOC2 S2G mutation, for use in screening for this apparently common mutation.

### Clinical Management

Capalbo et al. (2012) reported that recombinant human GH therapy did not significantly improve linear growth in their patient 1, who had GH insensitivity. Her growth stopped when treatment was withdrawn, and after 6 years of treatment her height remained -5 SDS. Patient 2, who had partial GH deficiency, showed only a blunted response to GH therapy.

Choi et al. (2015) started their patient with NSLH on recombinant human GH at 8 years of age. After moyamoya syndrome developed, she was treated with aspirin and a calcium channel blocker.

The SLE in the patient with NSLH1 reported by Uehara et al. (2018) was successfully treated with prednisolone.

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed in C. elegans that wildtype human SHOC2 could functionally replace sur-8, but SHOC2 S2G could not. When S2G was induced in wildtype worms at the early L3 stage, it caused a protruding vulva, reduced egg laying, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. Wildtype SHOC2 fused to an N-myristoylation sequence produced the same result, but untagged wildtype SHOC2 did not. These neomorphic defects resulted from abnormal vulval morphogenesis rather than altered specification of vulval cell fate.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G]

Cordeddu et al. (2009) studied 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who had no mutations in the known genes. In 4 unrelated sporadic patients, they identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. In a further 410 mutation-negative patients with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, they found the same mutation in 21. This gave a total of 25 patients, who shared a phenotype of Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. The mutation was de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available, and it was present in several tissues, pointing to germline origin. The S2G substitution created an N-myristoylation site, leading to myristoylation of SHOC2 and its constitutive targeting to the plasma membrane. The mutant did not translocate to the nucleus after EGF stimulation, and in Neuro2A cells it enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation. In C. elegans, SHOC2 S2G could not replace sur-8 and caused neomorphic vulval morphogenesis defects.
