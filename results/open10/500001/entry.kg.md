### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is caused by homoplasmic or heteroplasmic mutation in the mitochondrially encoded MT-ND6 (516006), MT-ND4 (516003), MT-ND1 (516000), or MT-ND3 (516002) genes. ⚠︎

### Description

Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is a maternally inherited disorder in which vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia occur in variable combinations (Wang et al., 2009). Dystonia may be associated with subacute visual loss or asymptomatic optic atrophy and with bilateral symmetrical lucencies of the basal ganglia, especially the putamen (Marsden et al., 1986). The disorder is an oxidative phosphorylation disorder associated with complex I deficiency (Jun et al., 1996; De Vries et al., 1996; Wang et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Wallace (1970) described a new manifestation of Leber disease (Leber hereditary optic neuropathy) and proposed a new explanation for the agent responsible for its unusual pattern of inheritance.

Marsden et al. (1986) described a disorder in 7 members of 2 families in whom dystonia was variably associated with subacute visual loss or asymptomatic optic atrophy, and with striking bilateral symmetrical lucencies on CT scan, especially involving the putamen.

Novotny et al. (1986) studied a kindred in which 8 members had the neuroretinopathy of Leber disease, 14 had a progressive, generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration, and 1 had both disorders.

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family in which members had maternally transmitted Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), early-onset dystonia associated with bilateral basal ganglia lesions, or both.

Shoffner et al. (1995) described 2 additional pedigrees carrying the 14459 mutation in MT-ND6. In one pedigree, 2 family members developed LHON, and the daughter had basal ganglia lesions that caused no symptoms. In the second pedigree, 1 person had generalized dystonia beginning in childhood, along with bilateral basal ganglia lesions.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family with a rare form of LHON associated with hereditary spastic dystonia. Some family members had both the neurologic and ophthalmologic lesions, whereas others had only one type of abnormality.

Tarnopolsky et al. (2004) described 2 Caucasian pedigrees with maternally inherited LHON phenotypes that varied greatly from patient to patient. In 1 patient with spasticity/dystonia, the only abnormal screening test was a high T2 signal in both putamina. A male patient in the second pedigree had optic neuropathy without spasticity or dystonia.

Gropman et al. (2004) reported a family with the G14459A mtDNA mutation and a broad range of clinical features. The proband was a 3-year-old girl with anarthria, dystonia, spasticity, and mild encephalopathy. Brain MRI showed bilateral, symmetric basal ganglia lucencies, and she had cerebral and systemic lactic acidosis. Her maternal first cousin had a much milder phenotype, with a new-onset limp and mild hemiparesis, and similar MRI findings.

Watanabe et al. (2006) described a Japanese family in which affected members had generalized dystonia, attributed to striatal degeneration, beginning in childhood, and optic neuropathy beginning later in life.

Spruijt et al. (2007) described a family in which LHON and spastic dystonia occurred together. They stated that the clinical features in this family were similar to those of previously described families with LHON and dystonia caused by an ND6 mutation.

Wang et al. (2009) described a Chinese family with 18 patients affected by LDYT, in which vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia occurred in variable combinations. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Jun et al. (1996) found a 60% reduction (P less than 0.005) of complex I-specific activity in submitochondrial particles from EBV-transformed lymphoblasts of patients with the ND6 14459 mutation compared with controls, with no differences in the activity of complexes II plus III, III, and IV.

De Vries et al. (1996) found a severe complex I deficiency in a muscle biopsy from the Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia, which they stated linked the newly identified mtDNA mutations to the rare, complex phenotype.

### Inheritance

Novotny et al. (1986) found that the mode of inheritance in a kindred with Leber disease and dystonia was compatible with maternal transmission, and suggested that the disorder may be a mitochondrial disease. Maternal transmission was also observed in the families reported by Jun et al. (1994) and Tarnopolsky et al. (2004).

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (2009) noted that LDYT had previously been attributed to mutations in the mitochondrial ND6 or ND4 genes; their identification of an ND3 mutation in a Chinese family with LDYT added a further causative gene.

Gropman et al. (2004) noted that clinical expression of the homoplasmic G14459A mutation varied even within a single family, and suggested that this supports the hypothesis that nuclear genes may modify the clinical expression of mitochondrial disease.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-generation Hispanic family with LHON and/or dystonia, Jun et al. (1994) sequenced more than 90% of the mtDNA from a severely affected child and identified a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 14459 of the MT-ND6 gene, resulting in an ala72-to-val (A72V) substitution at a moderately conserved residue. The child's mtDNA belonged to Native American haplogroup D. The variant was not found in 38 Native American haplogroup D mtDNAs or in 108 Asian, 103 Caucasian, or 99 African mtDNAs. Six maternal relatives in 3 generations carried the mutation; 1 female with LHON was heteroplasmic. The authors suggested that it was the disease-causing mutation.

Shoffner et al. (1995) found the 14459 mutation in MT-ND6 in 2 pedigrees in addition to the original family, and concluded that this confirmed the association of the mutation with LHON and/or dystonia. They described it as the first genetic abnormality identified as a cause of generalized dystonia. The mutation was not found in patient groups with Leigh disease, dystonia plus complex neurodegeneration, or LHON.

Jun et al. (1996) noted that the heteroplasmic G14459A mutation in MT-ND6 (A72V) had been identified as the cause of LHON and/or pediatric-onset dystonia in 3 unrelated families. Using transmitochondrial cybrids made by fusing enucleated patient lymphoblasts to an mtDNA-deficient (rho-0) lymphoblastoid line, they assigned the complex I defect to the 14459 mutation: mutant cybrids showed a 39% reduction (p less than 0.02) in complex I-specific activity relative to wildtype cybrids, with normal activity of other complexes. Kinetic analysis showed reduced Vmax with unchanged Km, and activity was inhibited by increasing concentrations of decylubiquinol, suggesting that the mutation may alter the coenzyme Q-binding site of complex I.

In a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia, De Vries et al. (1996) found none of the previously reported LHON mtDNA mutations. Sequencing of the protein-coding mitochondrial genes identified 2 novel mutations: a heteroplasmic 11696A-G transition in the MT-ND4 gene, resulting in a val312-to-ile (V312I) substitution, and a homoplasmic 14596T-A transition in the MT-ND6 gene, resulting in an ile26-to-met (I26M) substitution. A muscle biopsy showed severe complex I deficiency.

Tarnopolsky et al. (2004) found the G14459A mutation in MT-ND6 in 2 Caucasian pedigrees with maternally inherited LHON, with or without spasticity/dystonia, and concluded that the findings further supported a causal role for this mutation in both LHON and spasticity/dystonia.

In a family with a broad range of clinical features, including a proband with pediatric-onset dystonia, Gropman et al. (2004) identified the homoplasmic G14459A (A72V) mutation in the MT-ND6 gene, within the most evolutionarily conserved region of ND6. Family members carrying the mutation included both asymptomatic and symptomatic individuals, who had variable clinical and laboratory features of mitochondrial disease.

Watanabe et al. (2006) sequenced the entire mtDNA of the proband from a Japanese family with LHON and dystonia and found 42 nucleotide changes compared with the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence, including the G14459A mutation in the MT-ND6 gene, which was absent from 200 healthy Japanese controls. They reported this as the first Japanese family with LHON and dystonia in which an mtDNA mutation was found. An A3203G change, also absent from the 200 controls, was of unknown significance.

In a patient with LHON who did not carry one of the 3 primary LHON mutations, Spruijt et al. (2007) screened the complete mtDNA and found a 3697G-A mutation in the MT-ND1 gene, which appeared to be associated with isolated complex I deficiency. They concluded that the mutation caused both LHON and spastic dystonia in the same family.

In a Chinese family with LDYT, Wang et al. (2009) used resequencing microarray analysis of the entire mitochondrial genome and identified a 10197G-A mutation in the MT-ND3 gene, resulting in an ala47-to-thr (A47T) substitution at a highly conserved residue, on a haplogroup D4b background. Quantitative heteroplasmy analysis showed that the mutation was homoplasmic in leukocytes of all affected individuals on the maternal side. The authors stated that this was the first ND3 mutation reported to cause LDYT.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shoffner et al. (1995) did not find the 14459 mutation in MT-ND6 in patient groups with Leigh disease, dystonia plus complex neurodegeneration, or LHON, and suggested that the mutation is highly tissue-specific and therefore produces a narrow range of phenotypes.

Tarnopolsky et al. (2004) observed that the G14459A mutation produced very different phenotypes: in one pedigree, a patient had spasticity/dystonia with putaminal lesions, whereas in the other pedigree, a male had optic neuropathy without spasticity or dystonia.

Spruijt et al. (2007) noted that the 3697G-A mutation in MT-ND1 can also cause MELAS syndrome, and suggested that other genetic factors may contribute to the clinical expression of the disease.

Wang et al. (2009) noted that the 10197G-A mutation in MT-ND3 had also been reported in French and Korean patients with Leigh syndrome, and suggested that the clinical spectrum of this mutation is much wider and includes both LDYT and Leigh syndrome.

### Diagnosis

Marsden et al. (1986) suggested that the condition might be a variant of Leigh disease but noted considerable differences from pathologically proven Leigh disease. They stated that it must be excluded in all patients thought to have idiopathic dystonia, subacute visual failure similar to Leber optic neuropathy, or a combination of these disorders.
