### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myopathy-10B, mild variant (CMYO10B), is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene (612453) on chromosome 5q23.2.

### Description

Mutations in the MEGF10 gene cause early-onset myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD) as well as a milder phenotype of minicore myopathy (Takayama et al., 2016). Boyden et al. (2012) described the milder disorder as an autosomal recessive myopathy resembling multiminicore disease. Weakness was mainly axial and proximal and included facial weakness. Patients also had contractures, scoliosis, and respiratory insufficiency requiring nocturnal ventilatory support. Muscle biopsy showed minicores, mainly in type I fibers, together with dystrophic features. Later reports extended the phenotype to respiratory insufficiency beginning in adulthood (Liewluck et al., 2016; Harris et al., 2018).

### Nomenclature

Logan et al. (2011) named the infantile myopathy caused by MEGF10 mutations early-onset myopathy, areflexia, respiratory distress and dysphagia (EMARDD). Croci et al. (2022) used the term mvEMARDD for the milder phenotype. They argued that MEGF10-related phenotypes form a continuum rather than a dichotomy between classic EMARDD and mvEMARDD.

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) reported a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an autosomal recessive myopathy that resembled multiminicore disease. Weakness began by 12 months of age, first in the neck. It was mainly axial and proximal, included facial weakness, and stabilized in adolescence. Neck contractures appeared in infancy, and elbow, knee, and heel-cord contractures developed by adulthood. Serum CK was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion. From age 10 to 11 years, all had respiratory problems requiring nighttime bilevel positive airway pressure. All 3 had hypernasal speech; 1 was born with a cleft palate and another had a midline palatal ridge. Muscle biopsies showed dystrophic features, including fatty replacement of myofibers, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers. They also showed myopathic features: moth-eaten fibers and minicores, the minicores mainly in type I fibers. Rimmed vacuoles, inclusions, central cores, nemaline rods, and ragged red fibers were absent. Type I fibers slightly predominated and varied more in size than type II fibers.

Boyden et al. (2012) also described a sporadic female patient (105-1) of Caucasian origin with distant Portuguese ancestry who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease. She had hypotonia at several weeks of age, a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive from poor feeding, blue sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. In childhood she had frequent pulmonary infections but no chronic breathing problems. At age 10 years her serum CK was 302 U/L. Her scoliosis reached 20 degrees by age 12, and she had mild, nonprogressive proximal and facial weakness. Muscle biopsy showed minicores, which electron microscopy confirmed.

Liewluck et al. (2016) reported 2 sisters whose respiratory insufficiency began in adulthood and was followed later by limb weakness. Both sisters had scoliosis, hyperlaxity of the distal joints, and high-arched feet. A right triceps muscle biopsy from one sister showed multiple minicores. Her EMG showed myopathic changes with fibrillation potentials and myotonic discharges.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. In his 50s, while still ambulant, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins.

AlMuhaizea et al. (2021) reported a boy (patient 2) from a consanguineous Saudi family, whose parents were first cousins, with a mild form of MEGF10-related congenital myopathy. He had generalized hypotonia from the newborn period, after a pregnancy with decreased fetal movements. He had feeding difficulties, choking, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, and motor delay. Even so, he sat at 21 months and was walking independently at age 9 years, though he ran slowly. He had no major cognitive deficits and did not need long-term oxygen. On examination he had plagiocephaly, facial diplegia, a high-arched palate, low-set ears, and mild thoracic scoliosis with spinal rigidity. He also had axial-predominant hypotonia, proximal weakness, a positive Gowers sign, areflexia, and joint hyperlaxity. Creatine kinase and nerve conduction were normal. EMG showed neurogenic changes, and a modified barium study showed mild oral dysphagia. A sister with a similar phenotype died at 18 months of respiratory infections.

Fujii et al. (2022) reported a man whose progressive weakness began in his thirties and who developed severe respiratory failure at age 66 years. His muscle biopsy showed no clear cores.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man, born to healthy consanguineous parents, with a congenital myopathic phenotype. Weak suction, elbow contractures, and scoliosis were present from the first months of life. He was born at 33 weeks' gestation and needed resuscitation for asphyxia. Motor development and intellect were normal. He then developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement. He needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years. On examination he had axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy, cleft palate, global areflexia, contractures of the elbows and ankles, and hyperlordosis. Serum CK, brain and spine MRI, and cardiac studies were normal. A first muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: variation in fiber size, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later biopsy showed myofibrillar alterations, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores. Electrophysiologic studies were normal apart from reduced amplitude of the muscle evoked potential. The authors proposed that the minicores seen in the late-onset group may result from longstanding myofibrillar pathology that begins in infancy.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CMYO10B is autosomal recessive. Boyden et al. (2012) showed that affected sibs in family 1030 were compound heterozygous for MEGF10 mutations in trans that segregated with the disease. Croci et al. (2022) found a homozygous MEGF10 mutation in a patient whose consanguineous parents were both heterozygous carriers. Other reports found compound heterozygous or homozygous MEGF10 mutations in affected individuals (Liewluck et al., 2016; Takayama et al., 2016; AlMuhaizea et al., 2021).

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) carried out a genomewide linkage scan in family 1030 that gave positive LOD scores at 16 loci. MEGF10 lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible LOD score of 1.329. The analysis excluded SEPN1, RYR1, ACTA1, and TPM3.

### Molecular Genetics

Boyden et al. (2012) combined whole genome sequencing with linkage data in family 1030. Affected members were compound heterozygous for 2 missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: c.976T-C (C326R) in exon 10 and c.2320T-C (C774R) in exon 19. The mutations were in trans and segregated with the disease in all 6 family members. Both were absent from dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, and the Exome Sequencing Project, and from 715 and 645 genotyped controls, respectively. Each mutation replaces a cysteine that is conserved in 43 of 43 species, in EGF-like domains 6 and 16, and the authors predicted that this disrupts disulfide bonds.

Boyden et al. (2012) also sequenced MEGF10 in 190 patients with unexplained myopathies. One patient (105-1) had a single heterozygous mutation, c.211C-T (R71W), in the emilin domain. The variant was absent from public databases and from 699 controls, and it was inherited from an unaffected parent. Sequencing of muscle cDNA did not reveal a second mutation. Because her clinical and histologic features resembled those of family 1030, the authors suggested that she may carry a second MEGF10 mutation they could not detect.

Mitsuhashi et al. (2013) overexpressed the C326R and C774R MEGF10 mutants in HEK293T and C2C12 cells and found reduced tyrosine phosphorylation, severe for C774R and mild for C326R. Neither mutation changed subcellular localization. C774R showed reduced N-glycosylation and weaker c-Src binding and phosphorylation.

In 2 sisters with adult-onset myopathy with minicores, respiratory insufficiency, and scoliosis, Liewluck et al. (2016) used next-generation sequencing to identify compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: c.230G-A (R77Q) and c.1833T-G (C611W). The authors concluded that MEGF10 mutations can cause myopathy with respiratory insufficiency beginning in adulthood.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy. The patient was compound heterozygous for 2 MEGF10 mutations: a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G) and a novel missense mutation (C810Y).

In a 66-year-old man with adult-onset MEGF10-related myopathy, Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify 2 novel variants in the MEGF10 gene. One was an essential splice-site variant (c.1426+1G-T) and the other a missense variant, c.352T-C (C118R).

In patient 2 of AlMuhaizea et al. (2021), a muscular dystrophy and myopathy gene panel found a homozygous splice-site mutation in the MEGF10 gene (c.2980+5G-C, NM_032446.2), and Sanger sequencing confirmed it. RT-PCR on RNA from the patient's lymphoblastoid cells showed a larger, aberrant transcript, and sequencing showed that it retained 85 bp of intron 23. The panel also found a heterozygous variant in the PRKAG3 gene (604976) (c.364dupA, p.Thr122Asnfs*11).

In a man with adult-onset progressive myopathy and respiratory failure, Fujii et al. (2022) identified a novel mutation in the MEGF10 gene, G739R.

In an Italian man with a progressive form of mvEMARDD, Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation in the MEGF10 gene, c.2096G-C (C699S; NM_001256545.2). Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, and both consanguineous parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD, changes a conserved cysteine in an EGF-like domain, and was predicted to be damaging by in silico tools. It was classified as likely pathogenic.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) observed that patients with MEGF10 missense mutations had a phenotype similar to, but milder than, the severe congenital myopathy previously reported with nonsense and frameshift mutations. They attributed this to residual MEGF10 function in their patients, in contrast to total loss of expression in the earlier families. Minicores were prominent in their patients with missense mutations but had not been noted in the families with null mutations.

Mitsuhashi et al. (2013) noted that the 3 affected members of the family carrying compound heterozygous C326R and C774R mutations were in their third decade. One was still ambulatory, and they needed only noninvasive nocturnal ventilatory support. C774R paired with a nonsense mutation had been seen in a severely affected family. The authors suggested that C774R is as deleterious as a nonsense mutation, while C326R may be less consequential. They noted that the pattern of reduced tyrosine phosphorylation in their cell studies, severe for C774R and mild for C326R, was consistent with this genotype-phenotype correlation.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported 2 Japanese patients. One had the severe EMARDD phenotype and a novel homozygous frameshift mutation (c.131_132del). The other had the milder phenotype and was compound heterozygous for mutations that included the cysteine missense mutation C810Y. The authors suggested that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain of MEGF10 may be responsible for the milder phenotype, and concluded that their findings indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed all 20 reported cases with MEGF10 mutations and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. In patients with at least 1 null mutation, respiratory dysfunction began in the first year of life. In patients with 2 missense mutations, it began at about 10 years of age or much later. The authors stated that their findings expanded the phenotype to include onset in the fifth decade.

AlMuhaizea et al. (2021) reported 2 boys from unrelated consanguineous Saudi families with different MEGF10 variants and different severity. Patient 1 had a homozygous frameshift mutation (c.3132dupA) and a severe phenotype. He had congenital hypotonia, neonatal seizures, facial diplegia, right diaphragmatic eventration, and absent tendon reflexes. He was never able to sit and died at 8 months of respiratory failure, and an affected sister died at 11 months. Patient 2 had a homozygous splice-site mutation and a milder, ambulant phenotype resembling an axial-predominant myopathy with scoliosis and spinal rigidity.

Fujii et al. (2022) summarized their patient and previously reported patients with MEGF10 mutations. Patients with missense mutations in at least 1 allele had significantly later onset than those with biallelic truncating mutations. The authors noted that some reported patients with the mild variant phenotype had no clear cores.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that missense variants in MEGF10 have been associated with later onset and a milder course. Their patient carried a homozygous missense variant but had early-onset myopathy with a progressive course. The authors proposed that MEGF10-related phenotypes form a continuum rather than a dichotomy between classic EMARDD and mvEMARDD.

### Pathogenesis

Logan et al. (2011) reported that MEGF10 is highly expressed in activated satellite cells and regulates their proliferation, differentiation, and fusion into multinucleated myofibers. These processes are greatly reduced in muscle from individuals with EMARDD. ⚠︎

Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that loss of MEGF10 function causes satellite cells to differentiate excessively, depleting the self-renewing cells that regenerate damaged muscle fibers. They suggested that satellite cell dysfunction could be a pathogenic mechanism shared with SEPN1-related multiminicore disease.

Mitsuhashi et al. (2013) identified Y1030 as the major tyrosine phosphorylation site of MEGF10. They showed that it is phosphorylated at least in part by c-Src, which co-immunoprecipitated with MEGF10. Overexpressing wildtype MEGF10 or the phosphomimetic Y1030D mutant increased C2C12 myoblast proliferation, but the Y1030F mutant did not. The authors suggested that MEGF10 myopathy may be caused by a defect in satellite cell proliferation resulting from impaired MEGF10 tyrosine phosphorylation signaling. They also suggested that the 16th EGF-like domain may matter more for signaling than the 6th.

Saha et al. (2017) found that shRNA-mediated knockdown of Megf10 impaired proliferation and migration of C2C12 myoblasts, as did overexpression of the human MEGF10 C774R mutation. Reciprocal coimmunoprecipitation showed that Megf10 and Notch1 interact via their respective intracellular domains, and the C774R mutation impaired these interactions. The authors suggested that Megf10 regulation of myoblast function is mediated at least in part via interactions with Notch signaling components. They proposed that defects in these interactions may contribute to the pathogenesis of EMARDD.

Li et al. (2021) suggested that impaired satellite cell function and impaired muscle regeneration caused by MEGF10 deficiency could help explain why the clinical manifestations of MEGF10 myopathy persist. They noted that muscle function generally does not improve in affected patients. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish with 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. The morphants had curled or bent tails, impaired swimming, reduced response to touch, and unhatched eggs. They also showed reduced birefringence, disrupted somite boundaries, and gaps between myofibers, with myofibrillar disorganization on electron microscopy. The authors took these findings as consistent with a myopathic process.

Saha et al. (2017) found that myoblasts from Megf10-null mice and from Megf10/mdx double-knockout mice showed impaired proliferation and migration compared with myoblasts from wildtype and mdx mice. The double-knockout mice showed histologic abnormalities not observed in either single mutant mouse.

Saha et al. (2019) ran a small-molecule screen in Megf10-deficient myoblasts and identified 2 selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), sertraline and escitalopram. Sertraline was then tested in several models: Megf10 -/- mouse myoblasts, induced pluripotent stem cell-derived myoblasts from patients with MEGF10 myopathy, Drosophila deficient in the MEGF10 homolog Drpr, and megf10 mutant zebrafish. It was highly effective at relieving abnormalities in all of these models and restored Notch1 deficiencies. The authors concluded that SSRIs, especially sertraline, show promise as therapies for MEGF10 myopathy, and suggested that the mechanism may involve the Notch pathway.

Li et al. (2021) cultured single myofibers isolated from the extensor digitorum longus muscles of Megf10-null and wildtype mice. At 24, 48, and 72 hours, fewer satellite cells had detached from Megf10-null myofibers than from wildtype myofibers. Fewer Pax7+/MyoD+ activated satellite cells were associated with Megf10-null myofibers, and these cells migrated shorter distances. Satellite cells detached from Megf10-null myofibers also had fewer MyoD-positive nuclei. The authors concluded that Megf10 contributes to an early stage of satellite cell activation, during which the cells migrate to target myofibers. Li et al. (2021) also injured the tibialis anterior muscle with 3 sequential intramuscular barium chloride injections, starting at 2 months of age, to deplete the satellite cell pool. Compared with wildtype muscle, injured Megf10-null muscle showed significantly reduced regeneration. It had pockets of atrophic fibers, clusters of small myofibers with internalized nuclei, and greater variability in fiber size. Fibronectin staining showed more fibrosis in injured Megf10-null muscle.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS326ARG [c.976T>C; p.C326R]

In 3 affected sibs from a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) with CMYO10B, Boyden et al. (2012) found compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One was a c.976T-C transition in exon 10, resulting in a cys326-to-arg (C326R) substitution in EGF-like domain 6. The other was a c.2320T-C transition, resulting in a C774R substitution. The mutations were in trans, segregated with the disease in all 6 family members, and were absent from public databases and from 715 genotyped controls. The substituted cysteine is conserved in 43 of 43 species.

Mitsuhashi et al. (2013) found that the C326R mutant, when overexpressed in HEK293T and C2C12 cells, showed mildly reduced tyrosine phosphorylation without a change in subcellular localization. They suggested that C326R may be less consequential than C774R.

**.0002 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS774ARG [c.2320T>C; p.C774R]

In 3 sibs with CMYO10B from family 1030, Boyden et al. (2012) identified a c.2320T-C transition in exon 19 of the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys774-to-arg (C774R) substitution in EGF-like domain 16. The mutation was in compound heterozygosity with C326R and was in trans. It segregated with the disease in all 6 family members and was absent from public databases and from 645 genotyped controls.

Mitsuhashi et al. (2013) found that the C774R mutant, overexpressed in HEK293T and C2C12 cells, had severely reduced tyrosine phosphorylation, reduced N-glycosylation, and weaker c-Src binding and phosphorylation. C774R paired with a nonsense mutation had been seen in a severely affected family, and the authors suggested that C774R is as deleterious as a nonsense mutation.

Saha et al. (2017) found that overexpression of the C774R mutation impaired proliferation and migration of C2C12 myoblasts. The mutation also impaired the interaction between the intracellular domains of Megf10 and Notch1.

**.0003 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, ARG71TRP [c.211C>T; p.R71W]

In a sporadic Caucasian female (105-1) with a minicore myopathy, Boyden et al. (2012) identified a single heterozygous c.211C-T transition in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in an arg71-to-trp (R71W) substitution in the emilin domain. The variant was absent from public databases and from 699 controls, and it was inherited from an unaffected parent. Sequencing of muscle cDNA did not reveal a second mutation. Because her features resembled those of family 1030, the authors suggested that she may carry a second, undetected MEGF10 mutation.
