### TEXT

This entry describes rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a common chronic inflammatory joint disease with a complex, multifactorial genetic basis. Susceptibility has long been associated with HLA-DRB1 alleles in the major histocompatibility complex, and association studies have implicated variants at additional loci, including PTPN22, PADI4, NFKBIL1, and STAT4; a trans-ethnic genomewide meta-analysis brought the total number of RA risk loci to 101. ⚠︎

### Description

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease of unknown etiology that primarily affects the synovial membranes of multiple joints and afflicts about 1% of the world population (Sakaguchi et al., 2003). Uncontrolled inflammation of synovial joints may cause progressive joint damage and long-term disability, and the disease is characterized by autoantibodies to immunoglobulins (rheumatoid factor) and to cyclic citrullinated peptides (anti-CCP antibodies) and by genetic associations with the class II region of the major histocompatibility complex (Barton et al., 2009). Approximately two-thirds of patients are seropositive for rheumatoid factor (RF) or anti-CCP autoantibodies (Stahl et al., 2010).

### Clinical Features

Yen et al. (2001) noted that RA is clinically heterogeneous with regard to progression of joint destruction, response to treatment, and involvement of extraarticular organs; homozygosity for disease-associated HLA-DRB1*04 alleles had been reported to predispose to major organ involvement such as Felty syndrome and rheumatoid vasculitis. They described rheumatoid vasculitis as a life-threatening extraarticular complication. Among Caucasian patients of Western European descent, they studied 30 patients with unequivocal rheumatoid vasculitis manifesting as ischemic mononeuritis, ischemic ulcerations, or peripheral gangrene, diagnosed by histomorphology or angiogram. A subset of RA patients has expanded populations of CD4+ T cells lacking surface CD28, and the highest frequencies of these cells are found in patients with rheumatoid vasculitis.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) described RA as a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by destruction of the synovial joints resulting in severe disability, particularly in patients who remain refractory to available therapies. Thomson et al. (2007) noted that RA is a phenotypically heterogeneous disease with a female gender bias and variation in features such as the presence of autoantibodies (rheumatoid factor and antibodies to cyclic citrullinated peptides) and joint erosions.

### Biochemical Features

Bell et al. (1992) described deficiency of the monocyte ectoenzyme nonspecific esterase in a heredofamilial pattern in 4 patients with RA. No association with HLA status or rheumatoid factor seropositivity was found.

Schaller et al. (2001) found that 64% of patients with RA, but not controls, had increased concentrations of IgG antibodies to glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI) in serum and synovial fluid; soluble GPI was also elevated in sera and synovial fluids of RA patients, leading to immune complex formation. Human monoclonal anti-GPI IgGs cloned from a patient with RA were highly somatically mutated, indicative of an antigen-driven, affinity-matured response, and GPI was present at high concentrations on the surface of the synovial lining and on the endothelial cell surface of arterioles.

Ito et al. (2014) found that a significantly higher proportion of RA patients (16.8%, n = 374) than healthy controls (1.3%, n = 74) were positive for serum IgG autoantibodies to the ubiquitously expressed 60S ribosomal protein L23a (RPL23A); 2 of 23 patients with psoriatic arthritis were positive, but none of the patients with osteoarthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, or polymyositis/dermatomyositis. CD4+ T cells producing IFN-gamma on stimulation with RPL23A were detected in synovial fluid of a subset of RA patients.

### Inheritance

Hasstedt et al. (1994) studied 28 pedigrees ascertained through pairs of first-degree relatives with RA, in which RA was confirmed in 77 members and absence of disease verified in 261. Likelihood analysis assuming tight linkage to HLA supported an HLA-linked susceptibility locus with a susceptibility allele frequency of 2.16% and lifetime penetrance of 41% in male homozygotes and 48% in female homozygotes. Inheritance was recessive in males and nearly recessive in females. The inferred locus accounted for approximately one-half of familial RA but only approximately one-fifth of RA in the population.

Lynn et al. (1995) performed family studies and segregation analyses based on consecutive RA patients ascertained without regard to family history, including first-degree relatives from 135 simplex and 30 multiplex families. A highly penetrant recessive major gene with a mutant allele frequency of 0.005 was the most parsimonious genetic risk factor. Proband gender was a more important determinant of risk than age at onset, relatives of male probands having the greatest cumulative risk.

Cornelis et al. (1998) cited a familial aggregation estimate (lambda-s) of 5 for RA and an estimate that HLA accounts for one-third of the genetic component. Barton et al. (2001) stated that the MHC is estimated to contribute only 30 to 40% of the total genetic component. Begovich et al. (2004) cited an estimated heritability of 60%. The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) cited lambda-s estimates of 5 to 10 and stated that the known associations with HLA-DRB1 and PTPN22 explain around 50% of the familial aggregation of RA.

### Mapping

Cornelis et al. (1998) performed a genome scan in 114 European Caucasian RA sib pairs from 97 nuclear families. Linkage was significant only for HLA (P less than 2.5 x 10(-5)) and nominal for 19 markers in 14 other regions. Two regions overlapping putative type 1 diabetes loci, 18q22-q23 and 3q13 (D3S1267), were studied in 194 additional sib pairs from 164 nuclear families; support for linkage was extended only for chromosome 3 (P = 0.002), and analysis of all 261 families gave P = 0.001 and suggested an interaction between this locus and HLA. Candidate genes in the region included those encoding CD80 and CD86.

Cox et al. (1999) used combined sib-TDT and TDT to study the interleukin-1 gene cluster on 2q13. Several tightly linked IL1 cluster markers gave suggestive evidence for linkage in families in which affected sibs did not share 2 HLA-DRB1 alleles identical by descent, and the evidence was significant in those with severe disease as assessed by the presence of bone erosions. There was no evidence of linkage by nonparametric linkage analysis.

Jawaheer et al. (2001) reported a genomewide screen by the North American Rheumatoid Arthritis Consortium (NARAC) of 257 families containing 301 affected sib pairs, using 379 microsatellite markers. Linkage to the HLA locus was confirmed (P less than 0.00005; lambda-HLA = 1.79), and non-HLA loci on chromosomes 1 (D1S235), 4 (D4S1647), 12 (D12S373), 16 (D16S403), and 17 (D17S1301) showed evidence for linkage at P less than 0.005. X-linked markers suggested linkage to DXS6807. Several nominally significant regions had been implicated in other autoimmune diseases.

Barton et al. (2001) investigated linkage to 5 human regions homologous to those identified in animal models of inflammatory arthritis. Linkage to 17q22, syntenic to a susceptibility locus common to 2 rat models, was detected in 200 RA affected sib pair families and replicated in a further 100. Additional markers refined linkage to a 4-cM region, association to marker D17S807 was demonstrated by extensions of the TDT, and association to 2 two-marker haplotypes including this marker in an independent cohort of single-case families narrowed the region to approximately 1 cM.

Fisher et al. (2003) applied a genome-search meta-analysis to 4 RA genomewide linkage studies. In addition to the HLA locus (P less than 0.00002), the strongest evidence for a susceptibility locus was on chromosome 16 (P = 0.004), a locus not significant in any of the individual scans. Twelve regions achieved a significant summed rank, and 4 of these (6p, 16cen, 6q, and 12p) reached P less than 0.01.

John et al. (2004) performed a whole-genome linkage screen of 157 families with multiple cases of RA using 11,245 SNPs. The SNP analysis detected HLA-DRB1 (P = 0.00004) with a linkage interval of 31 cM, compared with 50 cM in a microsatellite scan of the same cohort, and detected 4 additional loci at nominal significance not observed with microsatellites.

Tamiya et al. (2005) performed a whole-genome association study of RA using 27,039 microsatellites and DNA pooling in 3 successive screens of independent case-control populations. A total of 47 candidate regions were identified, and the top 7 loci were dissected down to individual genes and/or SNPs on 4 chromosomes, including HLA-DRB1 on 6p21.3.

Amos et al. (2006) performed a genomewide linkage scan using more than 5,700 SNPs in 642 Caucasian NARAC families containing affected sib pairs. They found new evidence of linkage at 2q33 (lod score 3.52) and 11p12 (lod 3.09), in addition to a strong and broad linkage interval surrounding the MHC (lod greater than 16); regions with lod greater than 2.5 were observed on chromosomes 5 and 10. Stratification by anti-CCP titer indicated significant variation in linkage evidence on chromosomes 4, 5, 6, and 7.

### Heterogeneity

Stastny and Fink (1979) performed HLA-D typing in 126 patients with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis. HLA-DW4, the antigen characterizing adult RA, had a significantly lower frequency in children with arthritis than in controls, whereas HLA-DW7 and HLA-DW8 were increased. The antigen TMo, related to HLA-DW7 and DW11, was present in 46% of patients with persistent pauciarticular arthritis of childhood compared with 1% of controls (relative risk 67.7). The authors concluded that there are fundamental differences between adult RA and arthritis of childhood.

### Molecular Genetics

Gregersen et al. (1987) put forward the shared epitope hypothesis as an approach to understanding the molecular genetics of susceptibility to RA. Weyand et al. (1992) stated that seropositive RA is genetically linked to a group of HLA-DRB1 alleles sharing a sequence motif within the third hypervariable region (see GENOTYPE/PHENOTYPE CORRELATIONS).

Zanelli et al. (2001) typed 6 microsatellites in the HLA region in 132 RA patients and 254 controls. As part of a microsatellite cluster often found with DQB1*0201-DRB1*0301, the allele MIB*350 was an RA-predisposing factor independent of DRB1*0301 and of the RA-predisposing haplotypes DQB1*03-DRB1*04 and DQB1*0501-DRB1*01, indicating that the telomeric part of the HLA region contains a predisposing locus independent of HLA class II.

Using 18 microsatellite markers across the 3.6-Mb HLA region, Ota et al. (2001) localized one RA susceptibility gene to DRB1 and a second to a 70-kb interval in the HLA class III region just telomeric of the TNF gene cluster (TNFA and LTA), between microsatellites TNFa and C1-2-A. The interval contained 4 expressed genes, NFKBIL1 (IKBL), ATP6G, BAT1, and MICB, and the results excluded involvement of TNFA. Allcock et al. (2001) described haplotypic SNPs in the central MHC gene IKBL, a potential regulator of NF-kappa-B function.

Jawaheer et al. (2002) performed haplotype analysis with 54 markers across the HLA complex in 469 multicase RA families. In addition to associations with DRB1 alleles, at least 2 additional genetic effects were present within the MHC: one within a 497-kb region in the central portion of the HLA complex that excludes DRB1, on a segment of the conserved ancestral A1-B8-DRB1*03 (8.1) haplotype, and possibly additional risk genes in the class I region in a subset of DRB1*0404 haplotypes.

In Japanese patients with RA and controls, Okamoto et al. (2003) used SNPs and insertion/deletion polymorphisms to identify the second MHC-linked RA susceptibility locus as the T allele of SNP 96452 (T/A) at position -62 in the promoter region of the IKBL (NFKBIL1) gene (P = 0.0062). This SNP disrupts a putative binding motif for the transcriptional repressor deltaEF1 and hence may influence IKBL transcription.

Suzuki et al. (2003) used a case-control linkage disequilibrium study to show that PADI4, encoding peptidylarginine deiminase type 4, is a susceptibility locus for RA (P = 0.000008). PADI4 was expressed in hematologic and RA synovial tissues. A PADI4 haplotype associated with susceptibility affected transcript stability and was associated with levels of antibody to citrullinated peptides in sera of RA patients.

Tokuhiro et al. (2003) investigated the cytokine gene cluster on 5q31 in the Japanese population by case-control linkage disequilibrium mapping and found significant association between RA and the organic cation transporter gene SLC22A4 (P = 0.000034). An intronic SNP affected the transcriptional efficiency of SLC22A4 in vitro owing to an allelic difference in affinity for RUNX1, and a SNP in RUNX1 was also strongly associated with RA (P = 0.00035).

In a case-control study of white individuals with replication in a second sample, Begovich et al. (2004) found association of RA with the minor allele of a missense SNP in PTPN22 (discovery allelic P = 6.6 x 10(-4); replication P = 5.6 x 10(-8)), which encodes the hematopoietic-specific protein tyrosine phosphatase LYP. The risk allele, present in approximately 17% of white individuals from the general population and approximately 28% of white individuals with RA, disrupts the P1 proline-rich motif important for interaction with CSK (see PTPN22, ARG620TRP). ⚠︎

Carlton et al. (2005) sequenced the coding regions of PTPN22 in 48 white North American patients with RA, identifying 15 previously unreported SNPs, and genotyped 37 SNPs in 475 patients and 475 matched controls, with replication in 661 patients and 1,322 controls. The sole haplotype carrying the W620 risk allele was strongly associated with disease, but R620W did not fully explain the association: 2 SNPs on a single common haplotype were associated with RA independent of R620W. ⚠︎

In a genomewide association study of approximately 2,000 cases of each of 7 diseases and a shared set of approximately 3,000 controls from the British population, the Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) identified 3 association signals for RA at P less than 5 x 10(-7), including strong associations with HLA-DRB1 and PTPN22; the most associated PTPN22 marker (rs6679677) was perfectly correlated with rs2476601. RA was the only disease for which a sex-differentiated analysis gave a strong signal: SNP rs11761231 on chromosome 7 had no effect in males but an apparently additive effect in females. SNPs near IL2RA and IL2RB were among 9 SNPs with P values between 10(-5) and 5 x 10(-7), and variants around IL2RA, PTPN2, and the 12q24 region were suggested as loci shared with type 1 diabetes.

Sigurdsson et al. (2007) analyzed 5 SNPs in IRF5 and 3 in TYK2 in a Swedish cohort of 1,530 RA patients and 881 controls, with replication in a Dutch cohort of 387 patients and 181 controls. Four of the 5 IRF5 SNPs in the 5-prime region were associated with RA; the minor alleles of 3 SNPs formed a haplotype with a frequency of approximately 0.33 that appeared to confer protection. The associations were found mainly in anti-CCP-negative patients. No association was observed with TYK2.

Plenge et al. (2007) genotyped 317,503 SNPs in 1,522 anti-CCP-positive RA cases and 1,850 matched controls from the NARAC and the Swedish EIRA study, with replication in independent anti-CCP-positive cases and controls. In addition to associations at the MHC and PTPN22, a SNP on chromosome 9 (rs3761847) was associated with disease (odds ratio 1.32; P = 4 x 10(-14)); this SNP is in linkage disequilibrium with TRAF1 and C5.

Remmers et al. (2007) tested SNPs in 13 candidate genes in a previously linked region of chromosome 2q and fine-mapped the STAT1-STAT4 region in 1,620 North American patients with established RA and 2,635 controls. A SNP haplotype in the third intron of STAT4 was associated with RA; the minor alleles were present in 27% of patient chromosomes and 22% of control chromosomes (for rs7574865, P = 2.81 x 10(-7); OR 1.32). The association was replicated in 1,529 Swedish patients with early RA (P = 0.02), and the haplotype was also strongly associated with systemic lupus erythematosus. Homozygosity for the risk allele was associated with a 60% increased risk for RA.

Thomson et al. (2007) genotyped SNPs at 9 loci suggested by the WTCCC study in an independent UK cohort of 5,063 RA cases and 3,849 controls. One SNP, rs6920220, mapping to an intergenic region of 6q23 between OLIG3 and TNFAIP3, was unequivocally replicated (trend P = 1.1 x 10(-8)); combined analysis with WTCCC data gave an OR of 1.22 (P = 3.6 x 10(-12)). There was no evidence for interaction with HLA-DRB1 or PTPN22.

Plenge et al. (2007) genotyped 397 individuals with RA for 116,204 SNPs and compared them with genotype data from the Framingham Heart Study. They identified a SNP at 6q23 (rs10499194), approximately 150 kb from TNFAIP3 and OLIG3, that was reproducibly associated with RA in the genomewide scan and in 5,541 additional case-control samples (combined P = 10(-9)). This association was statistically independent of that with rs6920220, located 3.8 kb away, and the 2 SNPs defined risk and protective haplotypes at 6q23.

Martinez et al. (2008) validated the association of the STAT4 polymorphism rs7574865 with RA in 575 Spanish patients (T vs G, P = 1.2 x 10(-6); OR 1.59) compared with 723 ethnically matched controls; the polymorphism was also associated with inflammatory bowel disease and type 1 diabetes but not multiple sclerosis.

In a meta-analysis of 2 genomewide association studies totaling 3,393 cases and 12,462 controls of European ancestry, followed by replication in 3,929 autoantibody-positive RA cases and 5,807 matched controls, Raychaudhuri et al. (2008) identified a common variant at the CD40 locus (rs4810485; overall P = 8.2 x 10(-9); OR 0.87) and association at the CCL21 locus (rs2812378; overall P = 2.8 x 10(-7)). They also found evidence of association at MMEL1-TNFRSF14, CDK6, PRKCQ, and KIF5A-PIP4K2C.

Suzuki et al. (2008) identified a linkage disequilibrium block associated with RA in the chromosome 1q region containing multiple SLAM family genes. The association peaked at 2 functional SNPs in CD244 (rs3766379 and rs6682654) in 2 independent RA cohorts from Japan (P = 3.23 x 10(-8) and P = 7.45 x 10(-8)). The RA-susceptible alleles and their haplotype increased expression in luciferase and allele-specific transcript quantification assays.

Barton et al. (2009) genotyped putative type 1 diabetes susceptibility loci in UK RA cases and controls and identified a novel RA susceptibility locus at AFF3, with association in a combined cohort of 6,819 RA cases and 12,650 controls (OR 1.12; P = 2.8 x 10(-7)). The associated variants mapped to the 5-prime and promoter region of AFF3. Associations at CTLA4 and 4q27 were replicated.

Orozco et al. (2009) fine-mapped the 6q23 region in 3,962 UK RA patients and 3,531 controls and found 18 associated SNPs, the strongest being rs6920220 (OR 1.22). The next strongest, rs13207033, was perfectly correlated with rs10499194, and a new marker, rs5029937, was located in intron 2 of TNFAIP3. These 3 SNPs remained significant after conditional logistic regression. Carriage of both risk alleles of rs6920220 and rs5029937 with absence of the protective allele of rs13207033 was strongly associated with RA compared with carriage of none (OR 1.86).

Gregersen et al. (2009) conducted a genomewide association study of 2,418 RA cases and 4,504 controls from North America and identified an association at the REL locus, encoding c-Rel, on chromosome 2p13 (rs13031237, P = 6.01 x 10(-10)). Replication in 2,604 cases and 2,882 controls confirmed the association (combined allelic OR 1.25; P = 3.08 x 10(-14)). The combined dataset also supported associations at CTLA4 (rs231735) and BLK (rs2736340).

Raychaudhuri et al. (2009) used GRAIL, a text-mining method, to score 179 loci from a GWAS meta-analysis for functional relationships to genes in established RA loci, and genotyped 22 representative SNPs in 7,957 cases and 11,958 matched controls. Three loci were convincingly validated, CD2-CD58 (rs11586238), CD28 (rs1980422), and PRDM1 (rs548234), and 4 more were replicated: TAGAP, PTPRC, TRAF6-RAG1, and FCGR2A.

Through a genomewide association study validated in 2 independent replication cohorts from Japan (a total of 7,069 cases and 20,727 controls), Kochi et al. (2010) identified a polymorphism in CCR6 at 6q27 (rs3093024; overall OR 1.19; P = 7.7 x 10(-19)) associated with RA. A triallelic dinucleotide polymorphism of CCR6 (CCR6DNP) in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs3093024 affected gene transcription, correlated with CCR6 expression, and was associated with the presence of IL17 in the sera of RA patients.

Stahl et al. (2010) conducted a GWAS meta-analysis of 5,539 autoantibody-positive RA cases and 20,169 controls of European descent, followed by replication in 6,768 cases and 8,806 controls. Seven new risk alleles reached genome-wide significance in all 41,282 samples, near genes including IL6ST, SPRED2, RBPJ, CCR6, IRF5, and PXK. They also refined associations at IL2RA and CCL21 and confirmed AFF3, bringing the number of confirmed RA risk loci in individuals of European ancestry to 31.

In a genomewide study of copy number variants (CNVs) in 8 common diseases, the Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium et al. (2010) found CNVs in the HLA region associated with RA, but had no evidence that these represented signals independent of the known associated haplotypes. They did not find formally significant evidence to replicate the reported association between CCL3L1 and RA.

Robinson et al. (2012) examined CNVs carrying FCGR3A and FCGR3B in 1,115 RA patients and 654 healthy volunteers. No association with duplications or deletions of FCGR3A was found, but FCGR3B deletions were overrepresented in RA (OR 1.50; P = 0.028), more apparently in rheumatoid factor-positive disease (OR 1.61; P = 0.011). Expression of Fc-gamma-RIIIb on neutrophils correlated with FCGR3B copy number.

Okada et al. (2014) performed a trans-ethnic GWAS meta-analysis in more than 100,000 subjects of European and Asian ancestries (29,880 RA cases and 73,758 controls), evaluating approximately 10 million SNPs. They discovered 42 novel risk loci, bringing the total to 101, and used an in silico pipeline to identify 98 biologic candidate genes at these loci. RA risk alleles were significantly enriched in H3K4me3 peaks in primary CD4+ regulatory T cells.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Weyand et al. (1992) stratified 81 RA patients by patterns of disease manifestation and identified their HLA-DRB1 alleles. Rheumatoid nodules were highly associated with heterozygosity for 2 disease-associated HLA-DRB1 alleles, and homozygosity for HLA-DRB1*0401 was characteristic of patients with major organ involvement, suggesting a codominant function of HLA-DRB1 alleles and a role of the disease-associated motif in determining severity.

Yen et al. (2001) found that CD4+CD28-null T-cell clones from RA patients with vasculitis expressed killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs), with stimulatory KIR2DS2 often present without opposing inhibitory receptors. In Caucasians of Western European descent, KIR2DS2 was found in 83% of 30 patients with rheumatoid vasculitis and was enriched compared with 76 controls (OR 5.56; P = 0.001) and 70 patients with RA without vasculitis (OR 7.96; P = 0.001), whereas KIR2DS2 did not affect the risk of developing RA itself. HLA-C*03 was almost doubled in patients with vasculitis (60% positive), and in logistic regression HLA-C*03 and KIR2DS2 each conferred an independent risk of vasculitis.

Lard et al. (2003) analyzed the -2849 A/G IL10 promoter polymorphism in 283 RA patients, 413 patients with other rheumatic diseases, and 1,220 healthy controls. The genotype was not associated with incidence of RA, but patients with the AG/GG genotype, associated with high IL10 production, had higher autoantibody titers at baseline and a higher rate of joint destruction.

Vandenbroeck et al. (2003) typed 4 microsatellites in the IFNG/IL26 region on 12q15 in 251 RA patients and 198 controls from Northern Ireland. Marker D12S2510, 3 kb 3-prime of IL26, was significantly associated with RA in women but not in men, and a 3-marker haplotype was underrepresented in women with RA (OR 0.50), suggesting that polymorphisms in this region contribute to sex bias in susceptibility.

Kallberg et al. (2007) examined interactions between HLA-DRB1 shared epitope alleles and the PTPN22 R620W allele in 3 case-control studies (Swedish EIRA, NARAC, and the Dutch Leiden Early Arthritis Clinic; 1,977 cases and 2,405 controls). Consistent interaction, defined as departure from additivity, was seen in all 3 studies for anti-CCP-positive RA. No interaction was seen between smoking and PTPN22 R620W.

Thomson et al. (2007) found that rs6920220 at 6q23 was significantly associated in anti-CCP-positive but not anti-CCP-negative individuals in the WTCCC study, and the difference between subgroups was significant in combined cohorts, supporting the view that RA comprises at least 2 genetically distinct subsets.

Mahdi et al. (2009) found antibodies to the immunodominant citrullinated alpha-enolase CEP-1 epitope in 43 to 63% of anti-CCP-positive individuals in 1,497 individuals from 3 RA cohorts; this subset was preferentially linked to HLA-DRB1*04. In 1,000 cases and 872 controls, the combined effect of shared epitope, PTPN22, and smoking was most strongly associated with the anti-CEP-1-positive subset (OR 37, compared with 2 for the anti-CEP-1-negative, anti-CCP-positive subset).

### Pathogenesis

Nanki et al. (1996) compared T-cell receptor BJ gene expression in peripheral lymphocytes from 12 pairs of monozygotic twins, including 6 pairs discordant for RA. BJ gene repertoires of monozygotic twins were more similar than those of unrelated individuals, and the inflammation of RA did not induce specific changes in the genetically determined pattern of BJ expression.

Muller-Ladner et al. (2000) detected IL4 STAT mRNA in synovium of patients with short-term and long-term RA, most intensively in follicular inflammatory infiltrates, and found that IL4 STAT and JAK1 protein were expressed by synoviocytes and upregulated by IL4.

Leadbetter et al. (2002) showed that effective activation of rheumatoid factor-positive B cells is mediated by IgG2a-chromatin immune complexes and requires synergistic engagement of the antigen receptor and a MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor; inhibitor studies implicated TLR9.

Nedvetzki et al. (2003) found that synovial fluid cells from RA joints express a CD44 variant (CD44vRA) with an extra CAG trinucleotide transcribed from intronic sequences flanking a variant exon; it was detected in 23 of 30 RA patients. Cells immobilizing FGF2 via CD44vRA bound more soluble FGFR1 and more efficiently stimulated proliferation of FGFR1-bearing cells than cells expressing CD44v3-v10.

Hill et al. (2003) showed in HLA-DRB1*0401 transgenic mice that conversion of arginine to citrulline at the peptide side-chain position interacting with the shared epitope significantly increases peptide-MHC affinity and leads to activation of CD4+ T cells, suggesting how DRB1 shared epitope alleles could initiate an autoimmune response to citrullinated self antigens.

Schonland et al. (2003) found that in healthy individuals HLA-DRB1*04 alleles were associated with excessive loss of telomeres in CD4+ T cells, with accelerated erosion during the first 2 decades of life; premature telomeric loss also affected granulocytes, suggesting the hematopoietic stem cell as the primary target. Telomeric repair mechanisms were intact.

Seyler et al. (2005) examined 72 synovial tissues representing 3 types of synovitis. Germinal center-positive synovitis had the highest levels of APRIL, produced exclusively by CD83+ dendritic cells, whereas BLyS was derived exclusively from CD68+ macrophages. In human synovium-SCID mouse chimeras, TACI-Fc treatment destroyed germinal centers in GC+ synovitis but enhanced IFN-gamma production in aggregate and diffuse synovitis.

Boilard et al. (2010) identified platelet microparticles in joint fluid from patients with RA and other inflammatory arthritides, but not osteoarthritis. The microparticles elicited cytokine responses from synovial fibroblasts via IL1, and the collagen receptor glycoprotein VI was a key trigger of their generation.

In an expression quantitative trait locus study of CD4+ T cells and monocytes from 461 healthy individuals, Raj et al. (2014) found that susceptibility alleles for RA, as for multiple sclerosis and type 1 diabetes, showed a preponderance of T cell-specific cis-regulatory effects.

### Diagnosis

Lundberg et al. (2005) noted that autoantibodies directed against citrulline-containing proteins have a specificity of nearly 100% in patients with RA.

### Clinical Management

Rizzo et al. (2006) found that methotrexate induced production of soluble HLA-G by peripheral blood mononuclear cells, with the highest sHLA-G1 concentrations associated with the -14/-14 bp genotype of the HLA-G 14-bp insertion/deletion polymorphism. Among methotrexate-treated RA patients, the -14/-14 bp genotype was increased in responders (OR 2.46), suggesting it as a therapy marker.

Okada et al. (2014) showed that biologic candidate genes at RA risk loci are targets of approved therapies for RA and suggested that drugs approved for other indications, such as CDK6/CDK4 inhibitors, may be repurposed for treatment of RA.

### Population Genetics

Kurreeman et al. (2011) established a multiethnic cohort from electronic health records (1,515 RA cases and 1,480 controls matched for genetic ancestry and anti-citrullinated protein antibody status). Odds ratios and aggregate genetic risk scores of known RA risk alleles in individuals of European ancestry were nearly identical to those from a GWAS, and there was a large overlap in genetic risk scores among individuals of European, African, East Asian, and Hispanic ancestry.

Okada et al. (2014) found significant correlations of risk allele frequencies and odds ratios at the 101 RA risk loci between Europeans and Asians, although 5 loci showed population-specific associations, supporting the view that genetic risk of RA is generally shared between the 2 populations.

### Animal Model

Nabozny et al. (1996) generated transgenic mice expressing HLA-DQA1*0301 and DQB1*0302 (DQ8) from an RA-predisposing haplotype on a mouse class II-deficient background. Immunization with bovine type II collagen induced severe polyarthritis in most HLA-DQ8-positive mice, whereas DQ8-negative littermates were completely resistant.

Vingsbo et al. (1996) described pristane-induced arthritis in rats, a severe and chronic, T cell-dependent arthritis restricted to the joints, with severity and chronicity influenced by both MHC haplotype and non-MHC genes.

Bradley et al. (1997) found that mice transgenic for HLA-DQ6, an allele from a nonsusceptible haplotype, were resistant to collagen-induced arthritis, whereas double-transgenic DQ6/DQ8 mice developed moderate arthritis compared with the severe disease of DQ8 transgenic mice.

Vingsbo-Lundberg et al. (1998) performed a genome screen in 153 (E3 x DA) F2 rats with pristane-induced arthritis using 330 microsatellites. Loci on chromosomes 4 and 6 (Pia2 and Pia3) influenced arthritis onset, a locus on chromosome 12 (Pia4) was associated with severity and joint erosion, and chronicity was associated with loci on chromosomes 4 and 14 (Pia5 and Pia6).

Holm et al. (2001) transferred a 46-cM telomeric region of rat chromosome 10 encompassing Oia3 from arthritis-prone DA rats to MHC-identical, minimally susceptible LEW.1AV1 rats. Squalene induced arthritis in the Oia3-congenic rats, which showed intermediate phenotypes and female preponderance, and a susceptibility gene in females was mapped to a telomeric 4- to 19-cM subregion.

Sakaguchi et al. (2003) showed that a spontaneous point mutation in the gene encoding an SH2 domain of ZAP70 causes chronic autoimmune arthritis in mice resembling human RA. The altered signal transduction changed the thresholds of thymic selection, leading to positive selection of otherwise negatively selected autoimmune T cells.

Seo et al. (2004) found that an agonistic anti-4-1BB monoclonal antibody inhibited the development of collagen-induced arthritis in DBA/1 mice, and even established disease, through expansion of CD11c+CD8+ T cells and an IFN-gamma- and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase-dependent mechanism.

Lundberg et al. (2005) showed in rats that citrullination of rat serum albumin broke immunologic tolerance, and that citrullinated type II collagen induced arthritis with higher incidence and earlier onset than native collagen. Citrullinated proteins and PAD4 were not detectable in healthy joints, and their appearance in arthritic joints correlated with severity of inflammation.

Camps et al. (2005) found that Pik3cg -/- mice were largely protected in mouse models of RA, correlating with defective neutrophil migration, and that oral treatment with a selective PI3K-gamma inhibitor suppressed progression of joint inflammation and damage.

Kawane et al. (2006) showed that Dnase2 -/- Ifnar -/- mice and mice with induced deletion of the DNase II gene develop a chronic polyarthritis resembling human RA, with high serum levels of anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody, rheumatoid factor, and matrix metalloproteinase-3. Anti-TNF-alpha antibody prevented the arthritis.

Boilard et al. (2010) found that depletion of platelets attenuated murine inflammatory arthritis, and pharmacologic and genetic approaches identified glycoprotein VI as a key trigger of platelet microparticle generation.

From SKG mice, which develop autoimmune arthritis because of a ZAP70 mutation, Ito et al. (2014) isolated arthritogenic T-cell receptors and found that one recognized the ubiquitously expressed ribosomal protein RPL23A.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
PTPN22, ARG620TRP [R620W; rs2476601]

Begovich et al. (2004) found association of RA susceptibility in white individuals with the minor allele of a missense SNP in PTPN22 (replication allelic P = 5.6 x 10(-8)). The risk allele was present in approximately 17% of white individuals from the general population and approximately 28% of white individuals with RA, and disrupts the P1 proline-rich motif important for interaction with CSK.

Carlton et al. (2005) found that the sole PTPN22 haplotype carrying the W620 risk allele (rs2476601) was strongly associated with RA in 2 sample sets of white North Americans, whereas a haplotype identical at all other SNPs but carrying R620 showed no association; however, R620W did not fully explain the association of PTPN22 with RA.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) found that the most associated PTPN22 marker in RA, rs6679677, was perfectly correlated with rs2476601; carriage of the T allele of rs2476601 had been reproducibly associated with RA with a genetic relative risk of approximately 1.8. Thomson et al. (2007) confirmed strong association of rs2476601 with RA (OR 1.53) in a UK validation cohort.
