### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that susceptibility to rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is associated with variation in several genes. These include HLA-DRB1 (142857) on chromosome 6p21.32, PTPN22 (600716) on chromosome 1p13.2, SLC22A4 (604190) on chromosome 5q31.1, RUNX1 (151385) on chromosome 21q22.12, STAT4 (600558) on chromosome 2q32.2-q32.3, TRAF1 (601711) on chromosome 9q33.2, CD244 (605554) on chromosome 1q23.3, AFF3 (601464) on chromosome 2q11.2, CTLA4 (123890) on chromosome 2q33.2, BLK (191305) on chromosome 8p23.1, CCR6 (601835) on chromosome 6q27 and FCGR3B (610665) on chromosome 1q23.3. A variant of CD44 (107269) on chromosome 11p13 has been found in RA synovial fluid cells, and a polymorphism in IL10 (124092) on chromosome 1q32.1 has been associated with disease progression. ⚠︎

### Description

Rheumatoid arthritis is the most common systemic autoimmune disease. It affects about 1% of adults worldwide and has an estimated heritability of 60% (Begovich et al., 2004). Tamiya et al. (2005) described RA as a destructive joint disease that affects up to 1% of the population. Barton et al. (2001) described RA as an oligogenic autoimmune disease. They noted that the only consistent genetic finding had been linkage and association to the MHC, which is estimated to account for only 30 to 40% of the total genetic component of susceptibility.

### Biochemical Features

Bell et al. (1992) described deficiency of the monocyte ectoenzyme non-specific esterase in 4 patients with rheumatoid arthritis. The deficiency followed a heredofamilial pattern and was not associated with HLA status or with rheumatoid factor seropositivity.

Schaller et al. (2001) found increased concentrations of IgG against glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI) in the serum and synovial fluid of 64% of patients with RA, but not in controls. Concentrations of soluble GPI were also elevated in the sera and synovial fluids of RA patients, which led to immune complex formation.

Mahdi et al. (2009) studied 1,497 individuals from 3 RA cohorts. Antibodies to the immunodominant CEP-1 epitope of citrullinated alpha-enolase were present in 43 to 63% of anti-CCP-positive individuals, and this subset was preferentially linked to HLA-DRB1*04.

Ito et al. (2014) used ELISA to show that serum IgG autoantibodies against RPL23A were present in a significantly higher proportion of RA patients (16.8% of 374) than of healthy controls (1.3% of 74). Two of 23 patients with psoriatic arthritis (8.7%) were positive. None of 11 patients with osteoarthritis, 30 with systemic lupus erythematosus or 10 with polymyositis/dermatomyositis were positive. Ito et al. (2014) tested synovial fluid from 24 RA patients and 9 healthy individuals and found CD4+ T cells that produced IFN-gamma on stimulation with recombinant RPL23A in a subset of the RA patients. RPL23A mRNA was expressed ubiquitously in healthy human tissues. RPL23A protein was present in the cytoplasm of synovial cells, including CD55+ fibroblast-like synoviocytes, in both RA and osteoarthritis synovium. The authors suggested that these responses may have a pathogenic role in at least a subset of patients with RA or psoriatic arthritis. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Familial factors in rheumatoid arthritis were studied by Gowans et al. (1964). Burch et al. (1964) reported family and genetic studies of rheumatoid arthritis and rheumatoid factor in Blackfeet Indians.

Hasstedt et al. (1994) studied 28 pedigrees ascertained through pairs of first-degree relatives with RA. RA was confirmed in 77 members, and absence of disease was verified in 261 members, all of whom were serologically typed for HLA. Likelihood analysis assuming tight linkage to HLA supported an HLA-linked susceptibility locus. The susceptibility allele had a frequency of 2.16%, and lifetime penetrance in homozygotes was 41% in males and 48% in females. Inheritance was recessive in males and nearly recessive in females. ⚠︎

Lynn et al. (1995) performed family studies and segregation analyses of RA in consecutive patients ascertained without regard to family history. The analyses included first-degree relatives from 135 simplex and 30 multiplex families. The most parsimonious genetic risk factor was a highly penetrant recessive major gene with a mutant allele frequency of 0.005. The authors suggested that families with an excess of affected males with young age at onset may be the most informative for identifying this putative recessive gene and its modifiers.

Cornélis et al. (1998) noted that familial aggregation (lambda-s = 5), twin studies and segregation analysis suggest a genetic component to RA. HLA, the only known susceptibility locus, had been estimated to account for one-third of this component.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2008) estimated that the variants they newly identified together explained only 0.6% of the variance in RA risk, and that all known non-MHC common variants explained 3.6%. About 60% of RA risk is thought to be genetic, with one-third of that from the MHC, so the authors concluded that known risk alleles explain less than half of the genetic variation. Stahl et al. (2010) estimated that the more than 30 validated non-MHC RA risk alleles explain 3.9% of total disease variance, of which 0.67% is due to the new alleles they reported. Known genetic factors overall, including an estimated 12% for the MHC, explained only 16% of disease variance. In contrast, more than half the risk of autoantibody-positive RA is thought to be genetic.

### Mapping

Nabozny et al. (1996) noted that genetic studies had mapped susceptibility to RA to the HLA-DR locus of the major histocompatibility complex. Strong linkage disequilibrium between certain HLA-DQ genes and RA-associated HLA-DR genes suggested that HLA-DQ molecules may also have a role in RA susceptibility.

Cornélis et al. (1998) carried out a genome scan in 114 European Caucasian RA sib pairs from 97 nuclear families. Linkage was significant only for HLA (P less than 2.5 x 10(-5)). Linkage was nominal (P less than 0.05) for 19 markers in 14 other regions, 4 of which possibly overlap with loci implicated in IDDM (IDDM6, IDDM9, IDDM13 and DXS998). They then examined the 18q22-q23 candidate region (D18S68-D18S61-D18S469) and the 3q13 candidate region (D3S1267) in a further 194 RA sib pairs from 164 nuclear families. Only the chromosome 3 region showed significantly extended support for linkage (P = 0.002). Across all 261 families the evidence for linkage was P = 0.001. The authors suggested that this putative RA locus interacts with HLA and could account for 16% of the genetic component of RA. They proposed the genes encoding CD80 and CD86 as candidates.

Cox et al. (1999) studied the interleukin-1 (IL-1) gene cluster on chromosome 2q13 in RA families using the combined sib-TDT and TDT together with parametric and nonparametric linkage methods. Several tightly linked IL-1 cluster markers gave suggestive evidence for linkage in the combined TDT in families in which affected sibs did not share 2 HLA-DRB1 alleles identical by descent. The evidence was significant in patients with severe, erosive disease. Nonparametric linkage analysis showed no evidence of linkage, and parametric analysis gave weak evidence only when marker-trait disequilibrium was included. The authors considered the data preliminary evidence for linkage of IL-1 cluster genes to RA. ⚠︎

Zanelli et al. (2001) analyzed the allele distributions of 6 microsatellites in the HLA region (D6S1014, D6S273, TNFalpha, MIB, C1-2-5 and C1-3-2) in 132 RA patients and 254 controls. They defined 19 microsatellite clusters that corresponded to ancestral haplotypes. The MIB*350 allele belongs to a cluster often associated with DQB1*0201-DRB1*0301. It predisposed to RA independently of DRB1*0301 and of the RA-predisposing haplotypes DQB1*03-DRB1*04 and DQB1*0501-DRB1*01. The authors concluded that the telomeric part of the HLA region contains a locus that predisposes to RA independently of HLA class II.

Ota et al. (2001) tested 18 microsatellite markers spanning the 3.6-Mb HLA region, from HSET to P5-15, for association with RA. As expected, one susceptibility gene was localized at HLA-DRB1. A second susceptibility gene was suggested in the HLA class III region, within a 70-kb interval just telomeric of the TNF gene cluster between the microsatellites TNFa and C1-2-A. The 4 expressed genes known in this segment were NFKBIL1, ATP6G, BAT1 and MICB, all of them candidate RA susceptibility genes. The authors stated that their results excluded TNFA, which had previously been suggested as a strong candidate in the class III region.

Jawaheer et al. (2001) performed a genomewide screen for allele sharing with 379 microsatellite markers in 257 multiplex RA families from the United States, collected by the North American Rheumatoid Arthritis Consortium. The families contained 301 affected sib pairs. Nonparametric analysis confirmed linkage to the HLA locus (P less than 0.00005; lambda-HLA = 1.79). Evidence for linkage at P less than 0.005 was also found to non-HLA loci on chromosomes 1 (D1S235), 4 (D4S1647), 12 (D12S373), 16 (D16S403) and 17 (D17S1301). MLOD analysis of X-linked markers suggested linkage to the telomeric marker DXS6807. Stratification into white or seropositive subgroups revealed some additional markers whose significance improved over that in the full data set.

Barton et al. (2001) studied linkage in RA affected sib-pair (ASP) families to 5 human regions homologous to loci found in animal models of inflammatory arthritis. They found linkage to 17q22 in 200 ASP families and replicated it in another 100 ASP families. This region is syntenic to a susceptibility locus shared by 2 experimental rat models. Additional markers narrowed the linked region to 4 cM. Extensions of the transmission disequilibrium test showed association between RA and marker D17S807. In an independent cohort of families with a single RA case, 2 two-marker haplotypes that included D17S807 were associated with RA, which narrowed the likely region to about 1 cM.

Jawaheer et al. (2002) performed haplotype analysis with 54 markers across the HLA complex in 469 multicase RA families. In addition to the associations with HLA-DRB1 alleles, they found at least 2 further genetic effects within the MHC. One lay within a 497-kb region in the central portion of the HLA complex, an interval that excludes DRB1, on a segment of the conserved ancestral A1-B8-DRB1*03 (8.1) haplotype. Additional risk genes may also be present in the HLA class I region in a subset of DRB1*0404 haplotypes. The authors concluded that the DRB1 locus cannot completely explain the HLA associations with RA.

In Japanese patients with RA and controls, Okamoto et al. (2003) had earlier reported a second RA susceptibility gene in the telomeric HLA class III region, separate from HLA-DRB1. Using microsatellites, they narrowed this region to 70 kb telomeric of the TNF cluster. The region contains 4 expressed genes: IkappaBL, ATP6G, BAT1 and MICB.

Fisher et al. (2003) carried out a genome-search meta-analysis of 4 published genome-wide linkage studies of RA, using 120 genomic bins of about 30 cM each. Apart from HLA (P less than 0.00002), the strongest evidence for a susceptibility locus was on chromosome 16 (P = 0.004). None of the 4 individual genome searches had found this locus to be significant. Twelve regions reached a significant summed rank (P less than 0.05), compared with 6 expected by chance. Four of these regions, on 6p, 16cen, 6q and 12p, reached P less than 0.01.

Tokuhiro et al. (2003) performed a case-control linkage disequilibrium mapping study of the cytokine gene cluster on chromosome 5q31 in the Japanese population using SNPs. They found a significant association between RA and the organic cation transporter gene SLC22A4 (P = 0.000034).

John et al. (2004) carried out a whole-genome linkage screen of 157 families with multiple cases of RA using 11,245 SNPs and compared the results with a 10-cM microsatellite scan of the same cohort. The SNP analysis detected HLA-DRB1 (P = 0.00004) with a linkage interval of 31 cM, compared with 50 cM in the microsatellite scan. Four more loci reached nominal significance (P less than 0.05) in the SNP analysis but were not seen in the microsatellite scan. The differences were mainly due to the significantly higher information content of the SNPs. Several loci fell below nominal significance when the SNP set was reduced to 3,300 markers (1-cM spacing).

Tamiya et al. (2005) carried out a whole-genome association study of RA using 27,039 microsatellites spaced at about 100-kb intervals. They used DNA pooling in 3 successive screens of independent case-control populations and then fine-mapped candidate regions with SNPs. Of 47 candidate regions, the top 7 loci withstood the most stringent statistical tests. These loci were narrowed to individual genes and/or SNPs on 4 chromosomes, including HLA-DRB1 on 6p21.3.

Amos et al. (2006) performed a genome-wide linkage scan with more than 5,700 informative SNP markers in 642 Caucasian families with affected sib pairs, ascertained by the North American Rheumatoid Arthritis Consortium. They found new evidence of linkage at 2q33 (lod score of 3.52) and 11p12 (lod score of 3.09). Linkage around the MHC was strong and broad (lod score greater than 16). Regions on chromosomes 5 and 10 had lod scores above 2.5, and regions on chromosomes 4, 7, 12, 16 and 18 had lod scores of 1.46 to 2.35. The authors attributed the new evidence partly to the higher information content of the SNP panel (75.6%) compared with a microsatellite panel (mean 52.6%).

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) performed a genome-wide association study of about 2,000 British Caucasian patients with RA, who met the 1987 American College of Rheumatology criteria, and 3,000 shared controls. They confirmed strong association with the MHC/HLA-DRB1 region on 6p21; the strongest MHC SNP was rs6457617 (trend P = 3.44 x 10(-76)). Rheumatoid arthritis was the only one of the 7 diseases studied in which a sex-differentiated analysis produced a strong signal. SNP rs11761231 on 7q32 gave P = 3.9 x 10(-7) on the 2-df sex-differentiated test. It had no effect in males (P = 0.68) but an additive effect in females (P = 6.8 x 10(-8); OR 1.32), although the authors advised caution in interpreting this result. Moderate evidence of association (P between 10(-5) and 5 x 10(-7)) was found at 9 SNPs, none at previously reported RA loci. These SNPs included rs2104286 near IL2RA, rs743777 near IL2RB and rs6920220 on 6q23 (trend P = 4.99 x 10(-6)). When RA and type 1 diabetes cases were combined, the IL2RA/CD25 region on 10p15 reached P = 5 x 10(-8). Weaker signals were seen in TNFAIP2, GZMB, PRKCQ and KAZALD1. Two CTLA4 SNPs were only nominally significant (P = 0.085 and 0.034).

Thomson et al. (2007) genotyped 9 SNPs that had been putatively associated with RA in the WTCCC screen in an independent UK cohort of 5,063 RA cases and 3,849 controls. Only rs6920220 (G>A) replicated (OR for the minor allele = 1.23; trend P = 1.1 x 10(-8)). The combined trend P value with the WTCCC data was 3.6 x 10(-12) (OR = 1.22). This SNP lies in a 60-kb linkage disequilibrium block on 6q23 that contains no known genes but includes part of a PTPN11 pseudogene. The block lies between OLIG3 and TNFAIP3; the authors noted that TNFAIP3 negatively regulates NF-kappa-B and that TNFAIP3-deficient mice develop multiorgan inflammation, including joint inflammation. rs6920220 did not interact with the HLA-DRB1 or PTPN22 susceptibility markers. Together, the HLA-DRB1 shared epitope, PTPN22 rs2476601 and rs6920220 accounted for 8.5% of the total variance in RA susceptibility. The PTPN22 rs2476601 association was confirmed in this cohort (OR = 1.53; trend P = 2.0 x 10(-18)). There was modest evidence of association for IL2RB rs743777 (combined trend P = 5.3 x 10(-6)) and borderline evidence for the sex-differentiated effect of rs11761231 near PODXL (trend P = 0.042). IL2RA rs2104286 was not associated with RA.

Plenge et al. (2007) genotyped 116,204 SNPs in 397 individuals with RA and compared the results with genotype data for 1,211 related individuals from the Framingham Heart Study. They identified a SNP at 6q23, rs10499194, about 150 kb from TNFAIP3 and OLIG3, that was reproducibly associated with RA (P = 10(-3) in the scan and P less than 10(-6) in 5,541 additional case-control samples; combined P = 10(-9)). This SNP lies 3.8 kb from rs6920220, the SNP reported by the WTCCC. Plenge et al. (2007) showed that the 2 associations were statistically independent, and together the 2 SNPs defined risk and protective haplotypes at 6q23.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2008) carried out a meta-analysis of 2 genome-wide association studies of RA comprising 3,393 cases and 12,462 controls of European ancestry. They then genotyped 31 top-ranked SNPs not previously associated with RA in 3,929 autoantibody-positive cases and 5,807 controls; 6 of the SNPs reached P of 0.01 or less. They identified association with rs4810485 in intron 2 of CD40 on 20q13 (overall P = 8.2 x 10(-9); OR 0.87 for the minor T allele). This SNP is in near-perfect linkage disequilibrium with rs1883832, a variant associated with autoimmune thyroid disease that affects CD40 translation efficiency. They also identified association with rs2812378 near the 5-prime UTR of CCL21 on 9p13 (overall P = 2.8 x 10(-7); joint OR 1.12). Evidence of association was found at 4 further loci, which required further validation: MMEL1-TNFRSF14 (1p36), CDK6 (7q21), PRKCQ (10p15) and KIF5A-PIP4K2C (12q13). The meta-analysis also showed association at PTPN22, 6q23 (TNFAIP3), STAT4, TRAF1-C5, 4q27 (IL2/IL21) and CTLA4, but not at PADI4.

Orozco et al. (2009) fine-mapped the 6q23 locus by genotyping 70 SNPs in 3,962 UK RA patients of Northern European descent and 3,531 controls; 18 SNPs were associated with RA. The strongest association was with rs6920220 (P = 2.61 x 10(-7), OR 1.22), which tagged a risk haplotype. The next strongest was rs13207033 (P = 0.0001, OR 0.86), which was perfectly correlated with rs10499194 and whose minor allele was protective. The authors described this as the first independent replication of the rs10499194 association. They also identified a new potential marker, rs5029937, in intron 2 of TNFAIP3. Conditional regression suggested 3 independent effects. The functional exon 3 TNFAIP3 polymorphism rs2230926, which had been associated with SLE, was not independently associated with RA.

Gregersen et al. (2009) performed a genome-wide association study of 2,418 RA cases, about 95% of whom were anti-CCP positive, and 4,504 controls of European origin from North America. They found association at the REL locus on 2p13 for rs13031237 (P = 6.01 x 10(-10)) and rs13017599 (P = 9.05 x 10(-9)), the second most significant non-MHC signal after PTPN22. The association was confirmed in 2,604 cases and 2,882 controls from the US and Canada. In the combined data, the allelic OR was 1.25 for rs13031237 (P = 3.08 x 10(-14)) and 1.21 for rs13017599. The scan confirmed associations with the MHC (maximum P = 9.50 x 10(-104)), PTPN22 rs2476601 (P = 1.62 x 10(-21)) and the TRAF1/C5 region (rs881375, P = 4.09 x 10(-8)).

Barton et al. (2009) replicated the association between RA and SNP rs6822844 in the IL2-IL21 region on 4q27 in UK patients; as previously reported, the common allele predisposed to disease (OR 0.86, P = 5.4 x 10(-4)).

Raychaudhuri et al. (2009) used the text-mining method GRAIL to score 179 loci from a published RA GWAS meta-analysis for functional relationships to genes at 16 established RA loci. They genotyped 22 representative SNPs in 7,957 autoantibody-positive cases and 11,958 controls of European ancestry, and 7 SNPs replicated. They confirmed association with rs11586238 near CD2 and CD58 on 1p13.1 (overall P = 1.0 x 10(-9)), with rs1980422 near CD28 on 2q33.2 (overall P = 1.3 x 10(-9)) and with rs548234 near PRDM1 on 6q21 (overall P = 2.1 x 10(-8)). The rs1980422 association was independent of the CTLA4-region risk SNP rs3087243. Four further loci replicated but did not reach a conservative genome-wide threshold: TAGAP, PTPRC, TRAF6 and FCGR2A.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium et al. (2010) studied 3,432 polymorphic CNVs in about 2,000 UK cases each of 8 common diseases and about 3,000 shared controls. An HLA-region CNV was associated with RA (CNVR2845.14; p = 1.4 x 10(-39); OR = 1.77), but there was no evidence that it was independent of the known HLA haplotypes. A CNV annotated to chromosome 2 (CNVR1065.1) was strongly associated with RA (p = 6.8 x 10(-49); OR = 1.85). It proved to be a dispersed duplication actually located in the HLA region. A novel HLA-region insertion CNV (AC_000138.1_44) was associated with RA (p = 8.3 x 10(-4); OR = 0.87), and the association replicated in 3,398 cases and 2,743 controls.

Stahl et al. (2010) performed a GWAS meta-analysis of 5,539 autoantibody-positive RA cases and 20,169 controls of European descent and replicated the results in 6,768 cases and 8,806 controls. Ten SNPs reached genome-wide significance, 7 of them at new RA risk loci, bringing the number of confirmed risk loci in individuals of European ancestry to 31. Three of the loci had not been implicated in any autoimmune disease: rs934734 in SPRED2 (2p14), rs6859219 in ANKRD55 (5q11), about 150 kb from IL6ST, and rs26232 in C5orf30 (5q21). Three loci had previously been implicated in other autoimmune diseases: rs13315591 near PXK (3p14), rs874040 near RBPJ (4p15) and rs3093023 in CCR6 (6q27). The IRF5 SNP rs10488631 was associated with autoantibody-positive RA (P overall = 4.2 x 10(-11)), with an independent effect of rs4728142. At CCL21, a new SNP, rs951005, had effects partly independent of rs2812378. At IL2RA, rs706778 was associated with RA, but the type 1 diabetes risk haplotype rs2104286(T)-rs11594656(T) explained RA risk better. The authors provided definitive evidence for association of AFF3 (rs11676922; P overall = 1.0 x 10(-14); OR = 1.14). Eleven more SNPs, near genes including IL6R, CD247, IL2/IL21, SH2B3 and UBE2L3, replicated at P less than 0.05 without reaching genome-wide significance.

Okada et al. (2014) performed a 3-stage trans-ethnic GWAS meta-analysis of about 10 million SNPs in 29,880 RA cases and 73,758 controls of European and Asian ancestry. Stage I combined 22 GWAS of 19,234 cases and 61,565 controls. It identified 57 loci, 17 of them novel, and replication was performed in 10,646 cases and 12,193 controls. Overall, 42 novel loci reached genome-wide significance, bringing the total number of RA risk loci, including the MHC, to 101. The novel loci included SNPs near TNFRSF9, IRF4, CDK6, ETS1, CD226, SYNGR1 (rs909685; trans-ethnic OR 1.13, P = 1.4 x 10(-16)), ATM, ILF3, IL20RB and P2RY10 on the X chromosome.

### Heterogeneity

Stastny and Fink (1979) performed HLA-D typing in 126 patients with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis. HLA-DW4, which earlier studies had associated with adult RA, was significantly less frequent in these children than in controls (P less than 0.04). HLA-DW7 (P less than 0.03) and HLA-DW8 (P less than 0.01) were increased. TMo, an HLA-D antigen related to DW7 and DW11, was present in 46% of patients with persistent pauciarticular childhood arthritis, compared with 1% of controls (relative risk 67.7). The authors concluded that adult RA and childhood arthritis differ fundamentally.

Hasstedt et al. (1994) estimated that the HLA-linked susceptibility locus accounts for approximately one-half of familial RA but only about one-fifth of RA in the population. They suggested that other genes may account for the remaining familial RA and that a large portion of cases may be sporadic.

Lynn et al. (1995) found significant heterogeneity in RA risk by proband gender but not by proband age at onset. In Kaplan-Meier analysis, both factors contributed to risk, but proband gender was the more important determinant, and relatives of male probands had the greatest cumulative risk. The authors concluded that the pattern of risk and its inheritance is heterogeneous.

Jawaheer et al. (2001) noted that several regions that reached nominal significance in their genome screen had been implicated in other autoimmune diseases, including SLE, inflammatory bowel disease, multiple sclerosis and ankylosing spondylitis. They suggested that RA shares genetic factors with these diseases.

Amos et al. (2006) stratified sib pairs according to whether they had elevated anti-CCP titers. The evidence for linkage on chromosomes 4, 5, 6 and 7 varied significantly between the strata.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) added variants around IL2RA (10p15), PTPN2 (18p11) and the 12q24 region to the loci shared by RA and type 1 diabetes. The RA association at PTPN2 was weak (P = 1.9 x 10(-2)), but the combined P value for Crohn disease, RA and type 1 diabetes was 9 x 10(-8).

Sigurdsson et al. (2007) found that IRF5 associations with RA in a Swedish cohort were found mainly in patients negative for anti-CCP antibodies. A suggestive association with anti-CCP-negative RA was also observed in a Dutch cohort of 387 patients and 181 controls.

Remmers et al. (2007) found that the STAT4 haplotype marked by rs7574865 was also strongly associated with systemic lupus erythematosus in 1,039 patients and 1,248 controls (31% vs 22% of chromosomes; P = 1.87 x 10(-9); OR 1.55). Martínez et al. (2008) found that, in a Spanish cohort, STAT4 rs7574865 was also associated with inflammatory bowel disease (P = 0.006; OR 1.29) and with type 1 diabetes (P = 0.008; OR 1.36), but not with multiple sclerosis (P = 0.83). Suzuki et al. (2008) found a CD244 genotype distribution in a Japanese SLE cohort similar to that in the RA cohorts.

Barton et al. (2009) found that the common allele of the nonsynonymous IL7R SNP rs6897932 was somewhat overrepresented in RA cases (OR 0.90, P = 0.007), but not significantly so after correction for multiple testing.

Gregersen et al. (2009) noted that 50 to 80% of patients meeting standard criteria for RA have anti-CCP antibodies. The classical HLA-DRB1 associations, and many other reported genetic associations, are restricted to this subgroup. They also noted evidence of genetic heterogeneity in RA risk among racial groups.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2009) found consistent effects of their 7 replicated SNPs across all 14 collections. There was no significant epistatic interaction among these SNPs and known RA risk alleles.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium et al. (2010) found no formally significant replication of the reported association between CCL3L1 copy number and RA (nominal p = 0.058). None of the multiallelic CNVs they tested (CCL3L1, beta-defensins, FCGR3A/B) was significantly associated after correction for multiple testing. Robinson et al. (2012) found no association between RA and either duplications or deletions of FCGR3A.

Kochi et al. (2010) found that the CCR6 dinucleotide polymorphism CCR6DNP was also associated with susceptibility to Graves disease and Crohn disease.

Kurreeman et al. (2011) found that the distribution of a genetic risk score based on 28 non-HLA RA risk alleles in ACPA-positive cases partially overlapped with that in ACPA-negative cases.

### Molecular Genetics

Okamoto et al. (2003) studied many SNPs and insertion/deletion polymorphisms in Japanese patients. They identified the second RA susceptibility locus in the HLA region as the T allele of SNP 96452 (T/A), at position -62 in the promoter of the IkappaBL gene (P = 0.0062). This SNP disrupts a putative binding motif for the transcriptional repressor deltaEF1, so it may affect IkappaBL transcription. IkappaBL is homologous to IkappaB-alpha, an inhibitor of NF-kappa-B. Allcock et al. (2001) had described haplotypic SNPs in IKBL, a central MHC gene and potential regulator of NF-kappa-B function. ⚠︎

Nedvetzki et al. (2003) found that synovial fluid cells from the joints of patients with RA expressed a novel CD44 variant, designated CD44vRA. CD44vRA carried an extra CAG trinucleotide transcribed from intronic sequences flanking a variant exon. It was detected in 23 of 30 RA patients.

Suzuki et al. (2003) used a case-control linkage disequilibrium study to show that PADI4, one of 4 PADI genes in a previously linked region, is a susceptibility locus for RA (P = 0.000008). They identified a PADI4 haplotype associated with susceptibility.

Lard et al. (2003) genotyped the -2849 A/G IL10 promoter polymorphism, which is associated with high (AG/GG) and low (AA) IL-10 production. They studied 283 RA patients, 413 patients with other rheumatic diseases and 1,220 healthy controls. IL10 genotype was not associated with the incidence of RA but was associated with disease progression. Patients with the high-production genotype had a higher rate of joint destruction and higher baseline titers of IgG and IgM rheumatoid factors and anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies. ⚠︎

Vandenbroeck et al. (2003) genotyped 4 microsatellites in a 118-kb interval containing IFNG and IL26 on 12q15 in 251 RA patients and 198 controls from Northern Ireland. D12S2510, located 3 kb 3-prime of IL26, was associated with RA in women (corrected P = 0.008) but not in men (P = 0.99). A 3-marker haplotype was underrepresented in women with RA (OR 0.50; corrected P = 0.03). The authors concluded that polymorphisms in this region may contribute to sex bias in RA susceptibility.

Tokuhiro et al. (2003) found that the RA-associated intronic SNP in SLC22A4 lies in a binding site for the hematopoietic transcriptional regulator RUNX1. In vitro, the 2 alleles differed in their affinity for RUNX1 and therefore affected the transcriptional efficiency of SLC22A4. A SNP in RUNX1 was also strongly associated with RA (P = 0.00035). The authors concluded that RUNX1 regulation of SLC22A4 expression is associated with RA susceptibility and suggested that this may be an example of an epistatic effect of 2 genes.

Begovich et al. (2004) tested putative functional SNPs for association with RA in white individuals in a case-control discovery study and a replication sample. The minor allele of a missense SNP in PTPN22, which encodes the hematopoietic-specific protein tyrosine phosphatase Lyp, was associated with RA (allelic P = 6.6 x 10(-4) in the discovery study and 5.6 x 10(-8) in the replication study). The risk allele was present in about 28% of white individuals with RA and about 17% of white individuals in the general population. It disrupts the P1 proline-rich motif, which is important for interaction with Csk. The authors noted that this could alter the normal function of these proteins as negative regulators of T-cell activation.

Carlton et al. (2005) sequenced the coding regions of PTPN22 in 48 white North American RA patients and identified 15 previously unreported SNPs, including 2 coding SNPs in the catalytic domain. They genotyped 37 SNPs in or near PTPN22 in 475 RA patients and 475 matched controls and replicated a subset in 661 patients and 1,322 controls. The only haplotype carrying the W620 risk allele (R620W, rs2476601) was strongly associated with disease in both sample sets. A haplotype identical at all other SNPs but carrying R620 showed no association. However, case-control differences persisted after stratification by R620W. Two SNPs on a single common haplotype were associated with RA independently of R620W, suggesting that at least one additional PTPN22-region variant influences susceptibility.

Kallberg et al. (2007) studied interaction between HLA-DRB1 shared epitope (SE) alleles and the PTPN22 R620W allele in 3 case-control studies, the Swedish EIRA, the North American NARAC and the Dutch Leiden Early Arthritis Clinic study (1,977 cases and 2,405 controls in total). When interaction was defined as departure from additivity, all 3 studies showed consistent interaction between SE alleles and the A allele of R620W for anti-CCP-positive RA. A multiplicative interaction was seen only in the pooled data.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) found strong association between RA and PTPN22 SNP rs6679677 on 1p13 (trend P = 4.9 x 10(-26); heterozygote OR 1.98, homozygote OR 3.32). This SNP is perfectly correlated with the functional SNP rs2476601. The authors noted that the HLA-DRB1 and PTPN22 associations together explain about 50% of the familial aggregation of RA.

Sigurdsson et al. (2007) analyzed 5 IRF5 SNPs and 3 TYK2 SNPs in a Swedish cohort of 1,530 RA patients and 881 controls. Four of the 5 SNPs in the 5-prime region of IRF5 were associated with RA, whereas the TYK2 SNPs were not. The minor alleles of 3 IRF5 SNPs formed a relatively common haplotype (frequency approximately 0.33) that appeared to protect against RA.

Plenge et al. (2007) performed a genomewide association study of 317,503 SNPs in 1,522 anti-CCP-positive RA cases and 1,850 matched controls from the NARAC and Swedish EIRA collections. They replicated top SNPs in 997 cases and 1,777 controls. In all samples, association was seen with the MHC, with PTPN22 and with rs3761847 on chromosome 9 (OR 1.32; 95% CI 1.23-1.42; P = 4 x 10(-14)). rs3761847 is in linkage disequilibrium with TRAF1 and C5. The authors concluded that a common variant at the TRAF1-C5 locus increases the risk of anti-CCP-positive RA.

Remmers et al. (2007) tested SNPs in and around 13 candidate genes in the previously linked chromosome 2q region. They then fine-mapped the STAT1-STAT4 region in 1,620 North American patients with established RA and 2,635 controls. A SNP haplotype in the third intron of STAT4, marked by rs7574865, was associated with RA. Minor alleles were present on 27% of patient chromosomes and 22% of control chromosomes (P = 2.81 x 10(-7); OR 1.32). The association was replicated in a Swedish series of 1,529 patients with early RA and 881 controls (P = 0.02).

Martínez et al. (2008) genotyped STAT4 rs7574865 in 575 Spanish RA patients and 723 ethnically matched controls and confirmed the association with RA (T vs G allele, P = 1.2 x 10(-6); OR 1.59).

Suzuki et al. (2008) identified an RA-associated linkage disequilibrium block in the chromosome 1q region that contains multiple SLAM family genes. The association peaked at 2 functional CD244 SNPs, rs3766379 and rs6682654, in 2 independent Japanese RA cohorts (P = 3.23 x 10(-8) and 7.45 x 10(-8)). The susceptibility alleles and their haplotype increased expression in luciferase and allele-specific transcript quantification assays.

Barton et al. (2009) genotyped 18 SNPs at 14 previously suggested type 1 diabetes loci in 3,962 UK RA cases and 3,531 controls. They identified association with rs10865035 at the AFF3 locus on chromosome 2. In a combined analysis of 6,819 cases and 12,650 controls, the OR was 1.12 (P = 2.8 x 10(-7)). The associated variants map to the 5-prime and promoter region of AFF3, which is preferentially expressed in lymphoid cells. They also replicated association with rs3087243 in the 3-prime region of CTLA4 (OR 0.87, P = 1.1 x 10(-4)), which was confirmed in a meta-analysis with 2 previous studies (OR 0.88, P = 2.6 x 10(-6)).

In their combined dataset of North Americans of European origin, Gregersen et al. (2009) found definitive support for association of RA with CTLA4 SNP rs231735 (OR = 0.85; P = 6.25 x 10(-9)), whose effect was nearly dominant. They presented BLK as a new RA risk locus, with association at rs2736340 (OR = 1.19; P = 5.69 x 10(-9)), and noted that this locus had recently been associated with systemic lupus. No significant gene-gene interaction was identified among CTLA4, REL and BLK SNPs.

In a genome-wide association study of RA in Japanese individuals, Kochi et al. (2010) identified rs3093024 in CCR6 at 6q27. The association was validated in 2 independent Japanese replication cohorts (7,069 cases and 20,727 controls in total; overall OR = 1.19, P = 7.7 x 10(-19)). A triallelic dinucleotide polymorphism of CCR6 (CCR6DNP) in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs3093024 affected gene transcription. CCR6DNP genotype correlated with CCR6 expression level and was associated with the presence of IL-17 in the sera of RA patients. ⚠︎

Kurreeman et al. (2011) used electronic health record data linked to biospecimens to assemble a multi-ethnic cohort of 1,515 RA cases and 1,480 controls matched for genetic ancestry and ACPA status. In individuals of European ancestry, the odds ratios and the aggregate genetic risk score of known RA risk alleles were nearly identical to those from a GWAS of 5,539 autoantibody-positive cases and 20,169 controls.

Robinson et al. (2012) studied copy number variants of FCGR3A and FCGR3B in 1,115 RA patients and 654 healthy volunteers using a quantitative sequence variant assay and an adapted paralogue ratio test. FCGR3B deletions were overrepresented in RA (OR 1.50, P = 0.028), and the association was stronger in rheumatoid factor-positive disease (OR 1.61, P = 0.011). FcgammaRIIIb expression on neutrophils correlated with FCGR3B copy number.

Okada et al. (2014) scored the 377 genes at the 100 non-MHC loci against 8 criteria, including missense variants, cis-eQTL effects, protein-protein interactions and mouse knockout phenotypes. They defined 98 genes as candidate biological RA risk genes. RA risk SNPs at 44 loci showed cis-eQTL effects, and 16% of risk SNPs were in linkage disequilibrium with missense SNPs.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Weyand et al. (1992) stratified 81 patients with seropositive RA by disease manifestations and typed HLA-DRB1 alleles. Rheumatoid nodules were highly associated with heterozygosity for 2 disease-associated HLA-DRB1 alleles. Homozygosity for HLA-DRB1*0401 was characteristic of patients with major organ involvement. ⚠︎

Yen et al. (2001) studied Caucasians of Western European descent: 76 controls, 70 RA patients without major organ involvement and 30 patients with rheumatoid vasculitis. KIR2DS2 was present in 83% of patients with vasculitis, compared with 47% of controls (OR 5.56) and 39% of patients with uncomplicated RA (OR 7.96). KIR2DS1 was not associated. The authors concluded that KIR2DS2 did not affect the risk of developing RA but was strongly associated with vascular complications. Compared with controls, RA patients had a decreased frequency of HLA-C*04 (OR 0.39) and an increased frequency of HLA-C*05 (OR 2.92). HLA-C*05 was further increased in patients with vasculitis (OR 5.71), and HLA-C*03 was enriched only in these patients (OR 3.74). HLA-C*03 and KIR2DS2 each conferred independent risk for vasculitis.

Remmers et al. (2007) reported that homozygosity for the STAT4 risk allele, compared with absence of the allele, was associated with a 60% increased risk for RA and a more than doubled risk for SLE.

Thomson et al. (2007) found that the rs6920220 association was stronger in anti-CCP-positive and rheumatoid factor-positive patients. In the WTCCC data, the OR was 1.38 in anti-CCP-positive patients and 1.03 in anti-CCP-negative patients, and the difference was significant in the combined cohorts (trend P = 0.003). The authors suggested that RA may comprise at least 2 genetically distinct subsets.

Barton et al. (2009) found similar strengths of association at the AFF3, CTLA4 and IL2/IL21 SNPs in autoantibody-positive and autoantibody-negative patients.

Orozco et al. (2009) found that rs6920220 and rs5029937 at 6q23 appeared to be associated with autoantibody-positive RA. In contrast, rs13207033 was associated with both RF-positive and RF-negative cases but only with anti-CCP-negative cases. All 3 SNPs were associated in both sexes. Carrying the risk alleles of rs6920220 and rs5029937 together with absence of the protective allele of rs13207033 was strongly associated with RA compared with carrying none of them (OR 1.86). This high-risk combination was carried by 4.4% of patients and 2.3% of controls.

### Pathogenesis

Gregersen et al. (1987) proposed the shared epitope hypothesis as an approach to understanding the molecular genetics of susceptibility to RA. Weyand et al. (1992) suggested that the disease-associated sequence motif in the third hypervariable region of HLA-DRB1 has a role in determining RA severity. They also suggested that HLA-DR function in thymic selection might be important in RA pathogenesis.

Nanki et al. (1996) studied T cell receptor BJ gene expression in peripheral T lymphocytes from 12 pairs of monozygotic twins, including 6 pairs discordant for RA, and from 5 normal individuals. BJ repertoires were more similar in monozygotic twins than in unrelated individuals, and RA inflammation did not induce specific changes in the pattern. The authors concluded that expression of particular TCRBV-TCRBJ recombinants is genetically controlled.

Müller-Ladner et al. (2000) found IL-4 STAT mRNA in synovium from patients with both short-term and long-term RA. Expression was most intense in follicular inflammatory infiltrates and was present in both fibroblasts and macrophages of the synovial lining. IL-4 STAT was phosphorylated, which showed that it was activated. The authors concluded that IL-4 STAT-dependent pathways operate in early and late RA. ⚠︎

Yen et al. (2001) found that 58 to 91% of CD4+CD28null T-cell clones from 3 patients with rheumatoid vasculitis expressed KIR2DS2, often unopposed by inhibitory receptors. Cross-linking enhanced IFN-gamma transcription and proliferation of these clones. The authors suggested that KIR2DS2 may contribute to vascular damage.

Schaller et al. (2001) used phage display to clone highly somatically mutated, high-affinity human monoclonal anti-GPI IgGs from a patient with RA, indicating an affinity-matured, antigen-driven response. GPI was present at high concentrations on the synovial lining surface and on the endothelial surface of arterioles in RA synovium. The authors concluded that the immunologic events leading to arthritis in the K/BxN mouse model may also occur in human RA.

Leadbetter et al. (2002) showed that effective activation of B cells expressing rheumatoid factor was mediated by IgG2a-chromatin immune complexes. Activation required synergistic engagement of the antigen receptor and a MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor, and inhibitor studies implicated TLR9.

Okamoto et al. (2003) suggested that the MHC may contain RA determinants that affect both innate immunity, through IkappaBL and NF-kappa-B, and adaptive immunity, through HLA-DRB1. ⚠︎

Nedvetzki et al. (2003) showed that cells transfected with CD44vRA or with wildtype CD44v3-v10 bound FGF2 equally. However, cells immobilizing FGF2 through CD44vRA bound more soluble FGFR1 and stimulated proliferation of FGFR1-bearing cells more efficiently. Primary RA synovial fluid cells expressing CD44vRA also bound more soluble FGFR1. The authors suggested that the enhanced FGF2-FGFR1 interaction may foster the destructive inflammatory cascade in RA.

Suzuki et al. (2003) found that the RA-susceptibility PADI4 haplotype affected transcript stability and was associated with levels of antibody to citrullinated peptide in RA sera. PADI4 was expressed in hematologic tissues and RA synovial tissues. The authors suggested that the haplotype increases production of citrullinated autoantigens.

Hill et al. (2003) noted that RA is associated with MHC class II molecules containing the shared epitope and that citrulline-containing proteins are specific targets of the IgG antibody response in RA. They showed that conversion of arginine to citrulline at the peptide position that interacts with the shared epitope significantly increased peptide-MHC affinity and activated CD4+ T cells in HLA-DRB1*0401 transgenic mice. They concluded that their results revealed how shared epitope-carrying DRB1 alleles could initiate an autoimmune response to citrullinated self-antigens.

Lard et al. (2003) found that adenoviral transfer of IL10 to fibroblast-like synoviocytes inhibited their invasiveness. However, the invasiveness of these cells in vitro did not differ between -2849 G carriers and non-carriers.

Schönland et al. (2003) found that HLA-DRB1*04 alleles were associated with excessive telomere loss in CD4+ T cells of healthy individuals. The erosion occurred during the first 2 decades of life and was followed by reduced homeostatic T-cell proliferation in adulthood. Granulocytes were also affected, suggesting that the hematopoietic stem cell is the primary target. The authors proposed that HLA-DRB1*04 alleles, or genes in linkage disequilibrium with them, regulate stem cell replication and contribute to the accumulation of senescent and autoreactive T cells in RA.

Tokuhiro et al. (2003) showed that SLC22A4 expression is specific to hematologic and immunologic tissues. SLC22A4 was also highly expressed in the inflamed joints of mice with collagen-induced arthritis.

Sakaguchi et al. (2003) reported that altered T-cell receptor signaling through an aberrant ZAP-70 changed the thresholds of thymic selection, so that autoimmune T cells were positively selected. They suggested that a genetically determined shift of the T-cell repertoire toward high self-reactivity might be crucial in a subset of RA patients. Ito et al. (2014) similarly proposed that genetic variation in T cell signaling molecules might alter thymic selection and thereby generate an autoimmune TCR repertoire.

Because the same PTPN22 allele had been implicated in type 1 diabetes, Begovich et al. (2004) suggested that the variant phosphatase may increase overall immune reactivity. ⚠︎

Lundberg et al. (2005) hypothesized that, in individuals with the appropriate genetic background, subclinical inflammation, possibly outside the joints (for example smoking-induced lung inflammation in carriers of the shared epitope), induces citrullination of endogenous proteins and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies that potentiate an inflammatory trigger in joints. They noted that an immune response to a citrullinated protein is not sufficient to induce arthritis and that genetic and environmental factors most probably both contribute.

Seyler et al. (2005) examined 72 rheumatoid synovial tissues. Synovitis containing ectopic germinal centers had the highest levels of APRIL, which was produced exclusively by CD83+ dendritic cells. BLyS was present at similar levels in all tissue types and derived exclusively from CD68+ macrophages.

Rizzo et al. (2006) showed that methotrexate induced production of soluble HLA-G by peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy individuals and from RA patients not treated with methotrexate. The highest sHLA-G1 concentrations were associated with the HLA-G -14/-14 bp genotype.

Kallberg et al. (2007) found no interaction between smoking and PTPN22 R620W in the EIRA study. They described a new pattern of interactions between HLA-DRB1 SE, PTPN22 and smoking for the risk of anti-CCP-positive RA. Mahdi et al. (2009) studied 1,000 RA patients and 872 controls. The combined effect of SE alleles, PTPN22 and smoking was most strongly associated with the anti-CEP-1-positive subset of RA (OR 37), compared with an OR of 2 for the anti-CEP-1-negative, anti-CCP-positive subset. The authors concluded that citrullinated alpha-enolase links smoking to genetic risk factors.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2008) concluded that the associations at CD40, TRAF1 and TNFAIP3 gave strong evidence for a central role of the CD40 signaling pathway in autoantibody-positive RA, possibly through NF-kappa-B activation. Gregersen et al. (2009) suggested that the associations at CD40, REL, TRAF1 and TNFAIP3 point to an important role for CD40 signaling pathways. Raychaudhuri et al. (2009) suggested that the new loci implicate T-cell activation, NF-kappa-B signaling, and B-cell activation and differentiation, and noted that several of them overlap with loci for other autoimmune diseases. Stahl et al. (2010) noted that the associations at RBPJ, CCR6 and CD247 further implicate T cells.

Boilard et al. (2010) found platelet microparticles in synovial fluid from all 20 RA patients tested, averaging just under 2 x 10(5) per microliter, whereas they were undetectable in 19 of 20 osteoarthritis samples. Platelet microparticles stimulated synovial fibroblasts to produce IL-6 and IL-8 in an IL-1-dependent manner, with IL-1-alpha predominating in the microparticles. ⚠︎

Okada et al. (2014) found that RA risk alleles were enriched in H3K4me3 histone peaks only in primary CD4+ regulatory T cells, among 34 cell types tested. Genes at RA risk loci overlapped significantly with genes for primary immunodeficiency and with genes somatically mutated in hematologic cancers. About two-thirds of RA loci were shared with other traits. Raj et al. (2014) performed an eQTL study of naive CD4+ T cells and monocytes from 461 healthy individuals of diverse ancestry. They found that RA susceptibility alleles were enriched for T cell-specific cis-regulatory effects. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Camps et al. (2005) found that oral treatment with selective small-molecule PI3K-gamma inhibitors slowed the progression of joint inflammation and damage in 2 mouse models of RA.

Rizzo et al. (2006) found that the HLA-G -14/-14 bp genotype of the 14-bp deletion/insertion polymorphism was more frequent among methotrexate responders than nonresponders (OR = 2.46, P = 0.009 by logistic regression). They proposed this polymorphism as a therapy marker in the early phases of disease.

Okada et al. (2014) found that targets of approved RA drugs, including tocilizumab (IL6R), tofacitinib (JAK3) and abatacept (CTLA4-Ig), were enriched 3.7-fold among biological RA risk genes and their interaction partners. They suggested that CDK6 and CDK4 could be candidates for drug repurposing.

### Population Genetics

Carlton et al. (2005) predicted 10 common PTPN22 haplotypes (frequency greater than 1%) in white North Americans.

Kurreeman et al. (2011) found a large overlap in the RA genetic risk score among individuals of European, African, East Asian and Hispanic ancestry. They concluded that the genetic basis of RA risk is similar across ancestries.

Okada et al. (2014) found that risk allele frequencies and odds ratios at the 101 loci were significantly correlated between Europeans and Asians, although 5 loci showed population-specific associations. The 100 non-MHC loci explained 5.5% of heritability in Europeans and 4.7% in Asians.

### Animal Model

Nabozny et al. (1996) generated transgenic mice that expressed HLA-DQA1*0301 and DQB1*0302, from the RA-predisposing DQ8/DR4Dw4 haplotype, on a mouse class II-deficient background. After immunization with bovine type II collagen, a majority of these mice developed severe polyarthritis. DQ8-negative sibs were completely resistant. Bradley et al. (1997) found that HLA-DQ6 transgenic mice were resistant to collagen-induced arthritis. DQ6/DQ8 double-transgenic mice developed moderate arthritis, whereas DQ8 mice developed severe arthritis.

Vingsbo et al. (1996) described pristane-induced arthritis in rats as a model of RA. A single intradermal injection of pristane caused severe, chronic, joint-restricted arthritis with pannus formation and T-lymphocyte infiltration. The disease was ameliorated by antibodies to the alpha-beta T-cell receptor. Severity varied with MHC haplotype and with non-MHC background. Joint erosions were accompanied by elevated serum cartilage oligomeric matrix protein. Vingsbo-Lundberg et al. (1998) performed a genome screen of 153 (E3 x DA) F2 rats and found that different loci controlled onset (Pia2, Pia3), severity (Pia4) and chronicity (Pia5, Pia6). Pia5 lies near the Cia3 and Lyp/Iddm1 loci.

Holm et al. (2001) produced rats congenic for the oil-induced arthritis locus Oia3 on chromosome 10. Squalene induced arthritis in these rats, with a female preponderance, and the female susceptibility gene or genes were mapped to a 4-19 cM subregion of Oia3.

Sakaguchi et al. (2003) found that a spontaneous point mutation in the region of the mouse gene encoding an SH2 domain of ZAP-70 caused chronic autoimmune arthritis resembling human RA. Ito et al. (2014) isolated arthritogenic T cell receptors from these SKG mice. Retrogenic mice expressing the 7-39 TCR developed arthritis (80.0%) and psoriasis-like dermatitis. The self-antigen recognized by this TCR was the ubiquitous ribosomal protein RPL23A.

Seo et al. (2004) found that an agonistic anti-4-1BB antibody inhibited both the development and established disease of collagen-induced arthritis in DBA/1 mice through expansion of CD11c+CD8+ T cells and an indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase-dependent mechanism.

Lundberg et al. (2005) showed that immunization of rats with citrullinated rat serum albumin broke B-cell tolerance. Citrullinated collagen type II induced arthritis with higher incidence and earlier onset than unmodified collagen. Citrullinated proteins and PAD4 appeared in arthritic joints only after disease onset and increased with severity.

Camps et al. (2005) found that Pik3cg-null mice were largely protected in mouse models of RA, and that this protection correlated with defective neutrophil migration. Seyler et al. (2005) treated human synovium-SCID mouse chimeras with TACI:Fc. Treatment destroyed germinal centers in GC-containing synovitis but enhanced IFN-gamma production in aggregate and diffuse synovitis.

Kawane et al. (2006) found that DNase II/Ifnar1 double-knockout mice, and mice with induced deletion of DNase II, developed chronic polyarthritis resembling RA. The mice had high serum levels of anti-CCP antibody, rheumatoid factor and MMP3. TNF-alpha was upregulated, and anti-TNF-alpha antibody prevented the arthritis.

Boilard et al. (2010) found that platelet depletion markedly reduced arthritis in the K/BxN serum-transfer model. Arthritis was significantly reduced in Gp6-null mice.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, WITH MAJOR ORGAN INVOLVEMENT**  
HLA-DRB1, DRB1*0401

Weyand et al. (1992) typed HLA-DRB1 alleles in 81 patients with seropositive RA, who were stratified by disease manifestations. Homozygosity was confirmed by locus-specific cDNA amplification and sequencing. Homozygosity for HLA-DRB1*0401 was characteristic of patients with major organ involvement. In contrast, rheumatoid nodules were associated with heterozygosity for 2 disease-associated DRB1 alleles.

**.0002 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS**  
CD44, CD44vRA

Nedvetzki et al. (2003) found that synovial fluid cells from 23 of 30 RA patients expressed a CD44 variant, CD44vRA. This variant carried an extra CAG trinucleotide transcribed from intronic sequences flanking a variant exon. Cells that immobilized FGF2 through CD44vRA bound more soluble FGFR1 and stimulated proliferation of FGFR1-bearing cells more efficiently than cells expressing wildtype CD44v3-v10.

**.0003 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, PROGRESSION OF**  
IL10, -2849A-G

Lard et al. (2003) genotyped the -2849 A/G IL10 promoter polymorphism in 283 RA patients, 413 patients with other rheumatic diseases and 1,220 controls. The high-IL-10-production genotype (AG/GG) was not associated with the incidence of RA. It was associated with a higher rate of joint destruction and higher baseline titers of rheumatoid factors and anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies.

**.0004 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
SLC22A4, INTRONIC SNP, RUNX1-BINDING SITE

In a case-control study in the Japanese population, Tokuhiro et al. (2003) found an association between RA and SLC22A4 (P = 0.000034). The associated intronic SNP lies in a RUNX1 binding site. In vitro, the 2 alleles differed in their affinity for RUNX1 and affected the transcriptional efficiency of SLC22A4.

**.0005 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
RUNX1, SNP

Tokuhiro et al. (2003) found that a SNP in RUNX1 was strongly associated with RA in the Japanese population (P = 0.00035). They suggested that its interaction with the SLC22A4 RUNX1-binding-site SNP may be an example of an epistatic effect of 2 genes.

**.0006 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
PTPN22, ARG620TRP [p.R620W]

Carlton et al. (2005) genotyped PTPN22 SNPs in 475 white North American RA patients and 475 matched controls and replicated the findings in 661 patients and 1,322 controls. The only haplotype carrying the W620 risk allele (R620W, rs2476601) was strongly associated with disease in both sample sets. An otherwise identical haplotype carrying R620 was not associated.

Kallberg et al. (2007) found consistent additive interaction between HLA-DRB1 shared epitope alleles and the A allele of PTPN22 R620W for anti-CCP-positive RA in 3 case-control studies totaling 1,977 cases and 2,405 controls. They found no interaction with smoking.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) found strong association between RA and rs6679677, which is perfectly correlated with rs2476601 (trend P = 4.9 x 10(-26); heterozygote OR 1.98, homozygote OR 3.32). Thomson et al. (2007) confirmed association of rs2476601 in 5,063 UK cases (OR = 1.53; trend P = 2.0 x 10(-18)). Gregersen et al. (2009) found association with rs2476601 in 2,418 North American cases (P = 1.62 x 10(-21)).

**.0007 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
STAT4, rs7574865

Remmers et al. (2007) found that a haplotype in intron 3 of STAT4, marked by rs7574865, was associated with RA in 1,620 North American patients and 2,635 controls (27% vs 22% of chromosomes; P = 2.81 x 10(-7); OR 1.32). The association was replicated in 1,529 Swedish patients with early RA (P = 0.02). Homozygosity for the risk allele was associated with a 60% increased risk for RA.

Martínez et al. (2008) confirmed the association in 575 Spanish RA patients and 723 controls (T vs G, P = 1.2 x 10(-6); OR 1.59).

**.0008 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
TRAF1, rs3761847
