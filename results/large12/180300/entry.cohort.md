### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that susceptibility to rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is associated with variation in the HLA-DRB1 gene (142857) on chromosome 6p21.32 and in the STAT4 gene (600558) on chromosome 2q32.2-q32.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is the most common systemic autoimmune disease. It affects about 1% of the adult population worldwide and has an estimated heritability of 60% (Begovich et al., 2004). Barton et al. (2001) described RA as an oligogenic autoimmune disease. Antibodies to cyclic citrullinated peptides (CCP) are a highly sensitive and specific diagnostic marker, present in 50 to 80% of patients who meet standard criteria, and the classic HLA-DRB1 associations are restricted to this subgroup (Gregersen et al., 2009).

### Clinical Features

Weyand et al. (1992) stratified 81 patients with seropositive RA by their patterns of disease manifestations. Some of the patients had rheumatoid nodules, and some had major organ involvement. ⚠︎

Hasstedt et al. (1994) ascertained 28 pedigrees through pairs of first-degree relatives with RA. RA was confirmed in 77 pedigree members, including the probands, and the absence of disease was verified in another 261 pedigree members.

Nanki et al. (1996) studied peripheral T lymphocytes from 12 pairs of monozygotic twins and 5 normal individuals. Six of the twin pairs were discordant for RA. ⚠︎

Yen et al. (2001) studied 30 RA patients with unequivocal rheumatoid vasculitis, which manifested as ischemic mononeuritis, ischemic ulcerations, or peripheral gangrene. For comparison they studied 70 unrelated patients who fulfilled the 1988 American College of Rheumatology criteria for RA and had no major organ involvement, such as Felty syndrome, interstitial lung disease, or rheumatoid vasculitis. All individuals were Caucasians of Western European descent. Expanded CD4+CD28null T cells were studied in some of the patients with vasculitis.

Orozco et al. (2009) studied 3,962 patients with RA recruited from 6 centers across the UK (Manchester, Sheffield, Leeds, Aberdeen, Oxford, and London). All were Caucasians of Northern European descent. Of these patients, 72% were female, 72% were positive for rheumatoid factor, and 67% carried anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA) detected by the anti-CCP antibody test.

### Other Features

Bell et al. (1992) described deficiency of the monocyte ectoenzyme non-specific esterase in 4 patients with RA, occurring in a heredofamilial pattern. The deficiency was not associated with HLA status or with rheumatoid factor seropositivity.

Okada et al. (2014) found that about two-thirds of RA risk loci were also associated with other human phenotypes. These included immune-related diseases, inflammatory or hematologic biomarkers, and cardiovascular diseases.

### Biochemical Features

Schaller et al. (2001) found increased concentrations of anti-glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI) IgG in serum and synovial fluid in 64% of patients with RA but not in controls. Concentrations of soluble GPI were also elevated in RA sera and synovial fluids, which led to immune complex formation.

### Inheritance

Hasstedt et al. (1994) performed likelihood analysis of 28 pedigrees ascertained through pairs of first-degree relatives with RA, assuming tight linkage to HLA. Their results supported an HLA-linked RA susceptibility locus with an allele frequency of 2.16% and a lifetime penetrance of 41% in male homozygotes and 48% in female homozygotes. Inheritance was recessive in males and nearly recessive in females. Of the variance within genotypes, 78% was attributable to genetic or environmental effects shared by sibs. The inferred HLA-linked locus accounted for about one-half of familial RA but only about one-fifth of RA in the population, and the authors suggested that a large portion of RA cases may occur sporadically.

Lynn et al. (1995) performed segregation analyses on first-degree relatives of consecutive RA probands who had been ascertained without regard to family history (135 simplex and 30 multiplex families). The most parsimonious genetic risk factor was a highly penetrant recessive major gene with a mutant allele frequency of 0.005. The authors suggested that families with an excess of affected males with a young age at onset may be the most informative for identifying the putative recessive gene and its modifiers.

Cornélis et al. (1998) noted that familial aggregation (lambda-s = 5), twin studies, and segregation analysis had suggested a genetic component to RA, and that HLA was estimated to account for one-third of this component. Barton et al. (2001) noted that the MHC was estimated to account for only 30 to 40% of the total genetic component of susceptibility.

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) noted that susceptibility to RA is determined by both genetic and environmental factors, with sib recurrence risk (lambda-s) estimates of 5 to 10. They stated that the known HLA-DRB1 and PTPN22 associations explain around 50% of the familial aggregation.

### Mapping

Cornélis et al. (1998) performed a genome scan in 114 European Caucasian RA sib pairs from 97 nuclear families. Linkage was significant only for HLA (P less than 2.5 x 10(-5)). Linkage was nominal (P less than 0.05) for 19 markers in 14 other regions, 4 of which potentially overlapped IDDM loci (IDDM6, IDDM9, IDDM13, and DXS998). They then studied candidate regions at 18q22-q23 (D18S68-D18S61-D18S469) and 3q13 (D3S1267) in 194 additional RA sib pairs from 164 nuclear families. Support for linkage was extended only for chromosome 3 (P = 0.002). Analysis of all 261 families gave P = 0.001 and suggested an interaction between this putative RA locus and HLA. The authors estimated that the locus could account for 16% of the genetic component of RA and suggested CD80 and CD86 as candidate genes.

Cox et al. (1999) studied the IL1 gene cluster on chromosome 2q13 in RA families using the combined sib-TDT and TDT. Several tightly linked markers in the IL1 cluster gave suggestive evidence for linkage in families whose affected sibs did not share 2 HLA-DRB1 alleles identical by descent. The evidence was significant in patients with severe disease, judged by the presence of bone erosions. Nonparametric linkage analysis gave no evidence of linkage, and parametric analysis gave weak evidence when marker-trait disequilibrium was included. The authors concluded that the data gave preliminary evidence for linkage of IL1 cluster genes to RA and suggested a possible role for the region in severe erosive disease.

Zanelli et al. (2001) analyzed 6 microsatellites in the HLA region (D6S1014, D6S273, TNFalpha, MIB, C1-2-5, and C1-3-2) in 132 RA patients and 254 controls. They defined 19 microsatellite clusters that corresponded to previously described ancestral haplotypes. The MIB*350 allele, part of a cluster often found with DQB1*0201-DRB1*0301, was an RA-predisposing factor independent of DRB1*0301 and of the RA-predisposing haplotypes DQB1*03-DRB1*04 and DQB1*0501-DRB1*01. The authors concluded that the telomeric part of the HLA region contains a locus that confers predisposition to RA independently of HLA class II.

Using 18 microsatellite markers spanning the 3.6-Mb HLA region, Ota et al. (2001) confirmed one RA susceptibility gene at HLA-DRB1. They also suggested a second susceptibility gene in the HLA class III region and narrowed it to a 70-kb interval just telomeric of the TNF gene cluster (TNFA and LTA), between microsatellites TNFa and C1-2-A. The critical segment contained 4 expressed genes, NFKBIL1, ATP6G, BAT1, and MICB, all candidate RA susceptibility genes. The authors concluded that their results excluded involvement of TNFA in the development of RA.

Jawaheer et al. (2001) performed a genomewide screen with 379 microsatellite markers in 257 North American multiplex RA families with 301 affected sib pairs, collected by the North American Rheumatoid Arthritis Consortium (NARAC) using well-defined clinical criteria. They confirmed linkage of RA to the HLA locus (P less than 0.00005; lambda-HLA = 1.79). They also found evidence for linkage (P less than 0.005) at non-HLA loci on chromosomes 1 (D1S235), 4 (D4S1647), 12 (D12S373), 16 (D16S403), and 17 (D17S1301), and analysis of X-linked markers suggested linkage to the telomeric marker DXS6807. Stratification into white or seropositive subgroups revealed some additional markers with improved significance. Several nominally significant regions overlapped loci previously implicated in RA or in other autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (D1S235), inflammatory bowel disease (D4S1647, D5S1462, D16S516), multiple sclerosis (D12S1052), and ankylosing spondylitis (D16S516). The authors concluded that HLA genes play a major role in RA susceptibility but that several other regions also contribute significantly.

Barton et al. (2001) investigated linkage in RA affected sib pair (ASP) families to 5 human regions homologous to loci found in animal models of inflammatory arthritis. They detected linkage to 17q22, which is syntenic to a susceptibility locus shared by 2 experimental rat models, in 200 RA ASP families and replicated it in a further 100 RA ASP families. Typing additional markers narrowed the linked region to 4 cM. Using extensions of the transmission disequilibrium test, they showed association with marker D17S807 in the ASP cohort. In an independent cohort of single-case RA families, they found association with two 2-marker haplotypes that included this marker, which narrowed the region likely to contain the causal mutation to about 1 cM.

Jawaheer et al. (2002) performed a haplotype analysis with 54 markers across the HLA complex in 469 multicase RA families. In addition to the associations with HLA-DRB1 alleles, they found at least 2 more genetic effects within the MHC. One lay in a 497-kb interval in the central HLA region that excluded DRB1 and was carried on a segment of the conserved ancestral A1-B8-DRB1*03 (8.1) haplotype. They suggested that more risk genes might lie in the HLA class I region on a subset of DRB1*0404 haplotypes, and concluded that HLA associations with RA are complex and cannot be fully explained by the DRB1 locus.

Using microsatellites in Japanese RA patients and controls, Okamoto et al. (2003) narrowed a second RA susceptibility region in the telomeric HLA class III region to 70 kb telomeric of the TNF cluster. This region contains 4 expressed genes: IKBL, ATP6G, BAT1, and MICB.

Fisher et al. (2003) performed a genome-search meta-analysis of 4 RA genome-wide linkage studies. Apart from the HLA locus (P less than 0.00002), the strongest evidence for an RA susceptibility locus was on chromosome 16 (P = 0.004), which none of the 4 individual genome searches had found to be statistically significant. Twelve genomic regions reached a significant summed rank (P less than 0.05), whereas 6 were expected by chance, and 4 regions (6p, 16cen, 6q, and 12p) reached P less than 0.01. The authors suggested that a subset of these regions contains RA susceptibility loci.

John et al. (2004) performed a whole-genome linkage scan with 11,245 SNPs in 157 families with multiple cases of RA. The scan detected linkage at HLA-DRB1, the major RA susceptibility locus (P = 0.00004), with a linkage interval of 31 cM, whereas a 10-cM microsatellite scan of the same cohort gave a 50-cM interval. The SNP analysis found 4 more loci at nominal significance (P less than 0.05) that the microsatellite scan had not detected, which the authors attributed mainly to the higher information content of the SNPs. When the marker set was reduced to 3,300 SNPs (1-cM spacing), several loci fell below nominal significance.

Tamiya et al. (2005) performed a whole-genome association study of RA using 27,039 microsatellites at 100-kb intervals and DNA pooling in 3 successive screens of independent case-control populations, followed by SNP fine mapping. They identified 47 candidate regions. The top 7 loci, which withstood the most stringent statistical tests, were dissected down to individual genes and/or SNPs on 4 chromosomes, including the previously known HLA-DRB1 gene on 6p21.3.

Amos et al. (2006) performed a genomewide linkage scan with more than 5,700 informative SNP markers in 642 Caucasian families with affected sib pairs with RA, ascertained by NARAC. In addition to a strong, broad linkage interval surrounding the MHC (lod score greater than 16), they found new evidence of linkage at chromosomes 2q33 (lod score 3.52) and 11p12 (lod score 3.09). Regions with lod scores greater than 2.5 were seen on chromosomes 5 and 10, and additional linkage evidence (lod scores 1.46 to 2.35) on chromosomes 4, 7, 12, 16, and 18. Stratification by anti-CCP titer status showed significant variation in linkage evidence among strata on chromosomes 4, 5, 6, and 7. The authors attributed the new linkage evidence partly to the higher information content of the Illumina SNP linkage panel (75.6%) compared with a previously used microsatellite panel (mean 52.6%).

The Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) found a sex-differentiated association between RA and SNP rs11761231 on chromosome 7q32 (P = 3.9 x 10(-7) on the 2-degree-of-freedom sex-differentiated test). The SNP had no effect in males but an additive effect in females (P = 6.8 x 10(-8); odds ratio, 1.32). The authors cautioned that the SNP has no correlated neighbors on the chip and that the finding needed further investigation.

### Heterogeneity

Stastny and Fink (1979) found the antigen TMo, which is related to HLA-DW7 and DW11, in 46% of patients with persistent pauciarticular arthritis of childhood and in only 1% of controls (relative risk, 67.7). They concluded that adult RA and childhood arthritis differ fundamentally.

Lynn et al. (1995) found significant heterogeneity in RA risk by proband gender but not by proband age at onset. Both factors were important, but proband gender appeared to be the stronger determinant, and relatives of male probands had the greatest cumulative risk. Log-linear modeling showed that proband gender, familiality (multiplex or simplex), and their interaction were enough to define the distribution of risk in families.

Barton et al. (2009) stated that 13 loci had been confirmed as associated with RA susceptibility: HLA-DRB1, PTPN22, STAT4, TRAF1/C5, 6q23, IL2RB, 12q13, 10p15, CD40, MMEL1, AFF3, 4q27, and CTLA4. All of these except TRAF1/C5 had also been associated with other autoimmune diseases.

### Molecular Genetics

Stastny and Fink (1979) performed HLA-D typing in 126 patients with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis. HLA-DW4, which earlier studies had linked to adult RA, was significantly less frequent in these children than in normal controls, whereas HLA-DW7 and HLA-DW8 were more frequent.

Gregersen et al. (1987) proposed the 'shared epitope hypothesis' as an approach to understanding the molecular genetics of susceptibility to RA.

Yen et al. (2001) found the KIR2DS2 gene in 83% of 30 patients with rheumatoid vasculitis. This was a significant enrichment compared with 76 normal controls (odds ratio, 5.56; P = 0.001) and with 70 RA patients without vasculitis, in whom it was found in 39% (odds ratio, 7.96; P = 0.001). KIR2DS2 was not more frequent in uncomplicated RA than in controls (odds ratio, 0.70; P = 0.284), so it did not affect the risk of developing RA itself. Compared with controls, RA patients had a lower frequency of HLA-C*04 (odds ratio, 0.39) and a higher frequency of HLA-C*05 (odds ratio, 2.92). HLA-C*03 was enriched only in patients with rheumatoid vasculitis (odds ratio, 3.74; P = 0.003). In logistic regression, HLA-C*03 and KIR2DS2 each conferred an independent risk of vasculitis, and no significant interaction between them was found.

Allcock et al. (2001) described haplotypic SNPs in IKBL, a gene in the central MHC that they proposed as a potential regulator of NF-kappa-B function. Using SNPs and insertion/deletion polymorphisms, Okamoto et al. (2003) identified the T allele of SNP 96452 (T/A) as the second RA susceptibility locus in the HLA region (P = 0.0062). This SNP lies at position -62 in the promoter of the IKBL gene. Because the -62T/A SNP disrupts a putative binding motif for the transcriptional repressor deltaEF1, the authors suggested that it may influence IKBL transcription.

In a case-control linkage disequilibrium study, Suzuki et al. (2003) showed that PADI4 is a susceptibility locus for RA (P = 0.000008). The locus had previously been identified by linkage studies and includes 4 PADI genes. They also identified a PADI4 haplotype associated with susceptibility to RA. PADI4 was expressed in hematologic tissues and in synovial tissues from patients with RA. ⚠︎

Lard et al. (2003) compared the -2849 A/G polymorphism in the IL10 promoter in 283 RA patients, 413 patients with other rheumatic diseases, and 1,220 healthy controls. The genotype was not associated with the incidence of RA, but it correlated with disease progression: patients with the AG/GG genotype, which is associated with high IL10 production, had a higher rate of joint destruction.

Vandenbroeck et al. (2003) typed 4 microsatellite markers in a 118-kb interval on chromosome 12q15 that contains the IFNG and IL26 genes in 251 RA patients and 198 unrelated healthy controls from Northern Ireland. Marker D12S2510, located 3 kb 3-prime of IL26, was significantly associated with RA in women (corrected P = 0.008) but not in men (P = 0.99).

Tokuhiro et al. (2003) used SNPs to carry out case-control linkage disequilibrium mapping of the cytokine gene cluster on chromosome 5q31 in the Japanese population. They found a significant association between RA and the organic cation transporter gene SLC22A4 (P = 0.000034). An intronic SNP in SLC22A4 changed the transcriptional efficiency of the gene in vitro because of an allelic difference in binding affinity for RUNX1, a transcriptional regulator in the hematopoietic system. A SNP in RUNX1 was also strongly associated with RA (P = 0.00035), and the authors suggested that RUNX1 regulation of SLC22A4 expression may be an example of an epistatic effect of 2 genes on the disorder.

Nedvetzki et al. (2003) found that synovial fluid cells from the joints of RA patients expressed a novel variant of CD44 (107269), designated CD44vRA, which encodes an extra trinucleotide (CAG) transcribed from intronic sequences flanking a variant exon. The CD44vRA variant was detected in 23 of 30 RA patients.

In a case-control study of white individuals, Begovich et al. (2004) found that the minor allele of a missense SNP in PTPN22, which encodes the hematopoietic-specific protein tyrosine phosphatase Lyp, was associated with susceptibility to RA (discovery-study allelic P = 6.6 x 10(-4); replication-study allelic P = 5.6 x 10(-8)).

Carlton et al. (2005) sequenced the coding regions of PTPN22 in 48 white North American patients with RA and identified 15 previously unreported SNPs, including 2 coding SNPs in the catalytic domain. They then genotyped 37 SNPs in 475 RA patients and 475 matched controls and replicated selected markers in 661 patients and 1,322 matched controls. The only haplotype carrying the W620 risk allele (rs2476601) was strongly associated with RA in both sample sets, whereas a haplotype identical at all other SNPs but carrying R620 showed no association. However, R620W did not fully explain the association: 2 SNPs on a single common haplotype were associated with RA independently of R620W, which suggested that at least 1 additional variant in the PTPN22 region influences RA susceptibility.

Rizzo et al. (2006) genotyped the HLA-G 14-bp deletion/insertion polymorphism in healthy individuals and in RA patients treated with methotrexate (MTX). The -14/-14-bp genotype was more common among patients who responded to MTX than among nonresponders (odds ratio, 2.46; 95% CI, 1.26-4.84; P = 0.009). The authors proposed this polymorphism as a marker of response to therapy in the early phases of the disease.

Kallberg et al. (2007) studied interaction between HLA-DRB1 shared epitope (SE) alleles and the PTPN22 R620W allele in 3 case-control studies (the Swedish EIRA, the North American NARAC, and the Dutch Leiden Early Arthritis Clinic), with 1,977 cases and 2,405 controls in total. In all 3 studies they found consistent interaction, defined as departure from additivity, between HLA-DRB1 SE alleles and the A allele of PTPN22 R620W in anti-CCP-positive RA. Multiplicative interaction between the 2 genes was seen only when the 3 studies were pooled. A linkage disequilibrium approach (deviation from independence of penetrance of 2 unlinked loci) indicated interaction between HLA-DRB1 and PTPN22 in the EIRA and NARAC studies and in the pooled analysis.

In a genome-wide association study of about 2,000 British RA cases and 3,000 shared controls genotyped with the Affymetrix 500K chip, the Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium (2007) found strong associations at the MHC (HLA-DRB1) and at PTPN22. The most associated PTPN22 marker, rs6679677 on 1p13, was perfectly correlated with the functional SNP rs2476601, and its effect size matched previous estimates. RA and type 1 diabetes shared susceptibility variants around IL2RA (10p15), PTPN2 (18p11), and the 12q24 region, all apparently novel in RA. The PTPN2 locus showed a weaker but consistent association with RA (P = 1.9 x 10(-2)), with a combined P of 9 x 10(-8) across Crohn disease, type 1 diabetes, and RA. Among SNPs with moderate evidence of association (P between 10(-5) and 5 x 10(-7)) were rs2104286 in IL2RA on 10p15 and rs743777 in IL2RB on 22q13; a combined analysis of RA and type 1 diabetes cases gave P = 5 x 10(-8) for the IL2RA (CD25) region. Association with 2 CTLA4 SNPs was only nominal (P = 0.085 and 0.034). Among weaker signals (P 10(-4) to 10(-5)) were SNPs in TNFAIP2, GZMB, PRKCQ, and KAZALD1, and the authors suggested that KAZALD1 might be relevant to bone erosions. ⚠︎

Sigurdsson et al. (2007) analyzed 5 SNPs in IRF5 and 3 SNPs in TYK2 in a Swedish cohort of 1,530 RA patients and 881 controls. Four of the 5 SNPs in the 5-prime region of IRF5 were associated with RA, whereas none of the TYK2 SNPs were. The minor alleles of 3 IRF5 SNPs were in linkage disequilibrium and formed a relatively common haplotype (frequency about 0.33) that appeared to protect against RA. The association was seen mainly in anti-CCP-negative patients, and a suggestive association with anti-CCP-negative RA was also seen in a Dutch replication cohort of 387 patients and 181 controls.

In a genomewide association study, Plenge et al. (2007) genotyped 317,503 SNPs in 1,522 anti-CCP-positive RA cases and 1,850 matched controls from the NARAC and Swedish EIRA cohorts. A SNP on chromosome 9, rs3761847, was associated with RA in all samples tested, including an independent replication set (odds ratio, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.23-1.42; P = 4 x 10(-14)). Because rs3761847 is in linkage disequilibrium with TRAF1 and C5, 2 genes relevant to chronic inflammation, the authors concluded that a common variant at the TRAF1-C5 locus is associated with an increased risk of anti-CCP-positive RA. The study also confirmed associations with the MHC and PTPN22.

Remmers et al. (2007) tested SNPs in and around 13 candidate genes in the previously linked region of chromosome 2q in 1,620 North American patients with established RA and 2,635 controls, and fine-mapped the STAT1-STAT4 region. A SNP haplotype in the third intron of STAT4 was associated with susceptibility to RA. The minor alleles of the haplotype-defining SNPs were present on 27% of chromosomes from patients and 22% from controls (for rs7574865, P = 2.81 x 10(-7); odds ratio, 1.32). The association was replicated in 1,529 Swedish patients with early RA and 881 controls (P = 0.02). Homozygosity for the risk allele, compared with absence of the allele, was associated with a 60% increased risk of RA. ⚠︎

Thomson et al. (2007) genotyped 10 SNPs, 9 of which were newly suggested by the WTCCC genome-wide association screen, in an independent UK cohort of 5,063 RA cases and 3,849 controls. They replicated the association of rs6920220 (G-A) at 6q23 (minor-allele odds ratio, 1.23; trend P = 1.1 x 10(-8)); in the combined WTCCC and validation data the odds ratio was 1.22 (trend P = 3.6 x 10(-12)). rs6920220 lay in an intergenic linkage disequilibrium block of about 60 kb that contained no known genes but included part of a PTPN11 pseudogene and lay between OLIG3 and TNFAIP3. The authors noted that TNFAIP3, a negative regulator of NF-kappa-B, was a plausible candidate because TNFAIP3-deficient mice develop multiorgan inflammation that includes the joints. They found no evidence of interaction between rs6920220 and the HLA-DRB1 or PTPN22 susceptibility markers. Together, the HLA-DRB1 shared epitope, PTPN22 rs2476601, and rs6920220 accounted for 8.5% of the total variance in RA susceptibility. They confirmed the PTPN22 rs2476601 association (odds ratio, 1.53; trend P = 2.0 x 10(-18)), found only modest evidence for rs743777 in IL2RB (combined trend P = 5.3 x 10(-6)) and borderline evidence for rs11761231 near PODXL (trend P = 0.042), and found no association with rs2104286 in IL2RA. ⚠︎

In a separate study, Plenge et al. (2007) genotyped 116,204 SNPs in 397 individuals with RA and compared them with 1,211 related individuals from the Framingham Heart Study. They identified a SNP at 6q23, rs10499194, about 150 kb from TNFAIP3 and OLIG3, that was associated with RA. The association was reproducible in 5,541 additional case-control samples (P = 10(-3) in the GWA scan, P less than 10(-6) in replication, and P = 10(-9) combined). rs10499194 and rs6920220, which lies 3.8 kb away and had been reported by the WTCCC, were statistically independent. Each association was reproducible when their data were compared with the WTCCC data, and together the 2 SNPs defined risk and protective haplotypes for RA at 6q23.

Martínez et al. (2008) genotyped the STAT4 tagging SNP rs7574865 in 575 Spanish patients with RA and 723 ethnically matched controls and confirmed the association of the T allele with RA (P = 1.2 x 10(-6); odds ratio, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.31-1.92). The same variant was also associated with inflammatory bowel disease and type 1 diabetes, but not with multiple sclerosis. The authors concluded that STAT4 is emerging as a common risk factor for diverse complex diseases.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2008) combined 2 published GWA studies of European RA in a meta-analysis (3,393 cases and 12,462 controls) and tested 31 top-ranked SNPs in a replication set of 3,929 autoantibody-positive cases and 5,807 matched controls from 8 collections. A common variant at the CD40 locus on chromosome 20q13, rs4810485, reached P = 8.2 x 10(-9) overall (odds ratio, 0.87). This SNP lies in the second intron of CD40 and is in near-perfect linkage disequilibrium (r2 = 0.95) with rs1883832, a variant that disrupts a Kozak sequence and has been shown to affect the efficiency of CD40 protein translation. The authors concluded that this pointed to a putative functional variant and supported a central role for the CD40 signaling pathway in autoantibody-positive RA. They also found association at the CCL21 locus on 9p13 (rs2812378; replication P = 0.00097, overall P = 2.8 x 10(-7)); this SNP lies about 0.1 kb from the CCL21 5-prime UTR in an LD block that contains the CCL21 coding sequence. Evidence of association at 4 more loci, MMEL1-TNFRSF14 (rs3890745, overall P = 1.1 x 10(-7)), CDK6 (rs42041, P = 4.0 x 10(-6)), PRKCQ (rs4750316, P = 4.4 x 10(-6)), and KIF5A-PIP4K2C (rs1678542, P = 8.8 x 10(-8)), still needed further validation. The meta-analysis confirmed associations at PTPN22, 6q23 (TNFAIP3), STAT4, and TRAF1-C5, and association was also observed at 4q27 (IL2/IL21) and CTLA4 but not at PADI4.

Suzuki et al. (2008) identified a linkage disequilibrium block on chromosome 1q containing multiple SLAM family genes that was associated with RA. In 2 independent Japanese RA cohorts, the association peaked at 2 functional SNPs in CD244, rs3766379 and rs6682654 (P = 3.23 x 10(-8) and P = 7.45 x 10(-8)). A Japanese cohort with systemic lupus erythematosus had a genotype distribution at CD244 similar to that of the RA cohorts, which suggested that CD244 may have a role in an autoimmune process shared by RA and SLE. ⚠︎

Barton et al. (2009) genotyped 18 SNPs from 14 putative type 1 diabetes susceptibility loci in 3,962 UK RA cases and 3,531 controls and found a new RA susceptibility locus at AFF3. In a combined analysis of 6,819 RA cases and 12,650 controls, rs10865035 was associated with RA (odds ratio, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.07-1.17; P = 2.8 x 10(-7)). The associated variants mapped to the 5-prime and promoter region of AFF3, which is preferentially expressed in lymphoid cells and encodes a nuclear factor with transcriptional activation domains. They replicated the association of RA with the CTLA4 SNP rs3087243 (odds ratio, 0.87; P = 1.1 x 10(-4)), which was confirmed in a meta-analysis with 2 earlier well-powered studies (odds ratio, 0.88; P = 2.6 x 10(-6)), and with rs6822844 in the IL2-IL21 region on 4q27, where the common allele predisposed to disease (odds ratio, 0.86; P = 5.4 x 10(-4)). The common allele of the IL7R SNP rs6897932 showed a trend toward association (odds ratio, 0.90; P = 0.007) that did not reach their corrected significance threshold.

Orozco et al. (2009) fine-mapped 70 SNPs at 6q23 in 3,962 UK RA patients and 3,531 controls and found 18 SNPs associated with RA. The strongest association was with rs6920220 (P = 2.6 x 10(-6); odds ratio, 1.22). The next strongest was with rs13207033 (P = 0.0001; odds ratio, 0.86), which was perfectly correlated with rs10499194; the authors described this as the first independent replication of that SNP. In conditional logistic regression, 3 SNPs stayed significant: rs6920220, rs13207033 (protective), and rs5029937 in intron 2 of TNFAIP3 (191163). Carrying the risk alleles of both rs6920220 and rs5029937 without the protective allele of rs13207033 gave an odds ratio of 1.86 compared with carrying none, equivalent to an effect size of 1.50 compared with controls. The authors noted that the rs5029937 effect needed replication in an independent cohort. ⚠︎

In a genome-wide association study of 2,418 RA cases and 4,504 controls from North America, Gregersen et al. (2009) identified an association at the REL locus on chromosome 2p13 (rs13031237, P = 6.01 x 10(-10)), the second most significant non-MHC association after PTPN22. They replicated the association in 2,604 cases and 2,882 controls; in the combined dataset the allelic odds ratio was 1.25 (P = 3.08 x 10(-14)) for rs13031237 and 1.21 (P = 2.60 x 10(-11)) for rs13017599. The scan confirmed the strongest association in the MHC region (P = 9.50 x 10(-104)) and the associations at PTPN22 (rs2476601, P = 1.62 x 10(-21)) and the TRAF1/C5 region (rs881375, P = 4.09 x 10(-8)). The combined dataset gave definitive support for associations with CTLA4 (rs231735; odds ratio, 0.85; P = 6.25 x 10(-9)) and BLK (rs2736340; odds ratio, 1.19; P = 5.69 x 10(-9)). Genotype-specific results showed codominance at all loci except CTLA4, which was nearly dominant, and no significant gene-gene interactions were found after correction for multiple testing.

In a case-control analysis of 1,000 RA patients and 872 controls, Mahdi et al. (2009) found that the combined effect of the HLA-DRB1 shared epitope, PTPN22, and smoking was most strongly associated with the subset positive for antibodies to the citrullinated alpha-enolase epitope CEP-1 (odds ratio, 37). For the corresponding anti-CEP-1-negative, anti-CCP-positive subset, the odds ratio was only 2. They concluded that citrullinated alpha-enolase is a specific citrullinated autoantigen that links smoking to genetic risk factors in the development of RA.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2009) used GRAIL, a text-mining method, to score 179 loci with P less than 0.001 in a published RA GWAS meta-analysis (3,393 cases and 12,462 controls) for functional relationships to genes in 16 known RA loci. They genotyped the 22 loci that scored significantly in 7,957 autoantibody-positive cases and 11,958 matched controls of European ancestry. Three new RA risk loci were confirmed at genomewide significance: rs11586238 on 1p13.1 near CD2 and CD58 (overall P = 1.0 x 10(-9)), rs1980422 on 2q33.2 near CD28 (overall P = 1.3 x 10(-9)), and rs548234 on 6q21 near PRDM1 (overall P = 2.1 x 10(-8)). Four more loci replicated but did not reach a conservative genomewide threshold: rs394581 near TAGAP on 6q25.3 (overall P = 3.8 x 10(-7)), rs10919563 in a PTPRC intron on 1q31.3 (overall P = 6.7 x 10(-7)), rs540386 in a TRAF6 intron on 11p12 (overall P = 3.9 x 10(-6)), and rs12746613 near FCGR2A on 1q23.3 (overall P = 1.5 x 10(-5)); the authors concluded that these likely represent true RA loci. The CD28 SNP rs1980422 lies 129 kb from the known risk allele rs3087243 in the CTLA4 region and is in minimal linkage disequilibrium with it, and conditional analysis confirmed that the 2 SNPs confer risk independently. There were no significant epistatic interactions among the 7 replicated SNPs and known RA risk alleles, and the 7 associations had consistent effects across all 14 collections. Several of the new loci lie near alleles associated with other autoimmune diseases, including FCGR2A (systemic lupus erythematosus), TAGAP (celiac disease and type 1 diabetes), PTPRC and CD58 (multiple sclerosis), and PRDM1 (Crohn disease), although none of the RA risk alleles corresponded perfectly to a previously established autoimmune allele.

In a genome-wide association study of common copy number variants (CNVs) in about 2,000 cases of each of 8 common diseases, including RA, and 3,000 shared controls, the Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium et al. (2010) found CNVs in the HLA region associated with RA (CNVR2845.14, p = 1.4 x 10(-39); odds ratio, 1.77), but had no evidence that these were signals independent of the known associated HLA haplotypes. CNVR1065.1, annotated as mapping to chromosome 2, is actually a dispersed duplication with its polymorphism in the HLA region; it showed a strong signal in RA and type 1 diabetes and was well tagged by HLA SNPs known to be associated with both diseases. After correction for multiple testing, the multiallelic CNVs at CCL3L1, beta-defensin, and FCGR3A/B showed no significant association with RA, and the reported association between CCL3L1 and RA was not formally replicated (nominal p = 0.058).

In a genome-wide association study of RA in Japanese individuals, Kochi et al. (2010) identified association with the CCR6 polymorphism rs3093024 at 6q27 and validated it in 2 independent Japanese replication cohorts (total of 7,069 cases and 20,727 controls; overall odds ratio, 1.19; P = 7.7 x 10(-19)). A triallelic dinucleotide polymorphism in CCR6 (CCR6DNP), in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs3093024, affected gene transcription. The CCR6DNP genotype correlated with CCR6 expression level and was associated with the presence of IL17 in the sera of RA patients, and the authors suggested that CCR6 is critically involved in IL17-driven autoimmunity. CCR6DNP was also associated with susceptibility to Graves disease and Crohn disease. ⚠︎

Stahl et al. (2010) performed a GWAS meta-analysis of 5,539 autoantibody-positive RA cases and 20,169 controls of European descent, replicated the findings in 6,768 cases and 8,806 controls, and identified 7 new RA risk loci at genome-wide significance (P less than 5 x 10(-8)) in the combined analysis of 41,282 samples. Three loci had not been implicated in any earlier genetic study of RA or other autoimmune disease: 2p14 (rs934734, in intron 1 of SPRED2; P = 5.3 x 10(-10)), 5q11 (rs6859219, in ANKRD55 near IL6ST; P = 9.6 x 10(-12)), and 5q21 (rs26232, in C5orf30; P = 4.1 x 10(-8)). Loci already implicated in other autoimmune diseases were also associated with RA: 3p14 near PXK (rs13315591; P = 4.6 x 10(-8)), 4p15 near RBPJ (rs874040; P = 1.0 x 10(-16)), and 6q27 in CCR6 (rs3093023; P = 1.5 x 10(-11)); the IRF5 SNP rs10488631, chosen for its association with SLE, was also associated (P = 4.2 x 10(-11)). At IRF5, RA was associated with the group 1 SNP rs10488631 and the group 3 SNP rs4728142, whose effects were independent, but not with the group 2 SNP rs729302; the group 3 SNP rs3807306 was associated with autoantibody-positive RA (P = 4.5 x 10(-6)). At 2 known loci they found new alleles with effects at least partly independent of the previously implicated SNPs: CCL21 (rs951005; P = 3.9 x 10(-10)) and IL2RA (rs706778; P = 3.3 x 10(-11)). At IL2RA, RA risk was better explained by the rs2104286(T)-rs11594656(T) haplotype, which confers type 1 diabetes risk, than by rs706778. They also gave definitive evidence for association at AFF3 (rs11676922; P = 1.0 x 10(-14); odds ratio, 1.14). Eleven further SNPs, near IL6R, CD247, IL2/IL21, ZEB1, SH2B3, BATF, CD19/NFATC2IP, IKZF3, UBASH3A, and UBE2L3, replicated at P less than 0.05 without reaching genome-wide significance, and the authors suggested that most were likely genuine RA risk alleles. ⚠︎

Kurreeman et al. (2011) studied 1,515 RA cases and 1,480 controls drawn from electronic health records and matched for genetic ancestry and ACPA status. In individuals of European ancestry, the odds ratios and the aggregate genetic risk score of known RA risk alleles were nearly identical to those from a GWAS of 5,539 autoantibody-positive RA cases and 20,169 controls.

In a study of 1,115 RA patients and 654 healthy controls, Robinson et al. (2012) used a quantitative sequence variant assay with an adapted paralogue ratio test. They found no association of FCGR3A duplications or deletions with RA. FCGR3B deletions were overrepresented in RA (odds ratio, 1.50; P = 0.028), more apparently in rheumatoid factor-positive disease (odds ratio, 1.61; P = 0.011).

Okada et al. (2014) performed a trans-ethnic GWAS meta-analysis of 29,880 RA cases and 73,758 controls of European and Asian ancestry, testing about 10 million SNPs. They identified 42 novel RA risk loci at genome-wide significance, which brought the total number of RA risk loci to 101. Using an in silico pipeline with 8 criteria (including missense variants, cis-eQTLs, primary immunodeficiency genes, cancer somatic mutation genes, knockout mouse phenotypes, and pathway analysis), they prioritized 98 biological candidate genes among the 377 genes at the 100 non-MHC risk loci. Sixteen percent of RA risk SNPs were in linkage disequilibrium with missense SNPs, and risk SNPs at 44 loci showed cis-eQTL effects.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Weyand et al. (1992) identified HLA-DRB1 alleles by PCR amplification and oligonucleotide hybridization in 81 RA patients stratified by disease manifestations and found that combinations of disease-associated HLA-DRB1 alleles correlated strongly with clinical manifestations. Rheumatoid nodules were highly associated with heterozygosity for 2 disease-associated HLA-DRB1 alleles, whereas homozygosity for HLA-DRB1*0401 was characteristic of patients with major organ involvement.

Lard et al. (2003) found that RA patients with the -2849 AG/GG IL10 genotype had higher autoantibody titers at baseline, and concluded that the polymorphism is associated with autoantibody production and the joint damage that follows.

Vandenbroeck et al. (2003) inferred a 3-marker haplotype, IFNGCA*13;D12S2510*8;D12S2511*9, that was significantly underrepresented in women with RA (odds ratio, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.32-0.78; corrected P = 0.03) but not in men with RA. They concluded that common polymorphisms in the IFNG/IL26 gene region may contribute to the sex bias in susceptibility to RA.

Thomson et al. (2007) found that the association of rs6920220 at 6q23 was stronger in anti-CCP-positive and rheumatoid factor-positive patients than in antibody-negative patients; the difference between the anti-CCP-positive and anti-CCP-negative subgroups was significant (trend P = 0.003). The authors said this supported the idea that RA comprises at least 2 genetically distinct subsets.

Barton et al. (2009) stratified RA patients by rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibody status and found that the strength of association at the AFF3, CTLA4, and 4q27 SNPs was similar in autoantibody-positive and autoantibody-negative patients.

Orozco et al. (2009) found that rs6920220 and rs5029937 at 6q23 were associated with autoantibody-positive RA, both rheumatoid factor-positive and anti-CCP-positive. In contrast, rs13207033 was associated with both rheumatoid factor-positive and rheumatoid factor-negative RA, but only with anti-CCP-negative RA.

Kurreeman et al. (2011) found that the distribution of a genetic risk score based on 28 non-HLA risk alleles in ACPA-positive RA cases partially overlapped with that in ACPA-negative cases.

### Pathogenesis

Weyand et al. (1992) suggested that the disease-associated sequence motif in HLA-DRB1 plays a role in determining disease severity. They also proposed that the codominant effect of HLA-DRB1 alleles points to a role for HLA-DR molecules in thymic selection in the pathogenesis of RA.

Nanki et al. (1996) used a PCR-ELISA method to study TCRBJ gene usage in the TCRBV5, 13, and 17 families in peripheral T cells from 12 monozygotic twin pairs, 6 of them discordant for RA. BJ repertoires were more similar between monozygotic twins than between unrelated individuals, and the inflammation of RA did not cause specific changes in the genetically determined BJ pattern. TCR transcripts from each TCRBV family showed a distinctive BJ gene profile, which was seen more clearly in CD4+ than in CD8+ lymphocytes.

Müller-Ladner et al. (2000) used in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry to study synovium from patients with short-term (less than 1 year) and long-term (more than 2 years) RA. IL4 STAT mRNA was detected in both groups, with the most intense expression in follicular inflammatory infiltrates, and both fibroblasts and macrophages of the synovial lining expressed it. Synoviocytes expressed IL4 STAT and Jak1 protein, stimulation with IL4 increased this expression, and phosphorylation showed that IL4 STAT was activated. The authors concluded that IL4 STAT-dependent pathways operate in both early and late RA and presumably contribute to inhibitory immune mechanisms in rheumatoid synovium. ⚠︎

Yen et al. (2001) studied CD4+CD28null T-cell clones from 3 patients with rheumatoid vasculitis and found that 58 to 91% of the clones expressed the stimulatory receptor KIR2DS2, often without the opposing inhibitory receptors KIR2DL2 or KIR2DL3. Cross-linking of CD158b on these clones costimulated IFN-gamma production. The authors suggested that KIR2DS2 may have a role in vascular damage by regulating CD4+CD28null T cells.

Using phage display, Schaller et al. (2001) cloned high-affinity human monoclonal anti-GPI IgGs from an RA patient. The antibodies were highly somatically mutated, indicating an antigen-driven, affinity-matured response. Immunohistochemistry of RA synovium showed high concentrations of GPI on the synovial lining surface and on arteriolar endothelium, suggesting a mechanism by which anti-GPI antibodies may precipitate joint disease.

Leadbetter et al. (2002) showed that effective activation of rheumatoid factor-positive B cells, which express an antigen receptor specific for self-IgG, was mediated by IgG2a-chromatin immune complexes. Activation required synergistic engagement of the antigen receptor and a member of the MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor family, and inhibitor studies implicated TLR9. The authors concluded that their data established a critical link between the innate and adaptive immune systems in the development of systemic autoimmune disease and explained why autoantibodies reacting with nucleic acid-protein particles are so common.

Okamoto et al. (2003) noted that IKBL is homologous to IKBA, a known inhibitor of NF-kappa-B, and suggested that the MHC may harbor RA genetic determinants affecting both the innate and adaptive arms of the immune system.

Suzuki et al. (2003) found that the RA susceptibility haplotype of PADI4 affected transcript stability and was associated with levels of antibody to citrullinated peptide in sera from RA patients. They proposed that this haplotype increases production of citrullinated peptides that act as autoantigens, raising the risk of developing the disease.

Nedvetzki et al. (2003) found that Namalwa cells transfected with CD44vRA or with wildtype CD44v3-v10 bound the same amount of FGF2 through heparan sulfate chains, but CD44vRA cells bound substantially more soluble FGFR1 and stimulated proliferation of FGFR1-bearing BaF-32 cells more efficiently. RA synovial cells expressing CD44vRA also bound more soluble FGFR1 than synovial cells expressing CD44v3-v10 or synovial cells from patients with osteoarthritis, and antibodies against the CD44 v3 exon product reduced this binding. The authors suggested that this mechanism may foster the destructive inflammatory cascade in RA. ⚠︎

Lard et al. (2003) found that adenoviral transfer of IL10 into fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) inhibited their invasiveness. However, FLS from RA patients carrying the -2849 G allele were no more or less invasive in vitro than FLS from patients without it.

Schönland et al. (2003) found that HLA-DRB1*04 alleles were associated with excessive loss of telomeres in CD4+ T cells in healthy individuals. The accelerated loss occurred during the first 2 decades of life, and homeostatic T-cell proliferation was reduced in adulthood. Granulocytes also showed early telomere loss, suggesting that the hematopoietic stem cell is the primary target, and telomeric repair mechanisms were intact. They proposed that HLA-DRB1*04 alleles, or genes in linkage disequilibrium with them, regulate stem cell replication and thereby contribute to the build-up of senescent and autoreactive T cells in RA.

Sakaguchi et al. (2003) showed in mice that altered T-cell antigen receptor signaling through an aberrant ZAP-70 changed the thresholds of T cells to thymic selection, so that autoimmune T cells that would otherwise be negatively selected were positively selected. They suggested that thymic production of arthritogenic T cells, caused by a genetically determined shift of the T-cell repertoire toward high self-reactivity, might also be crucial to disease development in a subset of patients with RA.

Begovich et al. (2004) showed that the PTPN22 risk allele disrupts the P1 proline-rich motif that is important for interaction with Csk, potentially altering the normal function of these proteins as negative regulators of T-cell activation. Because the same allele had been implicated in type 1 diabetes, they suggested that the variant phosphatase may increase overall immune reactivity and heighten a carrier's risk of autoimmune disease.

Seyler et al. (2005) examined 72 rheumatoid synovial tissues representing 3 types of synovitis: ectopic germinal center (GC)-positive, T cell-B cell aggregate, and diffuse. GC+ synovitis had the highest levels of APRIL, which came only from CD83+ dendritic cells. BLyS was present at similar levels in all tissue types and came only from CD68+ macrophages.

Rizzo et al. (2006) found that methotrexate induced production of soluble HLA-G (sHLA-G) when peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy individuals and from RA patients not treated with methotrexate were activated. The highest sHLA-G1 concentrations were significantly associated with the -14/-14-bp genotype. The authors proposed that methotrexate induces anti-inflammatory sHLA-G molecules that contribute to the therapy response.

In the EIRA study, Kallberg et al. (2007) found no interaction between smoking and PTPN22 R620W, and described a new pattern of interactions between the 2 major genetic risk factors and the major environmental risk factor for anti-CCP-positive RA.

Sigurdsson et al. (2007) concluded that genetic variants of IRF5 contribute to a distinct disease etiology and pathogenesis in anti-CCP-negative RA, a subset that differs from anti-CCP-positive RA in its genetic and environmental risk factors.

Remmers et al. (2007) found that the STAT4 haplotype associated with RA was also strongly associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (31% vs 22% of chromosomes; P = 1.87 x 10(-9); odds ratio, 1.55), and suggested that RA and lupus share a pathway.

In luciferase and allele-specific transcript quantification assays, Suzuki et al. (2008) showed that the RA-susceptible alleles of rs3766379 and rs6682654 in CD244, and their haplotype, increased expression.

Gregersen et al. (2009) suggested that the association of REL with RA points to disease pathways involving other RA susceptibility genes, including CD40, TRAF1, TNFAIP3, and PRKCQ, and proposed that CD40 signaling pathways have an important role in RA pathogenesis.

In 1,497 individuals from 3 RA cohorts, Mahdi et al. (2009) detected antibodies to the immunodominant citrullinated alpha-enolase CEP-1 epitope in 43 to 63% of anti-CCP-positive individuals. This subset was preferentially linked to HLA-DRB1*04.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2009) concluded that the new RA loci further implicate T-cell activation, NF-kappa-B signaling, and B-cell activation and differentiation, and that the PRDM1 (BLIMP1) locus pointed to a new process, terminal differentiation of B cells into plasma cells. They noted that functional studies would be needed to confirm the causal genes.

Robinson et al. (2012) showed that neutrophil expression of Fc-gamma-RIIIb correlated with FCGR3B gene copy number and suggested that neutrophils may play an important role in RA pathogenesis, possibly through reduced Fc-gamma-RIIIb-mediated clearance of immune complexes.

Okada et al. (2014) found that RA risk alleles were significantly enriched in H3K4me3 peaks in primary CD4+ regulatory T cells, out of 34 cell types tested. Pathway analysis showed enrichment for T-cell pathways and for B-cell and cytokine signaling pathways (IL10, interferon, GM-CSF). Genes at RA risk loci overlapped significantly with primary immunodeficiency genes (14 of 194), with the highest overlap in the 'immune dysregulation' category, and with genes carrying somatic mutations in hematologic cancers (17 of 251), but not with genes mutated in nonhematologic cancers.

In an expression QTL study of purified CD4+ T cells and CD14+CD16- monocytes from 461 healthy individuals of European, African, and East Asian ancestry, Raj et al. (2014) found that RA susceptibility alleles, like those for multiple sclerosis and type 1 diabetes, showed a preponderance of T cell-specific cis-regulatory effects, whereas Alzheimer and Parkinson disease alleles were enriched for monocyte-specific effects. Of 425 GWAS SNPs associated with 1 or more autoimmune diseases, 143 had cis-regulatory effects on 164 genes in monocytes and/or T cells.

### Diagnosis

Gregersen et al. (2009) noted that antibodies to cyclic citrullinated peptides are a highly sensitive and specific diagnostic marker for RA, present in 50 to 80% of patients who meet standard criteria.

### Clinical Management

Camps et al. (2005) developed specific, selective, orally active small-molecule inhibitors of PI3K-gamma. In 2 distinct mouse models of RA, oral treatment with one of these inhibitors suppressed the progression of joint inflammation and damage, reproducing the protection seen in Pik3cg -/- mice. The authors suggested that selective PI3K-gamma inhibitors might be useful for treating chronic inflammatory disorders such as RA.

Okada et al. (2014) found that the targets of approved RA drugs, such as tocilizumab, tofacitinib, and abatacept, were significantly enriched (3.7-fold) among the biological RA risk genes and their direct protein-protein interaction partners. They proposed that CDK6/CDK4 inhibitors approved for cancer could be repurposed for RA.

### Population Genetics

Begovich et al. (2004) found the PTPN22 risk allele in about 17% of white individuals from the general population and in about 28% of white individuals with RA.

Carlton et al. (2005) predicted 10 common (frequency greater than 1%) PTPN22 haplotypes in white North Americans.

Raychaudhuri et al. (2008) estimated that all known non-MHC common variants together explained only 3.6% of the variance in RA risk. Because about 60% of risk is thought to be genetic, they concluded that known risk alleles accounted for less than half of the genetic variation and that more common alleles of modest effect probably remained to be found.

Stahl et al. (2010) estimated that the more than 30 validated non-MHC RA risk alleles explained 3.9% of total disease variance, of which 0.67% was due to the newly reported alleles. Including an estimated 12% for the MHC, genetic discoveries explained only 16% of disease variance, whereas more than half of the risk of autoantibody-positive RA is thought to be genetic.

Kurreeman et al. (2011) found a large overlap in the RA genetic risk score among individuals of European, African, East Asian, and Hispanic ancestry and concluded that the genetic basis of RA risk is similar across cases of diverse ancestry.

Okada et al. (2014) found significant correlations of risk allele frequencies and odds ratios at the 101 RA loci between Europeans and Asians, although 5 loci showed population-specific associations. The 100 non-MHC loci explained 5.5% of heritability in Europeans and 4.7% in Asians. The authors concluded that the genetic risk of RA is generally shared between the 2 populations.

### Animal Model

Vingsbo-Lundberg et al. (1998) studied pristane-induced arthritis (PIA) in rats, a model of RA in which a single subcutaneous injection of the non-immunogenic oil pristane into DA rats caused arthritis limited to peripheral joints, with a chronic relapsing course. As in RA, the affected joints were mostly symmetric (85% at day 35). In a genome screen of 153 (E3 x DA) F2 rats, different phases of the disease were linked to different loci: loci on chromosomes 4 and 6 (Pia2 and Pia3) affected arthritis onset, a locus on chromosome 12 (Pia4) was linked to severity and joint erosion, and chronicity was linked to loci on chromosome 4 (Pia5) and chromosome 14 (Pia6). High serum levels of cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) were linked to the Pia4 region, and COMP levels correlated with the number of affected paws at day 35. Pia5 lay near the collagen-induced arthritis locus Cia3 and the Lyp/Iddm1 locus, which the authors suggested may indicate a common susceptibility gene for several autoimmune diseases. F1 rats from the DA x E3 cross developed chronic PIA, but with later onset and less severe disease than the DA parents. Females were more susceptible than males, and the Pia2 linkage became significant only when females were analyzed alone.

Schaller et al. (2001) noted that in K/BxN T cell receptor-transgenic mice, a spontaneous inflammatory arthritis resembling human RA is initiated by T cells but sustained almost entirely by antibodies to GPI. They concluded that the immunologic events leading to disease in this model may also occur in human RA.

Tokuhiro et al. (2003) showed that Slc22a4 was highly expressed in the inflamed joints of mice with collagen-induced arthritis, and that SLC22A4 expression was specific to hematologic and immunologic tissues.

Sakaguchi et al. (2003) found that in mice, a spontaneous point mutation in the gene encoding an SH2 domain of ZAP-70, a key T-cell signal transduction molecule, caused chronic autoimmune arthritis that resembled human RA in many respects.

Camps et al. (2005) found that Pik3cg -/- mice were largely protected in mouse models of RA, and that the protection correlated with defective neutrophil migration.

In human synovium-SCID mouse chimeras, Seyler et al. (2005) found that blocking APRIL and BLyS with the decoy receptor TACI:Fc destroyed germinal centers in GC+ synovitis and strongly inhibited IFN-gamma and immunoglobulin transcription. In aggregate and diffuse synovitis, the same treatment left immunoglobulin levels unaffected and increased IFN-gamma production. These different effects correlated with the presence of TACI+ T cells in aggregate and diffuse synovitis and their absence in GC+ synovitis. The authors proposed that BLyS and APRIL have both proinflammatory and antiinflammatory activities in RA.

Kawane et al. (2006) found that Dnase2 -/- Ifnar -/- mice, and mice with an induced deletion of the Dnase2 gene, developed a chronic polyarthritis resembling human RA. A set of cytokine genes was strongly activated in the affected joints, and the serum contained high levels of anti-CCP antibody, rheumatoid factor, and matrix metalloproteinase-3. Early in disease, Tnfa expression was upregulated in the bone marrow of DNase II-deficient mice, and anti-TNF-alpha antibody prevented arthritis. The authors concluded that when macrophages cannot degrade mammalian DNA from erythroid precursors and apoptotic cells, they produce TNF-alpha, which activates synovial cells to produce various cytokines and leads to chronic polyarthritis.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, WITH MAJOR ORGAN INVOLVEMENT, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
HLA-DRB1, DRB1*0401

Weyand et al. (1992) identified HLA-DRB1 alleles by PCR amplification and oligonucleotide hybridization in 81 patients with seropositive RA who had been stratified by their patterns of disease manifestations. Homozygosity for the HLA-DRB1*0401 allele was characteristic of RA patients with major organ involvement, whereas rheumatoid nodules were highly associated with heterozygosity for 2 disease-associated HLA-DRB1 alleles. ⚠︎

**.0002 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, VASCULITIS IN, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
KIR2DS2, GENE PRESENCE

Yen et al. (2001) found the KIR2DS2 gene in 83% of 30 Caucasian patients of Western European descent with rheumatoid vasculitis, compared with 39% of 70 RA patients without major organ involvement. This was a significant enrichment compared with normal controls (odds ratio, 5.56; P = 0.001) and with RA patients without vasculitis (odds ratio, 7.96; P = 0.001). KIR2DS2 was not more frequent in uncomplicated RA than in controls, so it did not affect the risk of RA itself. In logistic regression, KIR2DS2 conferred a risk of vasculitis independently of HLA-C*03. In CD4+CD28null T-cell clones from 3 patients with vasculitis, 58 to 91% of the clones expressed KIR2DS2, and cross-linking of CD158b costimulated IFN-gamma production. ⚠︎

**.0003 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, VASCULITIS IN, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
HLA-C, HLA-C*03

Yen et al. (2001) found that HLA-C*03 was enriched only in RA patients with rheumatoid vasculitis (odds ratio, 3.74; P = 0.003) in a study of 30 patients with vasculitis and 70 patients without major organ involvement, all Caucasians of Western European descent. In logistic regression, HLA-C*03 and KIR2DS2 each conferred an independent risk of vasculitis, and no significant interaction between them was found.

**.0004 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS**  
CD44, CD44vRA, 3-BP INS, CAG

Nedvetzki et al. (2003) found that synovial fluid cells from the joints of RA patients expressed a CD44 variant, designated CD44vRA, that encodes an extra trinucleotide (CAG) transcribed from intronic sequences flanking a variant exon. CD44vRA was detected in 23 of 30 RA patients. Namalwa cells transfected with CD44vRA bound the same amount of FGF2 as cells transfected with wildtype CD44v3-v10, but bound substantially more soluble FGFR1 and stimulated proliferation of FGFR1-bearing BaF-32 cells more efficiently. RA synovial cells expressing CD44vRA also bound more soluble FGFR1 than synovial cells expressing CD44v3-v10 or synovial cells from patients with osteoarthritis, and antibodies to the CD44 v3 exon product reduced this binding. ⚠︎

**.0005 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
STAT4, rs7574865

Remmers et al. (2007) identified a SNP haplotype in the third intron of STAT4 associated with RA in 1,620 North American patients with established RA and 2,635 controls. The minor alleles of the haplotype-defining SNPs were present on 27% of chromosomes from patients and 22% from controls (for rs7574865, P = 2.81 x 10(-7); odds ratio, 1.32). The association was replicated in 1,529 Swedish patients with early RA and 881 controls (P = 0.02), and homozygosity for the risk allele, compared with its absence, was associated with a 60% increased risk of RA.

Martínez et al. (2008) genotyped rs7574865 in 575 Spanish patients with RA and 723 ethnically matched controls and confirmed the association of the T allele with RA (P = 1.2 x 10(-6); odds ratio, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.31-1.92).

**.0006 RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
TNFAIP3, rs5029937

In a fine-mapping study of 70 SNPs at 6q23 in 3,962 UK RA patients of Northern European descent and 3,531 controls, Orozco et al. (2009) found that rs5029937, located in intron 2 of TNFAIP3, remained significantly associated with RA in conditional logistic regression, together with rs6920220 and the protective rs13207033. Carrying the risk alleles of both rs6920220 and rs5029937 without the protective allele of rs13207033 gave an odds ratio of 1.86 compared with carrying none. rs5029937 was associated with autoantibody-positive RA.
