### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant torsion dystonia-1 (DYT1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOR1A gene (605204) on chromosome 9q34. ⚠︎

### Description

Torsion dystonia is a movement disorder in which control of voluntary movements is lost. It appears as sustained, involuntary muscle contractions that often cause twisting and repetitive movements or abnormal postures (Ozelius et al., 1989; Kwiatkowski et al., 1991). In the early-onset form, these twisting muscle contractures begin in childhood, and the symptoms are believed to result from altered neuronal communication in the basal ganglia (Ozelius et al., 1997). Most familial forms are transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait with reduced penetrance and variable expressivity (Ozelius et al., 1989; Kwiatkowski et al., 1991). The GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene is a major cause of early-onset dystonia, but only 30% of mutation carriers develop the disease (Risch et al., 2007). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

In a review of the clinical and molecular genetics of primary dystonias, Muller et al. (1998) proposed referring to the known forms as dystonias 1-12. Most of these forms can be distinguished by genetic criteria.

### Clinical Features

Zeman et al. (1960) discussed formes frustes (incomplete or mild forms) of idiopathic dystonia musculorum deformans.

Yanagisawa et al. (1972) reported familial dystonia musculorum deformans occurring together with tremor.

In a family study of 32 patients with torsion dystonia, Bundey et al. (1975) found at least 2 forms of generalized dystonia with onset in childhood. Most of the other 15 patients, 12 of whom had onset in adult life, appeared to have a nongenetic torsion dystonia. However, the authors observed one example of a benign adult-onset dominant form associated with tremor.

Marsden et al. (1976) reported on the natural history of idiopathic torsion dystonia.

Bressman et al. (1994) studied Ashkenazi Jewish individuals with idiopathic torsion dystonia and identified 90 carriers of the DYT1-associated haplotype. In carriers, the mean age at onset was 12.5 +/- 8.2 years. In 94% of carriers, symptoms began in a limb, with arm and leg affected equally often. Onset was rarely in the neck (3.3%) or larynx (2.2%).

Kramer et al. (1994) studied 7 non-Jewish families of northern European and French Canadian descent with idiopathic torsion dystonia. Affected individuals were clinically similar to Ashkenazi Jewish cases: onset was predominantly in the limbs, and at least 1 individual in each pedigree had onset before age 12 years.

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) reported a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia who carried the 3-bp (GAG) deletion in the DYT1 gene and had a unique clinical presentation.

Leung et al. (2001) described a patient with atypical early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features.

Hjermind et al. (2002) described 3 Danish probands with the DYT1 GAG deletion. All 3 had the classic phenotype of DYT1 dystonia, but only 1 had a family history of dystonia.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found the DYT1 GAG deletion in 6 German patients with dystonia. Only 2 of them had what is thought to represent the classic features of early-onset primary generalized dystonia. Two others had multifocal dystonia, 1 of whom had involvement of cranial and cervical muscles. The remaining 2 had writer's cramp of both hands with only slight progression.

Ghilardi et al. (2003) compared motor performance and sequence learning in 12 clinically unaffected DYT1 mutation carriers and 12 age-matched controls. Movement time and spatial accuracy were similar in the 2 groups, but sequence learning was reduced in gene carriers (p less than 0.01).

Heiman et al. (2004) gave a standardized psychiatric interview to members of families with a DYT1 mutation: 96 carriers with dystonia, 60 carriers without dystonia, and 65 noncarriers. Compared with noncarriers, the risk of recurrent major depressive disorder was higher in both nonmanifesting carriers (RR = 4.95) and manifesting carriers (RR = 3.62). Carriers also developed recurrent depression at an earlier age.

Kostic et al. (2006) described a large Serbian family with the DYT1 GAG deletion in which the phenotype varied considerably within the family, from asymptomatic carrier status to late-onset focal dystonia and generalized jerky dystonia. Of 7 mutation carriers, only 2 were affected, giving a penetrance of 29%.

Calakos et al. (2010) reported a man who presented in his fifth decade with involuntary jaw movements and grimacing that had begun 3 years earlier. On neurologic examination he had an orofacial movement, mainly jaw retraction, consistent with oromandibular dystonia. He also had cogwheel tone without rigidity, a mild action and postural tremor of the upper extremities, and absent Achilles reflexes. He had bipolar disorder, and brain MRI was normal.

### Other Features

Carbon et al. (2004) used FDG PET to study 12 nonmanifesting and 11 manifesting DYT1 gene carriers, 6 nonmanifesting and 7 manifesting DYT6 gene carriers, and 11 controls. In both genotypes, manifesting carriers showed bilateral hypermetabolism in the presupplementary motor area (BA 6) and in the parietal association cortices (BA 40/7) compared with nonmanifesting carriers.

### Biochemical Features

Wooten et al. (1973) reported elevated plasma dopamine-beta-hydroxylase activity in autosomal dominant torsion dystonia.

Ziegler et al. (1976) studied plasma norepinephrine and dopamine-beta-hydroxylase levels in patients with dystonia.

Askenasy et al. (1980) carried out a familial study of 13 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with torsion dystonia of various origins. Serum dopamine-beta-hydroxylase (DBH) activity was significantly higher in the patients and their relatives than in controls, especially in the childhood-onset group. Elevated serum DBH activity was not specifically associated with Jewish origin, a dominant trait, slow progression, or axial topography. Intravenous metoclopramide (20 mg) did not change serum DBH activity in 12 patients and 17 relatives. However, in 1 patient with symptomatic dystonia and 2 of her maternal relatives, serum DBH activity doubled or tripled after the injection. The authors suggested that the metoclopramide-induced rise in DBH activity in relatives of patients indicated a genetic disposition.

### Inheritance

Bundey et al. (1975) found at least 2 clinically indistinguishable forms of generalized childhood-onset torsion dystonia: one autosomal dominant and one autosomal recessive. They concluded that the dominant forms could begin either in childhood or in adult life.

Eldridge (1981) discussed the inheritance of torsion dystonia in Jews.

Zilber et al. (1984) found that X-linked and simple autosomal recessive models did not fit their data well. An autosomal dominant model with low penetrance could account for the observations and gave an estimated gene frequency in European Jews of 3 to 4 per 100,000. Multifactorial inheritance was also possible, and the authors noted that not all cases need share the same genetic basis.

Breakefield et al. (1986) studied a family with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia in which more than 50 members had a complete neurologic examination. Their linkage model assumed autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance and allowed for age at onset.

Bressman et al. (1989) studied 43 Ashkenazi Jewish probands with onset of idiopathic torsion dystonia before 28 years of age, 146 of 162 living first-degree relatives, and 96 of 239 living second-degree relatives. Nineteen relatives had definite dystonia and 2 had probable dystonia. Counting definite cases only, the age-adjusted risk was 15.5% for first-degree relatives and 6.5% for second-degree relatives. Risk did not differ significantly between the sexes or among parents, offspring, and sibs. The risks were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, with an estimated penetrance of 29.4% (definite cases only) or 32.2% (definite and probable cases).

Risch et al. (1990) performed segregation analysis in Ashkenazi Jews with idiopathic torsion dystonia, based on 43 probands with onset before age 27 years from 42 nuclear families. The data were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with 30% penetrance. Recessive and polygenic inheritance were strongly rejected, and there was no evidence for sporadic cases or new mutations.

Kramer et al. (1990) stated that most familial forms of idiopathic torsion dystonia follow autosomal dominant transmission with reduced penetrance. Ozelius et al. (1992) found that most affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals were heterozygous for the disease-associated ABL-ASS haplotype, which supported autosomal dominant inheritance of DYT1.

Kramer et al. (1994) estimated penetrance in non-Jewish DYT1 families at 0.40 to 0.75. After identifying disease gene carriers and adjusting for age at onset, they obtained a direct penetrance estimate of 0.46, consistent with estimates of 30 to 40% in the Ashkenazi Jewish population. Ozelius et al. (1997) stated that early-onset torsion dystonia is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with a reduced penetrance of 30 to 40%.

Among 3 Danish probands with the DYT1 GAG deletion, Hjermind et al. (2002) found that 1 had a family history of dystonia. Of the 2 sporadic probands, 1 had a de novo mutation and the other had inherited the mutation from an asymptomatic carrier parent.

### Mapping

Breakefield et al. (1986) carried out linkage analysis with RFLPs in a family with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia. They excluded the pro-opiomelanocortin and glutamic acid decarboxylase genes, which had been implicated in rat models, as the cause of disease in this family. Other regions of the genome were also excluded.

Kramer et al. (1987) performed multipoint linkage analysis in a single large non-Jewish pedigree with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia. They excluded linkage to large portions of chromosomes 11p and 13q and to virtually the entire long arm of chromosome 21.

In a large non-Jewish kinship whose affected members had a clinical syndrome similar to that in the Jewish population, Ozelius et al. (1989) mapped a torsion dystonia susceptibility gene (ITD1) by linkage analysis. They found tight linkage to the gelsolin gene, and multipoint linkage analysis placed the gene in 9q32-q34 between ABO and D9S26. The dopamine-beta-hydroxylase locus also lies in this region.

Kramer et al. (1990) performed linkage analysis with DNA polymorphisms in 12 Ashkenazi Jewish families with idiopathic torsion dystonia. They found close linkage of the dystonia gene to the argininosuccinate synthetase (ASS) gene, and multipoint analysis placed it in 9q32-34. This region also contains the gelsolin and dopamine-beta-hydroxylase loci.

In a review, Muller and Kupke (1990) noted that linkage analysis in one form of autosomal dominant torsion dystonia had assigned a torsion dystonia locus to the long arm of chromosome 9.

Using GT repeat polymorphisms, Kwiatkowski et al. (1991) localized the dystonia gene in Ashkenazi Jewish families to the 11-cM interval between AK1 and D9S10 on 9q32-34. Linkage analysis in a large non-Jewish family was also consistent with the gene lying in this region, although in that family positive lod scores extended over more than 20 cM.

Ozelius et al. (1992) identified obligate recombination events in Ashkenazi Jewish families. These narrowed the DYT1 region on chromosome 9q to a 6-cM interval bounded by AK1 and ASS, with DYT1 centromeric to ASS.

Warner et al. (1993) performed linkage analysis with the ASS and ABL loci on 9q34 in 27 small families with idiopathic torsion dystonia: 26 British and 1 French, 3 of them Ashkenazi Jewish. The cumulative lod score for ASS at a recombination fraction of 0.001 was -6.72. HOMOG analysis nevertheless supported linkage to 9q34 in a subset of families, in both Jewish and non-Jewish kindreds in the UK.

In 7 non-Jewish families of northern European and French Canadian descent with early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia, Kramer et al. (1994) found results consistent with linkage to the DYT1 region on 9q34. None of the families carried the Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype, and the authors concluded that they probably had different mutations in the DYT1 gene. The 2 French Canadian families shared the same haplotype.

Ozelius et al. (1997) built a YAC contig covering 600 kb of the 9q34 region that includes D9S62a. Using several new polymorphic loci, they extended the linkage disequilibrium analysis of the Ashkenazi Jewish founder mutation. The most likely location of the DYT1 gene was a 150-kb region between D9S2161 and D9S63.

### Heterogeneity

Bundey et al. (1975) identified an autosomal recessive childhood-onset form of torsion dystonia in at least 3 families, probably involving about 6 to 8 patients. Citing American evidence, they suggested that this form is particularly common in Ashkenazi Jews. They concluded that most isolated adult-onset cases seemed to have exogenous but unidentified causes.

Kramer et al. (1990) suggested that the same gene may cause idiopathic torsion dystonia both in a previously mapped non-Jewish kindred and in Ashkenazi Jewish families. Because penetrance differed substantially between the groups, however, 2 different mutations may be operating.

In a review, Muller and Kupke (1990) noted that primary torsion dystonia may be inherited as an autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked recessive trait. They stated that at least 6 forms of autosomal dominant torsion dystonia can be distinguished clinically.

Warner et al. (1993) studied 3 non-Jewish families that were clinically indistinguishable from the others; in each, individual lod scores excluded a disease locus tightly linked to ASS on 9q34. The authors suggested genetic heterogeneity in idiopathic torsion dystonia.

Kramer et al. (1994) found that 2 other non-Jewish families were not linked to DYT1. These families had atypical idiopathic torsion dystonia, with later onset and/or cranial or cervical involvement. The authors concluded that other genes are involved in dystonia.

Gasser et al. (1996) determined haplotypes at 5 marker loci closely linked to DYT1 on chromosome 9q in 10 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with focal hand dystonia (8 with musician's cramp and 2 with writer's cramp). None of the 20 chromosomes could be constructed as the founder haplotype, which is associated with more than 90% of generalized dystonia cases in this population, and no common haplotype was found. The authors concluded that these findings argued against a role for the DYT1 founder mutation in occupational hand dystonia.

Ozelius et al. (1997) stated that the DYT1 gene appears to cause most cases of early-onset torsion dystonia in both Ashkenazi Jewish and non-Jewish families.

Muller et al. (1998) noted at least 8 clinically distinct autosomal dominant forms and 2 X-linked recessive forms of primary dystonia. Gene loci had been identified for at least 6 of the autosomal dominant forms, including idiopathic torsion dystonia at 9q34, in which mutations in a gene coding for an ATP-binding protein had been detected (DYT1). Nemeth (2002) reviewed the genetics of the primary dystonias, including idiopathic torsion dystonia (DYT1), focal dystonias (DYT7), and mixed dystonias (DYT6 and DYT13), as well as the 'dystonia-plus' syndromes and heredodegenerative disorders.

In the Serbian family with the DYT1 GAG deletion, Kostic et al. (2006) found 3 members without the deletion who also developed dystonia: 2 with multifocal dystonia and 1 with torticollis. The authors suggested that the dystonia in these members was due to another etiologic factor, such as another dystonia gene, or may be psychogenic.

Contarino et al. (2008) described a large Dutch family from a small village on a former island with mild craniocervical-brachial focal dystonia. Eight members were definitely affected and 4 were possibly affected, with a mean age at onset of 45.5 years (range, 39-56). No mutations were detected in the DYT1 or SGCE genes, and linkage to the DYT6, DYT7, DYT13, and DYT16 loci was excluded.

### Molecular Genetics

Ozelius et al. (1997) identified the DYT1 gene (TOR1A) on chromosome 9q34 as responsible for dominant early-onset torsion dystonia. DYT1 encodes a novel ATP-binding protein, torsinA, which has homologs in nematode, rat, mouse, and human and some resemblance to the heat-shock protein and Clp protease families. Almost all cases of early-onset dystonia carried the same 3-bp deletion, which causes loss of one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of torsinA. The deletion appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations.

Kamm et al. (1999) screened 57 German patients with idiopathic torsion dystonia for the 3-bp GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene. They found it in 3 of 5 patients with early limb-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia, 1 of whom had a positive family history, and in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short course of disease. The mutation was absent in 2 patients with early-onset generalized dystonia beginning in the cervical muscles, 5 other patients with multifocal dystonia, 14 with segmental dystonia, and 30 with focal cervical dystonia.

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) identified the 3-bp (GAG) deletion in the DYT1 gene in a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia.

Valente et al. (1999) analyzed haplotypes surrounding DYT1 in 9 Ashkenazi Jewish and 15 non-Jewish British patients carrying the GAG deletion (see Population Genetics).

Leung et al. (2001) screened the TOR1A gene in 40 patients with early-onset dystonia or early-onset parkinsonism (EOP) and identified 3 variations, none of them in EOP patients. In a patient with early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, they found an 18-bp deletion (Phe323_Tyr328del) that would remove 6 amino acids close to the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site. This was the first TOR1A mutation identified other than the GAG deletion (Glu302/303del), and it was associated with a distinct type of early-onset dystonia. Two infrequent intronic single-bp changes of unknown consequence were found in a dystonia patient and in the mother of an EOP patient.

Hjermind et al. (2002) tested 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia for the DYT1 GAG deletion and found it in 3. One proband had a family history of dystonia. Of the 2 sporadic probands, 1 carried a de novo mutation and the other had inherited the mutation from an asymptomatic carrier parent. The authors noted that de novo mutations in the DYT1 gene are seldom reported, although independent founder mutations are known to have occurred. They also discussed how the heterogeneity of dystonia, as illustrated by DYT1 dystonia, makes genetic counseling difficult.

Grundmann et al. (2003) screened 256 unselected German patients with dystonia and found the DYT1 GAG deletion in 6. Only 2 of the 6 had classic early-onset primary generalized dystonia. Of the other 4, 2 had multifocal dystonia and 2 had writer's cramp of both hands.

In a large Serbian family, Kostic et al. (2006) identified 7 carriers of the DYT1 GAG deletion: 6 by direct analysis and 1 by inferred haplotype. Only 2 of them were affected with dystonia.

Frederic et al. (2008) carried out a nationwide census of French carriers of TOR1A mutations. They studied 53 index cases and 104 relatives and constructed haplotypes linked to the mutation (see Population Genetics). ⚠︎

In a patient with early-onset dystonia, Zirn et al. (2008) identified a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5. The change was absent from 1,000 control chromosomes, and arginine-288 has been conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. The authors concluded that it may be a novel pathogenic TOR1A mutation in DYT1.

In a man with late-onset oromandibular focal dystonia, Calakos et al. (2010) identified a heterozygous c.613T-A change in exon 3 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution. The affected residue is highly conserved and lies in the AAA domain. The variant was not found in 800 population controls (1,600 chromosomes). The authors suggested that the variant may contribute to the expression of dystonia. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bressman et al. (1994) classified 174 Ashkenazi Jewish individuals with idiopathic torsion dystonia by their 9q34 haplotype (D9S62, D9S63, ASS). Ninety were carriers of the DYT1-associated haplotype, 70 were noncarriers, and 14 were ambiguous. Mean age at onset was significantly lower in carriers than in noncarriers (12.5 +/- 8.2 vs 36.5 +/- 16.4 years). In noncarriers, onset was in the neck, larynx, or other cranial muscles in 79% and in the arm in 21%; onset was never in the leg.

Kamm et al. (1999) suggested that in the German population the GAG deletion is responsible for a major portion of typical early limb-onset dystonia, but not for other types of dystonia.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found that only 2 of 6 carriers of the DYT1 GAG deletion had classic early-onset generalized dystonia; the others had multifocal dystonia or bilateral writer's cramp. They concluded that the mutation may be associated with segmental and multifocal forms, which makes it difficult to predict carrier status in advance or to give genetic counseling. ⚠︎

Carbon et al. (2004) found increased metabolism specific to DYT1 carriers in the putamen, anterior cingulate (BA 24/32), and cerebellar hemispheres. DYT6 carriers, in contrast, showed putaminal hypometabolism and temporal cortex (BA 21) hypermetabolism. The authors concluded that clinical manifestations are related to cortical dysfunction, whereas subcortical metabolic abnormalities may be trait features specific to each dystonia genotype.

Heiman et al. (2004) found that the severity of motor signs was not associated with the likelihood of recurrent depression in DYT1 mutation carriers. Carriers did not have a higher risk of other affective disorders, such as single major depression or bipolar disorder. The authors concluded that early-onset recurrent depression may be a clinical expression of the DYT1 GAG mutation, independent of motor manifestations.

Risch et al. (2007) studied 119 DYT1 GAG-deletion carriers with dystonia, 113 carriers without dystonia, and 197 controls. Compared with controls, the 216H allele of the TOR1A D216H variant was more frequent in carriers without dystonia and less frequent in carriers with dystonia. Haplotype analysis showed that the H allele was highly protective when in trans with the GAG deletion. It also gave suggestive evidence that the D216 allele must be present in cis for the disease to be penetrant.

Kamm et al. (2008) reported that the D216H polymorphism modified susceptibility to DYT1 dystonia in European patients.

### Pathogenesis

Hornykiewicz et al. (1986) studied the brains of 2 patients with generalized childhood-onset dystonia musculorum deformans after death. They found no important histologic changes in the basal ganglia, cerebral cortex, higher brainstem nuclei, locus ceruleus, or raphe nuclei. Choline acetyltransferase activity and GABA and glutamate levels were normal. Norepinephrine was markedly and consistently decreased in the lateral and posterior hypothalamus, mamillary body, subthalamic nucleus, and locus ceruleus. Serotonin was subnormal in the dorsal raphe nucleus, and dopamine was subnormal in the nucleus accumbens and, in 1 of the 2 cases, in the striatum. Norepinephrine was raised in the septum, thalamus, colliculi, red nucleus, and dorsal raphe nucleus, and serotonin was elevated in the globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and locus ceruleus. Homovanillic acid levels showed little consistent change. The authors suggested that a disturbance of noradrenergic brain mechanisms may be a basic neurochemical abnormality in the disorder.

Gasser et al. (1996) suggested that excessive, repetitive use, possibly together with ulnar neuropathy, may be the inciting cause of some focal dystonias. They also stated that if the DYT1 gene is involved in these later-onset dystonias, there was no evidence for a common mutation in the Ashkenazi Jewish population.

Ghilardi et al. (2003) used H2(15)O PET to study nonmanifesting DYT1 carriers. During motor execution, carriers showed increased activation in the left premotor cortex and right supplementary motor area and reduced activation in the posterior medial cerebellum. During sequence learning, they showed increased activation in the left ventral prefrontal cortex and lateral cerebellum. The authors suggested that localized increases in neural activity may allow normal movement execution but may not compensate for the defect in sequence learning.

Carbon et al. (2004) suggested that dystonia may be associated with abnormal movement preparation caused by defective sensorimotor integration.

Using diffusion tensor MRI, Carbon et al. (2004) found reduced fractional anisotropy, a measure of axonal integrity and coherence, in the subgyral white matter of the sensorimotor cortex in DYT1 mutation carriers compared with controls (p less than 0.005). The authors suggested that abnormal anatomic connectivity of the supplementary motor area may contribute to the susceptibility of DYT1 carriers to develop clinical manifestations of dystonia.

McNaught et al. (2004) studied 4 clinically documented and genetically confirmed DYT1 patients at autopsy. They found perinuclear inclusion bodies in cholinergic and other neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus, cuneiform nucleus, and griseum centrale mesencephali, but not in controls. The inclusions stained positively for ubiquitin, torsinA, and the nuclear envelope protein lamin A/C. All 4 cases, but no controls, also had tau/ubiquitin-immunoreactive aggregates in pigmented neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus ceruleus. No inclusion bodies were detected in the substantia nigra pars compacta, striatum, hippocampus, or selected regions of the cerebral cortex. The authors concluded that DYT1 dystonia is associated with impaired protein handling and with the nuclear envelope. They suggested that alterations in these brainstem structures, which mediate motor activity and muscle tone, could underlie the pathophysiology of the disorder.

Asanuma et al. (2005) used PET with [11C]-raclopride to study nonmanifesting carriers of the DYT1 mutation. Striatal D2 receptor binding was about 15% lower in the caudate and putamen (p less than 0.005). The authors suggested that reduced striatal D2 binding is a trait feature of the DYT1 genotype.

Zirn et al. (2008) found that HEK293 cells overexpressing TOR1A with the R288Q mutation had an enlarged perinuclear space. Cells overexpressing the common delta-GAG mutation showed the same change, but cells overexpressing wildtype TOR1A did not.

Carbon et al. (2009) used [11C]raclopride PET to study manifesting and nonmanifesting carriers of DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. Compared with healthy controls, both groups of carriers had significantly lower D2 receptor availability in the caudate and putamen (p less than 0.001). The reduction was larger in DYT6 carriers than in DYT1 carriers (-38.0% vs -15.0%), and manifesting and nonmanifesting carriers of either genotype did not differ significantly. Voxel-based analysis also showed reduced raclopride binding in the ventrolateral thalamus of both DYT1 and DYT6 carriers. This reduction was more pronounced in DYT6 carriers and did not depend on clinical status. The authors concluded that the reduced D2 receptor availability fits with dysfunction or loss of D2-bearing neurons, increased synaptic dopamine, or both, and that it likely represents a susceptibility factor for developing clinical manifestations.

Calakos et al. (2010) transfected BHK cells with EGFP-tagged TOR1A. Inclusions formed in 44% of cells expressing F205I, compared with 79% for deltaE TOR1A and 10% for wildtype. The deltaE inclusions colocalized with the nuclear envelope marker LaminB1, whereas the larger F205I inclusions colocalized mainly with the ER marker KDEL. The authors suggested that the F205I and deltaE mutations do not have identical pathophysiology.

### Diagnosis

Batshaw et al. (1985) reported Munchausen syndrome simulating torsion dystonia, pointing to factitious disorder as part of the differential diagnosis of torsion dystonia. ⚠︎

Ozelius et al. (1992) proposed that status for the extended ABL-ASS haplotype could predict carrier status in Jewish individuals and be used for molecular diagnosis.

Bressman et al. (1994) found that limb onset, leg involvement during the course of the disease, and age at onset distinguished DYT1 haplotype carriers from noncarriers with 90% accuracy.

Bressman et al. (2002) assessed 147 carriers of the DYT1 GAG deletion and 113 blood-related noncarriers from 43 families. Only the category of definite dystonia was 100% specific. Probable dystonia was more common in carriers than in noncarriers, but possible dystonia was not. The authors recommended that only individuals with definite signs of dystonia be considered affected in linkage and other genetic studies of DYT1 families.

### Population Genetics

Bundey et al. (1975) estimated that fitness in childhood-onset dystonia was 1/20 of normal, so most childhood-onset dominant cases appeared sporadically. Because of a probable paternal age effect, they thought that 9 to 11 sporadic patients with generalized childhood dystonia represented new dominant mutations.

In a nationwide survey of idiopathic torsion dystonia in Israel, Zilber et al. (1984) collected 47 patients between 1969 and 1980, 40 of them of European origin. The frequency in Jews of European origin was about 1 in 23,000 live births, 5-fold greater than in Jews of Afro-Asian origin. Paternal ages were increased for isolated cases, and the authors suggested that some of these cases may have been new mutations.

Ozelius et al. (1989) stated that the frequency of the defective torsion dystonia gene in the Ashkenazi Jewish population is about 1/10,000. Assuming a disease frequency of 1 in 15,000 among Ashkenazi Jews, Bressman et al. (1989) estimated the gene frequency at 1 in 9,000.

Risch et al. (1990) suggested that the high incidence and dominant inheritance of early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews indicate genetic homogeneity within this population, which makes it especially useful for linkage studies. Kramer et al. (1990) noted that idiopathic torsion dystonia is 5 to 10 times more frequent in the Ashkenazi Jewish population than in other groups.

Ozelius et al. (1992) typed 53 definitely affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals, 13 of whom appeared to be sporadic cases. They found strong linkage disequilibrium between DYT1 and an extended ABL-ASS haplotype (4/A12), which was present on 69% of disease-bearing chromosomes in affected Ashkenazi Jews but on only 1% of control Jewish chromosomes. They suggested that a single mutation event accounts for most hereditary dystonia in this population and that DYT1 lies within 1 to 2 cM of these loci. Sporadic cases carried the ASS A12 allele at a rate (8/13) similar to that in familial cases (28/40). This suggested that many sporadic cases are hereditary, that the disease gene frequency in Ashkenazi Jews is greater than 1/15,000, and that penetrance is lower than 30%. ⚠︎

Warner et al. (1993) found that the allelic association between ASS/ABL and idiopathic torsion dystonia, previously seen in Ashkenazi families in the USA, was also present in British Jewish kindreds.

Bressman et al. (1994) noted that linkage disequilibrium between DYT1 and a particular 9q34 haplotype in Ashkenazi Jews implies that a founder mutation in DYT1 accounts for a large proportion of early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia in this population. Kramer et al. (1994) noted strong linkage disequilibrium in this population between DYT1 and adjacent markers within a 2-cM region, with the associated haplotype present in more than 90% of early limb-onset Ashkenazi Jewish cases.

Zoossmann-Diskin (1995) discussed whether genetic drift or selection accounts for idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews.

Risch et al. (1995) studied 59 Ashkenazi Jewish families with idiopathic torsion dystonia using 6 closely linked microsatellite loci on 9q34. They found that more than 90% of early-onset cases in the Ashkenazi population were due to a single founder mutation, which they estimated first appeared about 350 years ago. Carriers of the founder mutation came mainly from the northern part of the historic Jewish Pale of Settlement (Lithuania and Byelorussia), and the current frequency of the mutation was estimated at between 1/6,000 and 1/2,000. The recent origin and high frequency of this dominant mutation suggested that the Ashkenazi population descends from a limited group of founders and that genetic drift has been important in determining disease allele frequencies in this population.

Valente et al. (1999) analyzed haplotypes surrounding DYT1 in 9 Ashkenazi Jewish and 15 non-Jewish British patients carrying the GAG deletion. All the British Ashkenazi Jewish patients carried the same haplotype as North American Ashkenazi Jews, which supported descent of the British Ashkenazi Jewish community from the same small group of individuals. Among the non-Jewish British patients, only a limited number of distinct founder haplotypes were observed. The authors concluded that the deletion is probably not a very frequent mutation and has arisen only a limited number of times over the centuries.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found the DYT1 GAG deletion in only 3 of 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia. This frequency was low even though several probands had early-onset generalized dystonia. No proband with other types of dystonia carried the deletion.

Grundmann et al. (2003) screened 256 unselected patients with various subtypes of dystonia from 4 movement disorder outpatient clinics in Germany and found the DYT1 GAG deletion in 6 (about 2.3%).

In a nationwide census of French carriers of TOR1A mutations, Frederic et al. (2008) estimated the frequency of early-onset torsion dystonia due to TOR1A at 0.13 per 100,000 and the mutation frequency at 0.17 per 100,000, showing that the disease is rare in France. The previously reported Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype was present in 11 of 53 French families with the TOR1A mutation. The remaining families had distinct haplotypes, suggesting independent mutation events. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Lorden et al. (1984) described dystonic (dt), an autosomal recessive mutation in Sprague-Dawley rats. Signs began on postnatal day 10 and included twisting of the axial muscles, hyperflexion of the trunk, self-clasping of the limbs, and poor limb placement during walking. Routine light microscopy found no morphologic lesions in neural or nonneural tissue. Norepinephrine levels were significantly raised in the cerebellum but normal in other locus ceruleus terminal fields, and the noradrenergic innervation of the cerebellum looked normal. The mutant rats were less sensitive than their littermates to the akinesic effects of haloperidol, but their striatal dopamine levels and their response to apomorphine were normal. The authors suggested that these findings point to biochemical disturbances in the extrapyramidal system and that the dt rat may be a useful model for human dystonia musculorum deformans.

Shashidharan et al. (2005) generated 4 independent lines of transgenic mice overexpressing human DeltaE-torsinA under a neuron-specific enolase promoter. About 40% of transgenic mice in each line developed abnormal involuntary movements, starting as early as 3 weeks after birth. These included dystonic-appearing self-clasping of the limbs, hyperkinesia, and rapid bidirectional circling. Striatal dopamine was decreased in affected transgenic mice but increased in transgenic mice without behavioral changes. Neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus and other brainstem regions contained perinuclear inclusions and aggregates staining for ubiquitin, torsinA, and lamin, in a pattern similar to that described in DYT1 patients.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

In almost all cases of early-onset torsion dystonia (DYT1), Ozelius et al. (1997) found a heterozygous 3-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene. The deletion causes loss of one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of the ATP-binding protein torsinA. It appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations. ⚠︎

Kamm et al. (1999) found the GAG deletion in 3 of 5 German patients with early limb-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia, 1 of whom had a positive family history. They also found it in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short course of disease.

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) reported a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia who carried the GAG deletion and had a unique clinical presentation.

Valente et al. (1999) studied 9 Ashkenazi Jewish and 15 non-Jewish British patients carrying the GAG deletion. All the Ashkenazi Jewish patients shared the haplotype found in North American Jews, whereas the non-Jewish British patients showed a limited number of distinct founder haplotypes.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found the GAG deletion in 3 Danish probands with classic DYT1 dystonia. In 1 proband the mutation was familial, in 1 it had arisen de novo, and in 1 it had been inherited from an asymptomatic carrier parent.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found the GAG deletion in 6 of 256 unselected German patients with dystonia. Two of the 6 had classic early-onset generalized dystonia, 2 had multifocal dystonia, and 2 had bilateral writer's cramp.

Heiman et al. (2004) found that both manifesting carriers of the GAG mutation (n = 96) and nonmanifesting carriers had an increased risk of recurrent major depressive disorder compared with noncarriers.

In a large Serbian family, Kostic et al. (2006) identified 7 carriers of the heterozygous GAG deletion, only 2 of whom had dystonia. The phenotype ranged from late-onset focal dystonia to generalized jerky dystonia. ⚠︎

Risch et al. (2007) studied 119 carriers of the GAG deletion with dystonia and 113 carriers without dystonia. The 216H allele of the TOR1A D216H variant was highly protective when in trans with the GAG deletion.

**.0002 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 18-BP DEL [p.F323]

In a patient with atypical early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, Leung et al. (2001) identified an 18-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene (Phe323_Tyr328del). The deletion would remove 6 amino acids close to the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site. It was the first TOR1A mutation identified other than the GAG deletion.

**.0003 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, ARG288GLN [c.863G>A; p.R288Q]

In a patient with early-onset dystonia, Zirn et al. (2008) identified a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5. The change was absent from 1,000 control chromosomes, and arginine-288 has been conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. HEK293 cells overexpressing the R288Q mutant had an enlarged perinuclear space, as did cells overexpressing the delta-GAG mutant, but cells overexpressing wildtype TOR1A did not.

**.0004 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, PHE205ILE [c.613T>A; p.F205I]

In a man with late-onset oromandibular dystonia beginning in his fifth decade, Calakos et al. (2010) identified a heterozygous c.613T-A change in exon 3 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the AAA domain. The variant was not found in 1,600 control chromosomes. In transfected BHK cells, F205I TOR1A produced inclusions in 44% of cells, compared with 79% for deltaE and 10% for wildtype. These inclusions colocalized mainly with the ER marker KDEL rather than with the nuclear envelope. The authors suggested that the variant may contribute to the expression of dystonia. ⚠︎
