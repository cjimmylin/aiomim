### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant torsion dystonia-1 (DYT1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOR1A gene (605204) on chromosome 9q34.

### Description

Kramer et al. (1990) and Kwiatkowski et al. (1991) described idiopathic torsion dystonia as a neurologic disorder of sustained, involuntary muscle contractions. These often cause twisting and repetitive movements of the limbs, trunk, and/or neck and can progress to abnormal postures. Most familial forms show autosomal dominant inheritance with reduced penetrance. Ozelius et al. (1997) described early-onset torsion dystonia as a movement disorder that begins in childhood. Its symptoms were believed to result from altered neuronal communication in the basal ganglia. Risch et al. (2007) noted that the DYT1 GAG deletion is a major cause of early-onset dystonia, but only 30% of mutation carriers express clinical disease. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

In a review of the clinical and molecular genetics of primary dystonias, Muller et al. (1998) proposed naming the known forms dystonias 1 to 12, and they designated autosomal dominant idiopathic torsion dystonia as DYT1. They noted that most of these forms can be told apart by genetic criteria.

### Clinical Features

ZEMAN et al. (1960) reported on the formes frustes, meaning incomplete or mild forms, of idiopathic dystonia musculorum deformans. Zeman and Dyken (1967) reported clinical, genetic, and pathoanatomic studies of dystonia musculorum deformans.

Yanagisawa et al. (1972) reported familial dystonia musculorum deformans occurring together with tremor.

Ozelius et al. (1989) reported that affected members of a large non-Jewish kindred with torsion dystonia had a clinical syndrome similar to the one seen in the Ashkenazi Jewish population. They characterized the disorder by loss of control of voluntary movements, appearing as sustained muscle contractions and/or abnormal postures.

Bressman et al. (1994) studied 174 Ashkenazi Jewish individuals with idiopathic torsion dystonia. Using a DYT1-associated haplotype of 9q34 markers (D9S62, D9S63, and ASS), they classified 90 individuals as carriers, 70 as noncarriers, and 14 as ambiguous. Mean age at onset was significantly lower in carriers than in noncarriers (12.5 +/- 8.2 vs 36.5 +/- 16.4 years). In 94% of carriers, symptoms began in a limb, with arm and leg affected equally often. Onset in the neck (3.3%) or larynx (2.2%) was rare.

Kramer et al. (1994) found that affected members of 7 non-Jewish families linked to DYT1 were clinically similar to Ashkenazi Jewish cases. Onset was mainly in the limbs, and in each pedigree at least 1 person had onset before age 12 years.

Ghilardi et al. (2003) compared movement and sequence learning in 12 clinically unaffected DYT1 mutation carriers and 12 age-matched controls. Motor performance did not differ between the groups, with no significant differences in movement time or spatial accuracy. Sequence learning, however, was reduced in gene carriers relative to controls (p less than 0.01). The authors suggested that abnormalities of motor behavior exist in clinically nonmanifesting DYT1 carriers.

Heiman et al. (2004) gave a standardized psychiatric interview to members of families with an identified DYT1 mutation. They compared 96 mutation carriers with dystonia, 60 carriers without dystonia, and 65 noncarriers. Compared with noncarriers, the risk of recurrent major depressive disorder was higher in nonmanifesting carriers (RR = 4.95, CI 1.72-14.29) and in manifesting carriers (RR = 3.62, CI 1.00-10.53). Carriers also developed recurrent depression at an earlier age.

Kostic et al. (2006) described a large Serbian family in which the DYT1 phenotype varied widely, ranging from asymptomatic carrier status to late-onset focal dystonia and generalized jerky dystonia.

Calakos et al. (2010) reported a man in his fifth decade of life who had had involuntary jaw movements and grimacing for 3 years, consistent with oromandibular dystonia. He had bipolar disorder treated with lithium and remote exposure to dopamine receptor-blocking agents. Examination showed cogwheel tone without rigidity, a mild action/postural tremor of the upper extremities, and absent Achilles reflexes. Brain MRI and laboratory studies were normal. There was no family history of dystonia, but tremor and depression were present in the family. The movements responded well to anticholinergic agents.

### Biochemical Features

Wooten et al. (1973) reported elevated plasma dopamine-beta-hydroxylase activity in autosomal dominant torsion dystonia. Ziegler et al. (1976) measured plasma norepinephrine and dopamine-beta-hydroxylase in patients with dystonia.

Askenasy et al. (1980) studied 13 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with torsion dystonia of various origins, together with their families. Serum dopamine-beta-hydroxylase (DBH) activity was significantly higher in patients and their relatives than in controls, particularly in the childhood-onset group. Jewish origin, dominant inheritance, slow progression, and axial distribution were each not specific for elevated serum DBH activity. An intravenous injection of 20 mg metoclopramide did not change serum DBH activity in 12 patients with torsion dystonia or in 17 relatives. In 1 patient with symptomatic dystonia and in 2 of her maternal relatives, who had different fathers, serum DBH activity doubled or tripled after the injection. The authors suggested that a metoclopramide-induced rise in serum DBH activity in relatives of patients with torsion dystonia indicates a genetic predisposition.

Hornykiewicz et al. (1986) examined the brains of 2 patients with generalized childhood-onset dystonia musculorum deformans after death. Histology showed no important changes in the basal ganglia, cerebral cortex, higher brainstem nuclei, locus ceruleus, or raphe nuclei. Choline acetyltransferase activity and levels of GABA and glutamic acid in the cortex and basal ganglia were within the control range. Norepinephrine concentrations were markedly and consistently decreased in the lateral and posterior hypothalamus, mamillary body, subthalamic nucleus, and locus ceruleus. Serotonin was subnormal in the dorsal raphe nucleus. Dopamine was low in the nucleus accumbens in both cases and in the striatum in 1 of the 2 cases. Norepinephrine, serotonin, and 5-HIAA were raised in several other regions. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

ZEMAN et al. (1959) studied the hereditary pattern of idiopathic dystonia musculorum deformans.

In a family study of torsion dystonia, Bundey et al. (1975) suggested that 9 to 11 of the patients with generalized childhood-onset dystonia represented new dominant mutations, because there appeared to be a paternal age effect. Fitness in patients with childhood onset was 1/20 of normal, so most childhood dominant cases appeared sporadically.

Eldridge (1981) addressed the mode of inheritance of torsion dystonia in Jews.

Zilber et al. (1984) analyzed idiopathic torsion dystonia (ITD) in 47 Israeli Jewish patients. Assuming that all cases fit one genetic model, X-linked and simple autosomal recessive models did not fit the data well. An autosomal dominant model with low penetrance could account for the observations and gave an estimated ITD gene frequency of 3 to 4 per 100,000 in European Jews. Multifactorial inheritance was also possible. The authors cautioned that it may be inappropriate to assume that all cases share the same genetic basis, or even that all are inherited. Fathers of isolated cases were older than expected, and on this basis the authors suggested that some isolated cases may have been new mutations.

Breakefield et al. (1986) studied a family in which torsion dystonia showed autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance. Ozelius et al. (1989) noted that most of the hereditary forms of torsion dystonia described up to that time showed autosomal dominant transmission with variable expressivity. ⚠︎

Bressman et al. (1989) studied the families of 43 Ashkenazi Jewish probands whose idiopathic torsion dystonia began before age 28 years. They examined 146 first-degree and 96 second-degree relatives. Nineteen relatives had definite dystonia and 2 had probable dystonia. Counting definite cases only, the age-adjusted risk was 15.5% for first-degree relatives and 6.5% for second-degree relatives. Risk did not differ significantly by sex, or among parents, offspring, and sibs. On maximum likelihood analysis, the risks were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, with penetrance estimated at 29.4% for definite cases or 32.2% when probable cases were included.

Risch et al. (1990) carried out segregation analysis in a family study of idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews. The study included 43 probands with onset before age 27 years from 42 nuclear families. The pedigrees were extended through all available first- and second-degree relatives, who were examined directly and videotaped. Final diagnoses were based on the examination findings and a blinded review of the videotapes. The data were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with 30% penetrance. Recessive and polygenic inheritance were strongly rejected, and there was no evidence for sporadic cases or new mutations.

Ozelius et al. (1992) typed 53 definitely affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals, 13 of whom appeared to be sporadic cases. A similar proportion of sporadic cases (8 of 13, 62%) and familial cases (28 of 40, 70%) carried the ASS A12 haplotype. From this, the authors suggested that many sporadic cases are hereditary, that the disease gene frequency is greater than 1/15,000, and that penetrance is lower than the previously estimated 30%. Most affected individuals were heterozygous for the ABL-ASS haplotype, supporting autosomal dominant inheritance. ⚠︎

Kramer et al. (1994) estimated penetrance in non-Jewish families at 0.40 to 0.75. After identifying disease gene carriers and adjusting for age at onset, they obtained a direct penetrance estimate of 0.46, consistent with the 30 to 40% estimated in the Ashkenazi Jewish population. Ozelius et al. (1997) stated that early-onset torsion dystonia due to DYT1 is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with reduced penetrance of 30 to 40%.

### Mapping

Breakefield et al. (1986) performed linkage analysis with restriction fragment length polymorphisms in a family with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia in which more than 50 members had neurologic examination. Their lod score analysis allowed for age at onset and incomplete penetrance. They excluded the pro-opiomelanocortin and glutamic acid decarboxylase genes, which had been implicated in rat models, as well as other genomic regions, as the site of the disease gene in this family.

Kramer et al. (1987) performed multipoint linkage analysis in a single large non-Jewish pedigree with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia, using linkage groups on chromosomes 11p, 13q, and 21q. They excluded large portions of chromosomes 11p and 13q and virtually the entire long arm of chromosome 21.

Ozelius et al. (1989) carried out linkage analysis with polymorphic DNA and protein markers in a large non-Jewish kindred with torsion dystonia. They found that the dystonia gene, which they called ITD1, was tightly linked to the gelsolin gene. Multipoint linkage analysis placed it in the 9q32-q34 region, between ABO and D9S26, a region that also contains the dopamine-beta-hydroxylase locus.

Kramer et al. (1990) performed linkage analysis with DNA polymorphisms in 12 Ashkenazi Jewish families with ITD. They found the dystonia gene to be closely linked to the argininosuccinate synthetase gene (ASS). Multipoint analysis placed it in the 9q32-q34 region, which also contains the gelsolin and dopamine-beta-hydroxylase loci. In a review, Muller and Kupke (1990) noted that linkage analysis in one form of autosomal dominant torsion dystonia had assigned a torsion dystonia locus to the long arm of chromosome 9.

Using GT repeat polymorphisms from the 9q32-q34 region, Kwiatkowski et al. (1991) localized the gene causing idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jewish families to the 11-cM interval between AK1 and D9S10, a region with no known candidate genes. Linkage analysis in a large non-Jewish family was also consistent with localization to 9q32-q34, although positive lod scores in that family extended over an interval of more than 20 cM.

Ozelius et al. (1992) used (GT)n and RFLP markers on 9q32-q34 in Ashkenazi Jewish families with early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Obligate recombination events narrowed the DYT1 region to a 6-cM interval bounded by AK1 and ASS and placed DYT1 centromeric to ASS. Allelic association predicted that DYT1, ABL, and ASS lie within 1 to 2 cM of each other.

Warner et al. (1993) performed linkage analysis in 27 small families with idiopathic torsion dystonia, 26 British and 1 French, 3 of which were Ashkenazi Jewish. They used the highly polymorphic 9q34 loci ASS and ABL. The cumulative lod score for ASS at a recombination fraction of 0.001 was -6.72. HOMOG analysis nevertheless gave evidence for linkage to 9q34 in a subset of families. The authors concluded that a locus at 9q34 exists in both Jewish and non-Jewish kindreds in the UK.

Kramer et al. (1994) studied 7 non-Jewish families of northern European and French Canadian descent with idiopathic torsion dystonia. Their results were consistent with linkage to the DYT1 region on chromosome 9q34.

Ozelius et al. (1997) built a YAC contig covering 600 kb of the 2-cM region between D9S62a and ASS on chromosome 9q34. Adding new polymorphic loci to linkage disequilibrium analysis of the Ashkenazi Jewish founder mutation, they placed the DYT1 gene most likely within a 150-kb region between D9S2161 and D9S63.

### Heterogeneity

Bundey et al. (1975) carried out a family study of 32 patients with torsion dystonia. They identified at least 2 forms of generalized dystonia with onset in childhood, one autosomal dominant and one autosomal recessive, which could not be told apart clinically. At least 3 families, and probably about 6 to 8 patients, had the autosomal recessive form. Most of the other 15 patients, 12 of whom had onset in adult life, appeared to have a nongenetic torsion dystonia. However, the authors observed one example of a benign adult-onset dominant form associated with tremor. They concluded that there are at least 2 genetic forms of torsion dystonia: an autosomal recessive childhood-onset form and one or more dominant forms with onset in childhood or adult life.

In a review, Muller and Kupke (1990) stated that primary torsion dystonia may be inherited as an autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked recessive trait. They also stated that at least 6 forms of autosomal dominant torsion dystonia can be distinguished clinically.

Risch et al. (1990) suggested that the high incidence and dominant inheritance of early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews pointed to genetic homogeneity within this population. They proposed that this would make the population especially useful for linkage studies. Kramer et al. (1990) suggested that the same gene may cause ITD both in the Ashkenazi Jewish families they studied and in a previously reported non-Jewish kindred linked to 9q32-q34. Because penetrance of the dominant allele differed substantially between the 2 groups, they suggested that 2 different mutations may confer susceptibility in the 2 groups.

Warner et al. (1993) found that lod scores in 3 non-Jewish British families excluded a disease locus tightly linked to ASS on 9q34, although these families were clinically indistinguishable from the others. HOMOG analysis showed significant heterogeneity. The authors suggested that idiopathic torsion dystonia is genetically heterogeneous. ⚠︎

Kramer et al. (1994) reported that none of the affected individuals in 7 non-Jewish families linked to DYT1 carried the Ashkenazi Jewish DYT1 haplotype. They concluded that these families probably have different mutations in the DYT1 gene. The 2 French Canadian families shared the same haplotype. Two other non-Jewish families with atypical idiopathic torsion dystonia, meaning later onset and/or cranial or cervical involvement, were not linked to DYT1, which the authors took as evidence that other genes are involved in dystonia.

Gasser et al. (1996) determined haplotypes at 5 marker loci closely linked to DYT1 in 10 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with focal hand dystonia, 8 with musician's cramp and 2 with writer's cramp. The founder haplotype associated with more than 90% of generalized dystonia cases in the Ashkenazi Jewish population could not be constructed from any of the 20 chromosomes, and no common haplotype was seen. The authors concluded that these findings argued against a role for the DYT1 founder mutation in occupational hand dystonia in this population. They further concluded that if DYT1 is involved in these later-onset dystonias, there was no evidence for a common mutation. They suggested that excessive repetitive use, possibly combined with ulnar neuropathy, may trigger some focal dystonias.

In a review, Nemeth (2002) described the dystonias as a heterogeneous group of disorders with a strong inherited basis. The review covered primary dystonias, including DYT1, as well as 'dystonia-plus' syndromes and heredodegenerative disorders.

Kostic et al. (2006) found that 3 members of a Serbian DYT1 family who did not carry the GAG deletion also had dystonia: 2 had multifocal dystonia and 1 had torticollis. The authors suggested that these involuntary movements were caused by some other factor, such as another dystonia gene, or might be psychogenic.

Contarino et al. (2008) described a large 3-generation Dutch family with primary focal dystonia from a small village on a former island. Of the 24 family members examined, 8 were definitely affected and 4 were possibly affected. Affected members had mild cranio-cervical-brachial dystonia. Mean age at onset was 45.5 years (range, 39 to 56 years), the mean BFMDRS motor score was 4.4 (range, 1 to 8), and the mean TWSTRS part I score was 11.3 (range, 8 to 23). No mutations were found in the DYT1 or SGCE genes, and linkage to the DYT6, DYT7, DYT13, and DYT16 loci was excluded, indicating genetic heterogeneity. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Ozelius et al. (1997) identified the DYT1 gene on chromosome 9q34 as the gene responsible for autosomal dominant early-onset torsion dystonia. Almost all cases of early-onset dystonia carried a unique 3-bp deletion, which results in loss of one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of the encoded protein, torsinA. The deletion appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations. TorsinA is a novel ATP-binding protein with homologs in nematode, rat, mouse, and human. It shows some resemblance to the heat-shock protein and Clp protease families.

Kamm et al. (1999) screened 57 German patients with idiopathic torsion dystonia for the 3-bp GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene. They found the deletion in 3 of 5 patients with early limb-onset dystonia, one of whom had a positive family history. They also found it in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short disease course.

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) reported a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia who carried the 3-bp GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene. The authors described the patient's clinical presentation as unique.

In a patient with early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, Leung et al. (2001) found an 18-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene (Phe323_Tyr328del). The deletion was predicted to remove 6 amino acids close to the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site. The authors stated that this was the first TOR1A mutation identified other than the GAG deletion (Glu302/303del), and that it was associated with a distinct type of early-onset dystonia. Screening of TOR1A in 40 patients with early-onset dystonia or early-onset parkinsonism (EOP) found 3 variations, none of them in EOP patients. These included 2 infrequent intronic single-bp changes of unknown consequence, one in a dystonia patient and one in the mother of an EOP patient.

Hjermind et al. (2002) screened 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia for the GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene, after secondary dystonia had been excluded clinically and biochemically. They found the mutation in 3 probands, all of whom had the classic DYT1 phenotype. None of the probands with other types of dystonia carried the deletion. Two of the 3 carriers were apparently sporadic cases. In one, the mutation had arisen de novo; in the other, a parent was an asymptomatic carrier. The authors noted that de novo mutations in the DYT1 gene are seldom reported, although independent founder mutations are known to have occurred.

Using PCR, Grundmann et al. (2003) screened 256 patients with different subtypes of dystonia from 4 movement disorder outpatient clinics in Germany for the DYT1 GAG deletion. They found it in 6 patients.

In a large Serbian family, Kostic et al. (2006) identified 7 carriers of the 3-bp GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene. Six were confirmed by direct analysis and 1 was inferred from the haplotype. Only 2 of the carriers had dystonia, giving a penetrance of 29%.

By sequencing in a patient with early-onset DYT1 dystonia, Zirn et al. (2008) identified a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5. The change was absent from 1,000 control chromosomes, and arg288 is conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. HEK293 cells overexpressing the R288Q mutant showed an enlarged perinuclear space. The same change was seen in cells overexpressing the common delta-GAG mutant but not in cells overexpressing wildtype TOR1A.

In a man with late-onset focal (oromandibular) dystonia, Calakos et al. (2010) sequenced TOR1A and found a novel heterozygous missense mutation in exon 3, c.613T-A, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution. The variant was not present in 800 population controls (1,600 chromosomes). It affects a highly conserved phenylalanine in a beta strand of the AAA domain, and SIFT predicted it to be pathogenic (score 0.03). In BHK cells expressing EGFP-tagged TOR1A, inclusions formed in 44% of cells expressing F205I, 79% of cells expressing deltaE, and 10% of cells expressing wildtype protein. The F205I inclusions were larger than the deltaE inclusions. They colocalized mostly with the ER marker KDEL (96%) rather than with the nuclear envelope marker lamin B1 (19%), whereas deltaE inclusions colocalized with lamin B1 (89%). The authors concluded that F205I impairs TOR1A function, probably through a pathophysiology that differs from that of deltaE, and that the variant may contribute to the expression of dystonia. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bressman et al. (1994) found that among Ashkenazi Jewish dystonia patients who did not carry the DYT1-associated haplotype, onset was in the neck, larynx, or other cranial muscles in 79% and in the arm in 21%; onset never occurred in the leg. Limb onset, leg involvement during the disease course, and age at onset distinguished haplotype carriers from noncarriers with 90% accuracy.

Kamm et al. (1999) did not find the GAG deletion in 2 German patients with early-onset generalized dystonia that began in the cervical muscles. Nor did they find it in 5 other patients with multifocal dystonia, 14 with segmental dystonia, or 30 with focal cervical dystonia. They suggested that in their population the deletion accounts for a major portion of typical early limb-onset dystonia but not for other types of dystonia.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found that only 2 of 6 German GAG deletion carriers had classic early-onset primary generalized dystonia. Two had multifocal dystonia, 1 of them with involvement of cranial and cervical muscles, and 2 had writer's cramp of both hands with only slight progression. The authors concluded that the mutation may be associated with segmental and multifocal as well as generalized dystonia. They noted that this phenotypic variability may make it difficult to predict carrier status and to give genetic counseling.

Heiman et al. (2004) found that the severity of motor signs did not predict the likelihood of recurrent depression in DYT1 mutation carriers. Carriers did not have a higher risk of other affective disorders, such as single major depression or bipolar disorder. The authors concluded that early-onset recurrent major depression is associated with the DYT1 GAG mutation independently of motor symptoms, and suggested that it is a clinical expression of the mutation.

Risch et al. (2007) genotyped 3 DYT1 SNPs, including the coding variant D216H, in GAG-deletion carriers with dystonia (n = 119), GAG-deletion carriers without dystonia (n = 113), and controls (n = 197). Compared with controls, the 216H allele was more frequent in nonmanifesting carriers and less frequent in carriers with dystonia. Haplotype analysis showed that the H allele in trans with the GAG deletion was highly protective. There was also suggestive evidence that the D216 allele in cis is required for the disease to be penetrant. The authors concluded that this was the first clinically relevant gene modifier of DYT1. Kamm et al. (2008) reported that the D216H polymorphism modified susceptibility to DYT1 dystonia in European patients.

Calakos et al. (2010) suggested that the cellular differences between the F205I and deltaE TOR1A mutants may reflect the milder clinical phenotype of their patient with F205I, who had late-onset focal dystonia, compared with the early-onset generalized dystonia caused by deltaGAG.

### Pathogenesis

Hornykiewicz et al. (1986) concluded that some of the brain monoamine changes they found in 2 patients with dystonia musculorum deformans may represent a basic neurochemical abnormality in the disorder. They pointed in particular to the pronounced apparent disturbance of noradrenergic brain mechanisms, which they suggested may be relevant to pathophysiology and treatment.

Ozelius et al. (1997) noted that the abnormal involuntary movements of torsion dystonia are believed to be caused by unbalanced neural transmission in the basal ganglia.

Ghilardi et al. (2003) used H2(15)O PET during task performance in nonmanifesting DYT1 carriers. During motor execution, carriers showed increased activation in the left premotor cortex and right supplementary motor area and reduced activation in the posterior medial cerebellum. During sequence learning, activation was increased in the left ventral prefrontal cortex and lateral cerebellum. The authors suggested that localized increases in neural activity may allow normal movement execution in carriers but may not compensate for their defect in sequence learning.

Carbon et al. (2004) performed FDG PET in 12 nonmanifesting and 11 manifesting DYT1 gene carriers, 6 nonmanifesting and 7 manifesting DYT6 gene carriers, and 11 controls. Compared with nonmanifesting carriers, manifesting carriers of both genotypes showed bilateral hypermetabolism in the presupplementary motor area (Brodmann area 6) and in parietal association cortices (BA 40/7). DYT1 carriers showed genotype-specific increases in metabolism in the putamen, anterior cingulate (BA 24/32), and cerebellar hemispheres. In contrast, DYT6 carriers showed hypometabolism of the putamen and hypermetabolism in the temporal cortex (BA 21). The authors suggested that clinical manifestations are related to cortical dysfunction, whereas subcortical metabolic abnormalities may be genotype-specific trait features. They also suggested that dystonia may be associated with abnormal movement preparation caused by defective sensorimotor integration.

Using diffusion tensor MRI, Carbon et al. (2004) found reduced fractional anisotropy (p less than 0.005), a measure of axonal integrity and coherence, in the subgyral white matter of the sensorimotor cortex of DYT1 mutation carriers compared with controls. The authors suggested that abnormal anatomic connectivity of the supplementary motor area may contribute to the susceptibility of DYT1 carriers to develop clinical manifestations of dystonia.

McNaught et al. (2004) described perinuclear inclusion bodies in the midbrain reticular formation and periaqueductal gray of 4 clinically documented and genetically confirmed DYT1 patients but not in controls. The inclusions lay within cholinergic and other neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus, cuneiform nucleus, and griseum centrale mesencephali, and they stained for ubiquitin, torsinA, and the nuclear envelope protein lamin A/C. No inclusion bodies were detected in the substantia nigra pars compacta, striatum, hippocampus, or selected regions of the cerebral cortex. Tau/ubiquitin-immunoreactive aggregates were found in pigmented neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus ceruleus in all 4 cases but not in controls. The authors concluded that the findings supported an association of DYT1 dystonia with impaired protein handling and the nuclear envelope. They suggested that alterations in the pedunculopontine and cuneiform nuclei and related brainstem structures, which mediate motor activity and muscle tone, could underlie the pathophysiology of DYT1 dystonia.

Using PET with [11C]-raclopride, Asanuma et al. (2005) found that striatal D2 receptor binding was reduced by about 15% in the caudate and putamen of nonmanifesting DYT1 mutation carriers (p less than 0.005). They suggested that reduced striatal D2 binding is a trait feature of the DYT1 genotype.

Carbon et al. (2009) used [11C]raclopride PET to study manifesting and nonmanifesting carriers of the DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. Compared with healthy controls, caudate and putamen D2 receptor availability was significantly reduced in both groups of carriers. The reduction was greater in DYT6 than in DYT1 carriers (-38.0% vs -15.0%) and did not differ significantly between manifesting and nonmanifesting carriers of either genotype. Voxel-based analysis showed reduced raclopride binding in the ventrolateral thalamus of both DYT1 and DYT6 carriers. This reduction was more pronounced in DYT6 carriers and was not influenced by clinical manifestation. The authors concluded that reduced D2 receptor availability is compatible with dysfunction or loss of D2-bearing neurons, increased synaptic dopamine, or both. They considered it likely to be a susceptibility factor for clinical manifestations in mutation carriers.

### Diagnosis

Batshaw et al. (1985) reported that Munchausen syndrome can simulate torsion dystonia, which makes it a consideration in the differential diagnosis. ⚠︎

Ozelius et al. (1992) proposed that extended ABL-ASS haplotype status could predict DYT1 carrier status in Jewish individuals and could be used for molecular diagnosis.

Bressman et al. (2002) evaluated the hierarchical diagnostic categories of definite, probable, and possible dystonia used in family studies. They assessed 147 DYT1 GAG deletion carriers and 113 blood-related noncarriers from 43 families. Only the category of definite dystonia was 100% specific. Probable dystonia, but not possible dystonia, was more frequent in carriers than in noncarriers. The authors recommended that only individuals with definite signs of dystonia be considered affected in linkage and other genetic studies. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Zilber et al. (1984) analyzed data from a nationwide survey of ITD in Israel. They identified 47 patients between 1969 and 1980, 40 of them of European origin. Among European Jews, the frequency of ITD was about 1 in 23,000 live births, about 5 times higher than in Jews of Afro-Asian origin.

Ozelius et al. (1989) stated that the frequency of the defective torsion dystonia gene in the Ashkenazi Jewish population is about 1 in 10,000. Assuming a disease frequency of 1 in 15,000 among Ashkenazi Jews, Bressman et al. (1989) estimated the gene frequency at 1 in 9,000. Kramer et al. (1990) noted that ITD is 5 to 10 times more frequent in the Ashkenazi Jewish population than in other groups.

Ozelius et al. (1992) found highly significant linkage disequilibrium between DYT1 and an extended ABL-ASS haplotype in 52 unrelated affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals. The 4/A12 haplotype was present on 69% of disease-bearing chromosomes, compared with 1% of control Jewish chromosomes (chi-square = 91.07). The authors concluded that a single mutation event is responsible for most hereditary cases of dystonia in the Jewish population.

Warner et al. (1993) found in British Jewish kindreds the same allelic association between ASS/ABL and idiopathic torsion dystonia that had been reported in Ashkenazi Jewish families in the USA.

Bressman et al. (1994) noted that the linkage disequilibrium between DYT1 and a particular 9q34 haplotype in Ashkenazi Jews implies that a large proportion of early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia in this population is due to a founder mutation. Kramer et al. (1994) noted that the strong linkage disequilibrium in this population lies within a 2-cM region, and that the associated haplotype is present in more than 90% of early limb-onset Ashkenazi Jewish cases.

Zoossmann-Diskin (1995) discussed whether genetic drift or selection accounts for idiopathic torsion dystonia in the Ashkenazi Jewish population.

Risch et al. (1995) analyzed 6 closely linked microsatellite loci on chromosome 9q34 in 59 Ashkenazi Jewish families with idiopathic torsion dystonia. They concluded that a single founder mutation accounts for more than 90% of early-onset cases in this population and estimated that it first appeared about 350 years ago. Carriers of the founder mutation tended to come from the northern part of the historic Jewish Pale of Settlement (Lithuania and Byelorussia). The authors estimated the current frequency of the mutation at between 1/6,000 and 1/2,000. They suggested that its recent origin and high frequency indicate that the Ashkenazi population descends from a limited group of founders, and they emphasized the role of genetic drift. ⚠︎

Ozelius et al. (1997) concluded that the recurrent 3-bp DYT1 deletion appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations.

Valente et al. (1999) analyzed haplotypes around the DYT1 gene in patients with the GAG deletion. All 9 British Ashkenazi Jewish patients carried the same haplotype as North American Ashkenazi Jewish patients, which the authors took as support for the theory that the British and North American Ashkenazi Jewish communities descend from the same small group of individuals. Among 15 non-Jewish British patients, they found only a limited number of distinct founder haplotypes. They concluded that the GAG deletion is not a very frequent mutation and has arisen only a limited number of times over the centuries.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found DYT1 dystonia to be uncommon among Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia (3 of 107), even though several probands had early-onset generalized dystonia.

Frederic et al. (2008) carried out a nationwide census of French TOR1A mutation carriers, studying 53 index cases and 104 relatives. They estimated the frequency of early-onset torsion dystonia due to TOR1A in France at 0.13 per 100,000 and the mutation frequency at 0.17 per 100,000, and they concluded that the disease is rare in France. On haplotype analysis, 11 of the 53 families carried the previously reported Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype. The remaining families carried distinct haplotypes, which the authors suggested reflected independent mutation events.

### Animal Model

Lorden et al. (1984) described dystonic (dt), an autosomal recessive neurologic disorder in Sprague-Dawley rats characterized by sustained twisting movements during waking. Before postnatal days 9 to 10, mutants were indistinguishable from normal littermates. Signs beginning on day 10 included twisting of the axial musculature, hyperflexion of the trunk, self-clasping of the forelimbs and hindlimbs, and poor limb placement during locomotion. Routine light microscopy showed no morphologic lesions of neural or nonneural tissues. Cerebellar norepinephrine levels were significantly elevated, whereas levels in other locus ceruleus terminal fields were normal, and histochemical fluorescence showed no difference in cerebellar noradrenergic innervation. The mutants were less sensitive to the akinesic effects of haloperidol, although striatal dopamine levels were not reliably different and apomorphine responses appeared normal. The authors suggested that the dt rat has biochemical disturbances in the extrapyramidal system and proposed it as a model for human dystonia musculorum deformans.

Shashidharan et al. (2005) generated 4 independent lines of transgenic mice overexpressing human deltaE-torsinA under a neuron-specific enolase promoter. As early as 3 weeks after birth, the mice developed abnormal involuntary movements with dystonic-appearing self-clasping of the limbs, as well as hyperkinesia and rapid bidirectional circling. About 40% of the transgenic mice in each line showed these severe behavioral abnormalities. Striatal dopamine was decreased in behaviorally affected animals but increased in transgenic mice without behavioral changes. Immunohistochemistry showed perinuclear inclusions and aggregates that stained for ubiquitin, torsinA, and the nuclear envelope marker lamin. The inclusions were in neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus and other brainstem regions, in a pattern similar to that described in DYT1 patients.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Ozelius et al. (1997) found that almost all cases of early-onset torsion dystonia carried a unique 3-bp deletion in the DYT1 gene. The deletion results in loss of one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of torsinA. It appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations.

Kamm et al. (1999) found the 3-bp GAG deletion in 4 of 57 German patients with idiopathic torsion dystonia: 3 of 5 patients with early limb-onset dystonia and 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short disease course.

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) reported a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia and a unique clinical presentation who carried the GAG deletion.

Valente et al. (1999) found that 9 British Ashkenazi Jewish carriers of the GAG deletion shared the haplotype seen in North American Ashkenazi Jewish patients. In 15 non-Jewish British carriers, they found only a limited number of distinct founder haplotypes.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found the GAG deletion in 3 of 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia, all of whom had the classic DYT1 phenotype. In 1 apparently sporadic case the mutation had arisen de novo; in another, a parent was an asymptomatic carrier.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found the GAG deletion in 6 of 256 German patients with dystonia. Only 2 of them had classic early-onset generalized dystonia; 2 had multifocal dystonia and 2 had writer's cramp of both hands.

In a large Serbian family, Kostic et al. (2006) identified 7 carriers of the GAG deletion. Only 2 of them had dystonia, giving a penetrance of 29%.

Frederic et al. (2008) found the recurrent TOR1A mutation in 53 French families. Eleven of the families carried the Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype, and the others carried distinct haplotypes suggesting independent mutation events.

**.0002 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 18-BP DEL [p.F323]

In a patient with early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, Leung et al. (2001) found an 18-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene (Phe323_Tyr328del). The deletion was predicted to remove 6 amino acids close to the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site.

**.0003 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, MODIFIER OF**  
TOR1A, ASP216HIS [p.D216H]

Risch et al. (2007) found that the 216H allele of the TOR1A coding polymorphism D216H was more frequent than in controls among GAG-deletion carriers without dystonia (n = 113) and less frequent among GAG-deletion carriers with dystonia (n = 119). The H allele in trans with the GAG deletion was highly protective, and there was suggestive evidence that the D216 allele in cis is required for penetrance.

Kamm et al. (2008) reported that the D216H polymorphism modified susceptibility to DYT1 dystonia in European patients.

**.0004 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, ARG288GLN [c.863G>A; p.R288Q]

In a patient with early-onset DYT1 dystonia, Zirn et al. (2008) identified a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5. The change was absent from 1,000 control chromosomes, and arg288 is conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. HEK293 cells overexpressing the mutant showed an enlarged perinuclear space, the same change seen with the delta-GAG mutant but not with wildtype TOR1A.

**.0005 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, PHE205ILE [c.613T>A; p.F205I]

In a man in his fifth decade of life with late-onset focal (oromandibular) dystonia, Calakos et al. (2010) identified a novel heterozygous c.613T-A mutation in exon 3 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution at a highly conserved residue in a beta strand of the AAA domain. The variant was not found in 1,600 control chromosomes. In BHK cells, the F205I mutant formed inclusions in 44% of cells, compared with 79% for deltaE and 10% for wildtype. These inclusions were larger than deltaE inclusions and colocalized mostly with the ER marker KDEL rather than with the nuclear envelope marker lamin B1. The authors suggested that F205I impairs TOR1A function through a mechanism different from that of deltaE, which may reflect the milder clinical phenotype. ⚠︎
