### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant torsion dystonia-1 (DYT1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOR1A gene (also called DYT1), which encodes torsinA, on chromosome 9q34. ⚠︎

### Description

Idiopathic torsion dystonia (ITD) is a movement disorder characterized by sustained muscle contractions that appear as twisting movements of the limbs, trunk, and/or neck and can progress to abnormal postures (Kramer et al., 1990). The DYT1 form typically begins in childhood (Ozelius et al., 9288096 (1997)). In carriers of the Ashkenazi Jewish founder mutation, symptoms usually begin in a limb (Bressman et al., 1994). Inheritance is autosomal dominant with reduced penetrance, estimated at 30 to 40% (Ozelius et al., 9149944 (1997)).

### Nomenclature

Ozelius et al. (1989) referred to the dystonia gene they mapped to chromosome 9q32-q34 as ITD1. Later reports used the symbol DYT1 (Kramer et al., 1990), and Leung et al. (2001) referred to the gene as TOR1A (DYT1). Müller et al. (1998) proposed that the known forms of primary dystonia, most of which could be distinguished by genetic criteria, be referred to as dystonias 1 through 12.

### Clinical Features

Bundey et al. (1975) carried out a family study of 32 patients with torsion dystonia. They concluded that there were at least 2 forms of generalized dystonia with childhood onset, an autosomal dominant form and an autosomal recessive form, and that the 2 forms could not be told apart clinically. Because of a probable paternal age effect, they thought that 9 to 11 patients with generalized childhood dystonia represented new dominant mutations. Fitness with childhood onset was 1/20 of normal. Most of the other 15 patients, 12 of whom had onset in adult life, appeared to have nongenetic torsion dystonia.

Bressman et al. (1994) used 9q34 markers (D9S62, D9S63, and ASS) to classify 174 Ashkenazi Jewish individuals with idiopathic torsion dystonia as carriers, noncarriers, or ambiguous with respect to a DYT1-associated haplotype. There were 90 carriers, 70 noncarriers, and 14 ambiguous individuals. Mean age at onset was 12.5 +/- 8.2 years in carriers and 36.5 +/- 16.4 years in noncarriers. In 94% of carriers, symptoms began in a limb, with arm and leg equally affected; onset was rarely in the neck (3.3%) or larynx (2.2%). In noncarriers, the neck, larynx, and other cranial muscles were the site of onset in 79%, the arms in 21%, and the legs never. Limb onset, leg involvement during the course of the disease, and age at onset distinguished carriers from noncarriers with 90% accuracy.

Kramer et al. (1994) studied 7 non-Jewish families with ITD of northern European and French Canadian descent. Affected members were clinically similar to Ashkenazi Jewish cases: onset was mainly in the limbs, and at least 1 person in each pedigree had onset before age 12 years.

Grundmann et al. (2003) tested 256 unselected German patients with dystonia and found the GAG deletion in 6. Only 2 of the 6 carriers had classic early-onset primary generalized dystonia. Two had multifocal dystonia, 1 of them with involvement of cranial and cervical muscles, and 2 had writer's cramp of both hands with only slight progression.

Heiman et al. (2004) gave a standardized psychiatric interview to members of families with a DYT1 mutation: 96 manifesting carriers, 60 nonmanifesting carriers, and 65 noncarriers. Compared with noncarriers, the risk of recurrent major depressive disorder was increased in nonmanifesting carriers (RR = 4.95) and in manifesting carriers (RR = 3.62). Carriers also had an earlier age at onset of recurrent major depression. The severity of motor signs was not associated with the likelihood of recurrent depression, and carriers did not have an increased risk of single major depression or bipolar disorder. The authors suggested that early-onset recurrent depression is a clinical expression of the DYT1 mutation.

Kostic et al. (2006) described a large Serbian family in which the DYT1 phenotype varied widely within the family, from asymptomatic carrier status to late-onset focal dystonia and generalized jerky dystonia. Of 7 mutation carriers (6 shown by direct analysis and 1 by inferred haplotype), only 2 had dystonia, a penetrance of 29%.

### Other Features

Ghilardi et al. (2003) compared 12 clinically unaffected DYT1 carriers with 12 age-matched controls. Motor performance did not differ between the groups, but sequence learning was reduced in carriers (p less than 0.01). H2(15)O PET during motor execution showed increased activation in carriers in the left premotor cortex and right supplementary motor area and reduced activation in the posterior medial cerebellum.

Carbon et al. (2004) used FDG PET to scan 12 nonmanifesting and 11 manifesting DYT1 carriers, 6 nonmanifesting and 7 manifesting DYT6 carriers, and 11 controls. Manifesting carriers of both genotypes showed bilateral hypermetabolism in the presupplementary motor area and parietal association cortices compared with nonmanifesting carriers. DYT1 carriers had genotype-specific increases in metabolism in the putamen, anterior cingulate, and cerebellar hemispheres.

Using diffusion tensor MRI, Carbon et al. (2004) found reduced fractional anisotropy (p less than 0.005) in the subgyral white matter of the sensorimotor cortex of DYT1 carriers.

Using [11C]raclopride PET, Asanuma et al. (2005) found that striatal D2 receptor binding was reduced by about 15% in the caudate and putamen of nonmanifesting DYT1 carriers. They suggested that reduced striatal D2 binding is a trait feature of the DYT1 genotype.

Carbon et al. (2009) found reduced caudate and putamen D2 receptor availability in both DYT1 and DYT6 mutation carriers compared with controls. The reduction was greater in DYT6 than in DYT1 carriers (-38.0 +/- 3.0% vs -15.0 +/- 3.0%), and there was no significant difference between manifesting and nonmanifesting carriers of either genotype. Voxel-based analysis also showed reduced raclopride binding in the ventrolateral thalamus.

### Biochemical Features

Wooten et al. (1973) reported elevated plasma dopamine-beta-hydroxylase (DBH) activity in autosomal dominant torsion dystonia.

Askenasy et al. (1980) studied 13 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with torsion dystonia of various causes and their relatives. Serum DBH activity was significantly higher in patients and their relatives than in controls, especially in the childhood-onset group. However, none of the criteria tested (Jewish origin, dominant trait, slow progression, axial topography) was specific for elevated DBH activity.

### Inheritance

Zilber et al. (1984) analyzed a nationwide Israeli survey of 47 patients with ITD collected between 1969 and 1980, 40 of whom were of European origin. Neither an X-linked model nor a simple autosomal recessive model fit the data well. An autosomal dominant model with low penetrance could account for the observations, although multifactorial inheritance was also possible. The fathers of isolated cases were older, which suggested that some isolated cases might be new mutations.

Bressman et al. (1989) studied 43 Ashkenazi Jewish probands with onset before 28 years of age, 146 of 162 living first-degree relatives, and 96 of 239 living second-degree relatives. Counting definite cases only, the age-adjusted risk was 15.5% for first-degree relatives and 6.5% for second-degree relatives. These risks were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with a penetrance of 29.4% (definite cases only) or 32.2% (definite and probable cases).

Risch et al. (1990) performed segregation analysis in a family study of 43 Ashkenazi Jewish probands from 42 nuclear families, with age at onset before 27 years. The data were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with 30% penetrance, and recessive and polygenic inheritance were strongly rejected. They found no evidence for sporadic cases or new mutations.

Ozelius et al. (1992) found that most affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals were heterozygous for the DYT1-associated ABL-ASS haplotype, which supported autosomal dominant inheritance. Sporadic cases carried the haplotype in a proportion similar to familial cases (8 of 13 vs 28 of 40). The authors concluded that many sporadic cases are hereditary and that penetrance is lower than the previously estimated 30%.

### Mapping

In a family with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia, Breakefield et al. (1986) used linkage analysis to exclude the pro-opiomelanocortin and glutamic acid decarboxylase genes, as well as other genomic regions. Using multipoint linkage analysis in a single large non-Jewish pedigree, Kramer et al. (1987) excluded large parts of chromosomes 11p and 13q and almost the entire long arm of chromosome 21.

By linkage analysis in a large non-Jewish kinship, Ozelius et al. (1989) mapped a dystonia gene (ITD1) to chromosome 9q32-q34. The gene showed tight linkage with the gelsolin gene and, by multipoint analysis, lay between ABO and D9S26.

In 12 Ashkenazi Jewish families, Kramer et al. (1990) showed close linkage of ITD to the argininosuccinate synthetase (ASS) gene in the 9q32-34 region. Because penetrance differed substantially between the Jewish families and the non-Jewish kindred, they suggested that 2 different mutations might be operating in the 2 groups.

Using GT repeat polymorphisms, Kwiatkowski et al. (1991) localized the gene causing dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews to the 11-cM interval between AK1 and D9S10. In the non-Jewish family, positive lod scores extended over more than 20 cM.

From obligate recombination events in Jewish families, Ozelius et al. (1992) narrowed the DYT1 region to a 6-cM interval bounded by AK1 and ASS. In 52 unrelated affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals, they found highly significant linkage disequilibrium between DYT1 and an extended ABL-ASS haplotype. The 4/A12 haplotype was present on 69% of disease-bearing chromosomes and on 1% of control Jewish chromosomes. DYT1 was centromeric to ASS.

Warner et al. (1993) performed linkage analysis with ASS and ABL in 27 small families with ITD (26 British, 1 French), 3 of which were Ashkenazi Jewish. The cumulative lod score at ASS was -6.72. HOMOG analysis showed significant heterogeneity but supported linkage to 9q34 in a subset of families. The allelic association seen in Ashkenazi families in the USA was also present in British Jewish kindreds.

Kramer et al. (1994) found that linkage in 7 non-Jewish ITD families of northern European and French Canadian descent was consistent with the DYT1 region. None of these families carried the Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype, suggesting different mutations in the DYT1 gene, although the 2 French Canadian families shared a haplotype. Penetrance estimates in the non-Jewish families ranged from 0.40 to 0.75, and a direct estimate adjusted for age at onset was 0.46.

Ozelius et al. (9149944, 1997) built a YAC contig spanning 600 kb of the DYT1 region. Using linkage disequilibrium analysis of the Ashkenazi Jewish founder mutation, they placed the DYT1 gene most likely within a 150-kb region between D9S2161 and D9S63.

### Heterogeneity

Warner et al. (1993) found 3 non-Jewish British families, clinically indistinguishable from the others, in which a disease locus tightly linked to ASS was excluded, and concluded that ITD is genetically heterogeneous. Kramer et al. (1994) found that 2 non-Jewish families with atypical ITD (later onset and/or cranial or cervical involvement) were not linked to DYT1. ⚠︎

In 10 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with focal hand dystonia (8 with musician's cramp, 2 with writer's cramp), Gasser et al. (1996) could not reconstruct the founder haplotype from any of the 20 chromosomes. They concluded that the DYT1 founder mutation does not have a role in occupational hand dystonia in this population. ⚠︎

In a large Serbian DYT1 family, Kostic et al. (2006) found that 3 relatives without the GAG deletion also had dystonia (2 with multifocal dystonia and 1 with torticollis). They suggested that these cases were due to other factors, such as another dystonia gene, or were psychogenic.

Contarino et al. (2008) described a large Dutch family with mild craniocervical-brachial focal dystonia and a mean age at onset of 45.5 years. No mutations were found in the DYT1 or SGCE genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Ozelius et al. (9288096, 1997) identified the DYT1 gene on chromosome 9q34 as responsible for early-onset torsion dystonia. Almost all cases of early-onset dystonia had a unique 3-bp deletion that appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations. The deletion removes 1 of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of a novel ATP-binding protein, termed torsinA, which has homologs in nematode, rat, mouse, and human and some resemblance to the heat-shock protein and Clp protease families.

Kamm et al. (1999) tested 57 German patients with ITD for the GAG deletion. The mutation was present in 3 of 5 patients with early limb-onset ITD, 1 of whom had a positive family history, and in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short disease course. It was absent from 2 patients with early-onset generalized dystonia beginning in the cervical muscles and from patients with multifocal (5), segmental (14), and focal cervical (30) dystonia.

Valente et al. (1999) analyzed haplotypes around DYT1 in 9 Ashkenazi Jewish and 15 non-Jewish British patients with the GAG deletion. All the British Ashkenazi Jewish patients carried the same haplotype as North American Jews, and only a limited number of distinct founder mutations was seen in the non-Jewish British patients.

Leung et al. (2001) screened the TOR1A gene in 40 patients with early-onset dystonia or early-onset parkinsonism. In a patient with early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, they found an 18-bp deletion (Phe323_Tyr328del) that removes 6 amino acids near the C terminus, including a putative phosphorylation site of torsinA. This was the first TOR1A mutation reported other than the GAG deletion (Glu302/303del).

Hjermind et al. (2002) tested 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia and found the GAG deletion in 3, all with the classic DYT1 phenotype. Only 1 had a family history of dystonia. Of the 2 sporadic probands, 1 had a de novo mutation and the other had a parent who was an asymptomatic carrier.

Zirn et al. (2008) sequenced exon 5 of TOR1A in a patient with DYT1 and found a c.863G-A transition resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5. The change was absent from 1,000 control chromosomes, and arg288 is conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. Overexpression of the R288Q mutant in HEK293 cells caused enlargement of the perinuclear space, as did the common GAG deletion, but wildtype TOR1A did not.

Calakos et al. (2010) sequenced the TOR1A exons in a man with late-onset focal (oromandibular) dystonia and found a c.613T-A transversion in exon 3, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution in the AAA domain. The change was not found in 800 population controls (1,600 chromosomes). In transfected BHK cells, the F205I mutant produced inclusions in 44% of cells, compared with 10% for wildtype and 79% for the deltaE mutant. Unlike the deltaE inclusions, which colocalized with the nuclear envelope marker lamin B1, the F205I inclusions colocalized mainly with the endoplasmic reticulum marker KDEL. The authors suggested that this variant may contribute to late-onset focal dystonia.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Risch et al. (2007) studied 3 DYT1 SNPs, including the coding variant D216H, in GAG-deletion carriers with dystonia (n = 119), carriers without dystonia (n = 113), and controls (n = 197). Compared with controls, the 216H allele was more frequent in carriers without dystonia and less frequent in carriers with dystonia. Haplotype analysis showed a highly protective effect of the H allele in trans with the GAG deletion, and there was suggestive evidence that the D216 allele in cis is needed for the disease to be penetrant. Kamm et al. (2008) reported that the D216H polymorphism modifies susceptibility to DYT1 dystonia in European patients.

### Pathogenesis

Hornykiewicz et al. (1986) examined the brains of 2 patients with generalized childhood-onset dystonia musculorum deformans and found no important histologic changes. Norepinephrine was markedly and consistently decreased in the lateral and posterior hypothalamus, mamillary body, subthalamic nucleus, and locus ceruleus. Serotonin was subnormal in the dorsal raphe nucleus, and dopamine was low in the nucleus accumbens and, in 1 case, the striatum. The authors suggested that a disturbance of noradrenergic brain mechanisms may be a basic neurochemical abnormality in the disorder. ⚠︎

In 4 clinically documented, genetically confirmed DYT1 patients, McNaught et al. (2004) found perinuclear inclusion bodies in the midbrain reticular formation and periaqueductal gray that were not seen in controls. The inclusions were in cholinergic and other neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus, cuneiform nucleus, and griseum centrale mesencephali, and they stained for ubiquitin, torsinA, and lamin A/C. All 4 cases also had tau/ubiquitin-immunoreactive aggregates in pigmented neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus coeruleus.

### Diagnosis

Ozelius et al. (1992) suggested that ABL-ASS extended-haplotype status could predict carrier status in Jewish individuals. Bressman et al. (2002) assessed 147 DYT1 GAG-deletion carriers and 113 blood-related noncarriers from 43 families. Only the category of definite dystonia was 100% specific, and the authors recommended that only people with definite signs of dystonia be considered affected in genetic studies.

Batshaw et al. (1985) reported Munchausen syndrome simulating torsion dystonia.

### Population Genetics

Zilber et al. (1984) estimated an ITD frequency of about 1 in 23,000 live births among European Jews in Israel, 5-fold higher than in Jews of Afro-Asian origin. Bressman et al. (1989), assuming a disease frequency of 1/15,000, estimated the gene frequency among Ashkenazi Jews at 1/9,000.

Using 6 closely linked 9q34 microsatellite loci in 59 Ashkenazi Jewish families, Risch et al. (1995) found that more than 90% of early-onset ITD cases in Ashkenazi Jews are due to a single founder mutation, which they estimated first appeared about 350 years ago. Carriers came preferentially from the northern part of the historic Jewish Pale of Settlement (Lithuania and Byelorussia). The current mutation frequency was between 1/6,000 and 1/2,000.

In a nationwide French study of 53 index cases and 104 relatives with the TOR1A mutation, Frederic et al. (2008) found the Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype in 11 families. The other families carried distinct haplotypes, suggesting independent mutation events. They estimated the disease frequency in France at 0.13:100,000 and the mutation frequency at 0.17:100,000. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Lorden et al. (1984) described the dystonic (dt) rat, an autosomal recessive mutant of Sprague-Dawley rats. Sustained twisting movements begin on postnatal day 10. The mutants had no morphologic lesions but had significantly raised cerebellar norepinephrine levels and were less sensitive to the akinesic effects of haloperidol.

Shashidharan et al. (2005) generated 4 transgenic mouse lines overexpressing human deltaE-torsinA from a neuron-specific enolase promoter. As early as 3 weeks after birth, the mice developed dystonic-appearing self-clasping of the limbs, hyperkinesia, and rapid bidirectional circling, with about 40% of mice in each line severely affected. Affected mice had decreased striatal dopamine, whereas unaffected transgenic mice had increased levels. Perinuclear inclusions positive for ubiquitin, torsinA, and lamin were found in the pedunculopontine nucleus and other brainstem regions, a pattern similar to that described in DYT1 patients. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG [GAG deletion; Glu302/303del; deltaE(302/303)]

Ozelius et al. (9288096, 1997) found that almost all cases of early-onset torsion dystonia had a unique 3-bp deletion in the DYT1 gene. The deletion removes 1 of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of torsinA and appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found the GAG deletion in 3 of 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia. In 1 sporadic proband the mutation was de novo; another sporadic proband had a parent who was an asymptomatic carrier.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found the GAG deletion in 6 of 256 German patients with dystonia. Phenotypes included classic early-onset generalized dystonia (2 patients), multifocal dystonia (2), and writer's cramp of both hands (2).

**.0002 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 18-BP DEL [Phe323_Tyr328del]

In a patient with early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, Leung et al. (2001) found an 18-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene (Phe323_Tyr328del). The deletion removes 6 amino acids near the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site.

**.0003 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, ARG288GLN [c.863G>A; p.Arg288Gln]

In a patient with DYT1, Zirn et al. (2008) found a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution. The change was absent from 1,000 control chromosomes and affects a residue conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. HEK293 cells overexpressing the mutant showed an enlarged perinuclear space, as did cells expressing the GAG deletion.

**.0004 DYSTONIA, LATE-ONSET FOCAL (POSSIBLE CONTRIBUTION)**  
TOR1A, PHE205ILE [c.613T>A; p.F205I]

In a man with late-onset oromandibular dystonia, Calakos et al. (2010) found a c.613T-A transversion in exon 3 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution in the AAA domain. The variant was absent from 800 population controls. In BHK cells, the F205I mutant produced inclusions more often than wildtype TOR1A (44% vs 10%). The inclusions colocalized mainly with an endoplasmic reticulum marker rather than the nuclear envelope. The authors suggested that the variant may contribute to dystonia.
