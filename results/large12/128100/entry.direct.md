### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant torsion dystonia-1 (DYT1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOR1A gene, which encodes torsinA, on chromosome 9q34. ⚠︎

### Description

Idiopathic torsion dystonia is a neurologic disorder characterized by sustained muscle contractions that appear as twisting movements of the limbs, trunk, and/or neck, which can progress to abnormal postures (Kramer et al., 1990; Kwiatkowski et al., 1991). The DYT1 form begins early, usually in childhood, and typically starts in a limb (Bressman et al., 1994; Ozelius et al., 1997). It is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner with reduced penetrance of 30 to 40% (Ozelius et al., 1997), and it is particularly frequent among Ashkenazi Jews (Kramer et al., 1990). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Ozelius et al. (1989) designated the dystonia gene mapped to chromosome 9q32-q34 ITD1. Kramer et al. (1990) called it DYT1. Muller et al. (1998) reviewed the primary dystonias and proposed numbering the known forms, most of which can be told apart by genetic criteria, as dystonias 1 through 12. In that scheme, idiopathic torsion dystonia mapping to 9q34 was DYT1.

### Clinical Features

Zeman et al. (1959, 1960), Johnson et al. (1962), Zeman and Dyken (1967), and Eldridge (1970) published early clinical and genetic studies of idiopathic dystonia musculorum deformans. Zeman et al. (1960) dealt specifically with formes frustes of the disorder. Yanagisawa et al. (1972) described familial dystonia musculorum deformans with tremor. Marsden et al. (1976) described the natural history of idiopathic torsion dystonia.

Bundey et al. (1975) studied the families of 32 patients with torsion dystonia. They identified at least 2 forms of generalized dystonia that began in childhood, an autosomal dominant form and an autosomal recessive form, which could not be distinguished clinically. Most of the other 15 patients, 12 of whom had adult onset, appeared to have a nongenetic torsion dystonia. However, the authors observed one example of a benign, adult-onset dominant form associated with tremor.

Bressman et al. (1994) studied 174 Ashkenazi Jewish individuals with idiopathic torsion dystonia. Using chromosome 9q34 markers, they classified 90 as carriers of the DYT1-associated haplotype, 70 as noncarriers, and 14 as ambiguous. Mean age at onset was 12.5 +/- 8.2 years in carriers and 36.5 +/- 16.4 years in noncarriers. In 94% of carriers, symptoms began in a limb, with arm and leg affected equally often. Onset was rarely in the neck (3.3%) or larynx (2.2%). In contrast, 79% of noncarriers had onset in the neck, larynx, or other cranial muscles, 21% had onset in the arms, and none had onset in the legs. Limb onset, leg involvement during the course of the disease, and age at onset together distinguished carriers from noncarriers with 90% accuracy.

Kramer et al. (1994) studied 7 non-Jewish families with idiopathic torsion dystonia of northern European and French Canadian descent. Affected members were clinically similar to Ashkenazi Jewish cases: onset was mainly in the limbs, and at least 1 person in each pedigree had onset before age 12 years.

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) reported a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia who carried the 3-bp (GAG) deletion in the DYT1 gene and had an unusual clinical presentation.

Kostic et al. (2006) described a large Serbian family in which the DYT1 phenotype varied widely, from asymptomatic carrier status to late-onset focal dystonia and generalized jerky dystonia. Of 7 mutation carriers, only 2 had dystonia, giving a penetrance of 29%.

Heiman et al. (2004) gave a standardized psychiatric interview to members of families with a DYT1 mutation. Compared with 65 noncarriers, the risk of recurrent major depressive disorder was increased both in 60 nonmanifesting carriers (relative risk 4.95, CI 1.72 to 14.29) and in 96 manifesting carriers (relative risk 3.62, CI 1.00 to 10.53). Carriers also had an earlier age at onset of recurrent major depression. The severity of motor signs was not associated with the likelihood of recurrent depression, and carriers did not have an increased risk of single major depression or bipolar disorder. The authors suggested that early-onset recurrent depression is a clinical expression of the DYT1 mutation.

Ghilardi et al. (2003) compared 12 clinically unaffected DYT1 carriers with 12 age-matched controls. Motor performance did not differ, but sequence learning was reduced in the carriers (p less than 0.01). On H2(15)O PET during motor execution, carriers showed increased activation in the left premotor cortex and right supplementary motor area and reduced activation in the posterior medial cerebellum. During sequence learning, activation was increased in the left ventral prefrontal cortex and the lateral cerebellum.

### Other Features

Carbon et al. (2004) performed FDG PET in 12 nonmanifesting and 11 manifesting DYT1 carriers, 6 nonmanifesting and 7 manifesting DYT6 carriers, and 11 controls. Manifesting carriers of both genotypes showed bilateral hypermetabolism in the presupplementary motor area and parietal association cortices compared with nonmanifesting carriers. Increased metabolism in the putamen, anterior cingulate, and cerebellar hemispheres was specific to DYT1 carriers.

Using diffusion tensor MRI, Carbon et al. (2004) found reduced fractional anisotropy (p less than 0.005) in the subgyral white matter of the sensorimotor cortex in DYT1 mutation carriers. They suggested that abnormal anatomic connectivity of the supplementary motor area may contribute to carriers' susceptibility to dystonia.

Asanuma et al. (2005) used [11C]-raclopride PET in nonmanifesting DYT1 carriers and found that striatal D2 receptor binding was reduced by about 15% in the caudate and putamen (p less than 0.005). They suggested that this reduction is a trait feature of the DYT1 genotype.

Carbon et al. (2009) found reduced D2 receptor availability in the caudate and putamen of both DYT1 and DYT6 mutation carriers relative to controls. The reduction was greater in DYT6 carriers (-38.0 +/- 3.0%) than in DYT1 carriers (-15.0 +/- 3.0%). Manifesting and nonmanifesting carriers did not differ for either genotype. Voxel-based analysis also showed reduced raclopride binding in the ventrolateral thalamus in both groups of carriers.

### Biochemical Features

Wooten et al. (1973) reported elevated plasma dopamine-beta-hydroxylase (DBH) activity in autosomal dominant torsion dystonia. Ziegler et al. (1976) studied plasma norepinephrine and DBH in dystonia.

Askenasy et al. (1980) studied 13 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with torsion dystonia of various origins. Serum DBH activity was significantly higher in patients and their relatives than in controls, particularly in the childhood-onset group. Intravenous metoclopramide (20 mg) did not change serum DBH activity in 12 patients and 17 relatives. However, DBH activity doubled or tripled after metoclopramide in 1 patient with symptomatic dystonia and in 2 of her maternal relatives.

Hornykiewicz et al. (1986) examined the brains of 2 patients with generalized childhood-onset dystonia musculorum deformans and found no important histologic changes. Choline acetyltransferase activity and levels of GABA and glutamic acid were in the control range. Norepinephrine concentrations were markedly and consistently decreased in the lateral and posterior hypothalamus, mamillary body, subthalamic nucleus, and locus ceruleus, and were elevated in the septum, thalamus, colliculi, red nucleus, and dorsal raphe nucleus. Serotonin was subnormal in the dorsal raphe nucleus. Dopamine was low in the nucleus accumbens in both patients and in the striatum in 1 of the 2. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Early studies of the hereditary pattern of idiopathic torsion dystonia included those of Zeman et al. (1959), Eldridge (1970), and Eldridge (1981), the last concerning inheritance in Jews. Bundey et al. (1975) concluded that genetic torsion dystonia had at least 2 forms: an autosomal recessive childhood-onset form and one or more dominant forms with onset in childhood or adult life.

Zilber et al. (1984) analyzed data from a nationwide survey in Israel. Neither an X-linked nor a simple autosomal recessive model fit the data well. An autosomal dominant model with low penetrance could account for the observations, although multifactorial inheritance was also possible. Because the fathers of isolated cases were older, the authors suggested that some isolated cases may have been new mutations.

Bressman et al. (1989) studied 43 Ashkenazi Jewish probands with onset before 28 years of age and their first- and second-degree relatives. Counting only definite cases, the age-adjusted risk was 15.5% for first-degree relatives and 6.5% for second-degree relatives, with no significant sex differences. These risks were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, with penetrance estimated at 29.4% (definite cases only) or 32.2% (definite and probable cases). Risch et al. (1990) performed segregation analysis on 43 probands from 42 nuclear families. The data were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with 30% penetrance. Recessive and polygenic inheritance were strongly rejected, and there was no evidence for sporadic cases or new mutations.

Ozelius et al. (1992) found that most affected individuals were heterozygous for the disease-associated ABL-ASS haplotype, which supported autosomal dominant inheritance of DYT1.

### Mapping

Breakefield et al. (1986) studied a family with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia in which more than 50 members were examined. Linkage analysis excluded the genes for pro-opiomelanocortin and glutamic acid decarboxylase, as well as other genomic regions. In a single large non-Jewish pedigree, Kramer et al. (1987) excluded large portions of chromosomes 11p and 13q and virtually all of 21q.

In a large non-Jewish kindred, Ozelius et al. (1989) found tight linkage between the dystonia gene and the gelsolin gene. Multipoint analysis placed the gene in 9q32-q34 between ABO and D9S26, a region that also contains the dopamine-beta-hydroxylase locus. In 12 Ashkenazi Jewish families, Kramer et al. (1990) found close linkage with the argininosuccinate synthetase (ASS) gene in 9q32-34. Because penetrance differed substantially between the 2 groups, they suggested that 2 different mutations might be operating.

Using GT repeat polymorphisms, Kwiatkowski et al. (1991) narrowed the gene in Ashkenazi Jews to the 11-cM interval between AK1 and D9S10. In the non-Jewish family, positive lod scores spanned more than 20 cM.

From obligate recombinants in Jewish families, Ozelius et al. (1992) placed DYT1 in a 6-cM region bounded by AK1 and ASS. In 52 unrelated affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals, they found highly significant linkage disequilibrium with an extended ABL-ASS haplotype (4/A12). This haplotype was present on 69% of disease-bearing chromosomes and on only 1% of control Jewish chromosomes. The association predicted that these loci lie within 1 to 2 cM of each other, and obligate recombinants placed DYT1 centromeric to ASS.

Warner et al. (1993) found evidence for linkage to 9q34 in a subset of 27 small British and French families. They also observed the ASS/ABL allelic association in British Jewish kindreds.

Kramer et al. (1994) found that 7 non-Jewish families with early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia were consistent with linkage to the DYT1 region. None of these families carried the Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype, suggesting different mutations, although the 2 French Canadian families shared the same haplotype.

Ozelius et al. (1997) built a YAC contig spanning 600 kb of the region. Expanded linkage disequilibrium analysis of the Ashkenazi Jewish founder mutation placed DYT1 most likely within a 150-kb region between D9S2161 and D9S63.

### Heterogeneity

Muller and Kupke (1990) noted that primary torsion dystonia may be inherited as an autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked recessive trait, and that at least 6 autosomal dominant forms can be distinguished clinically.

Warner et al. (1993) performed linkage analysis in 27 small families with idiopathic torsion dystonia (26 British, 1 French), 3 of which were Ashkenazi Jewish. The cumulative lod score at ASS was -6.72 at a recombination fraction of 0.001. Three clinically indistinguishable non-Jewish families excluded tight linkage to ASS, and HOMOG analysis showed significant heterogeneity.

Kramer et al. (1994) found that 2 non-Jewish families with atypical idiopathic torsion dystonia, with later onset and/or cranial or cervical involvement, were not linked to DYT1. This indicated that other genes are involved in dystonia.

Gasser et al. (1996) determined haplotypes at 5 DYT1-linked markers in 10 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with occupational hand dystonia: 8 had musician's cramp and 2 had writer's cramp. The founder haplotype could not be constructed from any of the 20 chromosomes, which argued against a role for the founder mutation in occupational hand dystonia.

Cheng et al. (1996) studied 260 probands with idiopathic adult-onset cranial or cervical dystonia, whose mean age at onset was 45.71 +/- 15.85 years. In 49 families, onset was earlier in the second generation than in the first by an average of 21.25 years, which the authors interpreted as compatible with genetic anticipation.

In the Serbian DYT1 family reported by Kostic et al. (2006), 3 family members without the GAG deletion also developed dystonia: 2 had multifocal dystonia and 1 had torticollis. Contarino et al. (2008) described a large Dutch family with primary focal dystonia. Of 24 individuals examined, 8 were definitely affected and 4 were possibly affected, with mild cranio-cervical-brachial dystonia and a mean age at onset of 45.5 years (range, 39-56). No DYT1 or SGCE mutations were found, and linkage to DYT6, DYT7, DYT13, and DYT16 was not detected.

### Molecular Genetics

Ozelius et al. (1997) identified the DYT1 gene on chromosome 9q34 as responsible for early-onset torsion dystonia. Almost all cases of early-onset dystonia had a unique 3-bp deletion that appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations. The deletion removes one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of a novel ATP-binding protein, torsinA. TorsinA has homologs in nematode, rat, mouse, and humans and some resemblance to the heat-shock protein and Clp protease families.

Kamm et al. (1999) screened 57 German patients with idiopathic torsion dystonia for the GAG deletion. It was present in 3 of 5 patients with early limb-onset dystonia, 1 of whom had a positive family history, and in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short disease course. The deletion was absent in 2 patients with early-onset generalized dystonia beginning in the cervical muscles, 5 other patients with multifocal dystonia, 14 with segmental dystonia, and 30 with focal cervical dystonia.

Leung et al. (2001) screened the TOR1A gene in 40 patients with early-onset dystonia or early-onset parkinsonism. In a patient with early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, they identified an 18-bp deletion (Phe323_Tyr328del) that would remove 6 amino acids near the C terminus, including a putative phosphorylation site.

Hjermind et al. (2002) tested 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia and found the GAG deletion in 3. All 3 had the classic DYT1 phenotype, but only 1 had a family history of dystonia. Of the 2 sporadic cases, 1 resulted from a de novo mutation, and the other had an asymptomatic carrier parent.

Grundmann et al. (2003) tested 256 unselected German patients with dystonia and found the GAG deletion in 6. Only 2 of the 6 had classic early-onset generalized dystonia. Two had multifocal dystonia, 1 of them with cranial and cervical involvement, and 2 had writer's cramp of both hands with only slight progression.

In a large Serbian family, Kostic et al. (2006) identified 7 GAG deletion carriers: 6 by direct analysis and 1 by inferred haplotype.

In a patient with DYT1, Zirn et al. (2008) found a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of TOR1A, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution.

In a man with late-onset focal oromandibular dystonia, Calakos et al. (2010) found a c.613T-A change in exon 3 of TOR1A, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution in the AAA-ATPase domain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Risch et al. (2007) studied the D216H coding variant in DYT1 GAG deletion carriers with dystonia (n = 119), carriers without dystonia (n = 113), and controls (n = 197). Compared with controls, the 216H allele was more frequent in carriers without dystonia and less frequent in carriers with dystonia. Haplotype analysis showed a highly protective effect of the H allele in trans with the GAG deletion, with suggestive evidence that the D216 allele in cis is required for penetrance. Kamm et al. (2008) reported that the D216H polymorphism also modifies susceptibility to DYT1 dystonia in European patients.

### Pathogenesis

McNaught et al. (2004) found perinuclear inclusion bodies in the midbrain reticular formation and periaqueductal gray of 4 genetically confirmed DYT1 patients but not in controls. The inclusions were in cholinergic and other neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus, cuneiform nucleus, and griseum centrale mesencephali, and they stained for ubiquitin, torsinA, and lamin A/C. No inclusions were found in the substantia nigra pars compacta, striatum, hippocampus, or selected cortical regions. Tau/ubiquitin-immunoreactive aggregates were seen in pigmented neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus ceruleus in all 4 patients.

### Diagnosis

Ozelius et al. (1992) suggested that ABL-ASS extended-haplotype status could predict carrier status in Jewish individuals.

Bressman et al. (2002) assessed 147 DYT1 GAG deletion carriers and 113 blood-related noncarriers from 43 families. Only the category of definite dystonia was 100% specific. Probable dystonia was more frequent in carriers than in noncarriers, but possible dystonia was not. The authors recommended that only individuals with definite signs of dystonia be considered affected in genetic studies.

Batshaw et al. (1985) reported Munchausen syndrome simulating torsion dystonia.

### Population Genetics

In a nationwide survey in Israel from 1969 to 1980, Zilber et al. (1984) ascertained 47 patients, 40 of European origin. Among European Jews, the frequency of idiopathic torsion dystonia was about 1 in 23,000 live births, 5 times higher than in Jews of Afro-Asian origin. Under a dominant model, the estimated gene frequency in European Jews was 3 to 4 per 100,000.

Ozelius et al. (1989) stated that the defective gene frequency in the Ashkenazi Jewish population was about 1 in 10,000. Assuming a disease frequency of 1 in 15,000, Bressman et al. (1989) estimated the gene frequency at 1 in 9,000. Of 53 definitely affected Ashkenazi individuals typed by Ozelius et al. (1992), 13 appeared to be sporadic. The proportion of sporadic cases potentially carrying the A12 haplotype at ASS (8 of 13, 62%) was similar to that of familial cases (28 of 40, 70%). This suggested that many sporadic cases are hereditary, that the gene frequency is higher than previously estimated, and that penetrance is lower than 30%. It also supported a single mutation event for most hereditary cases in this population.

Risch et al. (1995) analyzed 6 microsatellite loci at 9q34 in 59 Ashkenazi Jewish families. They concluded that more than 90% of early-onset cases in this population were due to a single founder mutation that first appeared about 350 years ago. Carriers preferentially originated from the northern part of the historic Jewish Pale of Settlement (Lithuania and Byelorussia). The recent origin and current high frequency of the mutation (between 1 in 6,000 and 1 in 2,000) suggested descent from a limited group of founders and genetic drift. Motulsky (1995) and Zoossmann-Diskin (1995) commented on this finding; Zoossmann-Diskin (1995) asked whether genetic drift or selection accounted for the frequency. ⚠︎

Valente et al. (1999) analyzed haplotypes in 9 Ashkenazi Jewish and 15 non-Jewish British patients with the GAG deletion. All British Ashkenazi patients carried the same haplotype as North American Jews. Only a limited number of distinct founder mutations were seen in the non-Jewish British patients.

In a nationwide census of French TOR1A mutation carriers, Frederic et al. (2008) studied 53 index cases and 104 relatives. The Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype was found in 11 families, and the remaining families had distinct haplotypes, suggesting independent mutation events. The estimated disease frequency in France was 0.13 per 100,000 and the mutation frequency 0.17 per 100,000.

### Animal Model

Lorden et al. (1984) characterized the autosomal recessive rat mutant 'dystonic' (dt). Beginning on postnatal day 10, these rats showed twisting of the axial musculature, hyperflexion of the trunk, self-clasping of the limbs, and poor limb placement, with no morphologic lesions. Cerebellar norepinephrine was significantly elevated, and the rats were less sensitive than littermates to the akinetic effects of haloperidol.

Shashidharan et al. (2005) generated 4 transgenic mouse lines overexpressing human delta-E torsinA under a neuron-specific enolase promoter. About 40% of transgenic mice in each line developed abnormal involuntary movements as early as 3 weeks after birth, including dystonic-appearing self-clasping of the limbs, hyperkinesia, and rapid bidirectional circling. Striatal dopamine was decreased in affected mice and increased in mice without behavioral changes. As in DYT1 patients, perinuclear inclusions staining for ubiquitin, torsinA, and lamin were found in the pedunculopontine nucleus and other brainstem regions.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG [3-bp (GAG) deletion; Glu302/303del]

Ozelius et al. (1997) found a unique 3-bp deletion in the DYT1 gene in almost all cases of early-onset torsion dystonia. The deletion appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations. It removes one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of torsinA, an ATP-binding protein.

Kamm et al. (1999) found the GAG deletion in 3 of 5 German patients with early limb-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia and in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia. It was not found in patients with other types of dystonia.

Valente et al. (1999) found that 9 British Ashkenazi Jewish patients with the GAG deletion shared the North American Ashkenazi haplotype. In 15 non-Jewish British carriers, only a limited number of distinct founder haplotypes were observed.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found the GAG deletion in 3 of 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia. In 1 sporadic proband the mutation was de novo, and another sporadic proband had an asymptomatic carrier parent.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found the GAG deletion in 6 of 256 German patients with dystonia. The phenotypes included generalized dystonia, multifocal dystonia, and writer's cramp of both hands.

Kostic et al. (2006) identified 7 GAG deletion carriers in a Serbian family. Only 2 of them were affected, with phenotypes ranging from late-onset focal to generalized jerky dystonia.

Frederic et al. (2008) studied 53 French index cases with the TOR1A mutation. The Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype was present in 11 families, and the remaining families had distinct haplotypes.

**.0002 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION**  
TOR1A, 18-BP DEL [Phe323_Tyr328del]

In a patient with early-onset dystonia and myoclonic features, Leung et al. (2001) identified an 18-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene (Phe323_Tyr328del). The deletion would remove 6 amino acids near the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site. It was found in a screen of 40 patients with early-onset dystonia or early-onset parkinsonism.

**.0003 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION**  
TOR1A, ARG288GLN [c.863G>A; p.Arg288Gln]

In a patient with DYT1, Zirn et al. (2008) identified a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5. The change was absent from 1,000 control chromosomes, and arg288 is conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. HEK293 cells overexpressing the mutant showed an enlarged perinuclear space. Cells overexpressing the GAG deletion showed the same change, whereas cells overexpressing wildtype TOR1A did not.

**.0004 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION**  
TOR1A, PHE205ILE [c.613T>A; p.F205I]

Calakos et al. (2010) studied a man in his fifth decade who had had involuntary jaw movements and grimacing for 3 years, consistent with oromandibular dystonia. He also had bipolar disorder treated with lithium and remote exposure to dopamine receptor-blocking agents, and his family history included tremor and depression but no dystonia. Sequencing of TOR1A identified a c.613T-A change in exon 3, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the AAA-ATPase domain. The variant was not found in 800 population controls. In transfected BHK cells, the F205I mutant produced inclusions in 44% of cells, compared with 10% for wildtype and 79% for the delta-E mutant. However, F205I inclusions colocalized mainly with the endoplasmic reticulum marker KDEL rather than with the nuclear envelope marker lamin B1. The authors raised the possibility that the variant may contribute to late-onset focal dystonia.
