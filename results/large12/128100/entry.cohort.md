## DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT; DYT1

DYSTONIA MUSCULORUM DEFORMANS 1

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant torsion dystonia-1 (DYT1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOR1A gene (605204) on chromosome 9q34.11. ⚠︎

### Description

Ozelius et al. (1989) described torsion dystonia as a movement disorder in which control of voluntary movements is lost, appearing as sustained muscle contractions and/or abnormal postures. Kwiatkowski et al. (1991) described idiopathic torsion dystonia as characterized by sustained, involuntary muscle contractions that frequently cause twisting and repetitive movements or abnormal postures, and noted that most familial forms show autosomal dominant inheritance with reduced penetrance. Ozelius et al. (1997) described early-onset torsion dystonia as a movement disorder with twisting muscle contractures beginning in childhood, with symptoms believed to result from altered neuronal communication in the basal ganglia. Risch et al. (2007) noted that the GAG deletion in DYT1 is a major cause of early-onset dystonia but that only 30% of mutation carriers develop the disease.

### Nomenclature

In a review of the clinical and molecular genetics of the primary dystonias, Muller et al. (1998) proposed referring to the known forms, most of which can be distinguished by genetic criteria, as dystonias 1-12.

### Clinical Features

ZEMAN et al. (1960) discussed formes frustes (incomplete or mild forms) of idiopathic dystonia musculorum deformans.

Yanagisawa et al. (1972) reported familial dystonia musculorum deformans occurring together with tremor.

In a family study of 32 patients with torsion dystonia, Bundey et al. (1975) found at least two forms of generalized dystonia with onset in childhood. Most of the other 15 patients, 12 of whom had onset in adult life, appeared to have a non-genetic torsion dystonia, although an example of a benign adult-onset dominant form associated with tremor was observed.

Marsden et al. (1976) reported on the natural history of idiopathic torsion dystonia.

Bressman et al. (1989) studied 43 probands with idiopathic torsion dystonia who had symptoms before 28 years of age, had at least one Ashkenazi grandparent, and were ascertained independently of family history. They examined 146 (90.1%) of 162 living first-degree relatives and 96 (40.2%) of 239 living second-degree relatives. Nineteen relatives had definite dystonia and 2 had probable dystonia.

Bressman et al. (1994) classified 174 Ashkenazi Jewish individuals with idiopathic torsion dystonia by their 9q34 haplotype and found 90 carriers of the DYT1-associated haplotype, 70 noncarriers, and 14 ambiguous individuals. Mean age at onset of symptoms was significantly lower in carriers than in noncarriers (12.5 +/- 8.2 vs 36.5 +/- 16.4 years). In 94% of carriers, symptoms began in a limb (arm or leg equally); the disorder rarely started in the neck (3.3%) or larynx (2.2%).

Kramer et al. (1994) studied 7 non-Jewish families of northern European and French Canadian descent with idiopathic torsion dystonia. Affected individuals were clinically similar to Ashkenazi Jewish cases: the site of onset was predominantly in the limbs, and at least 1 individual in each pedigree had onset before age 12 years.

Leung et al. (2001) reported a patient with early onset dystonia and myoclonic features who carried an 18-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found the DYT1 GAG deletion in 3 Danish probands. All 3 had the classical phenotype of DYT1 dystonia, but only 1 had a family history of dystonia.

Among 6 German carriers of the DYT1 GAG deletion identified in an unselected group of patients with dystonia, Grundmann et al. (2003) found that only 2 presented with classic features of early-onset primary generalized dystonia. Two had multifocal dystonia, 1 of them with involvement of cranial and cervical muscles, and 2 had writer's cramp of both hands with only slight progression.

Ghilardi et al. (2003) compared motor performance and sequence learning in 12 clinically unaffected DYT1 mutation carriers and 12 age-matched controls. Movement time and spatial accuracy were similar in the two groups, but sequence learning was reduced in gene carriers (p less than 0.01).

Heiman et al. (2004) gave a standardized psychiatric interview to members of families with a DYT1 mutation: 96 carriers with dystonia, 60 carriers without dystonia, and 65 noncarriers. Compared with noncarriers, the risk of recurrent major depressive disorder was increased in both non-manifesting carriers (RR = 4.95, CI = 1.72 to 14.29) and manifesting carriers (RR = 3.62, CI = 1.00 to 10.53). Carriers also developed recurrent depression at an earlier age.

Kostic et al. (2006) described a large Serbian family with the DYT1 GAG deletion in which the phenotype varied from asymptomatic carrier status to late-onset focal dystonia and generalized jerky dystonia. Of 7 mutation carriers (6 proven by direct analysis and 1 by inferred haplotype), only 2 were affected, giving a penetrance of 29%. ✗

Calakos et al. (2010) reported a man in his fifth decade of life who presented with involuntary jaw movements and grimacing that had begun 3 years earlier. Neurologic examination showed an orofacial movement, primarily involving jaw retraction, consistent with oromandibular dystonia. He also had cogwheel tone without rigidity, mild action and postural tremor of the upper extremities, and absent Achilles reflexes, and he had bipolar disorder. Brain MRI was normal. He carried a heterozygous missense variant in the TOR1A gene. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Carbon et al. (2004) used FDG PET to study 12 nonmanifesting and 11 manifesting DYT1 gene carriers, 6 nonmanifesting and 7 manifesting DYT6 gene carriers, and 11 control subjects. In both genotypes, manifesting carriers showed bilateral hypermetabolism in the presupplementary motor area (Brodmann area 6) and in the parietal association cortices (BA 40/7) compared with nonmanifesting carriers.

### Biochemical Features

Wooten et al. (1973) reported elevated plasma dopamine-beta-hydroxylase activity in autosomal dominant torsion dystonia.

Ziegler et al. (1976) studied plasma norepinephrine and dopamine-beta-hydroxylase levels in patients with dystonia.

In a familial study of 13 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with torsion dystonia of various origin, Askenasy et al. (1980) found significantly higher serum dopamine-beta-hydroxylase (DBH) activity in patients and their relatives than in controls, particularly in the childhood-onset group. None of the criteria of Jewish origin, dominant trait, slow progression, or axial topography were specific for elevated serum DBH activity. Intravenous metoclopramide (20 mg) did not change serum DBH activity in 12 patients and 17 relatives. In 1 patient with symptomatic dystonia and 2 of her maternal relatives, however, serum DBH activity doubled or tripled after the injection. The authors suggested that the metoclopramide-induced rise in DBH activity in relatives of patients indicated a genetic disposition.

### Inheritance

Bundey et al. (1975) found at least two clinically indistinguishable forms of generalized childhood-onset torsion dystonia, one autosomal dominant and one autosomal recessive. They concluded that the dominant forms could begin in childhood or in adult life.

Eldridge (1981) discussed the inheritance of torsion dystonia in Jews.

In a nationwide survey of idiopathic torsion dystonia in Israel, Zilber et al. (1984) found that X-linked or simple autosomal recessive models did not fit their data well. An autosomal dominant model with low penetrance could account for the observations and gave an estimated gene frequency in European Jews of 3 to 4 per 100,000. Multifactorial inheritance was also possible, and the authors noted that not all cases need share the same genetic basis.

Breakefield et al. (1986) studied a family showing autosomal dominant inheritance of torsion dystonia, in which over 50 members were examined. Their linkage model assumed autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance and allowed for age of onset.

In their study of 43 Ashkenazi Jewish probands with idiopathic torsion dystonia and their relatives, Bressman et al. (1989) found that the age-adjusted risk was 15.5% for first-degree relatives and 6.5% for second-degree relatives, counting definite cases only. There was no significant difference between the sexes or among the risks to parents, offspring, and sibs. The risks were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, with an estimated penetrance of 29.4% (definite cases only) or 32.2% (definite and probable cases).

Risch et al. (1990) performed segregation analysis on 43 Ashkenazi Jewish probands with onset of idiopathic torsion dystonia before age 27 years from 42 nuclear families; first- and second-degree relatives were directly examined and videotaped. The data were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with 30% penetrance. Recessive and polygenic inheritance were strongly rejected, and there was no evidence for sporadic cases or new mutations.

Kramer et al. (1990) stated that most familial forms of idiopathic torsion dystonia follow autosomal dominant transmission with reduced penetrance. Ozelius et al. (1992) found that most affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals were heterozygous for the disease-associated ABL-ASS haplotype, supporting autosomal dominant inheritance of DYT1.

Kramer et al. (1994) estimated penetrance in non-Jewish DYT1 families at 0.40 to 0.75. After identifying disease gene carriers and adjusting for age at onset, they obtained a direct penetrance estimate of 0.46, consistent with estimates of 30 to 40% in the Ashkenazi Jewish population. Ozelius et al. (1997) stated that early-onset torsion dystonia is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with reduced penetrance of 30 to 40%.

Of 3 Danish probands with the DYT1 GAG deletion reported by Hjermind et al. (2002), 1 had a family history of dystonia; of the 2 sporadic probands, 1 had a de novo mutation and the other had inherited the mutation from an asymptomatic carrier parent.

### Mapping

Breakefield et al. (1986) carried out linkage analysis with RFLPs in a family with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia. They excluded the pro-opiomelanocortin and glutamic acid decarboxylase genes, which had been implicated in rat models, as the cause of the disease in this family, and also excluded other regions of the genome.

Kramer et al. (1987) performed multipoint linkage analysis in a single large non-Jewish pedigree with autosomal dominant torsion dystonia and excluded linkage to large portions of chromosomes 11p and 13q and virtually the entire long arm of chromosome 21.

Ozelius et al. (1989) mapped a torsion dystonia susceptibility gene in a large non-Jewish kinship whose affected members had a clinical syndrome similar to that in the Jewish population. They found tight linkage to the gelsolin gene, and multipoint linkage analysis placed the gene in 9q32-q34 between ABO and D9S26. The dopamine-beta-hydroxylase locus also lies in this region.

By linkage analysis with DNA polymorphisms in 12 Ashkenazi Jewish families with idiopathic torsion dystonia, Kramer et al. (1990) found close linkage of the dystonia gene to the argininosuccinate synthetase (ASS) gene; multipoint analysis placed it in 9q32-34, a region also containing the gelsolin and dopamine-beta-hydroxylase loci. In a review, Muller and Kupke (1990) noted that linkage analysis in one form of autosomal dominant torsion dystonia had assigned a locus to the long arm of chromosome 9.

Using GT repeat polymorphisms, Kwiatkowski et al. (1991) localized the dystonia gene in Ashkenazi Jewish families to the 11-cM interval between AK1 and D9S10 on 9q32-34. Linkage analysis in a large non-Jewish family was also consistent with the gene lying in this region, although in that family positive lod scores extended over more than 20 cM.

Ozelius et al. (1992) identified obligate recombination events in Ashkenazi Jewish families that narrowed the DYT1 region on chromosome 9q to a 6-cM interval bounded by AK1 and ASS, with DYT1 centromeric to ASS.

Warner et al. (1993) performed linkage analysis in 27 small families (26 British, 1 French; 3 Ashkenazi Jewish) with idiopathic torsion dystonia, using the ASS and ABL loci on 9q34. The cumulative lod score for ASS at a recombination fraction of 0.001 was -6.72, but HOMOG analysis supported linkage to 9q34 in a subset of families, in both Jewish and non-Jewish kindreds in the UK.

Kramer et al. (1994) found that linkage results in 7 non-Jewish families of northern European and French Canadian descent with early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia were consistent with linkage to the DYT1 region on 9q34. None of these families carried the Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype, and the authors concluded that they probably had different mutations in the DYT1 gene. The 2 French Canadian families shared the same haplotype.

Ozelius et al. (1997) built a YAC contig covering 600 kb of the 9q34 region that includes D9S62a. Using several new polymorphic loci, they extended the linkage disequilibrium analysis of the Ashkenazi Jewish founder mutation and placed the most likely location of the DYT1 gene in a 150-kb region between D9S2161 and D9S63.

### Heterogeneity

Bundey et al. (1975) identified an autosomal recessive childhood-onset form of torsion dystonia in at least 3 families, probably involving about 6 to 8 patients, and suggested, on American evidence, that this form is particularly common in Ashkenazi Jews. They concluded that most isolated adult-onset cases seemed to have exogenous but unidentified causes.

In a review, Muller and Kupke (1990) noted that primary torsion dystonia may be inherited as an autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked recessive trait, and that at least six forms of autosomal dominant torsion dystonia can be distinguished clinically.

Kramer et al. (1990) suggested that the same gene may cause idiopathic torsion dystonia in a previously mapped non-Jewish kindred and in Ashkenazi Jewish families, but because penetrance differed substantially between the groups, two different mutations may be operating.

Warner et al. (1993) found that in 3 non-Jewish families that were clinically indistinguishable from the others, individual lod scores excluded a disease locus tightly linked to ASS on 9q34, suggesting genetic heterogeneity in idiopathic torsion dystonia.

Kramer et al. (1994) found that 2 other non-Jewish families with atypical idiopathic torsion dystonia (later onset and/or cranial or cervical involvement) were not linked to DYT1, and concluded that other genes are involved in dystonia.

Gasser et al. (1996) determined haplotypes at 5 marker loci closely linked to DYT1 in 10 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with focal hand dystonia (8 with musician's cramp, 2 with writer's cramp). None of the 20 chromosomes could be constructed as the founder haplotype, which is associated with more than 90% of generalized dystonia cases in this population, and no common haplotype was found. The authors concluded that these findings argued against a role for the DYT1 founder mutation in occupational hand dystonia.

Ozelius et al. (1997) stated that the DYT1 gene appears to cause most cases of early-onset torsion dystonia in both Ashkenazi Jewish and non-Jewish families. Muller et al. (1998) noted at least 8 clinically distinct autosomal dominant and 2 X-linked recessive forms of primary dystonia, with gene loci identified in at least 6 autosomal dominant forms.

In the Serbian DYT1 family, Kostic et al. (2006) found 3 members without the GAG deletion who also developed dystonia (2 multifocal dystonia, 1 torticollis), suggesting that their movements were due to another etiologic factor, such as another dystonia gene, or may be psychogenic.

### Molecular Genetics

Ozelius et al. (1997) identified the DYT1 gene on chromosome 9q34 as responsible for dominant early-onset torsion dystonia. The gene encodes a novel ATP-binding protein, termed torsinA, with homologs in nematode, rat, mouse, and human and some resemblance to the heat-shock protein and Clp protease families. Almost all cases of early-onset dystonia carried a unique heterozygous 3-bp deletion that results in loss of one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of torsinA; the deletion appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations. ⚠︎

Kamm et al. (1999) screened 57 German patients with idiopathic torsion dystonia for the 3-bp GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene and found it in 3 of 5 patients with early limb-onset disease, 1 of whom had a positive family history, and in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short course of disease. The mutation was absent in 2 patients with early-onset generalized dystonia beginning in the cervical muscles and in 5 other multifocal, 14 segmental, and 30 focal cervical dystonia patients.

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) reported a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia with a unique clinical presentation who carried the 3-bp (GAG) deletion in the DYT1 gene.

Leung et al. (2001) screened the TOR1A gene in 40 patients with early onset dystonia or early onset parkinsonism and identified an 18-bp deletion (Phe323_Tyr328del) in a patient with early onset dystonia and myoclonic features. The deletion would remove 6 amino acids close to the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site. It was the first TOR1A mutation identified other than the GAG deletion (Glu302/303del), and it was associated with a distinct type of early onset dystonia. Two infrequent intronic single-bp changes of unknown consequence were found in a dystonia patient and in the mother of a patient with early onset parkinsonism.

Hjermind et al. (2002) screened 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia for the DYT1 GAG deletion and detected it in 3. Only 1 had a family history of dystonia; of the 2 sporadic probands, 1 had a de novo mutation and the other had an asymptomatic carrier parent. The authors noted that de novo mutations in DYT1 are seldom reported, although independent founder mutations are known to have occurred, and discussed how the heterogeneity of DYT1 dystonia makes genetic counseling difficult. ⚠︎

Grundmann et al. (2003) screened 256 unselected patients with various subtypes of dystonia from 4 movement disorder outpatient clinics in Germany and found the DYT1 GAG deletion in 6: 2 with classic early-onset primary generalized dystonia, 2 with multifocal dystonia, and 2 with bilateral writer's cramp.

Zirn et al. (2008) identified a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5, in a patient with early-onset dystonia. The change was absent from 1000 control chromosomes, and arginine-288 has been conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. HEK293 cells overexpressing TOR1A with R288Q had an enlarged perinuclear space, as did cells overexpressing the common delta-GAG mutation, but not cells overexpressing wildtype TOR1A. The authors concluded that the change may be a novel pathogenic TOR1A mutation in DYT1.

In a man with late-onset oromandibular focal dystonia, Calakos et al. (2010) identified a heterozygous c.613T-A transversion in exon 3 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the AAA domain. The variant was not found in 800 population controls (1,600 chromosomes). In transfected BHK cells, F205I TOR1A-EGFP produced inclusions in 44% of cells, compared with 79% for deltaE TOR1A and 10% for wildtype; the F205I inclusions colocalized mainly with the ER marker KDEL rather than with the nuclear envelope marker LaminB1. The authors suggested that the variant may contribute to the expression of dystonia. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bressman et al. (1994) found that Ashkenazi Jewish noncarriers of the DYT1-associated 9q34 haplotype (D9S62, D9S63, ASS) had later onset of idiopathic torsion dystonia than carriers (mean 36.5 vs 12.5 years). In noncarriers, onset was in the neck, larynx, or other cranial muscles in 79% and in the arm in 21%, and never in the leg.

Kamm et al. (1999) suggested that the GAG deletion is responsible for a major portion of typical early limb-onset dystonia, but not for other types of dystonia, in the German population.

Grundmann et al. (2003) found that only 2 of 6 DYT1 GAG deletion carriers had classic early-onset generalized dystonia, the others having multifocal dystonia or bilateral writer's cramp. They concluded that the mutation may be associated with segmental and multifocal forms, making a priori prediction of carrier status and genetic counseling difficult.

Carbon et al. (2004) found increased metabolism specific to DYT1 carriers in the putamen, anterior cingulate (BA 24/32), and cerebellar hemispheres, whereas DYT6 carriers showed putaminal hypometabolism and temporal cortex (BA 21) hypermetabolism. They concluded that clinical manifestations are related to cortical dysfunction, whereas subcortical metabolic abnormalities may be trait features specific to each dystonia genotype.

Heiman et al. (2004) found that the severity of motor signs was not associated with the likelihood of recurrent depression, and that DYT1 carriers did not have a higher risk of other affective disorders, such as single major depression or bipolar disorder. They concluded that early-onset recurrent depression may be a clinical expression of the DYT1 GAG mutation, independent of motor manifestations.

Risch et al. (2007) studied 119 DYT1 GAG-deletion carriers with dystonia, 113 carriers without dystonia, and 197 controls. Compared with controls, the 216H allele of the TOR1A D216H polymorphism was more frequent in carriers without dystonia and less frequent in carriers with dystonia. Haplotype analysis showed that the H allele was highly protective when in trans with the GAG deletion, and gave suggestive evidence that the D216 allele in cis is needed for the disease to be penetrant. Kamm et al. (2008) reported that susceptibility to DYT1 dystonia in European patients was modified by the D216H polymorphism.

### Pathogenesis

Hornykiewicz et al. (1986) studied the brains of 2 patients with generalized childhood-onset dystonia musculorum deformans after death. They found no important histologic changes in the basal ganglia, cerebral cortex, higher brainstem nuclei, locus ceruleus, or raphe nuclei. Choline acetyltransferase activity and GABA and glutamate levels were normal. Norepinephrine was markedly and consistently decreased in the lateral and posterior hypothalamus, mamillary body, subthalamic nucleus, and locus ceruleus. Serotonin was subnormal in the dorsal raphe nucleus, as was dopamine in the nucleus accumbens and, in 1 of the 2 cases, in the striatum. Norepinephrine was raised in the septum, thalamus, colliculi, red nucleus, and dorsal raphe nucleus, and serotonin was elevated in the globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and locus ceruleus; homovanillic acid showed little consistent change. The authors suggested that a disturbance of noradrenergic brain mechanisms may be a basic neurochemical abnormality in the disorder. ⚠︎

Using H2(15)O PET, Ghilardi et al. (2003) found increased activation in the left premotor cortex and right supplementary motor area with reduced activation in the posterior medial cerebellum during motor execution in nonmanifesting DYT1 carriers, and increased activation in the left ventral prefrontal cortex and lateral cerebellum during sequence learning. They suggested that localized increases in neural activity may enable normal movement execution but may not compensate for the sequence learning defect.

From their FDG PET study, Carbon et al. (2004) suggested that dystonia may be associated with abnormal movement preparation caused by defective sensorimotor integration. Using diffusion tensor MRI, Carbon et al. (2004) found reduced fractional anisotropy, a measure of axonal integrity and coherence, in the subgyral white matter of the sensorimotor cortex in DYT1 mutation carriers compared with control subjects (p less than 0.005), and suggested that abnormal anatomical connectivity of the supplementary motor area may contribute to the susceptibility of DYT1 carriers to develop clinical manifestations of dystonia.

McNaught et al. (2004) found perinuclear inclusion bodies in cholinergic and other neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus, cuneiform nucleus, and griseum centrale mesencephali in the midbrain reticular formation and periaqueductal gray of 4 clinically documented and genetically confirmed DYT1 patients but not in controls. The inclusions stained positively for ubiquitin, torsinA, and the nuclear envelope protein lamin A/C. Tau/ubiquitin-immunoreactive aggregates were present in pigmented neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus coeruleus in all 4 cases, but no inclusion bodies were found in the substantia nigra pars compacta, striatum, hippocampus, or selected regions of the cerebral cortex. The authors concluded that DYT1 dystonia is associated with impaired protein handling and the nuclear envelope, and suggested that alterations in these brainstem structures, which mediate motor activity and muscle tone, could underlie the pathophysiology of the disorder.

Asanuma et al. (2005) used PET with [11C]-raclopride to study non-manifesting carriers of the DYT1 mutation and found striatal D2 receptor binding about 15% lower in the caudate and putamen (p less than 0.005). They suggested that reduced striatal D2 binding is a trait feature of the DYT1 genotype.

Using [11C]raclopride PET, Carbon et al. (2009) found that both manifesting and nonmanifesting DYT1 and DYT6 mutation carriers had significantly lower D2 receptor availability in the caudate and putamen than healthy controls (p less than 0.001). The reduction was larger in DYT6 than in DYT1 carriers (-38.0% vs -15.0%), and manifesting and nonmanifesting carriers of either genotype did not differ significantly. Voxel-based analysis also showed reduced raclopride binding in the ventrolateral thalamus of both DYT1 and DYT6 carriers, more pronounced in DYT6 carriers and independent of clinical signs. The authors concluded that reduced D2 receptor availability fits with dysfunction or loss of D2-bearing neurons, increased synaptic dopamine, or both, and likely represents a susceptibility factor for developing clinical manifestations.

Calakos et al. (2010) found that, unlike deltaE TOR1A inclusions, which colocalized with the nuclear envelope marker LaminB1 in transfected BHK cells, the larger inclusions formed by F205I TOR1A colocalized mainly with the ER marker KDEL. They suggested that the F205I and deltaE mutations do not have identical pathophysiology.

### Diagnosis

Batshaw et al. (1985) reported Munchausen syndrome simulating torsion dystonia, pointing to factitious disorder as part of the differential diagnosis of torsion dystonia. ⚠︎

Ozelius et al. (1992) proposed that ABL-ASS extended-haplotype status could predict carrier status in Jewish individuals and be used for molecular diagnosis. Bressman et al. (1994) found that limb onset, leg involvement during the course of disease, and age at onset distinguished DYT1 haplotype carriers from noncarriers with 90% accuracy.

Bressman et al. (2002) assessed 147 DYT1 GAG deletion carriers and 113 blood-related noncarriers from 43 families. Only the category of definite dystonia was 100% specific; probable dystonia was more common in carriers than in noncarriers, but possible dystonia was not. They recommended that only individuals with definite signs of dystonia be considered affected in linkage and other genetic studies of DYT1 families.

### Population Genetics

Bundey et al. (1975) estimated that fitness in childhood-onset dystonia was 1/20 of normal, so that most childhood-onset dominant cases appeared sporadically. Because of a probable paternal age effect, they thought that 9 to 11 sporadic patients with generalized childhood dystonia represented new dominant mutations. ⚠︎

In a nationwide survey of idiopathic torsion dystonia in Israel, Zilber et al. (1984) collected 47 patients between 1969 and 1980, 40 of whom were of European origin. The frequency in Jews of European origin was about 1 in 23,000 live births, five-fold greater than in Jews of Afro-Asian origin. Paternal ages were increased for isolated cases, and the authors suggested that some of these cases may have been new mutations.

Ozelius et al. (1989) stated that the frequency of the defective torsion dystonia gene in the Ashkenazi Jewish population is about 1/10,000. Assuming a disease frequency of 1 in 15,000 among Ashkenazi Jews, Bressman et al. (1989) estimated the gene frequency at 1 in 9,000. Risch et al. (1990) suggested that the high incidence and dominant inheritance of early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews indicate genetic homogeneity within this population. Kramer et al. (1990) noted that the frequency of idiopathic torsion dystonia in the Ashkenazi Jewish population is 5 to 10 times greater than in other groups.

Ozelius et al. (1992) typed 53 definitely affected Ashkenazi Jewish individuals, 13 of whom appeared to be sporadic. In 52 unrelated affected individuals, they found strong linkage disequilibrium between DYT1 and an extended ABL-ASS haplotype (4/A12), present on 69% of disease-bearing chromosomes but only 1% of control Jewish chromosomes, suggesting that a single mutation event accounts for most hereditary dystonia in this population and that DYT1 lies within 1-2 cM of these loci. Sporadic cases carried the ASS A12 allele at a similar rate (8/13) to familial cases (28/40), suggesting that many sporadic cases are hereditary, that the disease gene frequency in Ashkenazi Jews exceeds 1/15,000, and that penetrance is lower than 30%.

Warner et al. (1993) found that the allelic association between ASS/ABL and idiopathic torsion dystonia previously seen in Ashkenazi families in the USA was also present in British Jewish kindreds. Bressman et al. (1994) noted that this linkage disequilibrium implies that a large proportion of early-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews is due to a founder mutation in DYT1. Kramer et al. (1994) noted that the associated haplotype, within a 2-cM region, was present in more than 90% of early limb-onset Ashkenazi Jewish cases.

Zoossmann-Diskin (1995) discussed whether genetic drift or selection accounts for idiopathic torsion dystonia in Ashkenazi Jews.

Using 6 closely linked microsatellite loci on 9q34 in 59 Ashkenazi Jewish families with idiopathic torsion dystonia, Risch et al. (1995) found that more than 90% of early-onset cases in the Ashkenazi population were due to a single founder mutation, which they estimated first appeared about 350 years ago. Carriers came mainly from the northern part of the historic Jewish Pale of settlement (Lithuania and Byelorussia), and the current frequency of the mutation was estimated at between 1/6,000 and 1/2,000. The recent origin and high frequency of this dominant mutation suggested that the Ashkenazi population descends from a limited group of founders and that genetic drift has been important in determining disease allele frequencies in this population.

Valente et al. (1999) analyzed haplotypes surrounding DYT1 in 9 Ashkenazi Jewish and 15 non-Jewish British patients carrying the GAG deletion. All the British Ashkenazi Jewish patients carried the same haplotype as North American Ashkenazi Jews, supporting descent of the British Ashkenazi community from the same small group of individuals. Only a limited number of distinct founder haplotypes were observed among non-Jewish British carriers, and the authors concluded that the deletion is probably not a very frequent mutation and has arisen only a limited number of times over the centuries.

Hjermind et al. (2002) found the DYT1 GAG deletion in only 3 of 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia, a low frequency even though several probands had early-onset generalized dystonia; no proband with other types of dystonia carried the deletion. Grundmann et al. (2003) found the GAG deletion in 6 (about 2.3%) of 256 unselected German patients with various subtypes of dystonia.

In a nationwide census of French TOR1A mutation carriers including 53 index cases and 104 relatives, Frederic et al. (2008) estimated the disease frequency of early-onset torsion dystonia due to TOR1A at 0.13 per 100,000 and the mutation frequency at 0.17 per 100,000, showing that the disease is rare in France. The previously reported Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype was found in 11 of the 53 families; the remaining families had distinct haplotypes, suggesting independent mutation events.

### Animal Model

Lorden et al. (1984) described dystonic (dt), an autosomal recessive mutation in Sprague-Dawley rats. Signs began on postnatal day 10 and included twisting of the axial muscles, hyperflexion of the trunk, self-clasping of the limbs, and poor limb placement during walking. Routine light microscopy found no morphologic lesions in neural or non-neural tissue. Norepinephrine levels were significantly raised in the cerebellum but normal in other locus ceruleus terminal fields, and cerebellar noradrenergic innervation looked normal. The mutant rats were less sensitive than littermates to the akinesic effects of haloperidol, but striatal dopamine levels and the response to apomorphine were normal. The authors suggested that the dt rat may be a useful model for human dystonia musculorum deformans and that the findings point to biochemical disturbances in the extrapyramidal system. ⚠︎

Shashidharan et al. (2005) generated 4 independent lines of transgenic mice overexpressing human DeltaE-torsinA under a neuron-specific enolase promoter. About 40% of transgenic mice from each line developed abnormal involuntary movements with dystonic-appearing self-clasping of limbs as early as 3 weeks after birth, along with hyperkinesia and rapid bidirectional circling. Striatal dopamine was decreased in affected transgenic mice but increased in transgenic mice without behavioral changes. Perinuclear inclusions and aggregates staining for ubiquitin, torsinA, and lamin were found in neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus and other brainstem regions, in a pattern similar to that described in DYT1 patients.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Ozelius et al. (1997) found that almost all cases of early-onset torsion dystonia carried a unique heterozygous 3-bp deletion in the DYT1 (TOR1A) gene, resulting in loss of one of a pair of glutamic acid residues in a conserved region of the ATP-binding protein torsinA. The deletion appeared to have arisen independently in different ethnic populations. ⚠︎

**.0002 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Kamm et al. (1999) found the heterozygous 3-bp GAG deletion in the DYT1 gene in 3 of 5 German patients with early limb-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia, 1 of whom had a positive family history, and in 1 young patient with multifocal dystonia and a short course of disease. ⚠︎

**.0003 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Ikeuchi et al. (1999) reported a Japanese patient with primary torsion dystonia with a unique clinical presentation who carried the 3-bp (GAG) deletion in the DYT1 gene.

Valente et al. (1999) analyzed haplotypes in 9 Ashkenazi Jewish and 15 non-Jewish British patients with the GAG deletion. The Ashkenazi Jewish patients shared the haplotype seen in North American Jews, whereas the non-Jewish British patients showed only a limited number of distinct founder haplotypes.

In a large Serbian family, Kostic et al. (2006) identified 7 carriers of the heterozygous GAG deletion (6 by direct analysis and 1 by inferred haplotype), only 2 of whom were affected (penetrance 29%); the phenotype ranged from asymptomatic carrier status to late-onset focal dystonia and generalized jerky dystonia. ⚠︎

**.0004 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 18-BP DEL [p.F323]

In a patient with early onset dystonia and myoclonic features, Leung et al. (2001) identified an 18-bp deletion in the TOR1A gene (Phe323_Tyr328del), which would remove 6 amino acids close to the C terminus of torsinA, including a putative phosphorylation site. It was the first TOR1A mutation identified other than the GAG deletion and was associated with a distinct type of early onset dystonia.

**.0005 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Hjermind et al. (2002) detected the DYT1 GAG deletion in 3 of 107 Danish probands with primary torsion dystonia, all with the classical DYT1 phenotype. One proband had a family history of dystonia; of the 2 sporadic probands, 1 had a de novo mutation and the other had inherited it from an asymptomatic carrier parent.

**.0006 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Among 256 unselected German patients with dystonia, Grundmann et al. (2003) found 6 carriers of the 3-bp (GAG) deletion in the DYT1 gene, of whom 2 had classic early-onset primary generalized dystonia.

**.0007 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Grundmann et al. (2003) also found the DYT1 GAG deletion in 2 German patients with multifocal dystonia, 1 with involvement of cranial and cervical muscles, and in 2 patients with writer's cramp of both hands with only slight progression.

**.0008 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, DYT1 MUTATION

Carbon et al. (2004) studied 11 manifesting carriers of the DYT1 primary dystonia mutation by FDG PET and found bilateral hypermetabolism in the presupplementary motor area and parietal association cortices compared with nonmanifesting carriers.

**.0009 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Heiman et al. (2004) studied 96 manifesting carriers of the DYT1 GAG mutation and found an increased risk of recurrent major depressive disorder compared with noncarriers (RR = 3.62).

**.0010 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, DYT1 MUTATION

McNaught et al. (2004) described perinuclear inclusion bodies in the midbrain reticular formation and periaqueductal gray, and tau/ubiquitin-immunoreactive aggregates in pigmented neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus coeruleus, in 4 clinically documented and genetically confirmed DYT1 patients with a mutation in the gene encoding torsinA.

**.0011 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, 3-BP DEL, GAG

Risch et al. (2007) studied 119 DYT1 GAG-deletion carriers with dystonia and 113 carriers without dystonia. The 216H allele of the D216H polymorphism was highly protective when in trans with the GAG deletion, and there was suggestive evidence that the D216 allele in cis is needed for the disease to be penetrant.

**.0012 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, DYT1 MUTATION

Frederic et al. (2008) studied 53 French index cases with early-onset torsion dystonia due to a unique and recurrent heterozygous TOR1A mutation, and 104 relatives. The Ashkenazi Jewish haplotype was found in 11 of the 53 families, and the remaining families had distinct haplotypes, suggesting independent mutation events. ⚠︎

**.0013 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, ARG288GLN [c.863G>A; p.R288Q]

In a patient with early-onset dystonia, Zirn et al. (2008) identified a c.863G-A transition in exon 5 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in an arg288-to-gln (R288Q) substitution in subdomain alpha-5. The change was absent from 1000 control chromosomes, and arginine-288 is conserved throughout vertebrate evolution. HEK293 cells overexpressing the R288Q mutant, like those overexpressing the delta-GAG mutant but unlike those overexpressing wildtype TOR1A, had an enlarged perinuclear space. The authors concluded that the change may be a novel pathogenic TOR1A mutation.

**.0014 DYSTONIA 1, TORSION, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
TOR1A, PHE205ILE [c.613T>A; p.F205I]

In a man with oromandibular dystonia beginning in his fifth decade, Calakos et al. (2010) identified a heterozygous c.613T-A transversion in exon 3 of the TOR1A gene, resulting in a phe205-to-ile (F205I) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the AAA domain. The variant was not found in 1,600 control chromosomes. In transfected BHK cells, F205I TOR1A produced inclusions in 44% of cells (vs 79% for deltaE and 10% for wildtype) that colocalized mainly with the ER marker KDEL. The authors suggested that the variant may contribute to the expression of dystonia. ⚠︎
