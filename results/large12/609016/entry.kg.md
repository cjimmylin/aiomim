### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (LCHAD deficiency) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the HADHA gene (600890) on chromosome 2p23.3.

### Description

LCHAD deficiency is a defect of fatty acid breakdown. It is associated with life-threatening episodes of hypoketotic hypoglycemia during prolonged fasting, and neuropathy and retinal pigment changes have also been noted (Schrijver-Wieling et al., 1997). LCHAD catalyzes the third step of mitochondrial beta-oxidation, and its activity sits on the C-terminal portion of the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein (Sims et al., 1995). The trifunctional protein also carries long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase and long-chain 3-ketothiolase activities (IJlst et al., 1994).

### Clinical Features

Wanders et al. (1989) reported on sudden infant death in relation to long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase.

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) described a child who presented in early childhood with episodes of coma and hypoglycemia and a rapidly progressive myopathy and cardiomyopathy. The child died at 9 months of age. Ketosis was decreased (blood beta-hydroxybutyrate 0.07 mmol/L) even though plasma fatty acid levels were normal (0.81 mmol/L).

Hagenfeldt et al. (1990) described 5 patients with a suspected defect in fatty acid beta-oxidation. During acute illness, they excreted massive amounts of 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids in the urine and accumulated 3-hydroxy fatty acids in serum. Four of the patients died of cardiomyopathy and liver insufficiency between 3 and 14 months of age. Two of the patients had older sibs who had died unexpectedly in early infancy.

Jackson et al. (1991) described 2 children with LCHAD deficiency. Both presented with an acute metabolic crisis and hypoglycemia, and both excreted even-chain unsubstituted dicarboxylic and 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids in the urine.

Duran et al. (1991) reported 2 sibs with LCHAD deficiency. One sib died suddenly and unexpectedly on the third day of life with extreme hypoketotic hypoglycemia. The younger sib, a girl, developed feeding problems, lowered consciousness, and liver dysfunction at age 5 months, and had signs of cardiomyopathy.

Jackson et al. (1992) described a young girl with recurrent episodes of muscle weakness that culminated in a severe attack of generalized muscle weakness.

Bertini et al. (1992) described an 11-month-old girl with acute episodes of hypoglycemia and hepatic encephalopathy reminiscent of Reye syndrome, together with 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. She had severe sensory-motor neuropathy, pigmentary retinopathy, and cardiomyopathy, and she died of cardiac failure. Peripheral nerve pathology showed axonal neuropathy and demyelination. The authors noted that peripheral nerve involvement and retinal pigmentary degeneration had not previously been described in patients with proven defects of mitochondrial beta-oxidation. ⚠︎

Wilcken et al. (1993) reported 11 pregnancies in 5 mothers. Six of the babies had LCHAD deficiency, and each of these pregnancies was complicated by features such as maternal fatty liver and HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets) syndrome. Three of the mothers also gave birth to unaffected babies, and those pregnancies were largely uncomplicated.

Sewell et al. (1994) reported a patient with LCHAD deficiency who presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, hepatopathy, secondary carnitine deficiency, and increased plasma long-chain acylcarnitines. The authors noted that this newly recognized disorder usually has a fatal outcome, and they compared their patient with those in the literature.

Fryburg et al. (1994) described a black boy with features of Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome (BRRS) who also had a defect in long-chain fatty acid oxidation, expressed in cultured skin fibroblasts as LCHAD deficiency. He also had an abnormal brain MRI and enlargement of both lower limbs. The authors postulated that LCHAD deficiency may be the cause of the lipid myopathy in BRRS.

Treem et al. (1994) reported a full-term infant who seemed normal at birth and presented at 4 months of age with hypoglycemia, coma, and profound hepatic steatosis. The infant was found to have LCHAD deficiency. The mother had had acute fatty liver of pregnancy during this pregnancy.

Sims et al. (1995) studied 3 families with LCHAD deficiency in which the children had sudden unexplained death or hypoglycemia with abnormal liver enzymes (Reye-like syndrome). In all 3 families, the mothers had acute fatty liver of pregnancy with severe complications while pregnant with the affected infants.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported the clinical findings in 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency. At presentation, during the first 2 years of life, the patients had hypoglycemia, cardiomyopathy, muscle hypotonia, and hepatomegaly. Seven patients had recurrent metabolic crises and 6 had a steadily progressive course. Two patients had cholestatic liver disease, which the authors noted is uncommon in beta-oxidation defects. One patient had peripheral neuropathy, and 6 had retinopathy with focal pigmentary aggregations or retinal hypopigmentation. Neuroimaging showed bilateral periventricular or focal cortical lesions in 3 patients and brain atrophy in 1.

Schrijver-Wieling et al. (1997) reported 2 girls with LCHAD deficiency, aged 8 and 15 years. Both had extensive macular pigment deposits and a 'salt and pepper' scattering of pigment in the retina, and both had decreasing visual acuity.

Uusimaa et al. (1997) reported 2 patients with L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency who had pigmentary retinopathy.

Ibdah et al. (1999) studied 24 children with 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency. Nineteen had a deficiency only of LCHAD, and these children presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fatty liver.

In a separate report, Ibdah et al. (1999) described an infant with LCHAD deficiency that had not been suspected. At 2 months of age, the infant presented with acute infantile hypocalcemia and vitamin D deficiency, together with occult, unexplained cholestatic liver disease. The infant died suddenly and unexpectedly at 8 months. The authors concluded that infantile hypocalcemia is an unusual phenotype associated with LCHAD deficiency.

Thiel et al. (1999) reported LCHAD deficiency that became apparent on the first day of life, presenting with tachypnea.

den Boer et al. (2002) reviewed questionnaire data on 50 of 61 unselected patients with LCHAD deficiency diagnosed at their center. The mean age at clinical presentation was 5.8 months (range, 1 day to 26 months), and 7 patients (15%) presented in the neonatal period. Thirty-nine patients (78%) presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia. The other 11 (22%) presented with chronic problems consisting of failure to thrive, feeding difficulties, cholestatic liver disease, and/or hypotonia. In retrospect, 82% of the patients who presented with an acute metabolic derangement had also had a combination of chronic nonspecific symptoms before the metabolic crises. The authors concluded that LCHAD deficiency often presents with chronic nonspecific symptoms, which makes early diagnosis difficult in the absence of the classic metabolic derangement. Mortality was high (38%), and all deaths occurred before or within 3 months after diagnosis. Morbidity among survivors was also high, with recurrent metabolic crises and muscle problems despite therapy.

Baskin et al. (2010) reported a 22-month-old child with LCHAD deficiency who was identified on expanded newborn screening by an abnormal acylcarnitine pattern with increased C14OH.

### Other Features

Ibdah et al. (1999) reported that the mother of an LCHAD-deficient infant had preeclampsia in the third trimester of that pregnancy. In a later pregnancy she developed severe acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP), and the fetus died in utero at 33 weeks' gestation. The authors concluded that fetal LCHAD deficiency is associated with a range of maternal illnesses, from preeclampsia to life-threatening AFLP.

### Biochemical Features

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) found excessive C6 to C10 dicarboxylic acids in the urine of their patient. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry also showed an accumulation of medium- and long-chain (C8 to C14) 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids, which suggested a defect of the mitochondrial enzyme that dehydrogenates 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA esters. The patient's cultured fibroblasts showed impaired oxidation of medium- and long-chain fatty acids and deficiency of LCHAD. Activities of long-, medium-, and short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenases and 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase were normal. The authors concluded that the disorder affects liver, skeletal muscle, and myocardium.

Hagenfeldt et al. (1990) found normal long-chain and medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase activities in fibroblasts from all 5 of their patients with 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. They concluded that these patients differed from previously described patients with beta-oxidation defects.

Jackson et al. (1991) found low LCHAD activity but normal short-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from 2 patients. They further characterized the defect by immunoprecipitating the short-chain enzyme with monospecific antibodies.

In the surviving sib, Duran et al. (1991) used GC/MS to find large amounts of C6-C14 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids and conjugated 3-hydroxyoctanoic acid in the urine, and they found increased plasma long-chain acylcarnitine. A phenylpropionic acid loading test revealed small but significant amounts of conjugated 3-hydroxyphenylpropionic acid in the urine. LCHAD activity was deficient in cultured skin fibroblasts.

In the patient described by Jackson et al. (1992), muscle mitochondria showed an abnormal pattern of beta-oxidation intermediates, with accumulation of 3-hydroxyacyl- and 2-enoyl-CoA and carnitine esters and of 3-oxoacylcarnitines. LCHAD activity was low in mitochondria from all tissues, and long-chain 2-enoyl-CoA hydratase activity was low in muscle mitochondria. Thiolase activity measured with 3-oxohexadecanoyl-CoA was low in fibroblast, muscle, and cardiac mitochondria, whereas the deficiency was only partial when measured with 3-oxooctanoyl-CoA.

Wanders et al. (1992) described a patient with clear signs of a mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation disorder. The patient's fibroblasts showed impaired palmitate beta-oxidation and a combined deficiency of long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase, LCHAD, and long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase.

Bertini et al. (1992) found a deficiency of mitochondrial LCHAD in cultured fibroblasts from their patient.

Sewell et al. (1994) found 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria in their patient and confirmed the diagnosis of LCHAD deficiency by studies in cultured skin fibroblasts.

Fryburg et al. (1994) showed that cultured skin fibroblasts from their patient with BRRS features had a defect in long-chain fatty acid oxidation, expressed as a deficiency of LCHAD activity.

IJlst et al. (1996) noted that in most patients only the LCHAD activity of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein is deficient, while its long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase and long-chain 3-ketothiolase activities are near normal. They described the deficiency as impairing the oxidation of long-chain fatty acids, which can lead to sudden infant death. ⚠︎

Tyni et al. (1997) found dicarboxylic aciduria in 9 of 10 tested patients with LCHAD deficiency. Most patients had lactic acidosis, increased serum creatine kinase activity, and low serum carnitine concentration. The enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase activities of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein were variably decreased in skin fibroblasts.

Van Hove et al. (2000) reviewed acylcarnitines in plasma and blood spots from 23 patients with proven LCHAD deficiency. The long-chain 3-hydroxyacylcarnitines C14:1, C14, C16, and C18:1 were elevated, as were the long-chain acylcarnitines C12, C14:1, C14, C16, C18:2, and C18:1. Acetylcarnitine was decreased.

Elizondo et al. (2020) measured plasma acylcarnitines in 11 treated patients with LCHAD/TFP deficiency who were on a controlled diet low in long-chain fat and supplemented with MCT. After a 10-hour overnight fast, C18:1-OH was the most prominent long-chain acylcarnitine (0.56 +/- 0.52 umol/L; reference, 0.00-0.05), and C16-OH and C18-OH were also above the reference range. C18:1-OH fell by 59% after breakfast and rose by only 20% after 45 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise. This response was fairly consistent across subjects. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Jackson et al. (1991) suggested that LCHAD deficiency is probably inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, because fibroblasts from the parents of one affected child showed intermediate enzyme activity.

In a patient with a combined enzyme defect, Jackson et al. (1992) found that LCHAD and long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase activities in fibroblasts from the parents were intermediate between those of controls and the patient. They concluded that the combined defect appeared to be inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.

den Boer et al. (2002) described LCHAD deficiency as a rare autosomal recessive inborn error of fatty acid oxidation.

### Cytogenetics

Baskin et al. (2010) used short tandem repeat analysis to show that a child with LCHAD deficiency was homozygous for an HADHA mutation as a result of paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 2. They emphasized the importance of parental testing when a patient with an autosomal recessive disorder is homozygous, because of the impact on genetic counseling.

### Mapping

IJlst et al. (1996) showed that the gene encoding the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein is located on chromosome 2p24.1-p23.3.

### Heterogeneity

Wanders et al. (1992) used size-exclusion chromatography to show that their patient with combined deficiency of long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase, LCHAD, and long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase lacked the membrane-bound multifunctional beta-oxidation enzyme protein, which carries all 3 activities. They proposed that trifunctional protein deficiency is a new disorder of mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation, separate from isolated deficiency of a single enzyme activity. ⚠︎

Ibdah et al. (1999) described 5 children with complete trifunctional protein deficiency, meaning loss of activity of all 3 enzymes. They differed from 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency. These 5 children presented with neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy or progressive neuromyopathy. None carried the glu474-to-gln mutation, and none of their mothers had liver disease during pregnancy.

### Molecular Genetics

IJlst et al. (1994) sequenced the cDNAs encoding the alpha and beta subunits of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein in patients with LCHAD deficiency. They identified a G-to-C mutation at nucleotide 1528 (G1528C) in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase-encoding region of the alpha subunit (HADHA; 600890). The mutation results in a glutamate-to-glutamine substitution at amino acid position 510 and creates a PstI restriction site. RFLP analysis showed that 24 of 26 unrelated patients expressed only the C1528 allele and that the other 2 were heterozygous. The mutation was not found in 55 control subjects. ⚠︎

Sims et al. (1995) performed SSCV analysis of the exons of the LCHAD (trifunctional protein alpha subunit) gene in 3 families with LCHAD deficiency. Two affected children were homozygous for the G1528C mutation, which lies in the LCHAD domain and replaces glutamic acid with glutamine at residue 474 of the mature alpha subunit (E474Q). A third affected child was compound heterozygous for the E474Q mutation and a C1132T mutation that creates a premature stop codon at residue 342.

IJlst et al. (1996) found the G1528C mutation in the alpha subunit cDNA at an allele frequency of 87% in 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency. They expressed wildtype and mutant proteins in S. cerevisiae and showed that the G1528C mutation directly causes the loss of LCHAD activity.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported that all 13 of their patients with LCHAD deficiency were homozygous for the common G1528C mutation.

Ibdah et al. (1999) amplified and sequenced the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein in 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency. Eight children were homozygous for the E474Q mutation, and the other 11 were compound heterozygous for this mutation and a different mutation on the other allele. ⚠︎

In a separate report, Ibdah et al. (1999) found by molecular analysis that their infant with LCHAD deficiency who presented with hypocalcemia and cholestasis was homozygous for the common 1528G-C (E474Q) mutation.

Baskin et al. (2010) found that a child with LCHAD deficiency was homozygous for the common HADHA mutation c.1526G-C (p.Glu510Gln). The father was heterozygous for the mutation, but the mother did not carry it. Short tandem repeat testing with markers covering both the short and long arms of chromosome 2 showed that the child had paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 2.

Elizondo et al. (2020) reported that 9 of 11 patients with LCHAD/TFP deficiency were homozygous or compound heterozygous for the common HADHA variant c.1528G-C (E510Q). The other 2 patients did not carry it. One was a female with a c.901G-A (G301S) variant and an unidentified second allele. The other was a male who was compound heterozygous for c.1150-1G-T and c.208T-C (S70P). These variants had previously been reported as pathogenic and associated with a TFP deficiency phenotype. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Sims et al. (1995) concluded that mutations in the LCHAD domain of the trifunctional protein alpha subunit in affected offspring are associated with maternal acute fatty liver of pregnancy. This was the first delineation of the molecular basis of isolated LCHAD deficiency.

Ibdah et al. (1999) found that 79% of heterozygous mothers had acute fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome while carrying fetuses with the glu474-to-gln mutation. The authors concluded that women with acute liver disease during pregnancy may carry this mutation and that their infants are at risk for hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fatty liver.

Elizondo et al. (2020) found that the 9 patients who carried the common c.1528G-C variant on at least one HADHA allele had consistently higher C16-OH, C18-OH, and C18:1-OH levels than the 2 patients without it, who had a TFP deficiency phenotype. Mean fasting C18:0-OH was 0.229 umol/L in carriers versus 0.016 umol/L in noncarriers, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09). The authors suggested that variants that destabilize the protein and abolish mitochondrial TFP expression produce low hydroxyacylcarnitine levels because hydratase activity is absent. As a result, the acylcarnitine profile in these patients more closely resembles that of VLCAD deficiency.

### Pathogenesis

Jackson et al. (1992) concluded that their patient had a combined defect of LCHAD, long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase, and long-chain 2-enoyl-CoA hydratase. They suggested that a multifunctional enzyme catalyzes these activities in human mitochondria and is deficient in the patient.

Wilcken et al. (1993) concluded that a fetus with LCHAD deficiency may adversely affect maternal liver function. They argued that maternal heterozygosity alone cannot account for these effects, because the pregnancy complications segregated with fetal LCHAD status.

Treem et al. (1994) found that the mother of their LCHAD-deficient infant was heterozygous for the defect. They hypothesized that some cases of acute fatty liver of pregnancy are caused by the interaction, in the late third trimester, between an affected fetus and a mother who is heterozygous for this fatty acid oxidation defect.

To study how pigmentary retinopathy arises in LCHAD deficiency, Tyni et al. (2002) cultured porcine retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells. These cells shortened [U-13C]-hexadecanoic acid normally and produced acetylcarnitine. They also had LCHAD enzyme activity, expressed the mitochondrial trifunctional protein (MTP), and could take up carnitine. In human retinal sections, MTP antibodies labeled the RPE, photoreceptors, and ganglion cells. The authors concluded that this was strong in vitro evidence for mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation in the RPE. They noted that further studies were needed to clarify whether the pathway also acts in the retina in vivo.

### Diagnosis

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) noted that LCHAD deficiency shares many clinical similarities with systemic carnitine deficiency and with CPT and LCAD deficiencies. They stated that differential diagnosis relies on showing long-chain 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids in the urine and on measuring enzyme activity in cultured fibroblasts.

Duran et al. (1991) noted that a phenylpropionic acid loading test, which revealed conjugated 3-hydroxyphenylpropionic acid (phenylhydracrylic acid) in the urine, provided a clue to the diagnosis of LCHAD deficiency. The diagnosis was subsequently confirmed by enzyme assay in fibroblasts.

IJlst et al. (1996) developed a method for detecting the G1528C mutation in genomic DNA. Because the mutation had an allele frequency of 87% among LCHAD-deficient patients, they suggested that the test was valuable for prenatal diagnosis.

Schrijver-Wieling et al. (1997) noted that patients tend to have no symptoms between attacks. They recommended that testing for LCHAD deficiency be included in the workup of children with retinal dystrophy, especially when other clinical symptoms have occurred. They stated that early recognition could increase life expectancy by avoiding catabolism and by using appropriate diets.

Carpenter and Wilcken (1999) reported a neonatal diagnosis of LCHAD deficiency and discussed its implications for newborn screening by tandem mass spectrometry.

Van Hove et al. (2000) found that, in plasma, hydroxy-C18:1 acylcarnitine above the 95th centile of controls, combined with elevation of 2 of the 3 acylcarnitines C14, C14:1, and hydroxy-C16, identified more than 85% of LCHAD-deficient patients with a false-positive rate below 0.1%. Diagnostic specificity was lower in blood spots than in plasma, because normal erythrocytes contain high endogenous levels of long-chain acylcarnitines. The profile was also diagnostic in asymptomatic patients and was not influenced by genotype.

Ibdah et al. (2001) performed molecular prenatal diagnosis in 9 pregnancies. Eight were in 6 families with isolated LCHAD deficiency and 1 was in a family with complete mitochondrial trifunctional protein (TFP) deficiency. Chorionic villus samples were used in 7 pregnancies and amniocytes in 2. The fetal genotype was identified in all 9 pregnancies: 2 fetuses were affected and those pregnancies were terminated, 2 had a normal genotype, and 5 were heterozygotes. Genotypes were confirmed by molecular analysis and enzymatic assays in the aborted fetuses and neonates. The 7 pregnancies in which heterozygous women carried fetuses with wildtype or heterozygous genotypes were uncomplicated, and all of these offspring were alive and apparently healthy. The authors concluded that molecular prenatal diagnosis is possible and valid in families with known TFP defects.

Elizondo et al. (2020) found that the newborn-screening discriminating ratios for LCHAD deficiency, C16-OH/C16 and C18-OH/C18, stayed elevated in treated patients. They concluded that disease-specific acylcarnitines vary widely through the day with feeding, fasting, and activity. They therefore recommended that blood for plasma acylcarnitine analysis be collected under standardized conditions, timed in relation to food intake and physical activity.

### Clinical Management

Based on an MCT load, Duran et al. (1991) prescribed a diet enriched in medium-chain triglycerides for their surviving patient. On this regimen the patient thrived, signs of cardiomyopathy disappeared, and liver function normalized.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported that only 1 of their 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency was alive, at 14 years of age, after dietary treatment for 9 years. All of the others died before 2 years of age. The authors suggested that early dietary management may change the otherwise poor prognosis.

Van Hove et al. (2000) found that a diet low in fat and high in medium-chain triglyceride lowered all disease-specific acylcarnitines in LCHAD-deficient patients, often to normal levels. They suggested that the assay is useful for monitoring treatment.

den Boer et al. (2002) concluded that prompt diagnosis can improve survival in LCHAD deficiency but that morbidity remains alarmingly high despite current therapeutic regimens.

Jones et al. (2003) used an in vitro model to study the effects of dietary treatment in LCHAD deficiency. They grew cultured skin fibroblasts from LCHAD-deficient and normal individuals in media supplemented with physiologic combinations of the medium-chain fatty acids octanoate and decanoate and the long-chain fatty acid palmitate. They measured 3-hydroxy-fatty acids in the medium by stable-isotope dilution gas chromatography/mass spectrometry. Adding medium-chain fatty acids decreased the accumulation of long-chain fatty acid oxidation intermediates in LCHAD-deficient cells, both in untreated medium and with added palmitate, and decanoate alone was more effective than octanoate alone. Over an extended time, a 1:3 ratio of octanoate to decanoate worked best in untreated medium, whereas a 1:1 ratio worked best in medium containing palmitate. Because all dietary medium-chain triglyceride preparations contain more octanoate than decanoate, the authors suggested that a preparation higher in decanoate may be more effective in reducing the accumulation of potentially toxic long-chain 3-hydroxy-fatty acids in LCHAD deficiency. ⚠︎

Elizondo et al. (2020) studied 30 treated patients with long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders, including LCHAD deficiency. Feeding consistently suppressed disease-specific acylcarnitines by about 60%, and fasting plasma free fatty acids correlated with the fasting sum of long-chain acylcarnitines. The authors concluded that these findings support fasting avoidance and frequent meals. All patients tolerated moderate exercise, which the authors said supported current recommendations that patients be physically active.

Guffon et al. (2021) retrospectively studied 18 French patients (8 female, 10 male) with long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (VLCAD, LCHAD, CACT, CPTII, and MTP deficiencies). The patients received triheptanoin through early access for a median of 22 months (range, 9-228 months). At the last consultation, triheptanoin supplied 15 to 35% of daily calories. In the year after starting triheptanoin, patients reported less intermittent snacking and fewer nocturnal meals, and 3 patients no longer had severe hypoglycemic events. Ten of 12 pediatric and 4 of 6 adult patients reported less fatigue and fewer, less severe episodes of myalgia. Five of the 6 patients who had needed a wheelchair no longer needed it. Emergency hospitalizations decreased, with none in pediatric patients, and annual days of emergency home care fell from 286 to 51. Adverse events were limited to digestive problems that resolved over time. The authors suggested that long-term triheptanoin is safe and markedly improves symptoms and quality of life.

### Population Genetics

IJlst et al. (1994) found the G1528C mutation in the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein in all 26 unrelated LCHAD-deficient patients they studied. Twenty-four expressed only the mutant allele and 2 were heterozygous. The mutation was absent from 55 controls, indicating that it is highly frequent among patients with LCHAD deficiency.

IJlst et al. (1996) stated that LCHAD deficiency is a very frequent inborn error of fatty acid oxidation, second only to medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, GLU510GLN [c.1526G>C; c.1528G>C; p.E474Q; p.E510Q]

IJlst et al. (1994) sequenced the alpha and beta subunit cDNAs of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein in patients with LCHAD deficiency. They identified a G-to-C mutation at nucleotide 1528 (G1528C) in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase-encoding region of the HADHA gene, resulting in a glu510-to-gln (E510Q) substitution. The mutation was not found in 55 controls.

A third child was compound heterozygous for E474Q and a 1132C-T mutation that creates a premature stop codon at residue 342. IJlst et al. (1996) found the G1528C mutation at an allele frequency of 87% in 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency. Expression of wildtype and mutant proteins in S. cerevisiae showed that the mutation directly causes the loss of LCHAD activity.

Tyni et al. (1997) found that all 13 of their patients with LCHAD deficiency were homozygous for the G1528C mutation.
