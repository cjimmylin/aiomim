### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (LCHAD deficiency) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the HADHA gene (600890). HADHA encodes the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein.

### Description

Long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency is an autosomal recessive inborn error of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (den Boer et al., 2002). Sewell et al. (1994) described it as a newly recognized fatty acid oxidation disorder that is usually fatal. Most patients lack only the LCHAD activity of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein (MTP), and the long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase and long-chain 3-ketothiolase activities are near normal (IJlst et al., 1996). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Wanders et al. (1989) discussed long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase in relation to sudden infant death.

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) reported a child who presented in early childhood with episodes of coma and hypoglycemia and a rapidly progressive myopathy and cardiomyopathy. The child died at 9 months of age. Ketosis was decreased (blood beta-hydroxybutyrate, 0.07 mmol/L) even though plasma fatty acid levels were normal (0.81 mmol/L). The patient had C6-C10 dicarboxylic aciduria, and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry showed accumulation of medium- and long-chain (C8 to C14) 3-hydroxy-dicarboxylic acids in the urine.

Hagenfeldt et al. (1990) described 5 patients with a suspected beta-oxidation defect. During acute illness the patients excreted massive amounts of 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids in the urine and accumulated 3-hydroxy fatty acids in serum. Four of the patients died of cardiomyopathy and liver insufficiency at 3 to 14 months of age. Two of the patients had older sibs who had died unexpectedly in early infancy.

Jackson et al. (1991) described 2 children with LCHAD deficiency who both presented with an acute metabolic crisis. Both had hypoglycemia, and both excreted even-chain unsubstituted dicarboxylic acids and 3-hydroxy-dicarboxylic acids in the urine.

Duran et al. (1991) reported 2 sibs with LCHAD deficiency. The older sib died suddenly and unexpectedly on the third day of life with extreme hypoketotic hypoglycemia. The younger sib, a girl, developed feeding problems, lowered consciousness, and liver dysfunction at 5 months of age. Her urine contained large amounts of C6-C14 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids and conjugated 3-hydroxyoctanoic acid, and her plasma long-chain acylcarnitine was increased. LCHAD activity was deficient in her cultured skin fibroblasts.

Jackson et al. (1992) reported a young girl who had recurrent episodes of muscle weakness, culminating in a severe attack of generalized muscle weakness. Her muscle mitochondria accumulated 3-hydroxyacyl- and 2-enoyl-CoA and carnitine esters and 3-oxoacylcarnitines. LCHAD activity was low in mitochondria from all tissues, and long-chain 2-enoyl-CoA hydratase and long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase activities were also low (see BIOCHEMICAL FEATURES).

Bertini et al. (1992) reported an 11-month-old girl with acute episodes of hypoglycemia, hepatic encephalopathy reminiscent of Reye syndrome, and 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. She had severe sensory-motor neuropathy, pigmentary retinopathy, and cardiomyopathy, and she died of cardiac failure. Peripheral nerve showed signs of axonal neuropathy and demyelination. LCHAD activity was deficient in her cultured fibroblasts. The authors noted that peripheral nerve involvement and retinal pigmentary degeneration had not previously been described in patients with proven defects of mitochondrial beta-oxidation. ⚠︎

Wilcken et al. (1993) reported 11 pregnancies in 5 mothers, 6 of which produced a baby with LCHAD deficiency. All 6 pregnancies with an LCHAD-deficient fetus were complicated by features such as maternal acute fatty liver and HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets) syndrome. In 3 of the mothers, the pregnancies with unaffected babies were largely uncomplicated. ⚠︎

Sewell et al. (1994) reported a patient who presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, hepatopathy, secondary carnitine deficiency, increased plasma long-chain acylcarnitines, and 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. The diagnosis was confirmed in cultured skin fibroblasts.

Fryburg et al. (1994) described a black boy with findings of Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome (BRRS) who also had LCHAD deficiency in cultured skin fibroblasts. He also had an abnormal brain MRI and increased size of both lower limbs. The authors proposed that LCHAD deficiency might cause the lipid storage myopathy seen in BRRS.

Treem et al. (1994) reported a woman with acute fatty liver of pregnancy who gave birth to a seemingly normal full-term infant. At 4 months of age the infant presented with hypoglycemia, coma, and profound hepatic steatosis and was found to have LCHAD deficiency. The mother was shown to be heterozygous for the defect.

Sims et al. (1995) reported 3 families whose children had LCHAD deficiency and presented with sudden unexplained death or with hypoglycemia and abnormal liver enzymes (a Reye-like syndrome). In all 3 families, the mothers had acute fatty liver and associated severe complications during their pregnancies with the affected infants.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency, all homozygous for the G1528C mutation. In the first 2 years of life the patients had hypoglycemia, cardiomyopathy, muscle hypotonia, and hepatomegaly. Seven patients had recurrent metabolic crises, and 6 had a steadily progressive course. Two had cholestatic liver disease, which the authors noted is uncommon in beta-oxidation defects. One patient had peripheral neuropathy. Six had retinopathy with focal pigmentary aggregations or retinal hypopigmentation. Dicarboxylic aciduria was found in 9 of 10 patients tested. Some patients had lactic acidosis, increased serum creatine kinase, or low serum carnitine. Three patients had bilateral periventricular or focal cortical brain lesions, and 1 had brain atrophy. Only 1 patient was alive, at 14 years of age, after 9 years of dietary treatment; all the others died before 2 years of age. ⚠︎

Schrijver-Wieling et al. (1997) reported 2 girls with LCHAD deficiency, aged 8 and 15 years. Both had extensive macular pigmentary deposits and a 'salt and pepper' scattering of pigment in the retina, with decreasing visual acuity.

Uusimaa et al. (1997) reported 2 cases of L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency with pigmentary retinopathy.

Ibdah et al. (1999) studied 24 children with 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency. The 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fatty liver. The 5 children with complete trifunctional protein deficiency presented with neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy or progressive neuromyopathy. Of the heterozygous mothers who carried fetuses with the glu474-to-gln mutation, 79% had acute fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome during those pregnancies. None of the mothers of the 5 children with complete trifunctional protein deficiency had liver disease during pregnancy.

In a separate report, Ibdah et al. (1999) described a proband who presented at 2 months of age with acute infantile hypocalcemia and vitamin D deficiency, associated with occult, unexplained cholestatic liver disease. The infant died suddenly and unexpectedly at 8 months. The mother had preeclampsia in the third trimester of this pregnancy. In a subsequent pregnancy she developed severe acute fatty liver of pregnancy, with intrauterine fetal death at 33 weeks' gestation. The authors concluded that fetal LCHAD deficiency is associated with a spectrum of maternal illness ranging from preeclampsia to life-threatening acute fatty liver of pregnancy.

Thiel et al. (1999) reported a neonate with LCHAD deficiency who presented with tachypnea on the first day of life.

Ibdah et al. (2001) found that women heterozygous for mutations in the trifunctional protein alpha subunit had uncomplicated pregnancies when the fetus was wildtype or heterozygous. All 7 such offspring were alive and apparently healthy.

den Boer et al. (2002) reviewed questionnaires on 50 patients with LCHAD deficiency. The mean age at presentation was 5.8 months (range, 1 day to 26 months), and 7 patients (15%) presented in the neonatal period. Thirty-nine patients (78%) presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia. Eleven (22%) presented with chronic problems: failure to thrive, feeding difficulties, cholestatic liver disease, and/or hypotonia. In retrospect, 82% of the patients who presented with acute metabolic derangement had had chronic nonspecific symptoms before the crises. Mortality was 38%, and all deaths occurred before or within 3 months after diagnosis. The surviving patients had recurrent metabolic crises and muscle problems despite therapy.

### Biochemical Features

In cultured fibroblasts from their patient, Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) found impaired medium- and long-chain fatty acid oxidation and deficient LCHAD activity. Activities of long-, medium-, and short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenases and of 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase were normal. ⚠︎

Hagenfeldt et al. (1990) found normal long-chain and medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase activities in fibroblasts from 5 patients with 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. They concluded that these patients differed from previously described patients with beta-oxidation defects.

Jackson et al. (1991) found low LCHAD activity but normal short-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from 2 patients. They characterized the defect further by immunoprecipitating the short-chain enzyme with monospecific antibodies.

In the patient of Jackson et al. (1992), long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase activity was low in fibroblast, muscle, and cardiac mitochondria when measured with 3-oxohexadecanoyl-CoA, but only partially deficient with 3-oxooctanoyl-CoA as substrate. Long-chain 2-enoyl-CoA hydratase activity was also low in muscle mitochondria.

Wanders et al. (1992) studied a patient with clear manifestations of a mitochondrial beta-oxidation disorder. The patient's fibroblasts showed impaired palmitate beta-oxidation and combined deficiency of long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase, LCHAD, and long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase. Size-exclusion chromatography showed that the fibroblasts lacked the membrane-bound multifunctional beta-oxidation enzyme protein. The authors described this as a new disorder, trifunctional protein deficiency. ⚠︎

IJlst et al. (1996) noted that most patients lack only the LCHAD activity of MTP, while the long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase and long-chain 3-ketothiolase activities are near normal. However, Tyni et al. (1997) found that in skin fibroblasts from LCHAD-deficient patients homozygous for G1528C, the enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase activities of MTP were variably decreased.

Van Hove et al. (2000) reviewed plasma and blood spot acylcarnitines in 23 patients with proven LCHAD deficiency. Long-chain 3-hydroxyacylcarnitines (C14:1, C14, C16, and C18:1) and long-chain acylcarnitines (C12, C14:1, C14, C16, C18:2, and C18:1) were elevated, and acetylcarnitine was decreased.

Elizondo et al. (2020) studied 11 treated patients with LCHAD or trifunctional protein deficiency who were on a controlled low-fat diet with medium-chain triglycerides (MCT). The most prominent fasting long-chain acylcarnitine was C18:1-OH (0.56 +/- 0.52 micromol/L). C16-OH and C18-OH were also above the reference range, and the hydroxyacylcarnitines remained elevated despite treatment. C18:1-OH fell by 59% after breakfast and rose only 20% after moderate exercise. This exercise response was smaller and more consistent than in CPT2 or VLCAD deficiency.

### Inheritance

Jackson et al. (1991) concluded that LCHAD deficiency is probably inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, because fibroblasts from the parents of 1 affected child showed intermediate enzyme activity. Jackson et al. (1992) similarly found that LCHAD and long-chain thiolase activities in fibroblasts from their patient's parents were intermediate between those of controls and the patient. Treem et al. (1994) showed that the mother of an affected infant was heterozygous for the defect. den Boer et al. (2002) described LCHAD deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder.

Baskin et al. (2010) reported a child who was homozygous for an HADHA mutation that only the father carried. The homozygosity resulted from paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 2 (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

### Mapping

IJlst et al. (1996) mapped the gene encoding the MTP alpha subunit to chromosome 2p24.1-p23.3.

### Molecular Genetics

In patients with LCHAD deficiency, IJlst et al. (1994) sequenced cDNAs encoding the alpha and beta subunits of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein. They identified a 1528G-C change in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase coding region of the alpha subunit (HADHA). The change resulted in a glu510-to-gln (E510Q) substitution and created a PstI site. RFLP analysis showed that 24 of 26 unrelated patients expressed only C1528 and the other 2 were heterozygous for the mutation.

Sims et al. (1995) used SSCP analysis of the exons of the trifunctional protein alpha subunit gene to study children with LCHAD deficiency from 3 families. Two affected children were homozygous for the G1528C mutation in the LCHAD domain, which in their numbering substituted glutamine for glutamic acid at position 474 of the mature alpha subunit (E474Q). The third child was compound heterozygous for G1528C and a 1132C-T transition that created a premature termination codon at residue 342. The authors concluded that mutations in the LCHAD domain in affected offspring are associated with maternal acute fatty liver of pregnancy.

IJlst et al. (1996) expressed wildtype and mutant MTP alpha subunit in S. cerevisiae and showed that the G1528C mutation directly caused the loss of LCHAD activity. In 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency, the G1528C allele frequency was 87%.

All 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency reported by Tyni et al. (1997) were homozygous for the G1528C mutation.

Among 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency, Ibdah et al. (1999) found 8 who were homozygous for the glu474-to-gln mutation. The other 11 were compound heterozygous for this mutation and a different mutation on the other allele of the alpha-subunit gene. None of 5 children with complete trifunctional protein deficiency carried the glu474-to-gln mutation.

In a second report, Ibdah et al. (1999) found that an infant with LCHAD deficiency, hypocalcemia, and cholestatic liver disease was homozygous for the 1528G-C (E474Q) mutation.

Baskin et al. (2010) studied a 22-month-old child identified by expanded newborn screening, which showed an abnormal acylcarnitine pattern with increased C14OH. The child was homozygous for the common HADHA mutation, reported as c.1526G-C (p.Glu510Gln). Carrier testing showed that the father was heterozygous and the mother did not carry the mutation. Short tandem repeat analysis with markers on both arms of chromosome 2 showed paternal uniparental isodisomy. The authors stressed the importance of testing the parents when a patient with an autosomal recessive disorder is homozygous.

Elizondo et al. (2020) studied 9 patients with LCHAD deficiency, aged 7 to 29 years, who carried the c.1528G-C (E510Q) mutation in HADHA. Five were homozygous. One was compound heterozygous with a c.703C-T (R235W) mutation, and 1 was compound heterozygous with a c.479_482TAG-AATA frameshift mutation (I160fs). In 2 patients the second allele was not identified.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ibdah et al. (1999) studied 24 children with 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency. The 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency had hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fatty liver and carried the glu474-to-gln mutation on 1 or both alleles. The 5 children with complete trifunctional protein deficiency had neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy or progressive neuromyopathy and did not carry the glu474-to-gln mutation. The authors concluded that women with acute liver disease during pregnancy may carry the glu474-to-gln mutation, and that their infants are at risk for hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fatty liver.

Elizondo et al. (2020) found that the 9 patients with the c.1528G-C (E510Q) mutation on at least 1 allele had higher C16-OH, C18-OH, and C18:1-OH levels than the 2 patients without it, who had a trifunctional protein deficiency phenotype. The difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09). The authors suggested that mutations that destabilize the trifunctional protein abolish hydratase activity and so lower the hydroxyacylcarnitine species, giving a profile more like that of VLCAD deficiency.

### Pathogenesis

Jackson et al. (1992) concluded that their patient had a combined defect of LCHAD, long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase, and long-chain 2-enoyl-CoA hydratase, apparently inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. They suggested that a single multifunctional enzyme catalyzes these activities in human mitochondria and that this enzyme was deficient in the patient.

Wilcken et al. (1993) concluded that a fetus with LCHAD deficiency may harm maternal liver function. They argued that maternal heterozygosity alone cannot explain these effects, because the effects segregated with the LCHAD status of the fetus. Treem et al. (1994) hypothesized that some cases of acute fatty liver of pregnancy result from the interaction of an LCHAD-deficient fetus and a heterozygous mother in the late third trimester.

To investigate the pathogenesis of pigmentary retinopathy in LCHAD deficiency, Tyni et al. (2002) studied cultured porcine retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells. The cells showed normal chain shortening of [U-13C]-hexadecanoic acid with acetylcarnitine production, as well as LCHAD activity, MTP expression, and carnitine uptake. MTP immunolabeling was detected in the RPE, photoreceptors, and ganglion cells of human retinal sections. The authors concluded that these findings gave strong in vitro evidence for mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation in RPE cells, but that further studies were needed to determine whether the pathway also operates in the retina in vivo.

### Diagnosis

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) noted that LCHAD deficiency is clinically similar to systemic carnitine deficiency and to CPT and LCAD deficiencies. They noted that the differential diagnosis relies on showing long-chain 3-hydroxy-dicarboxylic acids in the urine and on measuring enzyme activity in cultured fibroblasts.

In the patient of Duran et al. (1991), a phenylpropionic acid loading test gave the first clue to the diagnosis. The test showed small but significant amounts of conjugated 3-hydroxyphenylpropionic acid (phenylhydracrylic acid) in the urine, and deficient LCHAD activity was then shown in cultured skin fibroblasts. ⚠︎

IJlst et al. (1996) developed a method to detect the G1528C mutation in genomic DNA. Because the mutation is so common, they proposed it as a useful test for prenatal diagnosis.

Schrijver-Wieling et al. (1997) recommended including LCHAD deficiency in the diagnostic evaluation of children with retinal dystrophy, particularly when other clinical symptoms have occurred. They noted that patients tend to be symptom-free between attacks.

Carpenter and Wilcken (1999) reported the neonatal diagnosis of LCHAD deficiency and discussed what it means for newborn screening by tandem mass spectrometry.

In plasma, Van Hove et al. (2000) found that a combination of markers identified more than 85% of LCHAD-deficient patients with less than 0.1% false positives. The criteria were hydroxy-C18:1 acylcarnitine above the 95th centile of controls together with elevation of 2 of the 3 acylcarnitines C14, C14:1, and hydroxy-C16. Blood spots were less specific because normal erythrocytes contain high levels of long-chain acylcarnitines. The results were diagnostic in asymptomatic patients and were not influenced by genotype. ⚠︎

Ibdah et al. (2001) performed molecular prenatal diagnosis in 9 pregnancies: 8 in 6 families with isolated LCHAD deficiency and 1 in a family with complete trifunctional protein deficiency. Chorionic villus samples were used in 7 pregnancies and amniocytes in 2. The fetal genotype was determined in all 9 pregnancies: 2 fetuses were affected, 2 had a normal genotype, and 5 were heterozygous. Molecular analysis and enzyme assays on the aborted fetuses and the neonates confirmed these genotypes.

### Clinical Management

On the basis of a medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) load, Duran et al. (1991) prescribed an MCT-enriched diet for their patient. On this diet the patient began to thrive, the signs of cardiomyopathy disappeared, and liver function returned to normal.

Tyni et al. (1997) noted that the only survivor among their 13 patients had received dietary treatment for 9 years and was alive at 14 years of age. They emphasized that early recognition allows dietary management that may alter the otherwise invariably poor prognosis. Schrijver-Wieling et al. (1997) stated that early recognition can increase life expectancy through avoidance of catabolism and appropriate diets.

Van Hove et al. (2000) found that a diet low in fat and high in MCT decreased all disease-specific acylcarnitines, often to normal. They suggested that the assay is useful for monitoring treatment.

den Boer et al. (2002) concluded that prompt diagnosis can improve survival. However, morbidity, including recurrent metabolic crises and muscle problems, remained high despite current therapy.

Jones et al. (2003) grew skin fibroblasts from LCHAD-deficient and normal individuals in medium containing octanoate, decanoate, and palmitate. Adding medium-chain fatty acids reduced the accumulation of long-chain 3-hydroxy fatty acid oxidation intermediates in LCHAD-deficient cells, and decanoate alone worked better than octanoate alone. Over an extended period, a 1:3 ratio of octanoate to decanoate worked best in untreated medium, whereas a 1:1 ratio worked best in medium containing palmitate. The authors noted that all dietary MCT preparations contain more octanoate than decanoate. They suggested that a preparation richer in decanoate may be more effective at reducing potentially toxic long-chain 3-hydroxy fatty acids. ⚠︎

Elizondo et al. (2020) found that plasma acylcarnitines in patients with long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders, including LCHAD deficiency, varied widely through the day. Feeding lowered them by about 60%, and exercise raised them. The authors questioned the usefulness of random outpatient acylcarnitine monitoring and recommended standardized sampling timed to food intake and physical activity. All patients tolerated moderate exercise, which the authors took as support for advising patients to stay physically active.

Guffon et al. (2021) retrospectively analyzed 18 French patients (8 female, 10 male) with long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders, including VLCAD, LCHAD, CACT, CPT II, and MTP deficiencies. The patients were treated with triheptanoin for a median of 22 months (range, 9 to 228 months), and triheptanoin made up 15 to 35% of daily caloric intake. In the year after starting triheptanoin, 10 of 12 pediatric and 4 of 6 adult patients reported less fatigue and fewer, less severe episodes of myalgia. Three patients no longer had severe hypoglycemic events, and 5 of 6 patients who had needed a wheelchair no longer did. Emergency hospitalizations decreased, and no pediatric patient had an emergency hospitalization in the following 12 months. Cumulative annual days of emergency home care fell from 286 to 51. Adverse events were limited to digestive issues that resolved over time. The authors concluded that long-term triheptanoin treatment appeared safe and improved symptoms. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

IJlst et al. (1994) did not find the G1528C mutation in 55 control subjects. They concluded that the mutation was highly frequent among patients with LCHAD deficiency. IJlst et al. (1996) found an allele frequency of 87% for G1528C in 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, GLU510GLN [c.1526G>C; c.1528G>C; p.E474Q; p.E510Q]

IJlst et al. (1994) identified a 1528G-C change in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase coding region of the HADHA gene in patients with LCHAD deficiency. The change resulted in a glu510-to-gln (E510Q) substitution and created a PstI site. RFLP analysis showed that 24 of 26 unrelated patients expressed only C1528 and the other 2 were heterozygous. The mutation was not found in 55 control subjects.

Sims et al. (1995) found the G1528C mutation, which they numbered glu474-to-gln (E474Q) in the mature alpha subunit, in homozygous state in 2 children with LCHAD deficiency. In a third child it was present in compound heterozygosity with a 1132C-T nonsense mutation. The mothers had acute fatty liver of pregnancy.

IJlst et al. (1996) found that the G1528C allele had a frequency of 87% in 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency. Expression in S. cerevisiae showed that the mutation was directly responsible for the loss of LCHAD activity.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency who were homozygous for the G1528C mutation. They had hypoglycemia, cardiomyopathy, hypotonia, and hepatomegaly, and 12 died before 2 years of age.

Ibdah et al. (1999) found the glu474-to-gln mutation in 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency, 8 homozygous and 11 compound heterozygous. Of the heterozygous mothers who carried fetuses with the mutation, 79% had acute fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome.

In a separate report, Ibdah et al. (1999) found homozygosity for the 1528G-C (E474Q) mutation in an infant who presented at 2 months of age with hypocalcemia, vitamin D deficiency, and cholestatic liver disease and died suddenly at 8 months. The mother had preeclampsia during this pregnancy.

Baskin et al. (2010) found homozygosity for this mutation, reported as c.1526G-C (p.Glu510Gln), in a child with LCHAD deficiency identified by newborn screening. Only the father carried the mutation, and the homozygosity resulted from paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 2.

Elizondo et al. (2020) reported 9 patients with LCHAD deficiency who carried c.1528G-C (E510Q). Five were homozygous, 2 were compound heterozygous with a second HADHA mutation, and in 2 the second allele was not identified.

**.0002 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, 1132C-T [c.1132C>T]

In a child with LCHAD deficiency whose mother had acute fatty liver of pregnancy, Sims et al. (1995) identified a 1132C-T transition in the HADHA gene that created a premature termination codon at residue 342. The child carried the G1528C mutation on the other allele.

**.0003 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, ARG235TRP [c.703C>T; p.R235W]

Elizondo et al. (2020) reported a 7-year-old boy with LCHAD deficiency who was compound heterozygous for a c.703C-T transition in the HADHA gene, resulting in an arg235-to-trp (R235W) substitution, and the c.1528G-C (E510Q) mutation.

**.0004 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, 479TAG-AATA [p.I160fs]
