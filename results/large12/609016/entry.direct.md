### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (LCHADD) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the HADHA gene, which encodes the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, on chromosome 2p24.1-p23.3.

### Description

Long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder of mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation (den Boer et al., 2002; Baskin et al., 2010). It usually presents in infancy or early childhood with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, hepatic dysfunction, cardiomyopathy, and hypotonia or myopathy. It may lead to sudden death (Rocchiccioli et al., 1990; Tyni et al., 1997; den Boer et al., 2002). Pigmentary retinopathy and peripheral neuropathy are also features (Bertini et al., 1992; Tyni et al., 1997). Pregnancies with an affected fetus may be complicated by maternal acute fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome (Wilcken et al., 1993; Ibdah et al., 1999). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

The title of the report by Wanders et al. (1989) linked long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase to sudden infant death.

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) described a child who presented in early childhood with episodes of coma and hypoglycemia, and with a myopathy and cardiomyopathy that progressed rapidly and led to death at 9 months of age. Ketosis was decreased (blood beta-hydroxybutyrate, 0.07 mmol/L) even though plasma fatty acid levels were normal (0.81 mmol/L). The urine contained excessive amounts of C6 to C10 dicarboxylic acids, and there was an accumulation of medium- and long-chain (C8 to C14) 3-hydroxy-dicarboxylic acids. Studies in cultured fibroblasts showed impaired oxidation of medium- and long-chain fatty acids and deficiency of LCHAD. The activities of long-, medium-, and short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenases and of 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase were normal. The authors noted that the disorder affects the liver, skeletal muscle, and myocardium.

Hagenfeldt et al. (1990) described 5 patients with a suspected defect in fatty acid beta-oxidation. The patients excreted massive amounts of 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids in the urine and accumulated 3-hydroxy fatty acids in serum during acute illness. Long-chain and medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase activities in fibroblasts were normal. Four of the patients died of cardiomyopathy and liver insufficiency at 3 to 14 months of age. Two of the patients had older sibs who had died unexpectedly in early infancy.

Jackson et al. (1991) described 2 children with LCHAD deficiency who presented with an acute metabolic crisis. Both had hypoglycemia and excreted even-chain unsubstituted dicarboxylic acids and 3-hydroxy-dicarboxylic acids in the urine. Cultured skin fibroblasts showed low LCHAD activity and normal short-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase activity.

Duran et al. (1991) reported 2 sibs with LCHAD deficiency. One died suddenly and unexpectedly on the third day of life with extreme hypoketotic hypoglycemia. The younger sib developed feeding problems, lowered consciousness, and liver dysfunction at 5 months of age. Her urine contained large amounts of C6-C14 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids and conjugated 3-hydroxyoctanoic acid, and her plasma long-chain acylcarnitine was increased. Cardiomyopathy was also present and resolved with treatment.

Bertini et al. (1992) reported an 11-month-old girl with LCHAD deficiency, confirmed in cultured fibroblasts. She had acute episodes of hypoglycemia and hepatic encephalopathy resembling Reye syndrome, together with 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. She also had severe sensorimotor neuropathy, pigmentary retinopathy, and cardiomyopathy, and she died of cardiac failure. Peripheral nerve pathology showed signs of axonal neuropathy and demyelination.

Wilcken et al. (1993) reported 11 pregnancies in 5 mothers. Six of the babies had LCHAD deficiency, and each of these pregnancies was complicated by features such as fatty liver and HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets). Three of the mothers also had unaffected babies, and those pregnancies were largely uncomplicated. Because the maternal complications segregated with the LCHAD status of the fetus, the authors concluded that heterozygosity in the mother could not by itself account for them.

Sewell et al. (1994) reported a patient who presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, hepatopathy, secondary carnitine deficiency, increased plasma long-chain acylcarnitines, and 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. The diagnosis was confirmed in cultured skin fibroblasts.

Fryburg et al. (1994) described a black boy with findings of Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome who also had LCHAD deficiency in cultured skin fibroblasts. He also had an abnormal brain MRI and enlargement of both lower limbs. The authors postulated that LCHAD deficiency may cause the lipid myopathy seen in Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome.

Treem et al. (1994) reported a woman with acute fatty liver of pregnancy who gave birth to an apparently normal full-term infant. At 4 months of age the infant presented with hypoglycemia, coma, and profound hepatic steatosis and was found to have LCHAD deficiency. The mother was shown to be heterozygous for the defect.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency, all homozygous for the G1528C mutation. At presentation the patients had had hypoglycemia, cardiomyopathy, muscle hypotonia, and hepatomegaly during the first 2 years of life. Seven patients had recurrent metabolic crises and 6 had a steadily progressive course. Two patients had cholestatic liver disease, 1 had peripheral neuropathy, and 6 had retinopathy with focal pigmentary aggregations or retinal hypopigmentation. Dicarboxylic aciduria was found in 9 of 10 patients. Most patients had lactic acidosis, increased serum creatine kinase, and low serum carnitine. Three patients had bilateral periventricular or focal cortical lesions on neuroimaging, and 1 had brain atrophy. Only 1 patient, who had received dietary treatment for 9 years, was alive (at age 14 years); all the others died before 2 years of age.

Schrijver-Wieling et al. (1997) reported 2 girls with LCHAD deficiency, aged 8 and 15 years. Both had extensive macular pigmentary deposits and 'salt and pepper' scattering of pigment in the retina, with decreasing visual acuity. Uusimaa et al. (1997) reported 2 further cases of LCHAD deficiency with pigmentary retinopathy.

Ibdah et al. (1999) reported a patient with LCHAD deficiency who presented at 2 months of age with acute infantile hypocalcemia and vitamin D deficiency associated with occult cholestatic liver disease. The patient died suddenly and unexpectedly at 8 months. The patient was homozygous for the E474Q mutation. The mother had preeclampsia during the third trimester of that pregnancy. In a later pregnancy she developed severe acute fatty liver of pregnancy, with intrauterine fetal death at 33 weeks' gestation.

Thiel et al. (1999) reported LCHAD deficiency that manifested on the first day of life with tachypnea.

den Boer et al. (2002) reviewed 50 patients with LCHAD deficiency. The mean age at clinical presentation was 5.8 months (range, 1 day to 26 months), and 7 (15%) presented in the neonatal period. Thirty-nine patients (78%) presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia. Eleven (22%) presented with chronic problems: failure to thrive, feeding difficulties, cholestatic liver disease, and/or hypotonia. Looking back, 82% of the patients who presented with acute metabolic derangement had also had a combination of chronic nonspecific symptoms before the crises. Mortality was 38%, and all deaths occurred before diagnosis or within 3 months after it. Surviving patients had high morbidity, with recurrent metabolic crises and muscle problems despite therapy.

### Biochemical Features

Jackson et al. (1991) characterized the defect further by using monospecific antibodies to immunoprecipitate the short-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase. Tyni et al. (1997) found that all their patients were homozygous for G1528C, but the enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase activities of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein were decreased to a variable degree in skin fibroblasts.

IJlst et al. (1996) noted that the mitochondrial trifunctional protein carries long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase, LCHAD, and long-chain 3-ketothiolase activities. In most patients only the LCHAD activity is deficient, and the other 2 activities are near normal.

Van Hove et al. (2000) reviewed acylcarnitines in plasma and blood spots from 23 patients with proven LCHAD deficiency. Long-chain 3-hydroxyacylcarnitines (C14:1, C14, C16, and C18:1) and long-chain acylcarnitines (C12, C14:1, C14, C16, C18:2, and C18:1) were elevated, and acetylcarnitine was decreased.

Elizondo et al. (2020) measured plasma acylcarnitines in 11 treated subjects with LCHAD or trifunctional protein deficiency. The most prominent fasting long-chain species was C18:1-OH (0.56 +/- 0.52 micromol/L; reference range, 0.00-0.05 micromol/L). C16-OH and C18-OH were also above the reference range. C18:1-OH fell by 59% after breakfast but rose only 20% after moderate-intensity exercise. The C16-OH/C16 and C18-OH/C18 ratios remained elevated despite treatment.

### Inheritance

Jackson et al. (1991) found intermediate enzyme activity in fibroblasts from the parents of 1 affected child and suggested that LCHAD deficiency is probably inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. den Boer et al. (2002) and Baskin et al. (2010) described LCHAD deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder.

### Cytogenetics

Baskin et al. (2010) reported a 22-month-old child identified by expanded newborn screening, who had an abnormal acylcarnitine pattern with increased C14OH. The child was homozygous for the common HADHA c.1526G-C (p.Glu510Gln) mutation. The father was heterozygous and the mother did not carry the mutation. Short tandem repeat markers covering both arms of chromosome 2 showed paternal uniparental isodisomy. The authors emphasized the importance of testing the parents when a patient with an autosomal recessive disorder is homozygous.

### Mapping

IJlst et al. (1996) showed that the gene encoding the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein is located on chromosome 2p24.1-p23.3.

### Heterogeneity

Jackson et al. (1992) described a young girl with recurrent episodes of muscle weakness, ending in a severe attack of generalized weakness. She had combined deficiency of LCHAD, long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase, and long-chain 2-enoyl-CoA hydratase, apparently inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. The authors suggested that a single multifunctional enzyme catalyzes these activities in human mitochondria. Wanders et al. (1992) found the same combined deficiency in fibroblasts from another patient. Using size-exclusion chromatography, they showed that this patient lacked the multifunctional beta-oxidation enzyme protein, and they designated the disorder trifunctional protein deficiency.

Among 24 children with 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency, Ibdah et al. (1999) found 19 with isolated LCHAD deficiency, who presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fatty liver. Five others presented with neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy or progressive neuromyopathy and had complete trifunctional protein deficiency, with loss of all 3 enzyme activities. None of these 5 children had the E474Q mutation, and none of their mothers had liver disease during pregnancy.

### Molecular Genetics

IJlst et al. (1994) sequenced the cDNAs encoding the alpha and beta subunits of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein in patients with LCHAD deficiency. They identified a G-to-C mutation at nucleotide 1528 in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase coding region of the alpha subunit (HADHA), resulting in a glu510-to-gln substitution. The change creates a PstI restriction site. RFLP analysis showed that 24 of 26 unrelated patients expressed only the C1528 allele and the other 2 were heterozygous for the mutation. The mutation was not found in 55 controls. ⚠︎

Sims et al. (1995) used SSCP analysis of the exons of the HADHA gene to study 3 families in which children presented with sudden unexplained death or with hypoglycemia and abnormal liver enzymes (Reye-like syndrome). In all 3 families the mothers had had acute fatty liver of pregnancy with severe complications while carrying the affected infants. Two affected children were homozygous for the G1528C mutation, which substitutes glutamine for glutamic acid at position 474 of the mature alpha subunit (E474Q). The third child was compound heterozygous for G1528C and a C1132T mutation that creates a premature termination codon at residue 342.

IJlst et al. (1996) expressed wildtype and mutant HADHA in S. cerevisiae and showed that the G1528C mutation is directly responsible for the loss of LCHAD activity. They developed a method to detect the mutation in genomic DNA and found an allele frequency of 87% for G1528C among 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency.

Tyni et al. (1997) found that all 13 of their patients with LCHAD deficiency were homozygous for the G1528C mutation.

Ibdah et al. (1999) used DNA amplification and sequencing to analyze the HADHA gene in 24 children with 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency. Of the 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency, 8 were homozygous for the E474Q mutation. The other 11 were compound heterozygous for E474Q and a different mutation on the other allele.

In a patient with LCHAD deficiency who presented with infantile hypocalcemia and cholestasis, Ibdah et al. (1999) found homozygosity for the 1528G-C (E474Q) mutation.

Baskin et al. (2010) found homozygosity for the c.1526G-C (p.Glu510Gln) mutation in a child with LCHAD deficiency. The homozygosity resulted from paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 2.

In a study of 11 subjects with LCHAD or trifunctional protein deficiency, Elizondo et al. (2020) found that 9 were either homozygous or compound heterozygous for the common HADHA c.1528G-C mutation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ibdah et al. (1999) found that 79% of heterozygous mothers carrying fetuses with the E474Q mutation had acute fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome during those pregnancies. Five children with complete trifunctional protein deficiency did not carry E474Q, and none of their mothers had liver disease during pregnancy.

Van Hove et al. (2000) found that acylcarnitine results were not influenced by genotype. In contrast, Elizondo et al. (2020) found that patients carrying the common c.1528G-C mutation on at least 1 allele had consistently higher levels of C16-OH, C18:0-OH, and C18:1-OH than 2 patients who lacked the common mutation and had a trifunctional protein deficiency phenotype. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09).

### Pathogenesis

Wilcken et al. (1993) and Treem et al. (1994) suggested that a fetus with LCHAD deficiency may adversely affect maternal liver function. Treem et al. (1994) proposed that the interaction of an affected fetus with a heterozygous mother in the late third trimester accounts for some cases of acute fatty liver of pregnancy. Ibdah et al. (2001) found that women heterozygous for HADHA mutations who carried fetuses with wildtype or heterozygous genotypes had uncomplicated pregnancies.

To investigate the pigmentary retinopathy of LCHAD deficiency, Tyni et al. (2002) studied cultured porcine retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells. The cells showed normal chain shortening of hexadecanoic acid, production of acetylcarnitine, LCHAD activity, expression of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, and carnitine uptake. In human retinal sections, antibodies to the trifunctional protein labeled the RPE, photoreceptors, and ganglion cells. The authors concluded that there was strong in vitro evidence for mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation in RPE cells.

### Diagnosis

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) stated that the differential diagnosis rests on showing urinary long-chain dicarboxylic acids with a hydroxyl group at the 3-position, together with study of enzyme activity in cultured fibroblasts. Duran et al. (1991) found that a phenylpropionic acid loading test gave a clue to the diagnosis: the patient's urine contained small but significant amounts of conjugated 3-hydroxyphenylpropionic acid.

IJlst et al. (1996) noted that the high frequency of the G1528C allele makes testing for it a valuable test for prenatal diagnosis. Ibdah et al. (2001) performed molecular prenatal diagnosis in 9 pregnancies: 8 pregnancies in 6 families with isolated LCHAD deficiency and 1 pregnancy in a family with complete trifunctional protein deficiency. Chorionic villus samples were used in 7 pregnancies and amniocytes in 2. The fetal genotype was identified in all 9 pregnancies. Two fetuses were affected and both pregnancies were terminated, and the genotypes were confirmed by molecular and enzymatic analysis.

Carpenter and Wilcken (1999) discussed neonatal diagnosis of LCHAD deficiency and its implications for newborn screening by tandem mass spectrometry. Van Hove et al. (2000) found that in plasma, an elevation of hydroxy-C18:1 acylcarnitine above the 95th centile of controls, combined with elevation of 2 of the 3 acylcarnitines C14, C14:1, and hydroxy-C16, identified more than 85% of patients with high specificity (less than 0.1% false positives). Results were also diagnostic in asymptomatic patients. In blood spots, specificity was lower because normal erythrocytes contain high levels of long-chain acylcarnitines.

### Clinical Management

Duran et al. (1991) based their treatment on the findings of a medium-chain triglyceride load and prescribed a diet enriched in medium-chain triglycerides. On this diet their patient began to thrive, the signs of cardiomyopathy disappeared, and liver function returned to normal.

Van Hove et al. (2000) found that a diet low in fat and high in medium-chain triglycerides lowered all disease-specific acylcarnitines, often to normal, and suggested that the assay is useful for monitoring treatment. Elizondo et al. (2020) found that disease-specific acylcarnitines were higher after an overnight fast and fell by about 60% after feeding. They concluded that the clinical usefulness of routine plasma acylcarnitine analysis for outpatient monitoring remains questionable, and that sample collection must be standardized if the measurements are to be useful.

Jones et al. (2003) cultured LCHAD-deficient skin fibroblasts with octanoate and decanoate. Adding medium-chain fatty acids reduced the accumulation of long-chain 3-hydroxy fatty acid oxidation intermediates, and decanoate alone was more effective than octanoate alone. The authors suggested that a medium-chain triglyceride preparation with a higher decanoate content may be more effective in LCHAD deficiency.

Guffon et al. (2021) retrospectively analyzed 18 French patients with long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders, including LCHAD deficiency, who were treated with triheptanoin for a median of 22 months. After triheptanoin was introduced, patients reported less fatigue and fewer and less severe episodes of myalgia, and emergency hospitalizations decreased. Adverse events were limited to digestive problems that resolved over time.

### Population Genetics

IJlst et al. (1994) found that 24 of 26 unrelated patients with LCHAD deficiency were homozygous for the G1528C mutation and the other 2 were heterozygous. IJlst et al. (1996) found an allele frequency of 87% for G1528C in 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency. They noted that this inborn error of fatty acid oxidation is very frequent, second only to medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, GLU510GLN [G1528C; c.1528G>C; c.1526G>C; p.Glu510Gln; E510Q; E474Q]

IJlst et al. (1994) identified a G-to-C change at nucleotide 1528 in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase coding region of the HADHA gene in patients with LCHAD deficiency. The change results in a glu510-to-gln (E510Q) substitution and creates a PstI site. RFLP analysis showed that 24 of 26 unrelated patients expressed only the C1528 allele, and 2 were heterozygous. The mutation was absent from 55 controls. ⚠︎

Sims et al. (1995) found the G1528C mutation in the homozygous state in 2 children with LCHAD deficiency, whose mothers had acute fatty liver of pregnancy. A third such child was compound heterozygous for G1528C and a C1132T nonsense mutation. Sims et al. (1995) numbered the substitution as glu474-to-gln (E474Q) in the mature alpha subunit.

IJlst et al. (1996) showed by expression in S. cerevisiae that the G1528C mutation is directly responsible for the loss of LCHAD activity. They found an allele frequency of 87% among 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency.

Tyni et al. (1997) found homozygosity for G1528C in all 13 of their patients with LCHAD deficiency.

Ibdah et al. (1999) found the E474Q mutation in the homozygous state in 8 of 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency and in the compound heterozygous state in the other 11. While carrying fetuses with the mutation, 79% of the heterozygous mothers had fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome.

Ibdah et al. (1999) found homozygosity for the 1528G-C (E474Q) mutation in a patient with LCHAD deficiency who presented with infantile hypocalcemia and cholestasis. The patient's mother had preeclampsia in that pregnancy and acute fatty liver of pregnancy in a later one.

Baskin et al. (2010) found homozygosity for this mutation, designated c.1526G-C (p.Glu510Gln), in a child with LCHAD deficiency. The child had inherited it through paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 2.

**.0002 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, 1132C-T [C1132T]

Sims et al. (1995) found a C1132T mutation in the HADHA gene in a child with LCHAD deficiency whose mother had acute fatty liver of pregnancy. The mutation creates a premature termination codon at residue 342. The child carried G1528C on the other allele and was therefore compound heterozygous.
