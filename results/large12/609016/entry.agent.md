### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (LCHADD) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the HADHA gene, which encodes the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, on chromosome 2p.

### Description

Long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency is an autosomal recessive inborn error of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (den Boer et al., 2002). LCHAD activity resides in the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein (Sims et al., 1995). Affected children present in infancy or early childhood with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, hepatopathy, cardiomyopathy, hypotonia, and sudden death; pigmentary retinopathy and peripheral neuropathy also occur (Tyni et al., 1997; den Boer et al., 2002). Mothers carrying affected fetuses may develop acute fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome (Ibdah et al., 1999). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Wanders et al. (1989) published a report on sudden infant death and long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase. Only the title of this report was available.

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) described a child who presented in early childhood with episodes of coma and hypoglycemia and a rapidly evolving myopathy and cardiomyopathy, leading to death at 9 months of age. Ketosis was decreased (blood beta-hydroxybutyrate 0.07 mmol/L) despite normal plasma fatty acid levels (0.81 mmol/L). Studies in cultured fibroblasts showed impaired medium- and long-chain fatty acid oxidation and deficiency of long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase. The authors concluded that the disorder affects the liver, skeletal muscle, and myocardium.

Hagenfeldt et al. (1990) described 5 patients with a suspected defect in fatty acid beta-oxidation. The patients had massive urinary excretion of 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids and accumulation of 3-hydroxy fatty acids in serum during acute illness. Long-chain and medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase activities were normal in fibroblasts of all patients. Four of the patients died of cardiomyopathy and liver insufficiency between 3 and 14 months of age, and 2 of the patients had older sibs who had died unexpectedly in early infancy. ⚠︎

Jackson et al. (1991) described 2 children with LCHAD deficiency who both presented with an acute metabolic crisis with hypoglycemia, and who excreted even-chain unsubstituted dicarboxylic and 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids in the urine. Cultured skin fibroblasts showed low LCHAD activity but normal short-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase activity.

Duran et al. (1991) reported 2 sibs with LCHAD deficiency. One died suddenly and unexpectedly on the third day of life with extreme hypoketotic hypoglycemia. The younger sib developed feeding problems, lowered consciousness, and liver dysfunction at 5 months of age. LCHAD activity was deficient in her cultured skin fibroblasts. On a diet enriched in medium-chain triglycerides she began to thrive, signs of cardiomyopathy disappeared, and her liver function normalized.

Bertini et al. (1992) reported an 11-month-old girl with acute episodes of hypoglycemia and Reye syndrome-like hepatic encephalopathy and 3-hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria. She had severe sensory-motor neuropathy, pigmentary retinopathy, and cardiomyopathy, and died of cardiac failure. Peripheral nerve pathology showed axonal neuropathy and demyelination. Cultured fibroblasts showed deficiency of mitochondrial LCHAD. The authors noted that peripheral nerve involvement and retinal pigmentary degeneration had not previously been described in patients with proven defects of mitochondrial beta-oxidation.

Wilcken et al. (1993) reported 11 pregnancies in 5 mothers. Six of the babies had LCHAD deficiency, and each of these pregnancies was complicated by features such as fatty liver and HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets) syndrome. By contrast, 3 of the mothers also gave birth to unaffected babies, and these pregnancies were largely uncomplicated. The authors concluded that a fetus with LCHAD deficiency may adversely affect maternal liver function, and that maternal heterozygosity alone could not account for this because the effects segregated with fetal LCHAD status.

Treem et al. (1994) reported a woman with acute fatty liver of pregnancy who gave birth to a seemingly normal full-term infant who was seen at 4 months of age with hypoglycemia, coma, and profound hepatic steatosis. The infant had LCHAD deficiency, and the mother was shown to be heterozygous for the defect.

Sewell et al. (1994) reported a patient with LCHAD deficiency who presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, hepatopathy, secondary carnitine deficiency, and increased plasma long-chain acylcarnitines. 3-Hydroxydicarboxylic aciduria was present, and the diagnosis was confirmed in cultured skin fibroblasts.

Fryburg et al. (1994) described a black boy with findings of Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome who also had a defect in long-chain fatty acid oxidation expressed in cultured skin fibroblasts as LCHAD deficiency. He also had an abnormal brain MRI and increased size of both lower limbs. The authors postulated that LCHAD deficiency may be the cause of the lipid myopathy in Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency, all homozygous for the common G1528C mutation. At presentation the patients had had hypoglycemia, cardiomyopathy, muscle hypotonia, and hepatomegaly during the first 2 years of life. Seven patients had recurrent metabolic crises and 6 had a steadily progressive course. Two patients had cholestatic liver disease, 1 had peripheral neuropathy, and 6 had retinopathy with focal pigmentary aggregations or retinal hypopigmentation. Most patients had lactic acidosis, increased serum creatine kinase activity, and low serum carnitine. Neuroradiologic studies showed bilateral periventricular or focal cortical lesions in 3 patients and brain atrophy in 1. Only 1 patient, who had had dietary treatment for 9 years, was alive at age 14 years; all others died before 2 years of age. ⚠︎

Schrijver-Wieling et al. (1997) reported 2 girls with LCHAD deficiency, aged 8 and 15 years, who had extensive macular pigmentary depositions and a 'salt and pepper' scattering of pigment in their retinas, with decreasing visual acuity. The authors suggested that testing for LCHAD deficiency be included in the evaluation of children with retinal dystrophy.

Uusimaa et al. (1997) reported 2 cases of L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency with pigmentary retinopathy. Only the title of this report was available.

Ibdah et al. (1999) described a patient who presented at 2 months of age with acute infantile hypocalcemia and vitamin D deficiency associated with occult, unexplained cholestatic liver disease. Sudden, unexpected death occurred at 8 months. The patient was homozygous for the 1528G-C (E474Q) mutation. The mother had preeclampsia during the third trimester of that pregnancy; in a subsequent pregnancy she developed severe acute fatty liver of pregnancy, with intrauterine fetal death at 33 weeks' gestation. The authors concluded that fetal LCHAD deficiency is associated with a spectrum of maternal illness ranging from preeclampsia to life-threatening acute fatty liver of pregnancy.

Thiel et al. (1999) reported LCHAD deficiency with neonatal manifestation on the first day of life, presenting with tachypnea. Only the title of this report was available.

Den Boer et al. (2002) reviewed 50 patients with LCHAD deficiency for whom questionnaires were returned by referring physicians (out of 61 unselected patients diagnosed in their center). The mean age at clinical presentation was 5.8 months (range, 1 day to 26 months), and 7 (15%) presented in the neonatal period. Thirty-nine patients (78%) presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, and 11 (22%) presented with chronic problems consisting of failure to thrive, feeding difficulties, cholestatic liver disease, and/or hypotonia. In retrospect, most (82%) of the patients who presented with acute metabolic derangement had also had a combination of chronic nonspecific symptoms before the crises. Mortality was 38%, all deaths occurring before or within 3 months after diagnosis. Morbidity among survivors was high, with recurrent metabolic crises and muscle problems despite therapy.

### Biochemical Features

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) found excessive urinary C6 to C10 dicarboxylic acids and, more specifically, accumulation of medium- and long-chain (C8 to C14) 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids in their patient. Activities of long-, medium-, and short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenases and 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase were normal.

In the patient of Duran et al. (1991), urine contained large amounts of C6-C14 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids and conjugated 3-hydroxyoctanoic acid, and plasma long-chain acylcarnitine was increased. A phenylpropionic acid loading test revealed small but significant amounts of conjugated 3-hydroxyphenylpropionic acid (phenylhydracrylic acid) in the urine.

IJlst et al. (1996) noted that in most patients only the LCHAD activity of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein is deficient, with near normal activity of the other 2 enzyme activities of the complex. However, Tyni et al. (1997) found that in their 13 patients homozygous for G1528C, the enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase activities of the trifunctional protein were variably decreased in skin fibroblasts. Dicarboxylic aciduria was detected in 9 of 10 patients.

Van Hove et al. (2000) reviewed plasma and blood spot acylcarnitines from 23 patients with proven LCHAD deficiency. Long-chain 3-hydroxyacylcarnitines of C14:1, C14, C16, and C18:1 chain length and long-chain acylcarnitines of C12, C14:1, C14, C16, C18:2, and C18:1 chain length were elevated, and acetylcarnitine was decreased.

Elizondo et al. (2020) studied plasma acylcarnitines in 11 treated patients with LCHAD/trifunctional protein deficiency under controlled conditions. The most prominent fasting long-chain acylcarnitine was C18:1-OH (0.56 +/- 0.52 micromol/L; reference range 0.00-0.05 micromol/L), and C16-OH and C18-OH were also above the reference range. C18:1-OH decreased by 59% after breakfast but increased by only 20% after moderate-intensity exercise.

### Inheritance

Jackson et al. (1991) suggested that LCHAD deficiency is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait because intermediate enzyme activity was found in fibroblasts from the parents of 1 affected child. Den Boer et al. (2002) and Baskin et al. (2010) described LCHAD deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder, and mothers of affected children have been shown to be heterozygous carriers (Treem et al., 1994; Ibdah et al., 1999).

### Cytogenetics

Baskin et al. (2010) reported a 22-month-old child, identified by expanded newborn screening with an abnormal acylcarnitine pattern and increased C14OH, who was homozygous for the common HADHA mutation (c.1526G-C; glu510-to-gln). The father was heterozygous for the mutation and the mother did not carry it. Short tandem repeat analysis with markers covering both arms of chromosome 2 showed paternal uniparental isodisomy.

### Mapping

IJlst et al. (1996) showed that the gene encoding the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, which carries the LCHAD activity, is located on chromosome 2p24.1-p23.3. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Jackson et al. (1992) described a young girl with recurrent episodes of muscle weakness who had a combined deficiency of long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase, long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase, and long-chain 2-enoyl-CoA hydratase, apparently inherited in an autosomal recessive manner; enzyme activities in the parents' fibroblasts were intermediate. The authors suggested that a multifunctional enzyme catalyzes these activities in human mitochondria and is deficient in their patient. Wanders et al. (1992) similarly found combined deficiency of long-chain enoyl-CoA hydratase, LCHAD, and long-chain 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase in fibroblasts of a patient and showed by size-exclusion chromatography a deficiency of the multifunctional beta-oxidation enzyme protein, which they termed human trifunctional protein deficiency.

Among 24 children with 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency studied by Ibdah et al. (1999), 19 had deficiency only of LCHAD, whereas 5 who presented with neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy or progressive neuromyopathy had complete deficiency of the trifunctional protein (loss of all 3 enzyme activities). None of the latter 5 had the glu474-to-gln mutation, and none of their mothers had liver disease during pregnancy.

### Molecular Genetics

IJlst et al. (1994) sequenced the cDNAs encoding the alpha and beta subunits of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein in patients with LCHAD deficiency and identified a G-to-C change at nucleotide 1528 in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase-encoding region of the alpha subunit (HADHA), resulting in a glutamate/glutamine substitution at amino acid position 510 (E510Q). The change created a PstI restriction site. By RFLP analysis, 24 of 26 unrelated patients expressed only the C1528 allele, and the other 2 were heterozygous for the mutation. The mutation was not found in 55 control subjects. ⚠︎

Sims et al. (1995) used SSCP analysis of the exons of the LCHAD (alpha subunit) gene to study 3 families with children presenting with sudden unexplained death or hypoglycemia and abnormal liver enzymes (Reye-like syndrome); in all 3 families, the mothers had acute fatty liver and associated severe complications during pregnancies with the affected infants. Two affected children were homozygous for the G1528C mutation, which substitutes glutamine for glutamic acid at position 474 of the mature alpha subunit (E474Q). The third child was compound heterozygous for G1528C and a C1132T mutation creating a premature termination codon (residue 342).

IJlst et al. (1996) expressed wildtype and mutant protein in S. cerevisiae and showed that the G1528C mutation is directly responsible for loss of LCHAD activity. They developed a method to identify the mutation in genomic DNA and found an allele frequency of 87% for G1528C among 34 patients with LCHAD deficiency.

Ibdah et al. (1999) used DNA amplification and sequencing to identify mutations in the alpha subunit of the trifunctional protein in 24 children with 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency. Of 19 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency, who presented with hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fatty liver, 8 were homozygous for the glu474-to-gln (E474Q) mutation and 11 were compound heterozygous for this mutation and a different mutation on the other allele. While carrying fetuses with the E474Q mutation, 79% of the heterozygous mothers had fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome.

Elizondo et al. (2020) reported genotypes of 11 patients diagnosed with LCHAD/trifunctional protein deficiency. Nine were homozygous or compound heterozygous for the common HADHA c.1528G-C variant; in compound heterozygotes the second alleles included c.703C-T (R235W) and c.479_482TAG-AATA (I160fs). Two patients did not carry the common mutation (one with c.901G-A (G301S) on one allele, the other compound heterozygous for c.1150-1G-T and c.208T-C (S70P)); these mutations had previously been reported in association with a trifunctional protein deficiency phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ibdah et al. (1999) found that children with isolated LCHAD deficiency carried the E474Q mutation in homozygous or compound heterozygous state, and that most of their heterozygous mothers had acute liver disease during pregnancy, whereas children with complete trifunctional protein deficiency, who presented with neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy or progressive neuromyopathy, lacked this mutation and their mothers had no liver disease during pregnancy.

Van Hove et al. (2000) found that the plasma acylcarnitine results were not influenced by genotype. In contrast, Elizondo et al. (2020) found that all patients carrying the common c.1528G-C mutation on at least 1 allele had consistently higher levels of C16-OH, C18-OH, and C18:1-OH than 2 patients without the common mutation who had a trifunctional protein deficiency phenotype, although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09).

### Pathogenesis

Treem et al. (1994) hypothesized that the interaction of an affected fetus with a mother heterozygous for LCHAD deficiency in the late third trimester accounts for some cases of acute fatty liver of pregnancy. Wilcken et al. (1993) had noted that maternal heterozygosity alone could not account for the adverse maternal effects because these segregated with fetal LCHAD status. Ibdah et al. (2001) found that women heterozygous for trifunctional protein alpha-subunit mutations who carried fetuses with wildtype or heterozygous genotypes had uncomplicated pregnancies.

To investigate the pathogenesis of the pigmentary retinopathy of LCHAD deficiency, Tyni et al. (2002) studied cultured porcine retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, which showed normal chain shortening of hexadecanoic acid, production of acetylcarnitine, LCHAD activity, expression of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, and carnitine uptake. Antibodies to the trifunctional protein labeled the RPE, photoreceptors, and ganglion cells in human retinal sections.

### Diagnosis

Rocchiccioli et al. (1990) noted that LCHAD deficiency shares many clinical similarities with systemic carnitine deficiency and with carnitine palmitoyltransferase and long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiencies, and that differential diagnosis relies on the demonstration of urinary long-chain 3-hydroxydicarboxylic acids and measurement of enzyme activity in cultured fibroblasts.

Carpenter and Wilcken (1999) reported on neonatal diagnosis of LCHAD deficiency and its implications for newborn screening by tandem mass spectrometry; only the title of this report was available. Van Hove et al. (2000) found that, in plasma, elevation of hydroxy-C18:1 acylcarnitine above the 95th centile of controls, combined with elevation of 2 of the 3 acylcarnitines C14, C14:1, and hydroxy-C16, identified over 85% of patients with high specificity (less than 0.1% false-positive rate). High endogenous long-chain acylcarnitine levels in normal erythrocytes reduced diagnostic specificity in blood spots compared with plasma.

Prenatal Diagnosis. IJlst et al. (1996) proposed that the high frequency of the G1528C mutation makes testing for it valuable for prenatal diagnosis. Ibdah et al. (2001) performed molecular prenatal diagnosis in 9 pregnancies, 8 in 6 families with isolated LCHAD deficiency and 1 in a family with complete trifunctional protein deficiency, using chorionic villus samples in 7 and amniocytes in 2. The fetal genotype was identified in all 9 pregnancies. Two fetuses were affected and the pregnancies were terminated; 2 fetuses had normal genotype and 5 were heterozygous, and these 7 pregnancies were uncomplicated.

### Clinical Management

Duran et al. (1991) prescribed a diet enriched in medium-chain triglycerides based on the results of a medium-chain triglyceride load; on this regimen their patient began to thrive, signs of cardiomyopathy disappeared, and liver function normalized. Tyni et al. (1997) emphasized that recognition of the clinical features is important for early institution of dietary management, which may alter the otherwise poor prognosis.

Van Hove et al. (2000) found that treatment with a diet low in fat and high in medium-chain triglycerides decreased all disease-specific acylcarnitines, often to normal, suggesting that the assay is useful for treatment monitoring. Elizondo et al. (2020), however, found that in patients on a controlled low-fat diet with MCT, the plasma acylcarnitine profile did not completely normalize in LCHAD deficiency, and that disease-specific acylcarnitines varied with feeding, fasting, and exercise; they considered the utility of random plasma acylcarnitine analysis for outpatient monitoring questionable.

Jones et al. (2003) studied cultured skin fibroblasts from LCHAD-deficient individuals and found that addition of the medium-chain fatty acids octanoate and decanoate decreased accumulation of long-chain 3-hydroxy fatty acid oxidation intermediates; decanoate alone was more effective than octanoate alone. They suggested that a medium-chain triglyceride preparation higher in decanoate may be more effective in reducing accumulation of potentially toxic long-chain 3-hydroxy fatty acids.

Guffon et al. (2021) retrospectively analyzed 18 French patients with long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders, including LCHAD deficiency, treated with triheptanoin for a median of 22 months (range, 9-228 months). In the year after introduction of triheptanoin, the patients as a group reported reduced intermittent snacking and nocturnal meals, reduced fatigue and myalgia, and fewer emergency hospitalizations; adverse events were limited to digestive issues. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

IJlst et al. (1994) found that 24 of 26 unrelated patients with LCHAD deficiency expressed only the C1528 allele, indicating a high frequency of this mutation among LCHAD-deficient patients, and IJlst et al. (1996) found an 87% allele frequency of G1528C among 34 patients. IJlst et al. (1996) stated that deficiency of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein is very frequent among inborn errors of fatty acid oxidation, second to medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, GLU510GLN [c.1528G>C; p.Glu510Gln; G1528C; E474Q (mature protein); c.1526G>C]

In patients with LCHAD deficiency, IJlst et al. (1994) identified a G-to-C change at nucleotide 1528 of the alpha subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, in the 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase-encoding region, resulting in a substitution at amino acid position 510 (glu510 to gln; E510Q) and creating a PstI site. Twenty-four of 26 unrelated patients expressed only the C1528 allele and 2 were heterozygous; the change was not found in 55 controls. ⚠︎

Sims et al. (1995) found the G1528C mutation, which substitutes glutamine for glutamic acid at position 474 of the mature alpha subunit (E474Q), in homozygous state in 2 children with LCHAD deficiency and in compound heterozygous state with a nonsense mutation (1132C-T) in a third child; all 3 mothers had acute fatty liver during the affected pregnancies.

IJlst et al. (1996) showed by expression in S. cerevisiae that the G1528C mutation is directly responsible for loss of LCHAD activity, and found an allele frequency of 87% among 34 LCHAD-deficient patients.

Tyni et al. (1997) reported 13 patients with LCHAD deficiency who were all homozygous for the G1528C mutation.

Ibdah et al. (1999) found the E474Q mutation in homozygous state in 8 children and in compound heterozygous state in 11 children with isolated LCHAD deficiency; 79% of heterozygous mothers carrying fetuses with the mutation had fatty liver of pregnancy or HELLP syndrome.

Ibdah et al. (1999) found homozygosity for the 1528G-C (E474Q) mutation in a child with LCHAD deficiency who presented with infantile hypocalcemia and cholestatic liver disease and died suddenly at 8 months; the mother had preeclampsia in that pregnancy and acute fatty liver of pregnancy in a subsequent one.

Baskin et al. (2010) reported a child, identified by newborn screening, who was homozygous for this mutation (designated c.1526G-C, p.Glu510Gln) as a result of paternal uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 2; the father was heterozygous and the mother did not carry the mutation.

**.0002 LONG-CHAIN 3-HYDROXYACYL-COA DEHYDROGENASE DEFICIENCY**  
HADHA, 1132C-T [C1132T]

In a child with LCHAD deficiency whose mother had acute fatty liver during the affected pregnancy, Sims et al. (1995) identified compound heterozygosity for the G1528C mutation and a 1132C-T transition in the alpha subunit of the trifunctional protein, creating a premature termination codon at residue 342.
