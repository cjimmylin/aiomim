### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina (GACR) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous (biallelic) mutation in the gene encoding ornithine-delta-aminotransferase (OAT) on chromosome 10q26.

### Description

Gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina is an autosomal recessive chorioretinal degeneration associated with hyperornithinemia. It is caused by deficiency of ornithine-delta-aminotransferase (OAT), an enzyme of the mitochondrial matrix (Mitchell et al., 1988). The disorder is characterized by progressive constriction of the visual fields, a 10- to 20-fold elevation in plasma ornithine, and reduced activity of L-ornithine:2-oxoacid aminotransferase. Atrophy of type II muscle fibers is morphologically conspicuous but clinically unimportant, and it progresses along with the eye disease (Sipilä et al., 1981). Vision declines in early life, eventually leading to complete blindness (Balfoort et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

In their reports on carriers of the gene, FRANCOIS et al. (1960) and François et al. (1966) called the disorder Fuchs' atrophia gyrata chorioideae et retinae.

### Clinical Features

Stoppoloni et al. (1978) reported hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina in a girl aged 3 years and 9 months. She also had mild mental retardation, delayed language development, and speech defects.

Sipilä et al. (1979) studied 21 patients with gyrate atrophy and hyperornithinemia. None of the patients was weak, but type 2 muscle fibers were almost universally atrophic and contained tubular aggregates. Males and females were affected equally, and the number of type 2 fibers decreased with age.

Kennaway et al. (1980) described 4 patients with hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy. Histochemical staining of punch muscle biopsies showed intracellular inclusions in type 2 muscle fibers. On electron microscopy, tubular aggregates about 60 nm in diameter were seen next to the sarcoplasmic membrane. An oral glucose tolerance test in 1 patient produced marked changes in serum ornithine and lysine concentrations, as well as mild glucose intolerance.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1983) described cataract in 9 phakic patients with gyrate atrophy. They also examined 3 cataractous lenses removed from patients aged 31 to 47 years. Clinically, the lens opacities lay mainly along the confluence of the posterior sutures and interfered with vision because they were in the visual axis. Histologically, the posterior suture region was filled with liquified and degenerated lens material typical of senile cataractous changes. The authors concluded that by the second decade, cataract is a uniform finding in gyrate atrophy, and that it appears to be unique compared with the cataracts of other forms of retinal degeneration.

Valtonen et al. (1996) examined the thigh muscles of 7 patients with gyrate atrophy using semi-open conchotome biopsies, CT, and ultralow-field MRI. Type 2 muscle fiber atrophy was present in all biopsy samples, and tubular aggregates were present in 6 of the 7. CT density of muscle was increased in the only child in the study, decreased in 3 elderly subjects, and normal in the other 3 patients. Mean T1 relaxation time was lower in patients (135 +/- 5 ms) than in 6 healthy controls (157 +/- 12 ms).

Valtonen et al. (1999) studied the central nervous system in 23 untreated patients with gyrate atrophy (mean age 32 +/- 18 years), comparing them with age-matched healthy controls and with 9 patients who had taken creatine or creatine precursor supplements for several years. Brain MRI showed degenerative white matter lesions in 50% of patients. Seventy percent had premature atrophic changes, with a striking increase in the number of Virchow spaces. Of the patients who had an EEG, 58% showed abnormal slow background activity, focal lesions, or high-amplitude beta rhythm. The EEG findings were not associated with the MRI changes or with the patients' age or sex.

Caruso et al. (2001) measured changes in visual function in 5 patients with gyrate atrophy, each followed for 4 to 6 years. Median visual field half-lives were 17.0 years with static perimetry and 11.4 years with kinetic perimetry. Median electroretinogram half-lives were 16.0 years for the maximal response and 10.7 years for the flicker response. The authors concluded that visual function often declines slowly.

Peltola et al. (2002) looked for peripheral nervous system involvement in 40 patients with gyrate atrophy (mean age 31.6 +/- 16.3 years; range, 5 to 74 years). Neurography was abnormal in 21 patients (53%), and the abnormalities were associated with the severity of the ophthalmologic changes and with age. Quantitative sensory threshold testing showed abnormal large-fiber function in 7 patients (18%) and abnormal small-fiber function in 4 (10%). Somatosensory and brainstem auditory evoked potential responses were abolished in 5 patients. The authors concluded that gyrate atrophy is a systemic disease that affects the peripheral as well as the central nervous system.

### Biochemical Features

Valle et al. (1977) measured ornithine aminotransferase activity in phytohemagglutinin-stimulated lymphocytes from a patient with gyrate atrophy and from her daughter. The patient's cells had no detectable activity, and the heterozygous daughter's cells had 44% of normal activity.

Kennaway et al. (1977) reported deficient ornithine ketoacid aminotransferase activity in cultured skin fibroblasts in gyrate atrophy.

O'Donnell et al. (1978) found that cultured fibroblasts from a patient with gyrate atrophy did not convert uniformly 14C-labeled L-ornithine to proline. They attributed this block to deficient L-ornithine:2-oxoacid aminotransferase activity. The patient's heterozygous father had intermediate enzyme activity.

Shih et al. (1978) found a virtual absence of ornithine ketoacid transaminase (OKT) activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from 5 patients. Fibroblasts from 4 carrier parents showed a 42 to 65% reduction in activity. Raising the pyridoxal phosphate concentration in the assay partly restored OKT activity in fibroblasts from 1 of the 5 patients.

Valle et al. (1980) studied 4 patients, all of whom had hyperornithinemia. Plasma histidine was raised, and plasma lysine and branched-chain amino acids were reduced. Renal clearances of these 4 amino acids were not high enough to explain their low plasma levels.

Kennaway et al. (1980) found deficient OKT activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from all 4 of their patients, and roughly half-normal levels in obligate heterozygotes. Patient fibroblasts responded to vitamin B6 in vitro, with OKT activity rising at higher pyridoxal phosphate concentrations. The mean serum ornithine of obligate heterozygotes was slightly above normal, but the values overlapped considerably with the normal range.

Sipilä et al. (1980) gave intravenous arginine to 6 patients and 6 controls. Serum arginine curves were similar in the 2 groups, but the rise in serum ornithine was more than 3 times greater in patients than in controls. Mean plasma ornithine half-times were 360 minutes in patients and 97 minutes in controls. Urinary guanidinoacetate excretion rose markedly in all controls, whereas the patients always excreted less than the controls.

Sipilä et al. (1981) found trace enzyme activity in liver tissue from 2 patients. The mutant enzyme's Km for ornithine was 200 mM, 50-fold above normal. Raising alpha-oxoglutarate and pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate to 10 times the concentrations giving maximal activity of the normal enzyme had no effect on the mutant enzyme. No substrate inhibition of the mutant enzyme occurred at 1,000 mM ornithine, whereas the normal enzyme was inhibited by ornithine above 70 mM. The authors suggested that the mutant enzyme had an altered ornithine-binding site.

Fukuda et al. (1983) measured free amino acids in the blood cells of 2 brothers with gyrate atrophy and hyperornithinemia. Ornithine was raised in erythrocytes, lymphocytes, and granulocytes to differing degrees, and aspartic acid was low in erythrocytes. After oral ornithine, the blood cell ornithine concentration peaked at 2 hours and stayed high for at least 4 hours.

Kennaway et al. (1989) characterized the mutant enzyme in 9 patients, 4 of whom were pyridoxine responsive. The apparent Km for pyridoxal phosphate in fibroblast mitochondria was higher in responsive patients than in nonresponsive patients, whose Km was normal. The apparent Km for ornithine was normal in all 7 patients tested. The responsive patient with the mildest clinical disease had the highest Km for pyridoxal phosphate, but also the most stable enzyme, which the authors suggested contributed to her milder phenotype. On Western blot, OAT protein was clearly detectable in the pyridoxine-responsive patients and in 2 of 5 nonresponders, but was low or undetectable in the other 3 patients.

### Inheritance

Takki and Simell (1974) discussed the genetic aspects of gyrate atrophy with hyperornithinemia. Shih et al. (1978) and Sipilä et al. (1981) described the disorder as autosomal recessive. In heterozygous carriers, enzyme activity was intermediate (Valle et al., 1977; O'Donnell et al., 1978; Shih et al., 1978). Barrett et al. (1987) reviewed 80 biochemically confirmed cases, which confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Barrett et al. (1987) used a cDNA clone encoding human OAT, together with somatic cell hybrids and in situ hybridization, to map OAT gene sequences to chromosome regions 10q26 and Xp11.2. Their review of 80 cases supported a functional OAT gene on chromosome 10.

Ramesh et al. (1988) defined 6 RFLPs at the OAT locus, all mapping to chromosome 10, with an overall heterozygosity of 83%. In the one pedigree available, the OAT locus segregated with gyrate atrophy. The RFLPs also showed significant linkage disequilibrium with the disorder, which the authors took as genetic evidence that a defect in the OAT structural gene causes it.

### Heterogeneity

Shih et al. (1978) concluded that the disease is genetically heterogeneous, because pyridoxal phosphate partly restored OKT activity in fibroblasts from only 1 of 5 patients.

Wirtz et al. (1985) studied fibroblasts from 4 pyridoxine-responsive and 3 nonresponsive patients. Responsive patients had higher OKT activity and greater incorporation of 14C-ornithine into protein. Complementation analysis of cells from all 7 patients showed no positive complementation. The authors suggested that the responsive and nonresponsive forms are different allelic mutations at the same locus encoding OKT.

Shih et al. (1988) found intermediate OKT activity in fibroblasts from heterozygotes for both the pyridoxine-responsive and the pyridoxine-nonresponsive variants. The 2 variants could be told apart by how much OKT activity rose with pyridoxal phosphate in vitro. The ratio of activity at 0.04 mM to 0 mM pyridoxal phosphate was 1.18 +/- 0.18 in controls (n = 12), 1.43 +/- 0.26 in nonresponsive heterozygotes (n = 5), and 2.20 +/- 0.14 in responsive heterozygotes (n = 3).

Kennaway et al. (1989) concluded that their enzyme and Western blot findings showed heterogeneity within, as well as between, the pyridoxine-responsive and nonresponsive groups of patients.

### Molecular Genetics

Mitchell et al. (1988) cloned and sequenced the human OAT cDNA and worked out the intron-exon structure of the gene. RNase A protection experiments with RNA from 4 Lebanese patients showed a probe-target mismatch at the 5-prime end of the first coding exon. PCR amplification and sequencing of this region of the patients' genomic DNA identified a G-to-A transition that changed the initiator ATG (methionine) codon to ATA. The mutation segregated with the disease allele in both pedigrees. ⚠︎

Brody et al. (1992) screened the OAT genes of patients with gyrate atrophy and characterized 21 newly recognized alleles. They studied these and 3 previously described mutations in cultured fibroblasts. Most alleles (20 of 24) produced normal amounts of normally sized OAT mRNA, but only 2 of 24 had normal amounts of OAT antigen. Several mutations were reproduced by site-directed mutagenesis and expressed in Chinese hamster ovary cells. These experiments confirmed that the mutations inactivate OAT and cause gyrate atrophy in these patients. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Sipilä et al. (1979) proposed that the muscle and eye changes are probably related to abnormal creatine synthesis caused by the enlarged body pool of ornithine.

Sipilä et al. (1980) concluded from their arginine-loading studies that high ornithine concentrations inhibit the transamidination step, and so the formation of guanidinoacetate, creatine, and possibly phosphocreatine. They suggested that a deficiency of high-energy phosphates may underlie the eye and muscle atrophies.

### Diagnosis

Kennaway et al. (1980) found that oral ornithine tolerance tests distinguished carriers from controls in only 1 of 5 cases.

Ramesh et al. (1988) noted that RFLPs at the OAT locus could be used for prenatal diagnosis and carrier detection in families with a previous history of gyrate atrophy.

### Clinical Management

In the girl reported by Stoppoloni et al. (1978), restricting protein intake to a minimum of 0.8 g/kg/day significantly reduced plasma ornithine levels.

Valle et al. (1980) treated 1 patient with an arginine-deficient diet, which progressively lowered plasma ornithine from 13 times normal to the upper limit of normal, and ornithinuria and lysinuria disappeared. Oral alpha-aminoisobutyric acid sped the fall in plasma ornithine by increasing renal ornithine losses. Long-term maintenance (1.5 years) on a diet of 10 to 20 g of protein plus essential amino acids kept plasma ornithine between 55 and 355 microM. During this time the chorioretinal degeneration did not progress, and vision apparently improved.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1980) reported that an arginine-deficient diet lowered plasma ornithine fivefold over 20 months in a patient with gyrate atrophy. She noticed subjective improvement in vision about 15 months after starting the diet. This was documented by improvements in the electroretinogram, dark adaptation, and color vision, involving rod function and, to a lesser extent, cone function.

Kennaway et al. (1980) documented an in vivo response to vitamin B6 in 3 of their 4 patients. In these patients, oral B6 supplementation significantly reduced fasting serum ornithine and increased lysine.

McInnes et al. (1981) gave a low-arginine diet to a 15-year-old patient with hyperornithinemia (0.6-1.2 mmol/l), aiming for a plasma ornithine of 0.15-0.25 mmol/l. After 5 weeks, vision improved in one eye that had been severely impaired, without improvement, for 3 years. The improvement lasted until dietary compliance worsened at 8 months, after which vision regressed toward its pretreatment state. Overrestriction of dietary arginine caused hyperammonemia, indicating that arginine is an essential amino acid in ornithine transaminase deficiency.

Sipilä et al. (1981) supplemented the diet of 7 patients with 1.5 g of creatine daily. Over 1 year of treatment, type II muscle fiber diameters increased from 34.1 +/- 7.1 to 49.9 +/- 7.0 microns, with no significant increase in type I fiber diameters. Visual fields showed no further constriction during therapy, and fundus photography showed slow worsening at an age otherwise associated with rapid progression.

Valtonen et al. (1999) found that brain MRI and EEG findings in patients who had taken creatine supplements for several years did not differ from those in untreated patients.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (2002) followed 2 pairs of sibs with gyrate atrophy treated with an arginine-restricted diet. After 16 to 17 years on the diet, the younger sib in each pair, who had started the diet at an earlier age, had slower progression of lesions than the older sib. The authors concluded that long-term reduction of plasma ornithine, if started early, may appreciably slow the progression of the chorioretinal lesions and, to a lesser extent, the loss of retinal function.

Balfoort et al. (2021) carried out a systematic review of 33 studies covering 107 patients; there were no randomized controlled trials or large cohort studies. Treatments included protein-restricted diets, pyridoxine supplementation, creatine or creatine precursor supplementation, L-lysine supplementation, and proline supplementation. Protein-restricted diets lowered ornithine levels by 16.0 to 91.2%. Pyridoxine responsiveness was reported for 30% of the included mutations, and lysine supplementation lowered ornithine levels by 21 to 34%. The authors found that the evidence did not allow conclusions about clinical effectiveness.

### Animal Model

Wang et al. (1995) found that OAT-deficient mice produced by gene targeting had neonatal hypoornithinemia and lethality, which short-term arginine supplementation could rescue. After weaning, the mice developed hyperornithinemia similar to that of human patients, and over several months they developed a retinal degeneration involving photoreceptors and pigment epithelium. Further studies in 1 human infant with gyrate atrophy also showed transient hypoornithinemia.

Wang et al. (2000) found that an arginine-restricted diet substantially lowered plasma ornithine in Oat -/- mice and completely prevented retinal degeneration over a 12-month period. They concluded that ornithine accumulation is a necessary factor in the retinal degeneration, and that restoring OAT activity in the retina is not required for effective treatment.

### History

Simell and Takki (1973) reported raised plasma ornithine in association with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 GYRATE ATROPHY OF CHOROID AND RETINA**  
OAT, ATG-ATA, INITIATOR CODON [G-to-A transition changing initiator ATG to ATA]

Mitchell et al. (1988) studied 4 Lebanese patients with gyrate atrophy from 2 pedigrees. RNase A protection showed a mismatch at the 5-prime end of the first coding exon of OAT, and PCR and sequencing found a G-to-A transition that changed the initiator ATG (methionine) codon to ATA. The mutation segregated with the disease allele in both pedigrees. Translation starting at the nearest in-frame methionine codon would truncate OAT by 138 amino acids, removing the entire mitochondrial leader sequence and 113 amino acids of the mature peptide.
