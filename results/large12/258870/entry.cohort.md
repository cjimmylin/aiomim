### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina (GACR) is caused by biallelic mutation in the gene encoding ornithine aminotransferase (OAT; 613349) on chromosome 10q26.13.

### Description

Gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina is an autosomal recessive chorioretinal degeneration caused by deficiency of ornithine-delta-aminotransferase (OAT), an enzyme of the mitochondrial matrix (Mitchell et al., 1988). Ornithine accumulates, and the chorioretinal degeneration is progressive (Wang et al., 2000). Balfoort et al. (2021) described GACR as a rare inborn error of amino acid metabolism. It is characterized by high plasma ornithine and by vision loss that begins early in life and eventually leads to complete blindness. No curative treatment exists.

### Clinical Features

Stoppoloni et al. (1978) reported a 3-year 9-month-old girl with hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina. She also had mild mental retardation, delayed language development, and speech defects.

Sipilä et al. (1979) studied 21 patients with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina and hyperornithinemia. The patients were not weak, but their type 2 muscle fibers were almost universally atrophic and contained tubular aggregates. The number of type 2 fibers decreased with age. Tubular aggregates occurred in both females and males, and the 2 sexes were affected equally. Gyrate atrophy was the first disease in which females were shown to have tubular aggregates.

Kennaway et al. (1980) described 4 patients with hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina. On histochemical staining, muscle biopsies showed intracellular inclusions in type 2 fibers. Electron microscopy showed these to be tubular aggregates about 60 nm in diameter lying next to the sarcoplasmic membrane. In 1 patient, an oral glucose tolerance test caused marked changes in serum ornithine and lysine and showed mild glucose intolerance.

McInnes et al. (1981) reported a 15-year-old patient with hyperornithinemia (0.6-1.2 mmol/l) and gyrate atrophy of the retina. Visual function in one eye had been severely impaired, without improvement, for 3 years.

Sipilä et al. (1981) described gyrate atrophy as marked by progressive constriction of the visual fields, a 10- to 20-fold rise in plasma ornithine, and reduced ornithine aminotransferase activity. In their 7 patients, type II muscle fiber atrophy was morphologically conspicuous but clinically unimportant, and it progressed along with the eye disease. ⚠︎

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1983) described the cataract in 9 phakic patients with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina. By the second decade, cataract was a uniform finding. The opacities lay mainly along the confluence of the posterior sutures, where their position in the visual axis interfered with vision. The authors considered them unique compared with the cataracts seen in other forms of retinal degeneration. They also examined 3 lenses removed from patients aged 31 to 47 years. Histologically, the posterior suture region was filled with liquified, degenerated lens material typical of senile cataractous changes.

Valtonen et al. (1996) examined the thigh muscles of 7 patients with gyrate atrophy using semi-open conchotome muscle biopsies, CT, and ultralow-field MRI at 0.02 T. All biopsy samples showed type 2 muscle fiber atrophy, and 6 of the 7 had tubular aggregates. Muscle CT density was increased in the only child in the study, decreased in 3 elderly subjects, and normal in the remaining 3 patients. Mean T1 relaxation time of thigh muscle was lower in patients than in 6 healthy controls (135 vs 157 ms; p = 0.002), while T2 was slightly but not significantly increased. The authors concluded that thigh muscles of GA patients universally show changes on CT and MRI, but that relaxation time measurements gave little additional information on muscle metabolism.

Valtonen et al. (1999) studied central nervous system involvement in 23 untreated patients with gyrate atrophy, comparing them with age-matched healthy controls and with 9 patients who had received creatine or creatine precursor supplementation daily for several years. Brain MRI showed degenerative white matter lesions in 50% of patients and premature atrophic changes in 70%, with a striking increase in the number of Virchow spaces. Of the patients who had EEG recordings, 58% had abnormal slow background activity, focal lesions, or high-amplitude beta rhythm (more than 50 microV). EEG findings were not associated with MRI changes, age, or sex.

Caruso et al. (2001) followed 5 patients with gyrate atrophy for 4 to 6 years in preparation for a gene replacement trial. Median visual field half-lives were 17.0 years by static perimetry and 11.4 years by kinetic perimetry. Median electroretinogram half-lives were 16.0 years for the maximal response and 10.7 years for the flicker response.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (2002) reported a natural history study of 2 pairs of sibs with gyrate atrophy who were treated with an arginine-restricted diet (see CLINICAL MANAGEMENT).

Peltola et al. (2002) looked for peripheral nervous system involvement in 40 patients with gyrate atrophy (mean age 31.6 +/- 16.3 years; range 5 to 74 years). Neurography was abnormal in 21 (53%), and these abnormalities were associated with patient age and with the severity of the ophthalmologic changes. Large-fiber sensory function was abnormal in 7 patients (18%) and small-fiber function in 4 (10%). Somatosensory and brainstem auditory evoked potentials were abolished in 5 patients. The authors concluded that gyrate atrophy is a systemic disease affecting both the central and the peripheral nervous system.

### Biochemical Features

Simell and Takki (1973) reported raised plasma ornithine in association with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina.

Valle et al. (1977) studied phytohemagglutinin-stimulated lymphocytes from a woman with gyrate atrophy and from her daughter, an obligate heterozygote. The patient's cells had no detectable ornithine aminotransferase activity. Activity in the daughter's cells was 44% of normal. [3H]thymidine incorporation and other enzymes affected by transformation confirmed that the patient's cells were transformed, which established an enzyme deficiency in gyrate atrophy.

Kennaway et al. (1977) found deficient ornithine ketoacid aminotransferase activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from patients with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina.

O'Donnell et al. (1978) found that cultured fibroblasts from a female patient with gyrate atrophy of the retina did not convert uniformly 14C-labeled L-ornithine to proline. This metabolic block was caused by deficient L-ornithine:2-oxoacid aminotransferase activity. Her heterozygous father had intermediate enzyme activity.

Shih et al. (1978) found virtually no ornithine ketoacid transaminase (OKT) activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from 5 patients with gyrate atrophy and hyperornithinemia. Fibroblasts from 4 carrier parents had 42 to 65% less OKT activity than normal.

Valle et al. (1980) studied 4 patients with gyrate atrophy, all of whom had hyperornithinemia. They also had elevated plasma histidine and reduced plasma lysine and branched-chain amino acids. Renal clearances of ornithine, histidine, lysine, and the branched-chain amino acids were not high enough to explain the low plasma levels of these amino acids. ⚠︎

Kennaway et al. (1980) found that obligate heterozygotes had a mean serum ornithine slightly above normal, with considerable overlap with the normal range. Oral ornithine tolerance tests distinguished carriers from controls in only 1 of 5 cases. Fibroblast OKT activity in obligate heterozygotes was about half of normal. Activity was deficient in fibroblasts from all 4 patients, and it rose in patient fibroblasts at higher pyridoxal phosphate concentrations, showing in vitro responsiveness to vitamin B6.

Sipilä et al. (1980) gave intravenous arginine to 6 patients with gyrate atrophy and 6 controls. Serum arginine curves were similar in the 2 groups. The rise in serum ornithine was more than 3 times greater in patients, and the mean half-time of plasma ornithine was 360 min in patients versus 97 min in controls. Patients always excreted less urinary guanidinoacetate than controls, and the differences within each sex were highly significant. Differences in creatine excretion were less marked.

Sipilä et al. (1981) found only trace OAT activity in liver from 2 patients with gyrate atrophy. The mutant enzyme had a Km for ornithine of 200 mM, 50 times higher than normal. Raising alpha-oxoglutarate and pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate to 10 times the concentrations that gave maximal activity of the normal enzyme had no effect on the mutant enzyme.

Fukuda et al. (1983) studied 2 brothers with gyrate atrophy and hyperornithinemia. Ornithine was elevated in their erythrocytes, lymphocytes, and granulocytes, to differing degrees depending on cell type, and aspartic acid was low in erythrocytes. After oral ornithine, blood cell ornithine peaked at 2 hours and stayed high for at least 4 hours, and after 2 hours the concentration curves differed among the 3 cell types.

Wirtz et al. (1985) examined fibroblasts from 4 pyridoxine-responsive and 3 nonresponsive patients with gyrate atrophy. Compared with cells from nonresponsive patients, cells from responsive patients had higher OKT activity in homogenates and greater incorporation of radioactivity from 14C-ornithine into protein in intact cells.

Shih et al. (1988) found intermediate OKT activity in fibroblasts from heterozygotes for both the pyridoxine-responsive and the pyridoxine-nonresponsive variants of gyrate atrophy.

Kennaway et al. (1989) studied OAT in fibroblast mitochondria from 9 patients with gyrate atrophy, 4 pyridoxine-responsive and 5 nonresponsive. The apparent Km for pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) was higher in the responsive patients than in the nonresponsive patients, whose Km was normal. The apparent Km for ornithine was normal in all 7 patients studied. On Western blot, OAT protein was clearly detectable in the responsive patients and in 2 of the 5 nonresponders, but was low or undetectable in the other 3 nonresponders.

Wang et al. (1995) reported transient hypoornithinemia in a human infant with gyrate atrophy (see ANIMAL MODEL).

### Inheritance

FRANCOIS et al. (1960) studied carriers ('conductors') of the gene for Fuchs gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina and discussed the disorder in relation to Alder anomaly. François et al. (1966) also wrote about gene carriers of the disorder. ⚠︎

Shih et al. (1978) described gyrate atrophy as a chorioretinal degeneration associated with hyperornithinemia and inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. In their study, carrier parents had reduced fibroblast enzyme activity. Barrett et al. (1987) reviewed 80 biochemically confirmed cases, and the review confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. Shih et al. (1988) likewise stated that gyrate atrophy caused by OKT deficiency is autosomal recessive.

### Mapping

Barrett et al. (1987) used a cDNA clone encoding human OAT, together with somatic cell hybrids and in situ hybridization, to map OAT gene sequences to chromosome regions 10q26 and Xp11.2. In a review of 80 biochemically confirmed cases, they found support for a functional OAT gene on chromosome 10. They noted that the X-chromosome OAT sequences mapped to the same region as L1.28 (Xp11.0-p11.3), a marker closely linked to X-linked recessive retinitis pigmentosa.

Ramesh et al. (1988) defined 6 RFLPs at the OAT locus, all mapping to chromosome 10, with an overall heterozygosity of 83%. In the one available pedigree, the OAT locus segregated concordantly with gyrate atrophy.

### Heterogeneity

Shih et al. (1978) found that raising the pyridoxal phosphate concentration in the assay partly restored OKT activity in fibroblasts from 1 of 5 patients. They concluded that gyrate atrophy is genetically heterogeneous.

Wirtz et al. (1985) performed complementation analysis on fused fibroblasts from 7 patients, measuring the ratio of 14C-ornithine to 3H-leucine incorporated into protein. They found no positive complementation. This suggested that pyridoxine-responsive and nonresponsive gyrate atrophy are caused by different allelic mutations at the same OKT locus.

From Western blot and kinetic data, Kennaway et al. (1989) concluded that gyrate atrophy is heterogeneous both within and between the pyridoxine-responsive and nonresponsive groups.

### Molecular Genetics

Ramesh et al. (1988) found significant linkage disequilibrium between OAT RFLPs and gyrate atrophy. They considered this genetic evidence that a defect in the OAT structural gene causes the disorder.

Mitchell et al. (1988) cloned and sequenced the human OAT cDNA and worked out the intron-exon structure of the gene. They performed RNase A protection experiments with RNA from 4 Lebanese patients with gyrate atrophy from 2 pedigrees and found a probe-target mismatch at the 5-prime end of the first coding exon. Sequencing showed a G-to-A transition that changed the initiator ATG (methionine) codon to ATA. The mutation segregated with the disease allele in both pedigrees. If translation started at the nearest in-frame methionine codon, OAT would be shortened by 138 amino acids, removing the whole mitochondrial leader sequence and 113 amino acids of the mature peptide.

Using a variety of techniques, Brody et al. (1992) surveyed the OAT genes of patients with gyrate atrophy and identified 21 new alleles. In cultured fibroblasts, they studied these together with 3 previously described mutations. Most alleles (20 of 24) produced normal amounts of normally sized OAT mRNA, but only 2 of 24 produced normal amounts of OAT antigen. The authors recreated the mutations by site-directed mutagenesis and expressed the mutant OAT in Chinese hamster ovary cells. These experiments confirmed that several of the mutations inactivate OAT and cause gyrate atrophy in the patients.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Among their pyridoxine-responsive patients, Kennaway et al. (1989) found that the patient with the mildest clinical disease had the highest Km for PLP. However, her enzyme was also the most stable, which the authors suggested presumably contributed to her milder phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Shih et al. (1978) concluded that OKT deficiency is closely associated with the genetic defect in gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina.

Sipilä et al. (1979) suggested that the muscle and eye changes in gyrate atrophy are probably related to abnormal creatine synthesis caused by the enlarged body pool of ornithine. They also suggested that muscle abnormalities may be present in other tapetoretinal dystrophies.

Sipilä et al. (1980) concluded that in gyrate atrophy, high ornithine concentrations inhibit the formation of guanidinoacetate, creatine, and possibly phosphocreatine at the arginine-glycine transamidinase step. They suggested that a deficiency of high-energy phosphates may underlie the eye and muscle atrophies. Sipilä et al. (1981) similarly proposed that part of the disease mechanism may be deficient creatine formation caused by hyperornithinemia, leading to depleted cellular phosphocreatine energy stores.

McInnes et al. (1981) suggested that lowering plasma ornithine may reverse a metabolic neuroretinopathy in gyrate atrophy and might halt progression of the retinal atrophy.

Sipilä et al. (1981) could not show substrate inhibition of the mutant liver OAT at 1,000 mM ornithine, whereas ornithine above 70 mM inhibited the normal enzyme. They concluded that the mutant enzyme in their 2 patients probably had an altered binding site for ornithine.

Wang et al. (1995) found neonatal hypoornithinemia in OAT-deficient mice and transient hypoornithinemia in a human infant with gyrate atrophy, followed by hyperornithinemia later in life. They concluded that the OAT reaction plays opposite roles in neonatal and adult mammals. ⚠︎

Valtonen et al. (1999) noted that the retinal and type II muscle fiber findings in gyrate atrophy had been suggested to result from a deficiency of creatine phosphate induced by hyperornithinemia. By analogy with guanidinoacetate methyltransferase deficiency, they investigated involvement of the central nervous system.

From studies in OAT-deficient mice, Wang et al. (2000) concluded that ornithine accumulation is a necessary factor in the retinal degeneration of gyrate atrophy. They also concluded that restoring OAT activity in the retina is not required for effective treatment.

### Diagnosis

Ramesh et al. (1988) suggested that OAT RFLPs could be used for prenatal diagnosis and carrier detection in families with a previous history of gyrate atrophy.

Shih et al. (1988) distinguished carriers of the 2 variants by how much fibroblast OKT activity rose with pyridoxal phosphate in vitro. The ratio of activity at 0.04 mM PLP to activity at 0 mM PLP was 1.18 +/- 0.18 in controls (N = 12), 1.43 +/- 0.26 in pyridoxine-nonresponsive heterozygotes (N = 5), and 2.20 +/- 0.14 in pyridoxine-responsive heterozygotes (N = 3).

### Clinical Management

Stoppoloni et al. (1978) found that restricting protein intake to a minimum of 0.8 g/kg/day significantly lowered plasma ornithine in their 3-year-old patient.

Valle et al. (1980) reported that in one patient an arginine-deficient diet progressively lowered plasma ornithine from 13 times normal to the upper limit of normal, and ornithinuria and lysinuria disappeared. Oral alpha-aminoisobutyric acid sped up the fall in plasma ornithine by increasing renal ornithine losses. As plasma ornithine approached normal, plasma lysine normalized and plasma arginine became subnormal. Over 1.5 years on a diet of 10 to 20 g of protein plus essential amino acids, plasma ornithine stayed between 55 and 355 microM. The chorioretinal degeneration did not progress, and vision apparently improved.

Kennaway et al. (1980) documented an in vivo response to oral vitamin B6 in 3 of 4 patients: fasting serum ornithine fell significantly and lysine rose.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1980) put a female patient with gyrate atrophy on an arginine-deficient diet, which lowered her plasma ornithine fivefold over 20 months. About 15 months after the diet began, she noted subjective improvement in visual function, documented by improvements in the electroretinogram, dark adaptation, and color vision. Rod function improved more than cone function. The authors cautioned that the results were preliminary and came from a single patient, but suggested that a low-arginine diet is beneficial.

McInnes et al. (1981) treated their 15-year-old patient with a low-arginine diet that lowered plasma ornithine to near normal (0.15-0.25 mmol/l). After 5 weeks, visual function improved in the eye that had been severely impaired for 3 years. The improvement lasted until compliance with the diet worsened at 8 months, after which vision regressed toward its pretreatment state. Overrestriction of dietary arginine caused hyperammonemia, and the authors concluded that arginine is an essential amino acid in ornithine transaminase deficiency.

Sipilä et al. (1981) gave 1.5 g of creatine daily to 7 patients with gyrate atrophy for 1 year. Type II muscle fiber diameter increased from 34.1 +/- 7.1 to 49.9 +/- 7.0 micron (P less than 0.001), with no significant change in type I fibers. The visual fields showed no further constriction, and fundus photography showed slow impairment at an age otherwise associated with rapid progression. The authors called these promising preliminary results.

Valtonen et al. (1999) found that brain MRI and EEG findings in 9 patients who had received creatine or creatine precursor supplementation daily for several years did not differ from those in 23 untreated patients.

Caruso et al. (2001) concluded that visual function often declines slowly in gyrate atrophy, so a long-term clinical trial would be needed to test whether gene replacement preserves visual function.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (2002) followed 2 pairs of sibs with gyrate atrophy on an arginine-restricted diet for 16 to 17 years. In each pair, the younger sib, who started the diet at an earlier age, showed slower progression of chorioretinal lesions than the older sib. The authors concluded that long-term substantial reduction of plasma ornithine, if started early, may appreciably slow progression of the lesions and, to a lesser extent, the progressive loss of retinal function.

Balfoort et al. (2021) systematically reviewed 33 studies covering 107 patients with GACR, most of them case reports. Protein-restricted diets lowered ornithine by 16.0 to 91.2%, and lysine supplementation lowered it by 21 to 34%. Pyridoxine responsiveness was reported for 30% of the included mutations. Other treatments reported were creatine or creatine-precursor supplementation and proline supplementation. The authors found no randomized controlled trials or large cohort studies. Because no study had predefined clinical outcome measures or structured follow-up, they concluded that the clinical effectiveness of treatment could not be judged, even though ornithine levels could be lowered.

### Animal Model

Wang et al. (1995) produced OAT-deficient mice by gene targeting. Unexpectedly, the newborn mice had hypoornithinemia and died as neonates, but short-term arginine supplementation rescued them. After weaning, the mice developed hyperornithinemia similar to that of human patients with gyrate atrophy. Over several months, they developed a retinal degeneration involving the photoreceptors and the pigment epithelium. The authors concluded that these mice appeared to be an excellent model of human gyrate atrophy. ⚠︎

Wang et al. (2000) put Oat -/- mice on an arginine-restricted diet to keep ornithine low over the long term. Over 12 months, they followed plasma amino acids, electroretinograms, and retinal histology and ultrastructure. The diet substantially lowered plasma ornithine and completely prevented retinal degeneration.
