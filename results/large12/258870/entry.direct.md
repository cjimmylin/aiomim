### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina (GACR) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous (bi-allelic) mutation in the gene encoding ornithine aminotransferase (OAT) on chromosome 10q26.

### Description

Gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina is an autosomal recessive chorioretinal degeneration. It is caused by deficiency of the mitochondrial matrix enzyme ornithine aminotransferase (OAT) (Mitchell et al., 1988). Vision declines early in life and eventually leads to complete blindness (Balfoort et al., 2021). The disease also affects other systems: type 2 skeletal muscle fibers atrophy and contain tubular aggregates (Sipila et al., 1979), and the central and peripheral nervous systems are involved (Valtonen et al., 1999; Peltola et al., 2002).

### Nomenclature

In their titles, Francois et al. (1960) and Francois et al. (1966) referred to the disorder as Fuchs' atrophia gyrata chorioideae et retinae. Francois et al. (1960) also called it Alder's anomaly.

### Clinical Features

Simell and Takki (1973) reported raised plasma ornithine in patients with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina.

Stoppoloni et al. (1978) described a 3-year-9-month-old girl with hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina. She also had mild mental retardation, delayed language development, and speech defects.

Kennaway et al. (1980) described 4 patients with hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy. In 1 patient, an oral glucose tolerance test caused marked changes in serum ornithine and lysine and revealed mild glucose intolerance. Histochemical staining of muscle biopsies showed intracellular inclusions in type 2 muscle fibers. On electron microscopy, these were tubular aggregates about 60 nm in diameter lying next to the sarcoplasmic membrane.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1983) described cataract in 9 phakic patients with gyrate atrophy. The lens opacities lay mainly along the confluence of the posterior sutures and interfered with vision because they were in the visual axis. In 3 cataractous lenses removed from patients aged 31 to 47 years, the posterior suture region was filled with liquefied, degenerated lens material typical of senile cataract. The authors found cataract to be a uniform finding by the second decade. They considered it distinct from the cataracts seen in other forms of retinal degeneration.

Fukuda et al. (1983) studied 2 brothers with gyrate atrophy and hyperornithinemia. Ornithine concentrations were raised in erythrocytes, lymphocytes, and granulocytes, to a degree that differed by cell type. Aspartic acid was low in erythrocytes.

Caruso et al. (2001) followed 5 patients with gyrate atrophy for 4 to 6 years while assessing them as candidates for gene replacement therapy. Median visual field half-lives were 17.0 years by static perimetry and 11.4 years by kinetic perimetry. Median electroretinogram half-lives were 16.0 years for the maximal response and 10.7 years for the flicker response. The authors concluded that visual function often declines slowly.

### Other Features

Sipila et al. (1979) studied 21 patients with gyrate atrophy and hyperornithinemia. None of the patients was weak, yet type 2 muscle fibers were almost always atrophic and contained tubular aggregates. Both sexes were affected equally, and the number of type 2 fibers fell with age. The authors stated that gyrate atrophy was the first disease in which tubular aggregates had been shown in females.

Valtonen et al. (1996) examined the thigh muscles of 7 patients with gyrate atrophy by muscle biopsy, CT, and ultralow-field MRI. Every biopsy showed type 2 fiber atrophy, and 6 of the 7 samples contained tubular aggregates. Muscle CT density was increased in the only child in the study, decreased in 3 elderly subjects, and normal in the other 3 patients. Compared with controls, patients had shorter mean T1 relaxation times (135 +/- 5 ms vs 157 +/- 12 ms) and slightly, but not significantly, longer T2 relaxation times.

Valtonen et al. (1999) studied 23 untreated patients with gyrate atrophy, comparing them with age-matched controls and with 9 patients who had taken creatine or creatine precursor supplements for several years. Brain MRI showed degenerative white matter lesions in 50% of patients and premature atrophic changes in 70%, with a striking increase in the number of Virchow spaces. Of the patients who had an EEG, 58% showed abnormal slow background activity, focal lesions, or high-amplitude beta rhythm. The EEG findings were not associated with the MRI changes or with age or sex. MRI and EEG findings did not differ between supplemented and untreated patients.

Peltola et al. (2002) assessed peripheral nervous system involvement in 40 patients with gyrate atrophy aged 5 to 74 years. Neurography was abnormal in 21 (53%), and the abnormalities were associated with age and with the severity of eye changes. Quantitative sensory testing showed abnormal large-fiber function in 7 patients (18%) and abnormal small-fiber function in 4 (10%). Somatosensory and brainstem auditory evoked potentials were abolished in 5 patients.

### Biochemical Features

Valle et al. (1977) found no detectable ornithine aminotransferase activity in phytohemagglutinin-stimulated lymphocytes from a patient with gyrate atrophy. In her heterozygous daughter's cells, activity was 44% of normal. Kennaway et al. (1977) reported deficient ornithine ketoacid aminotransferase activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from patients.

O'Donnell et al. (1978) found that a patient's cultured fibroblasts did not convert L-ornithine to proline because of deficient OAT activity. Her heterozygous father had intermediate enzyme activity. Shih et al. (1978) found OAT activity virtually absent in fibroblasts from 5 affected patients and reduced by 42 to 65% in fibroblasts from 4 carrier parents.

Valle et al. (1980) studied 4 patients, all with hyperornithinemia. They also had raised plasma histidine and lowered plasma lysine and branched-chain amino acids, which renal clearances were not high enough to explain.

Kennaway et al. (1980) found deficient OAT activity in cultured fibroblasts from all 4 of their patients, and about half-normal levels in obligate heterozygotes. Mean serum ornithine in obligate heterozygotes was slightly above normal but overlapped considerably with the normal range. Oral ornithine tolerance testing separated carriers from controls in only 1 of 5 cases.

Sipila et al. (1980) noted that patients have 10- to 20-fold raised ornithine in body fluids. After intravenous arginine, the rise in serum ornithine was more than 3 times greater in 6 patients than in 6 controls, and the mean plasma ornithine half-time was 360 minutes in patients versus 97 minutes in controls. Patients always excreted less urinary guanidinoacetate than controls.

Sipila et al. (1981) found trace OAT activity in liver from 2 patients. The mutant enzyme's Km for ornithine was 200 mM, 50-fold higher than normal. Raising alpha-oxoglutarate and pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate had no effect, and no substrate inhibition occurred at 1,000 mM ornithine, whereas the normal enzyme was inhibited above 70 mM. The authors suggested that the mutant enzyme had an altered ornithine binding site.

Kennaway et al. (1989) characterized mutant OAT from 9 patients. Fibroblast mitochondria from pyridoxine-responsive patients had a higher apparent Km for pyridoxal phosphate than those from nonresponders, whose Km was normal. The apparent Km for ornithine was normal in the 7 patients tested. The responsive patient with the mildest disease had the highest Km for pyridoxal phosphate but also the most stable enzyme, which the authors thought probably contributed to her milder phenotype. On Western blot, OAT protein was clearly detectable in pyridoxine-responsive patients and in 2 of 5 nonresponders, and low or undetectable in the other 3.

### Inheritance

Francois et al. (1960, 1966) studied carriers of the gyrate atrophy gene, and Takki and Simell (1974) addressed the genetics of the disorder. Shih et al. (1978) described the disease as autosomal recessive. Barrett et al. (1987) reviewed 80 biochemically confirmed cases and confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Barrett et al. (1987) used somatic cell hybrids and in situ hybridization to map OAT gene sequences to chromosomes 10q26 and Xp11.2. Their review of 80 cases supported a functional OAT gene on chromosome 10.

Ramesh et al. (1988) defined 6 RFLPs at the OAT locus, all of which mapped to chromosome 10. In 1 pedigree, the OAT locus segregated concordantly with gyrate atrophy. The RFLPs also showed significant disequilibrium with the disease, giving genetic evidence that a defect in the OAT structural gene causes the disorder.

### Heterogeneity

Shih et al. (1978) found that raising pyridoxal phosphate in the assay partly restored OAT activity in fibroblasts from 1 of 5 patients, and concluded that the disease is genetically heterogeneous. Kennaway et al. (1980) documented an in vivo response to vitamin B6 in 3 of 4 patients, with lower fasting serum ornithine and higher lysine. Fibroblasts from these patients showed increased OAT activity at higher pyridoxal phosphate concentrations. ⚠︎

Wirtz et al. (1985) studied fibroblasts from 4 pyridoxine-responsive and 3 nonresponsive patients. Responsive patients had higher OAT activity and more incorporation of 14C-ornithine into protein. Fused cells showed no positive complementation, suggesting that the responsive and nonresponsive forms are allelic mutations at the same locus.

Shih et al. (1988) found intermediate OAT activity in fibroblasts from heterozygotes for both the pyridoxine-responsive and pyridoxine-nonresponsive variants. The ratio of OAT activity at 0.04 mM to 0 mM pyridoxal phosphate distinguished the 2 carrier types. The ratio was 1.18 +/- 0.18 in controls, 1.43 +/- 0.26 in nonresponsive heterozygotes, and 2.20 +/- 0.14 in responsive heterozygotes.

### Molecular Genetics

Mitchell et al. (1988) studied 4 Lebanese patients from 2 pedigrees. RNase protection with antisense OAT probes revealed a mismatch at the 5-prime end of the first coding exon, and sequencing of PCR-amplified genomic DNA showed a G-to-A transition that changed the initiator ATG codon to ATA. The mutation segregated with the disease allele in both pedigrees. The authors predicted that translation starting at the nearest in-frame methionine would truncate OAT by 138 amino acids, removing the whole mitochondrial leader sequence and 113 amino acids of the mature peptide.

Brody et al. (1992) screened the OAT genes of patients with gyrate atrophy and characterized 21 newly recognized OAT alleles. They studied these and 3 previously described mutations in cultured fibroblasts. Most alleles (20 of 24) produced normal amounts of normal-sized OAT mRNA, but only 2 of 24 produced normal amounts of OAT antigen. When several of the mutations were recreated by site-directed mutagenesis and expressed in Chinese hamster ovary cells, they inactivated the enzyme.

### Pathogenesis

Sipila et al. (1979) proposed that the muscle and eye changes stem from abnormal creatine synthesis caused by the enlarged body pool of ornithine. Sipila et al. (1980) gave intravenous arginine to patients and found less guanidinoacetate formation than in controls. They concluded that high ornithine inhibits the transamidination step that forms guanidinoacetate, creatine, and possibly phosphocreatine. They suggested that a shortage of high-energy phosphates may underlie the eye and muscle atrophy.

### Clinical Management

Stoppoloni et al. (1978) found that restricting protein intake to a minimum of 0.8 g/kg/day significantly lowered plasma ornithine in their patient.

Valle et al. (1980) put 1 patient on an arginine-deficient diet, which lowered plasma ornithine from 13 times normal to the upper limit of normal. Ornithinuria and lysinuria disappeared. Oral alpha-aminoisobutyric acid sped the fall by increasing renal ornithine loss. During 1.5 years on a diet of 10 to 20 g protein plus essential amino acids, plasma ornithine stayed between 55 and 355 microM, the chorioretinal degeneration did not progress, and vision apparently improved. Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1980) reported that this diet lowered plasma ornithine fivefold over 20 months. After about 15 months, the electroretinogram, dark adaptation, and color vision improved, affecting rod function more than cone function.

McInnes et al. (1981) gave a low-arginine diet to a 15-year-old patient with plasma ornithine of 0.6 to 1.2 mmol/l, lowering it to 0.15 to 0.25 mmol/l. After 5 weeks, visual function improved in 1 eye that had been severely impaired for 3 years. The improvement lasted until dietary compliance worsened at 8 months, after which vision regressed. Restricting arginine too far caused hyperammonemia, showing that arginine is an essential amino acid in OAT deficiency.

Sipila et al. (1981) gave 7 patients 1.5 g of creatine daily for 1 year. Type II muscle fiber diameters rose from 34.1 +/- 7.1 to 49.9 +/- 7.0 microns, with no significant change in type I fibers. Visual fields did not constrict further, and fundus photographs showed slow worsening at an age usually associated with rapid progression.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (2002) followed 2 pairs of sibs treated with an arginine-restricted diet for 16 to 17 years. In each pair, the younger sib, who started the diet earlier, had slower progression of the chorioretinal lesions. The authors concluded that long-term reduction of ornithine started early may slow the lesions appreciably and slow the loss of retinal function to a lesser extent.

Balfoort et al. (2021) systematically reviewed 33 studies covering 107 patients. Treatments included protein-restricted diets, pyridoxine, creatine or creatine precursors, L-lysine, and proline. Protein-restricted diets lowered ornithine by 16.0 to 91.2%, and lysine supplementation lowered it by 21 to 34%. Pyridoxine responsiveness was reported for 30% of the mutations included. The studies were of low to moderate quality, and clinical effectiveness could not be established.

### Animal Model

Wang et al. (1995) produced OAT-deficient mice by gene targeting. Newborn mice had hypoornithinemia and died, but short-term arginine supplementation rescued them. After weaning, they developed hyperornithinemia like that of human patients. Over several months, they developed a retinal degeneration involving photoreceptors and pigment epithelium. Studies in 1 human infant with gyrate atrophy also showed transient hypoornithinemia.

Wang et al. (2000) kept Oat -/- mice on an arginine-restricted diet. Over 12 months, the diet substantially lowered plasma ornithine and completely prevented retinal degeneration. The authors concluded that ornithine accumulation is necessary for the retinal degeneration and that treatment does not require restoring OAT activity in the retina.

### History

Simell and Takki (1973) reported the association of gyrate atrophy with raised plasma ornithine. Valle et al. (1977) then showed ornithine aminotransferase deficiency in patient lymphocytes, demonstrating an enzyme deficiency in the disorder. ⚠︎
