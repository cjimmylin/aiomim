### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina (GACR) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous (biallelic) mutation in the gene encoding ornithine aminotransferase (OAT; 613349) on chromosome 10q26.

### Description

Gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina is an autosomal recessive chorioretinal degeneration associated with hyperornithinemia and caused by deficiency of the mitochondrial matrix enzyme ornithine-delta-aminotransferase (OAT) (Valle et al., 1977; Sipila et al., 1981; Mitchell et al., 1988; Brody et al., 1992). Balfoort et al. (2021) described it as a rare inborn error of amino acid metabolism, caused by biallelic variants in the OAT gene, in which vision declines in early life and eventually leads to complete blindness, with high plasma ornithine levels. Peltola et al. (2002) listed its features as hyperornithinemia, progressive centripetal loss of vision, minor CNS abnormalities, and type II muscle fiber atrophy with accumulation of tubular aggregates. They concluded from their peripheral nerve findings that gyrate atrophy is a systemic disease affecting both the peripheral nervous system and the CNS.

### Clinical Features

Stoppoloni et al. (1978) reported a girl aged 3 years and 9 months with hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina. She also had mild mental retardation, delayed language development, and speech defects.

Sipila et al. (1979) studied 21 patients with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina and hyperornithinemia. The patients were not weak. However, in almost all of them the type 2 muscle fibers were atrophic and contained tubular aggregates. Both sexes were affected equally, and this was the first disease in which tubular aggregates had been shown in females. The number of type 2 fibers decreased with age.

Kennaway et al. (1980) described 4 patients with hyperornithinemia and gyrate atrophy. In punch muscle biopsies, histochemical staining showed intracellular inclusions in type 2 muscle fibers. Electron microscopy showed tubular aggregates about 60 nm in diameter next to the sarcoplasmic membrane.

Sipila et al. (1981) described gyrate atrophy as characterized by progressive constriction of the visual fields, a 10- to 20-fold elevation of plasma ornithine, and reduced ornithine aminotransferase activity. They noted that atrophy of type II muscle fibers is morphologically conspicuous but clinically unimportant, and that it progresses together with the eye disease.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1983) described cataract in 9 phakic patients with gyrate atrophy. The lens opacities lay mainly along the confluence of the posterior sutures and interfered with vision because they were in the visual axis. The authors concluded that cataract is a uniform finding in gyrate atrophy by the second decade and appears to be unique compared with cataracts associated with other forms of retinal degeneration. They examined 3 cataractous lenses removed from patients aged 31 to 47 years, both grossly and microscopically. On histology, the posterior suture region was filled with liquefied, degenerated lens material resembling typical senile cataract changes.

Valtonen et al. (1996) examined the thigh muscles of 7 patients with gyrate atrophy using semi-open conchotome muscle biopsies, CT, and ultralow-field (0.02 T) MRI. All biopsy samples showed type 2 muscle fiber atrophy, and 6 of 7 showed tubular aggregates. CT density of thigh muscle was increased in the only child studied, decreased in 3 elderly patients, and normal in the remaining 3. Compared with 6 healthy controls, mean MRI T1 relaxation times were decreased in patients (135 vs 157 ms, p = 0.002), whereas T2 was slightly but not significantly increased. The authors concluded that the thigh muscles of patients with gyrate atrophy universally show changes on CT and MRI, but that relaxation time measurements gave little additional information on muscle metabolism.

Valtonen et al. (1999) studied central nervous system involvement in 23 untreated patients with gyrate atrophy (mean age 32 +/- 18 years) and compared them with age-matched healthy controls. On brain MRI, 50% of patients had degenerative white matter lesions and 70% had premature atrophic changes, with a striking increase in the number of Virchow (perivascular) spaces. Of the patients who had an EEG, 58% had abnormal slow background activity, focal lesions, or high-amplitude beta rhythm (more than 50 microV). The EEG findings were not associated with the MRI changes or with age or sex. The authors concluded that early degenerative and atrophic brain changes and an abnormal EEG are features of gyrate atrophy, in addition to the known eye and muscle manifestations.

Caruso et al. (2001) followed visual function in 5 patients with gyrate atrophy for 4 to 6 years before a gene replacement therapy trial. They used visual field sensitivity and electroretinogram amplitude as outcome variables and calculated the half-life of each. Median visual field half-lives were 17.0 years by static perimetry and 11.4 years by kinetic perimetry. Median electroretinogram half-lives were 16.0 years for the maximal response and 10.7 years for the flicker response. The authors concluded that visual function frequently declines slowly.

Peltola et al. (2002) studied peripheral nervous system involvement in 40 patients with gyrate atrophy (mean age 31.6 +/- 16.3 years; range 5 to 74 years) using neurography, quantitative sensory threshold testing, and evoked potential testing. Neurography was abnormal in 21 patients (53%), and these abnormalities were associated with patient age and with the severity of the ophthalmologic changes. Quantitative sensory threshold testing showed abnormal large-fiber function in 7 patients (18%) and abnormal small-fiber function in 4 patients (10%). Somatosensory and brainstem auditory evoked potential responses were abolished in 5 patients.

### Biochemical Features

Simell and Takki (1973) reported that raised plasma ornithine occurs with gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina.

Valle et al. (1977) measured ornithine aminotransferase (OAT; EC 2.6.1.13) activity in phytohemagglutinin-stimulated lymphocytes from a woman with gyrate atrophy and from her daughter. Thymidine incorporation and other transformation-sensitive enzymes confirmed that the patient's cells were transformed. The patient's cells had no detectable OAT activity, and the heterozygous daughter's cells had 44% of normal activity. The authors concluded that these results demonstrated an enzyme deficiency in gyrate atrophy.

Kennaway et al. (1977) showed that ornithine ketoacid aminotransferase activity was deficient in cultured skin fibroblasts from patients with gyrate atrophy.

O'Donnell et al. (1978) found that cultured fibroblasts from a female patient with gyrate atrophy did not convert uniformly 14C-labeled L-ornithine to proline. They showed that this metabolic block was due to deficient L-ornithine:2-oxoacid aminotransferase activity.

Shih et al. (1978) found virtually no ornithine ketoacid transaminase (OKT) activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from 5 patients with gyrate atrophy. They concluded that OKT deficiency is closely associated with the genetic defect in this disorder.

Valle et al. (1980) studied 4 patients with gyrate atrophy, all of whom had hyperornithinemia. The patients also had elevated plasma histidine and decreased plasma lysine and branched-chain amino acids. Renal clearances of these amino acids were not raised enough to explain the low plasma levels.

Kennaway et al. (1980) found deficient OKT activity in cultured skin fibroblasts from all 4 of their patients, and about half-normal activity in obligate heterozygotes. In 1 patient, an oral glucose tolerance test caused marked changes in serum ornithine and lysine, along with mild glucose intolerance.

Sipila et al. (1980) gave intravenous arginine to 6 patients with gyrate atrophy and 6 controls. Serum arginine curves were similar in the 2 groups, but the rise in serum ornithine was more than 3 times greater in patients. Mean plasma ornithine half-time was 360 min in patients versus 97 min in controls, and patients cleared ornithine from the extracellular fluid significantly more slowly. After the arginine load, urinary guanidinoacetate excretion rose sharply in all controls, more so in females. Patients always excreted less guanidinoacetate than controls, with highly significant differences within each sex. Differences in creatine excretion were less marked.

Sipila et al. (1981) found trace OAT activity in liver tissue from 2 patients with gyrate atrophy. The mutant enzyme's Km for ornithine was 200 mM, 50-fold higher than normal. Raising alpha-oxoglutarate and pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate to 10 times the concentrations giving maximal activity of the normal enzyme had no effect on the mutant enzyme. Ornithine concentrations above 70 mM inhibited the normal enzyme, but the mutant enzyme showed no substrate inhibition even at 1,000 mM ornithine.

McInnes et al. (1981) found that restricting dietary arginine too much caused hyperammonemia in their patient. They concluded that arginine is an essential amino acid in ornithine transaminase deficiency.

Fukuda et al. (1983) measured free amino acids in erythrocytes, lymphocytes, and granulocytes from 2 brothers with gyrate atrophy and hyperornithinemia. Ornithine was raised in the blood cells to a degree that depended on cell type, and aspartic acid was low in erythrocytes. After an oral dose of ornithine, they followed the concentrations for up to 24 hours. Blood cell ornithine peaked at 2 hours and stayed high for at least 4 hours, but after 2 hours the concentration curves differed among erythrocytes, lymphocytes, and granulocytes.

Wirtz et al. (1985) studied fibroblasts from 4 pyridoxine-responsive and 3 nonresponsive patients with gyrate atrophy. Compared with cells from nonresponsive patients, cells from responsive patients had higher OKT activity in homogenates and incorporated more radioactivity from 14C-ornithine into protein when cultured cells were tested in situ.

Shih et al. (1988) found intermediate OKT activity in cultured fibroblasts from heterozygotes for both the pyridoxine-responsive and the pyridoxine-nonresponsive variants of gyrate atrophy.

Kennaway et al. (1989) used a sensitive radiochemical assay to characterize mutant OAT in fibroblast mitochondria from 9 patients with gyrate atrophy, 4 of whom were pyridoxine-responsive. The apparent Km for pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) was higher in pyridoxine-responsive patients than in nonresponsive patients, whose Km for PLP was normal. The apparent Km for ornithine was normal in the 7 patients studied. The authors stated that these data clarified the mechanism of pyridoxine response.

After their findings in OAT-deficient mice, Wang et al. (1995) studied a human infant with gyrate atrophy and found transient hypoornithinemia. They concluded that the OAT reaction plays opposite roles in neonatal and adult mammals. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Francois et al. (1960) studied carriers of the gene for Fuchs gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina (atrophia gyrata chorioideae et retinae); the title of their report links the condition to Alder's anomaly. Francois et al. (1966) also wrote about gene carriers of this condition, and Takki and Simell (1974) discussed the genetic aspects of gyrate atrophy with hyperornithinemia.

Autosomal recessive inheritance was supported by intermediate enzyme activity in carriers. O'Donnell et al. (1978) found an intermediate level of L-ornithine:2-oxoacid aminotransferase activity in the father of an affected patient, who was an obligate heterozygote. Shih et al. (1978) found a 42 to 65% reduction in OKT activity in fibroblasts from 4 carrier parents of patients with gyrate atrophy, which they considered consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Barrett et al. (1987) reviewed 80 biochemically confirmed cases and found that the data confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Barrett et al. (1987) used a cDNA clone encoding human OAT and mapped OAT gene sequences to chromosome regions 10q26 and Xp11.2 by somatic cell hybrids and in situ hybridization. The Xp11.2 sequences mapped to the same region as L1.28 (Xp11.0-p11.3), a marker closely linked to X-linked recessive retinitis pigmentosa. In their review of 80 biochemically confirmed cases of gyrate atrophy, the data supported the presence of a functional OAT gene on chromosome 10.

Ramesh et al. (1988) defined 6 RFLPs at the OAT locus, all mapping to chromosome 10, with an overall heterozygosity of 83%. In the 1 pedigree available, the OAT locus segregated concordantly with gyrate atrophy. The RFLPs also showed significant linkage disequilibrium with the disorder, which the authors took as genetic evidence that a defect in the OAT structural gene causes gyrate atrophy.

### Heterogeneity

Shih et al. (1978) found that raising the pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6) concentration in the assay medium partially restored OKT activity in fibroblasts from 1 of 5 patients with gyrate atrophy. They concluded that the disease is genetically heterogeneous.

Wirtz et al. (1985) performed complementation analysis on fused fibroblasts from 4 pyridoxine-responsive and 3 nonresponsive patients, measuring the ratio of 14C/3H incorporation into protein after incubation with 14C-ornithine and 3H-leucine. None of the crosses showed positive complementation. The authors suggested that the pyridoxine-responsive and nonresponsive forms are caused by different allelic mutations at the same OKT locus.

Shih et al. (1988) showed that carriers of the pyridoxine-responsive and pyridoxine-nonresponsive forms could be distinguished by the in vitro response of fibroblast OKT activity to PLP. The ratio of OKT activity at 0.04 mM PLP to that at 0 mM PLP was lowest in controls (1.18 +/- 0.18; n = 12), intermediate in pyridoxine-nonresponsive heterozygotes (1.43 +/- 0.26; n = 5), and highest in pyridoxine-responsive heterozygotes (2.20 +/- 0.14; n = 3).

Kennaway et al. (1989) performed Western blot analysis of mitochondrial proteins with an antibody to human OAT. OAT protein was clearly detectable in pyridoxine-responsive patients and in 2 of 5 nonresponders, but was low or undetectable in the other 3 nonresponders. This indicated heterogeneity both within and between the pyridoxine-responsive and nonresponsive groups. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Mitchell et al. (1988) studied 4 Lebanese patients with gyrate atrophy from 2 pedigrees. RNase A protection with antisense RNA probes showed a probe-target mismatch at the 5-prime end of the first coding exon of OAT. PCR amplification and sequencing of genomic DNA identified a G-to-A transition that changed the initiator ATG (methionine) codon to ATA. The mutation segregated with the gyrate atrophy allele in both pedigrees.

Brody et al. (1992) surveyed the OAT genes of patients with gyrate atrophy and molecularly characterized 21 newly recognized OAT alleles, demonstrating extensive allelic heterogeneity. They then examined the effects of these 21 new and 3 previously described mutations on OAT mRNA, antigen, and enzyme activity in cultured patient fibroblasts. Of the 24 alleles, 20 produced normal amounts of normally sized OAT mRNA, but only 2 had normal amounts of OAT antigen. The authors reproduced OAT mutations by site-directed mutagenesis and expressed them in Chinese hamster ovary cells, confirming that several of the mutations inactivate OAT and cause gyrate atrophy in these patients.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Among their 9 patients, Kennaway et al. (1989) unexpectedly found that the pyridoxine-responsive patient with the mildest clinical disease had the highest Km for PLP. However, on heat-stability testing at 45 degrees C she also had the most stable OAT enzyme, which the authors presumed contributed to her milder phenotype. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Sipila et al. (1979) suggested that the muscle and eye changes in gyrate atrophy are probably related to abnormal creatine synthesis caused by the enlarged body pool of ornithine. They also suggested that muscle abnormalities may be present in other tapetoretinal dystrophies.

From their arginine-loading studies, Sipila et al. (1980) concluded that high ornithine concentrations in patients with gyrate atrophy inhibit the formation of guanidinoacetate, creatine, and possibly phosphocreatine at the transamidination step (arginine-glycine transamidinase). They suggested that a deficiency of high-energy phosphates may underlie the eye and muscle atrophies. Sipila et al. (1981) likewise proposed that part of the disease mechanism may be deficient creatine formation caused by hyperornithinemia, leading to a shortage of cellular phosphocreatine energy stores.

On the basis of kinetic studies of liver enzyme from 2 patients, Sipila et al. (1981) suggested that the abnormal OAT in these patients has an altered binding site for ornithine.

Valtonen et al. (1999) noted that the chorioretinal degeneration and the type II muscle fiber atrophy with tubular aggregates in gyrate atrophy had been suggested to result from phosphocreatine deficiency induced by hyperornithinemia. Guanidinoacetate methyltransferase deficiency, another disorder of creatine metabolism, causes abnormal brain MRI and EEG findings. For this reason they studied the CNS in gyrate atrophy, which appears to involve a milder degree of phosphocreatine deficiency.

Arginine restriction prevented retinal degeneration in Oat -/- mice. From this, Wang et al. (2000) concluded that ornithine accumulation is a necessary factor in the pathophysiology of the retinal degeneration and that effective treatment does not require restoring OAT activity in the retina.

### Diagnosis

Kennaway et al. (1980) found that the mean serum ornithine of obligate heterozygotes was slightly above normal, but their values overlapped considerably with the normal range. Oral ornithine tolerance tests distinguished carriers from controls in only 1 of 5 cases, whereas fibroblast OKT activity in carriers was about half of normal.

Ramesh et al. (1988) suggested that OAT RFLPs could be used for prenatal diagnosis and carrier detection in families with a previous history of gyrate atrophy. They added that the RFLPs would also allow identification of haplotypes associated with gyrate atrophy chromosomes.

### Clinical Management

Stoppoloni et al. (1978) found that restricting protein intake to a minimum of 0.8 g/kg/day significantly lowered plasma ornithine in their patient.

Valle et al. (1980) placed 1 patient on an arginine-deficient diet. Plasma ornithine fell steadily from 13 times normal to the upper limit of normal, and ornithinuria and lysinuria disappeared. Oral alpha-aminoisobutyric acid sped up the fall by increasing renal ornithine losses. As ornithine approached normal, plasma lysine normalized and plasma arginine became subnormal. Over 1.5 years on a 10-20 g protein diet supplemented with essential amino acids, plasma ornithine stayed between 55 and 355 microM. The chorioretinal degeneration did not progress, and vision apparently improved.

Kennaway et al. (1980) documented an in vivo response to vitamin B6 in 3 of 4 patients: oral B6 supplementation significantly lowered fasting serum ornithine and raised lysine. In vitro, the patients' fibroblasts showed increased OKT activity at higher pyridoxal phosphate concentrations. ⚠︎

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (1980) treated a female patient with an arginine-deficient diet, which lowered her plasma ornithine fivefold over 20 months. About 15 months after starting the diet, she noticed that her vision had improved. Her electroretinogram, dark adaptation, and color vision also improved, mostly in rod function and to a lesser extent in cone function. The authors considered these results preliminary but suggested that lowering plasma ornithine with a low-arginine diet is beneficial.

McInnes et al. (1981) treated a 15-year-old patient (plasma ornithine 0.6-1.2 mmol/l) with a low-arginine diet, which lowered plasma ornithine to 0.15-0.25 mmol/l, close to the normal range. After 5 weeks of treatment, visual function improved in one eye that had been severely impaired, without improvement, for 3 years. The improvement lasted until the patient stopped following the diet closely at 8 months, after which visual function regressed toward its pretreatment state. The authors suggested that lowering plasma ornithine may reverse a metabolic neuroretinopathy in this disease and might also halt progression of the retinal atrophy.

Sipila et al. (1981) gave 7 patients a dietary supplement of 1.5 g of creatine daily. After 1 year, type II muscle fiber diameter had increased from 34.1 +/- 7.1 to 49.9 +/- 7.0 microns (P less than 0.001), whereas type I fiber diameter did not increase significantly. Visual-field tests showed no further constriction during therapy, and fundus photography showed slow impairment at an age otherwise associated with rapid progression. The authors described these results as promising but preliminary.

Valtonen et al. (1999) found that the brain MRI and EEG findings of 9 patients who had taken creatine or creatine precursor supplements daily for several years did not differ from those of 23 untreated patients.

Because visual function in gyrate atrophy often declines slowly, Caruso et al. (2001) concluded that a long-term clinical trial would be needed to assess whether gene replacement therapy preserves visual function.

Kaiser-Kupfer et al. (2002) reported 2 pairs of sibs with gyrate atrophy who had been on an arginine-restricted diet to lower plasma ornithine for 16 to 17 years. In each pair, the younger sib started the diet at an earlier age, and that sib's chorioretinal lesions progressed more slowly. The authors concluded that long-term, substantial reduction of plasma ornithine, if begun early, may appreciably slow the progression of chorioretinal lesions and, to a lesser extent, the progressive loss of retinal function.

Balfoort et al. (2021) performed a systematic review of therapeutic interventions in gyrate atrophy following PRISMA guidelines. They identified 33 studies covering 107 patients, mostly case reports (27) or case series (4), and found no randomized controlled trials or large cohort studies. Protein-restricted diets lowered ornithine levels by 16.0 to 91.2%, and lysine supplementation lowered them by 21 to 34%. Pyridoxine responsiveness was reported in 30% of the included mutations. Other reported treatments were creatine or creatine precursor supplementation and proline supplementation. The authors concluded that a protein-restricted diet, pyridoxine supplementation (depending on the variant), and/or lysine supplementation can reduce ornithine levels. Because the included studies lacked predefined clinical outcome measures and structured follow-up, however, they could not draw conclusions about clinical effectiveness. They noted that there is no curative treatment and no international treatment protocol.

### Animal Model

Wang et al. (1995) produced OAT-deficient mice by gene targeting. As neonates the mice had hypoornithinemia and died, but short-term arginine supplementation rescued them. After weaning they developed hyperornithinemia similar to that of human patients with gyrate atrophy. Over several months they developed a retinal degeneration involving the photoreceptors and pigment epithelium. The authors concluded that OAT-deficient mice appear to be an excellent model of the human disease.

Wang et al. (2000) kept Oat -/- mice on an arginine-restricted diet for 12 months and monitored them by plasma amino acid measurement, electroretinography, and retinal histologic and ultrastructural studies. The diet substantially reduced plasma ornithine levels and completely prevented retinal degeneration. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 GYRATE ATROPHY OF THE CHOROID AND RETINA**  
OAT, MET1ILE

Mitchell et al. (1988) studied 4 Lebanese patients with gyrate atrophy from 2 pedigrees. RNase A protection with antisense RNA probes showed a probe-target mismatch at the 5-prime end of the first coding exon of the OAT gene. PCR amplification and sequencing of genomic DNA identified a G-to-A transition that changed the initiator ATG (methionine) codon to ATA, and the mutation segregated with the gyrate atrophy allele in both pedigrees. If translation began at the nearest in-frame methionine codon, OAT would be truncated by 138 amino acids, losing the entire mitochondrial leader sequence and 113 amino acids of the mature peptide.
