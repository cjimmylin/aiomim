### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the gene encoding acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA).

### Description

Late-onset Pompe disease, which includes juvenile and adult forms of glycogen storage disease type II, is caused by a partial deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase. Its course is more insidious than that of classic infantile Pompe disease. The typical symptoms are slowly progressive proximal muscle weakness and respiratory difficulties (Forsha et al., 2011). Weakness mainly affects the trunk, proximal lower limbs, and respiratory muscles, and onset can range from the first year of life to the sixth decade (Herbert et al., 2019).

### Nomenclature

Several names have been used for the forms of acid maltase deficiency that begin after infancy, including late infantile (Swaiman et al., 1968), childhood (Matsuishi et al., 1984), juvenile (Laforet et al., 2000), and adult-onset (Wokke et al., 1995) acid maltase deficiency. Forsha et al. (2011) used the term late-onset Pompe disease for both the juvenile and the adult forms.

### Clinical Features

Swaiman et al. (1968) reported late infantile acid maltase deficiency. Only the title of this report was available.

Hudgson et al. (1968) reported adult myopathy caused by glycogen storage disease due to acid maltase deficiency. Only the title of this report was available.

Engel (1970) studied 4 adults with acid maltase deficiency. He described it as a syndrome that may mimic muscular dystrophy or other myopathies. Only the title of this report was available.

Sivak et al. (1981) reported a 51-year-old man with a 6-year history of gradually progressive exertional dyspnea. He was found to have diaphragmatic paralysis, with only minimal weakness of the limb muscles. Electromyography suggested acid maltase deficiency, which was confirmed by electron microscopic and biochemical studies of a muscle biopsy.

Francesconi and Auff (1982) reported cardiac arrhythmias in the adult form of type II glycogenosis. Only the title of this report was available.

Matsuishi et al. (1984) studied 3 children with the childhood form of acid maltase deficiency: 2 sibs and 1 sporadic patient. Besides delayed developmental milestones and mild generalized muscle weakness, they found nasal vocalization and an EMG pattern of abnormal insertion voltage followed by pseudomyotonic discharge. They considered these 2 findings characteristic of the childhood form. Muscle histochemistry consistently showed selective type 2A fiber atrophy and type 2B fiber deficiency. Neutral maltase activity was highest in the muscle of the patient with the least severe weakness.

Trend et al. (1985) described 5 patients with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Pelvic girdle symptoms began in the late second or early third decade in all of them, and signs of respiratory insufficiency developed some years later. Typically the patients were tall and had weak, wasted paraspinal and gluteal muscles and lower limb weakness. All were orthopneic with marked diaphragmatic weakness.

Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 sons of healthy, nonconsanguineous parents. Each developed progressive proximal muscle weakness secondary to alpha-glucosidase deficiency, and each developed a fusiform basilar artery aneurysm. The aneurysm ruptured fatally in 2 of the brothers and was complicated by cerebellar infarction in the third. At postmortem examination there was severe vacuolation of skeletal muscle, liver, and vascular smooth muscle, with accumulation of periodic acid-Schiff (PAS)-positive, diastase-sensitive material. In the surviving brother, similar glycogen deposition was found in the smooth muscle of the superficial temporal artery.

Kretzschmar et al. (1990) reported a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency who had pronounced involvement of the liver and skeletal muscles. CT and MRI showed hypodense and hyperintense white matter areas. Myelin changes ranged from focal demyelination to necrosis. Small and large arteries showed vacuolar degeneration. Electron microscopy showed lysosomal and free cytoplasmic glycogen in the smooth muscle cells of large cerebral arteries and in the pericytes of arterioles and capillaries. There were many small aneurysms of the intracerebral arteries, which the authors attributed to cell damage in the vessel walls.

Chancellor et al. (1991) reported a 68-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency who presented with a myopathy and exercise-induced urinary incontinence. Only the title of this report was available.

Wokke et al. (1995) studied 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Worsening handicap was related to age, and decline in vital capacity was related to the duration of the symptomatic stage. Respiratory insufficiency was never the first manifestation.

Laforet et al. (2000) studied 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Progressive muscle weakness began in adulthood in all but 1 patient. However, 16 patients had mild, nonprogressive muscular symptoms that had appeared in childhood. Eighteen patients had a similar pattern of pelvic girdle weakness, predominantly affecting the glutei and thigh adductors. Eight patients had restrictive respiratory insufficiency with a vital capacity of less than 60%, and in 2 patients respiratory failure was the first manifestation.

Lam et al. (2003) described a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset GSD II. His 13-year-old brother carried the same 2 GAA mutations but was asymptomatic.

Anneser et al. (2005) described a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement that resembled the atherosclerotic angiopathy of the elderly.

Hagemans et al. (2005) collected questionnaire data on 255 children and adults with Pompe disease. Disease severity was associated with disease duration but not with age. Earlier manifestation of the disease meant earlier dependence on a wheelchair or ventilator. Among patients younger than 15 years there was a subgroup with a more severe and rapid course.

Amartino et al. (2006) reported 2 clinical forms of GSD II in 2 generations of the same family. Only the title of this report was available.

Groen et al. (2006) reported ptosis as a feature of late-onset glycogenosis type II. Only the title of this report was available.

Forsha et al. (2011) analyzed cardiovascular parameters in 87 patients with late-onset Pompe disease enrolled in a randomized placebo-controlled trial of enzyme replacement therapy. The median age was 44 years, and 51% were men. At baseline, 10% had a short PR interval, 7% had decreased left ventricular systolic function, and 5% had mildly elevated left ventricular mass on echocardiogram. Left ventricular hypertrophy was seen on ECG in 12%. Two patients had Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. The significant ventricular hypertrophy classically seen in infantile Pompe disease was not observed.

Herbert et al. (2019) reviewed 84 patients with late-onset Pompe disease carrying the c.-32-13T-G variant. Four patients (2 males and 2 females, all Caucasian) had been diagnosed clinically and had symptom onset in the first 2 years of life. Age at onset ranged from 10 days to 20 months, and age at diagnosis from 10 to 26 months. Initial symptoms included delayed gross motor milestones, signs of proximal muscle weakness, swallowing and feeding difficulties, and sleep apnea. All 4 patients showed early, characteristic alterations in posture and movement. Despite enzyme replacement therapy, musculoskeletal involvement progressed and residual muscle weakness was evident in all of them, with ptosis, myopathic facies, scoliosis, lumbar lordosis, scapular winging, and weakness of the trunk and lower limbs. Progressive scoliosis occurred in 2 of the 4 patients. Other features included elevated CK, AST, and ALT; hypernasal voice; Gowers sign; myotonic discharges on EMG; and gastrointestinal and genitourinary symptoms. ECG and echocardiography were normal.

Korlimarla et al. (2020) used brain MRI and developmental assessments in 14 children with Pompe disease receiving enzyme replacement therapy. Ten of the 12 children with infantile Pompe disease had white matter hyperintense foci. The 2 children with late-onset Pompe disease had no white matter hyperintense foci and scored high on most developmental assessments.

Jones et al. (2021) assessed tongue structure and function in 10 treatment-naive patients with late-onset Pompe disease, 30 patients with other acquired or hereditary myopathies, and 30 controls with neuropathy. Tongue strength was quantitatively lower and sonographic tongue thickness was decreased in the patients with late-onset Pompe disease compared with those with other myopathies. Ultrasound findings suggested fibrofatty replacement and atrophy.

Huggins et al. (2022) systematically evaluated 20 infants aged 6 to 21 months whose late-onset Pompe disease was diagnosed by newborn screening. None had cardiomyopathy or cardiac rhythm disturbances. CK, AST, and ALT were increased in 8 patients (40%), and Glc4 was increased in 2 (10%). All patients had postural and kinematic concerns. Three (17%) scored below the 10th percentile on the Alberta Infant Motor Scale. Speech-language assessments were normal, but 9 patients (45%) had mild feeding or swallowing abnormalities.

### Biochemical Features

Askanas et al. (1976) established muscle cultures from a biopsy of a patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. The newly grown cultured fibers showed the same abnormalities as the biopsied muscle: vacuoles filled with acid phosphatase-positive material and glycogen accumulated in membrane-bound sacs. Acid maltase was undetectable in both the cultured and the biopsied muscle by maltose hydrolysis, whereas acid phosphatase was increased in both.

Mehler and DiMauro (1977) used a sensitive fluorometric assay and found residual acid maltase activity in muscle biopsies from 15 patients with late-onset acid maltase deficiency (mean, 6.91%; range, 2.4 to 12.2%). No residual activity was found in muscle from 3 patients with the infantile form. The residual activity was lysosomal acid maltase rather than neutral maltase. It did not correlate with clinical severity or with glycogen accumulation. Acid maltase activity was present in muscle, liver, and especially leukocytes in late-onset but not infantile cases, and the 2 forms did not occur in the same family. The authors suggested that the 2 forms are genetically distinct.

Beratis et al. (1978) purified acid alpha-glucosidase from normal fibroblasts and from fibroblasts of a patient with the adult form. The enzyme from both sources had the same kinetic and physical properties. Fibroblasts from the adult form appeared to contain significantly less cross-reacting material (CRM) than normal or infantile-form cells. Enzyme activity was present in the immune complexes from adult-form fibroblasts but not in those from infantile-form fibroblasts. The authors concluded that in the adult form the mutation reduces the amount of enzyme protein in the cells.

Beratis et al. (1983) found that in 2 fibroblast strains from patients with the adult form, the amount of enzyme protein was reduced in direct proportion to the reduction in enzyme activity. In a third adult-form strain, activity was within the infantile range and no CRM was detected. The authors concluded that there is extensive genetic heterogeneity both between and within the clinical forms of acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency.

### Inheritance

Laforet et al. (2000), Anneser et al. (2005), Montalvo et al. (2006), and Jones et al. (2021) described late-onset Pompe disease, or acid maltase deficiency more generally, as autosomal recessive. In the reports with molecular data, patients carried mutations on both GAA alleles. Most were compound heterozygous (Wokke et al., 1995; Laforet et al., 2000), and some were homozygous for the c.-32-13T-G variant (Laforet et al., 2000; Huggins et al., 2022).

### Molecular Genetics

Wokke et al. (1995) performed genetic studies in 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All patients were compound heterozygous, with the IVS1-13T-G transversion in the GAA gene on 1 allele. The second allele carried a deletion of a T at position 525 in exon 2 in 7 probands (64%) and a deletion of exon 18 in 1 proband (9%).

Kroos et al. (1995) studied the frequency of 3 common mutant alleles, and their associated clinical phenotypes, in 121 patients with GSD II. Only the title of this report was available.

Laforet et al. (2000) performed molecular studies in 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Seventeen carried the IVS1-13T-G transversion, and 1 of these was homozygous for it. Both mutant alleles were identified in 10 patients. In total, 13 different GAA mutations were detected.

In a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset GSD II, Lam et al. (2003) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, P361L and R437C. The patient's asymptomatic 13-year-old brother was also compound heterozygous for both mutations.

In a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement, Anneser et al. (2005) identified 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, ala237-to-val (A237V) and gly293-to-arg (G293R).

Montalvo et al. (2006) analyzed the complete GAA gene in 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSD II and identified 12 novel alleles. Missense mutations were characterized functionally by enzyme activity and protein processing in a GAA-deficient human cell line. Splicing mutations were studied by RT-PCR and in silico analysis. One complex allele carried 3 different alterations in cis. The most frequent mutation was c.-32-13T-G, present in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of patients. It occurred together with c.2237G-A (W746X) in 9 of the 40 patients.

Herbert et al. (2019) noted that the splice variant c.-32-13T-G in intron 1 of the GAA gene allows production of low levels of normal GAA. In 4 Caucasian children with clinically diagnosed early-onset late-onset Pompe disease, they found compound heterozygosity for c.-32-13T-G and a second pathogenic variant. The second variants were c.525delT (p.Glu176ArgfsTer45), c.1827delC, c.2501_2502delCA (p.T834fs), and c.118C-T. Diagnosis was made by GAA enzyme assay and confirmed by variant analysis.

Among 20 infants with late-onset Pompe disease identified by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found that 4 were homozygous for the c.-32-13T-G variant, 14 were compound heterozygous, and 2 did not carry this variant.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wokke et al. (1995) concluded that adult-onset acid maltase deficiency is clinically and genetically fairly homogeneous. In their patients, serum creatine kinase and alpha-glucosidase activity in blood cells or muscle helped with diagnosis but had no prognostic value.

In contrast, Laforet et al. (2000) found high genetic heterogeneity among French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset disease and no clear correlation between mutation type and phenotype.

Lam et al. (2003) found that a 16-year-old Chinese proband with juvenile-onset GSD II and his asymptomatic 13-year-old brother carried the same 2 mutations. They suggested that other genes contribute to the phenotypic variability of GSD II.

Montalvo et al. (2006) discussed genotype-phenotype correlations among 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSD II. They gave particular emphasis to the subgroup with the c.-32-13T-G/c.2237G-A genotype. Only the abstract of this report was available.

De Filippi et al. (2010) studied the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) insertion/deletion polymorphism in 38 patients with late-onset Pompe disease aged 44.6 +/- 19.8 years. The genotype distribution resembled that of the general population. The DD genotype was associated with earlier disease onset (P = 0.041), higher creatine kinase at diagnosis (P = 0.024), muscle pain (P = 0.014), and faster disease progression (P = 0.037). The authors suggested that the ACE polymorphism may modulate the phenotype.

Herbert et al. (2019) found that the c.525delT and c.1827delC variants, which accompanied c.-32-13T-G in 2 of their 4 early-onset patients, were each present in 12 patients in their late-onset cohort, all with onset in adulthood. They concluded that these genotype combinations alone are not sufficient to cause the early-onset phenotype. Neither of the 2 early-onset patients whose ACE genotypes were available had the DD genotype.

### Diagnosis

Trend et al. (1985) found characteristic glycogen vacuoles in peripheral blood lymphocytes in all 5 of their adult patients. Muscle biopsy was characteristic in only 1 of 4 cases. The diagnosis was confirmed by enzyme assay in muscle and cultured fibroblasts.

Byrne et al. (1986) reported a family in which 7 members over 3 generations had a cardioskeletal myopathy with lysosomal glycogen storage. In 1 case, acid maltase levels were normal. The authors noted that this syndrome is unusual among glycogenoses because it shows dominant inheritance.

In a study of 18 patients with adult-onset GSD II, Ausems et al. (1999) found that measuring GAA activity in muscle and histopathologic analysis of muscle added nothing when leukocyte GAA activity was clearly deficient. Creatine kinase elevation was a sensitive marker of the disease.

Kallwass et al. (2007) showed that a fluorometric assay of GAA activity in dried blood spots, using the selective alpha-glucosidase inhibitor acarbose, efficiently detected patients with late-onset Pompe disease. Such patients are often misdiagnosed by conventional methods because of residual GAA activity in other tissues.

Bembi et al. (2008) stated that the diagnosis of glycogenosis type II in juveniles and adults is confirmed by markedly reduced GAA activity in blood samples, cultured fibroblasts, or muscle biopsies.

Jones et al. (2021) suggested that quantitative tongue weakness and/or atrophy may help distinguish late-onset Pompe disease from other acquired or hereditary myopathies.

### Clinical Management

Slonim et al. (1983) found that circulating branched-chain amino acids fell rapidly after protein ingestion in a child with acid maltase deficiency. They suggested that increased net muscle protein catabolism contributes to the disease. Twelve months of a high-protein diet reversed the child's weakness and wasting and improved muscle function, exercise tolerance, and growth.

In Trend et al. (1985), long-term home ventilatory support (25 patient-years) with a rocking bed or with intermittent positive pressure respiration via tracheostomy allowed all 5 adult patients to return to work, and their clinical condition remained stable.

Margolis and Hill (1986) treated a 55-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency and severe ventilatory muscle weakness with a high-protein (1.6 g/kg), weight-reducing diet for 27 months. Respiratory function improved strikingly, including ventilatory muscle strength and endurance.

Isaacs et al. (1986) reported a favorable response to a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet in an adult with lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency.

Bembi et al. (2008) recommended multidisciplinary management of glycogenosis type II and reviewed functional assessment, rehabilitation, nutrition, genetic counseling, prenatal diagnosis, screening, and emerging therapies.

In a double-blind trial, Forsha et al. (2011) gave patients with late-onset Pompe disease biweekly infusions of recombinant human acid alpha-glucosidase (20 mg/kg) or placebo for 78 weeks. Enzyme replacement therapy did not change ECG or echocardiographic parameters compared with placebo, and there were no significant cardiovascular safety concerns.

Wang et al. (2011) developed consensus guidelines for confirming the diagnosis and managing presymptomatic individuals with lysosomal storage diseases, including Pompe disease, who are identified by newborn screening, family testing, or carrier testing.

In the 4 children with early-onset disease reported by Herbert et al. (2019), median age at the start of enzyme replacement therapy was 23.5 months. All showed improvement on standardized gross motor assessments after starting treatment but remained below age level. Residual muscle deficits persisted and improved partially with higher doses of enzyme.

### Population Genetics

Bashan et al. (1988) reported 18 patients with alpha-glucosidase deficiency diagnosed in Israel over 15 years. All were Palestinian Arabs except 2 sibs from a Jewish Iraqi family. All had the infantile type except 1, who had the juvenile type. The authors noted a high prevalence of the disease in the Arab population.

Martiniuk et al. (1998) studied the carrier frequency of GSD II in New York and estimated the number of affected individuals born with the disease. Only the title of this report was available. ⚠︎

In 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSD II, Montalvo et al. (2006) found an allele frequency of 42.3% for the c.-32-13T-G mutation, similar to other Caucasian populations with late-onset disease. Herbert et al. (2019) stated that this variant is found on at least 1 allele in 68 to 90% of Caucasian patients with late-onset Pompe disease.

### Animal Model

Bijvoet et al. (1998) generated mice with targeted disruption of the Gaa gene. Homozygous knockout mice lacked Gaa mRNA and had virtually complete acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Glycogen-containing lysosomes appeared in liver, heart, and skeletal muscle soon after birth, and large focal glycogen deposits had formed by 13 weeks. At 9 months the mice had no overt clinical symptoms, but the heart was typically enlarged and the ECG was abnormal. ⚠︎

Raben et al. (1998) found that mice homozygous for disruption of the Gaa gene (6neo/6neo) lacked enzyme activity and accumulated glycogen in cardiac and skeletal muscle lysosomes by 3 weeks of age. Mobility and strength were markedly reduced by 3.5 weeks. As in the human adult disease, older mice accumulated glycogen in the diaphragm, and by 8 to 9 months they had obvious muscle wasting and a weak, waddling gait. In contrast, mice with deletion of exon 6 had unimpaired strength and mobility up to 6.5 months of age despite indistinguishable biochemical and pathologic changes. The authors suggested that the genetic background of the mouse strains contributed to these differences.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, IVS1AS, T-G, -13 [c.-32-13T>G; IVS1-13T>G]

Wokke et al. (1995) found the IVS1-13T-G transversion in the GAA gene in all 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All were compound heterozygous, with this mutation on 1 allele.

Laforet et al. (2000) found this mutation in 17 of 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- or adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. One of these patients was homozygous for it.

Montalvo et al. (2006) found the c.-32-13T-G mutation in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSD II, giving an allele frequency of 42.3%.

Herbert et al. (2019) reported 4 Caucasian children with symptom onset in the first 2 years of life, from a cohort of 84 patients with late-onset Pompe disease carrying this variant. All 4 were compound heterozygous for c.-32-13T-G and a second pathogenic GAA variant. The authors noted that this leaky splice-site variant allows production of low levels of normal GAA.

Among 20 infants with late-onset Pompe disease identified by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found 4 who were homozygous for c.-32-13T-G and 14 who were compound heterozygous.

**.0002 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, 1-BP DEL, 525T [c.525delT; p.Glu176ArgfsTer45]

In 7 of 11 probands (64%) with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Wokke et al. (1995) found a deletion of a T at position 525 in exon 2 of the GAA gene on the allele opposite the IVS1-13T-G mutation.

In a Caucasian boy with onset of late-onset Pompe disease at 5 months of age, Herbert et al. (2019) found compound heterozygosity for c.525delT (p.Glu176ArgfsTer45) and c.-32-13T-G in the GAA gene. In their cohort, c.525delT was also present in 12 patients, all of whom had onset in adulthood.

**.0003 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, EX18DEL

In 1 of 11 probands (9%) with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Wokke et al. (1995) found a deletion of exon 18 of the GAA gene on the allele opposite the IVS1-13T-G mutation.

**.0004 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, PRO361LEU [P361L]

In a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset GSD II, Lam et al. (2003) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene: a pro361-to-leu (P361L) substitution and R437C. The patient's asymptomatic 13-year-old brother carried both mutations.

**.0005 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, ARG437CYS [R437C]

In a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset GSD II, Lam et al. (2003) identified an arg437-to-cys (R437C) mutation in the GAA gene in compound heterozygosity with P361L. His asymptomatic 13-year-old brother was also compound heterozygous for both mutations.

**.0006 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, ALA237VAL [Ala237Val]

In a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement, Anneser et al. (2005) identified 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene: an ala237-to-val (A237V) substitution and G293R.

**.0007 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, GLY293ARG [Gly293Arg]

In a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease, Anneser et al. (2005) identified a gly293-to-arg (G293R) mutation in the GAA gene together with A237V.

**.0008 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, TRP746TER [c.2237G>A; p.W746X]

Montalvo et al. (2006) found a c.2237G-A transition in the GAA gene, resulting in a trp746-to-ter (W746X) substitution, together with c.-32-13T-G in 9 of 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSD II.

**.0009 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, 1-BP DEL, 1827C [c.1827delC]

In a Caucasian girl whose symptoms began at 10 days of life, Herbert et al. (2019) found c.1827delC and c.-32-13T-G in the GAA gene. In their cohort, c.1827delC was also present in 12 patients, all of whom had onset in adulthood.

**.0010 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**
