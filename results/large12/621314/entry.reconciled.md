### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAA gene (606800) on chromosome 17q25.3.

### Description

Bembi et al. (2008) described glycogenosis type II as a progressive, debilitating and often fatal neuromuscular disorder. Its clinical phenotypes form a continuum that varies in which organs are involved, age at onset and severity. In the late-onset form, residual acid maltase activity is present in muscle (Mehler and DiMauro, 1977). Patients have progressive proximal muscle weakness, mainly of the pelvic girdle, and later develop respiratory insufficiency with diaphragmatic weakness (Trend et al., 1985; Laforet et al., 2000).

### Clinical Features

Swaiman et al. (1968) reported a late-infantile form of acid maltase deficiency. Hudgson et al. (1968) described adult myopathy due to glycogen storage disease caused by acid maltase deficiency.

Engel (1970) studied 4 adults with acid maltase deficiency. He described the disorder in adults as a syndrome that can mimic muscular dystrophy or other myopathies.

Sivak et al. (1981) reported a 51-year-old man with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency who had a 6-year history of gradually progressive exertional dyspnea. His complaint was first attributed to increasing age, but evaluation eventually revealed diaphragmatic paralysis. Neurologic examination showed only minimal weakness of the limb muscles. Electromyography suggested acid maltase deficiency, and electron microscopic and biochemical studies of a muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis.

Francesconi and Auff (1982) reported cardiac arrhythmias in association with the adult form of type II glycogenosis.

Matsuishi et al. (1984) studied 3 children with the childhood form of acid maltase deficiency: 2 sibs and 1 sporadic patient. The patients had delayed developmental milestones and mild generalized muscle weakness. The authors considered nasal vocalization and an EMG finding of abnormal insertion voltage followed by pseudomyotonic discharge to be characteristic diagnostic findings for this form. Selective type 2A fiber atrophy and type 2B fiber deficiency were the common histochemical findings in affected muscles.

Trend et al. (1985) described 5 patients with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All developed their first pelvic girdle symptoms late in the second or early in the third decade, and some years later developed signs of respiratory insufficiency. Typically the patients were tall and had weak, wasted paraspinal and gluteal muscles with lower limb weakness. All were orthopneic with marked diaphragmatic weakness.

Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 sons of healthy, nonconsanguineous parents who developed progressive proximal muscle weakness secondary to alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Each brother developed a fusiform basilar artery aneurysm. In 2 brothers the aneurysm ruptured fatally, and in the third it was complicated by cerebellar infarction. Postmortem studies showed severe vacuolation of skeletal muscle with PAS-positive, diastase-sensitive material, as well as vacuolation of the liver with glycogen accumulation. In the surviving brother, similar glycogen deposition was shown in the smooth muscle of the superficial temporal artery.

Kretzschmar et al. (1990) reported a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency with pronounced involvement of the liver and skeletal muscles. The patient also had CNS pathology: CT and MRI showed hypodense and hyperintense white matter areas, and myelin changes ranged from focal demyelination to necrosis. Cerebral arteries showed vacuolar degeneration with PAS-positive material, and there were many small aneurysms of the intracerebral arteries.

Chancellor et al. (1991) reported a 68-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency who presented with a myopathy and exercise-induced urinary incontinence.

Wokke et al. (1995) performed a clinical, biochemical, and genetic study of 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. The patients had progressive muscle weakness, decreased vital capacity, and elevated serum creatine kinase. Worsening handicap was related to age, and decreasing vital capacity was related to how long the patient had had symptoms. Respiratory insufficiency was never the first manifestation. ⚠︎

Laforet et al. (2000) studied 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Progressive muscle weakness began in adulthood in all but 1 patient, but 16 patients had mild, nonprogressive muscular symptoms in childhood. Eighteen patients had a similar clinical pattern of pelvic girdle weakness predominating in the glutei and thigh adductors. Eight had restrictive respiratory insufficiency with vital capacity below 60%, and in 2 respiratory failure was the first manifestation.

Lam et al. (2003) reported a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II. The patient's 13-year-old brother was asymptomatic.

Anneser et al. (2005) described a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement resembling the atherosclerotic angiopathy of the elderly.

Hagemans et al. (2005) gathered information on 255 children and adults with Pompe disease by questionnaire. Disease severity was associated with disease duration and not with age. Earlier onset of the disease meant that patients became dependent on a wheelchair or ventilator earlier.

Amartino et al. (2006) reported a family in which two clinical forms of glycogen storage disease type II occurred in two generations. Groen et al. (2006) reported ptosis as a feature of late-onset glycogenosis type II.

Forsha et al. (2011) evaluated baseline ECGs and echocardiograms in 87 adults with late-onset Pompe disease enrolled in a randomized trial of enzyme replacement therapy. There were 44 males and 43 females, the median age was 44 years, and 82 patients were Caucasian. Forty-six percent had 1 or more abnormalities, most often LVH by ECG (12%), a short PR interval (10%), and decreased ejection fraction (7%). Six patients had a left ventricular ejection fraction below 55% (range, 41-54%). Only 4 patients (5%) had an elevated left ventricular mass index on echocardiogram, all in the mild range, and the severe ventricular hypertrophy typical of infantile Pompe disease was not seen. Other findings included sinus tachycardia (5 of 84), prolonged QTc (3 of 84), left atrial enlargement (3 of 84), incomplete bundle branch block (2 of 84), and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (2 of 84). No patient had atrioventricular block, right ventricular hypertrophy, or ventricular tachycardia. The authors noted that the abnormalities might be due to Pompe disease or to comorbid conditions.

Herbert et al. (2019) found that 4 of 84 patients (4.8%) followed at their center with late-onset Pompe disease and the GAA c.-32-13T-G variant had symptom onset in the first 2 years of life (10 days to 20 months). Initial symptoms included delayed gross motor milestones, proximal weakness, swallowing and feeding difficulties, and sleep apnea. All had residual muscle weakness despite enzyme replacement therapy (ERT).

Patient 1 of Herbert et al. (2019) was a Caucasian boy who presented at 5 months with delayed motor development and feeding difficulties. CK, ALT, and AST were elevated, and muscle fibers contained PAS-positive vacuoles. He had generalized hypotonia, head lag, myopathic facies, and hypertrophied calf muscles. EMG showed myotonic discharges, barium swallow showed aspiration, and he had obstructive and central apneas. Later he had motor regression, coxa valga, progressive scoliosis requiring surgery, neck flexor weakness, lumbar lordosis, scapular winging, a wide-based gait, proximal muscle weakness, and mild ptosis of the left eye. ECG and echocardiogram were normal. He was last reported at 12 years of age. ⚠︎

Patient 2 of Herbert et al. (2019) was a Caucasian girl, one of fraternal twins, born at term. She presented on day 10 of life with poor feeding, lethargy, and vomiting, and had apneic episodes during sleep. Other findings were hypotonia, delayed gross motor milestones, easy fatigability, elevated AST, ALT, and CK, and PAS-positive glycogen vacuoles on muscle biopsy. She had failure to thrive, myopathic facies, scapular winging, neck flexor weakness, a wide-based gait, a tendency to genu recurvatum, frequent falls, and leg pain. She had difficulty swallowing saliva and persistent gastroesophageal reflux and required a gastrostomy tube. She also had urinary retention, chronic constipation, a hypernasal voice, snoring, and aspiration pneumonia. Echocardiogram and ECG were normal. She was last reported at 3.5 years of age. ⚠︎

Patient 3 of Herbert et al. (2019) was a Caucasian boy who could not sit unsupported or hold his head upright at 6 months. At 12 months he had an unsteady, wide-based gait and frequent falls, and he tired easily. He had a weak cry and soft voice, truncal hypotonia, proximal muscle weakness, lordosis, scapular winging, and scoliosis with a Cobb angle of 40 degrees, for which a back brace was prescribed. Other findings were a Gowers sign, elevated CK, AST, and ALT, and PAS-positive glycogen storage on muscle biopsy. He also had hypernasality, hyporeflexia, atrophy of the proximal lower limb muscles, mild ptosis, lingual and neck flexor weakness, pectus excavatum, genu recurvatum, cramping, and diarrhea. Pulmonary function tests, ECG, and echocardiogram were normal. He was last reported at 9 years of age. ⚠︎

Patient 4 of Herbert et al. (2019) was a Caucasian girl born preterm at 36 weeks. In the newborn period she needed NICU admission and brief CPAP for apnea and bradycardia. She had delayed motor milestones, leg pain, a Gowers sign, a waddling gait, proximal muscle weakness, frequent falls, lumbar lordosis, bilateral winged scapulae, bilateral pronation of the feet, and neck flexor weakness. She also had snoring with apneic pauses, and asthma. Cardiology evaluations were normal. She was 2.5 years old at report.

Korlimarla et al. (2020) used brain MRI to study 14 children aged 6 to 18 years with Pompe disease who were receiving enzyme replacement therapy. White matter hyperintense foci were quantified with a summed Fazekas scale score. Ten of 12 children with infantile Pompe disease had mild to severe foci, with total scores of 2 to 23. The 2 children with late-onset disease had no such foci and scored high on most developmental assessments.

Jones et al. (2021) found tongue involvement in 10 untreated patients with LOPD. It consisted of weakness and sonographic abnormalities suggesting atrophy and fibrofatty replacement, including decreased tongue thickness and increased echointensity. ⚠︎

Huggins et al. (2022) evaluated 20 infants with LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening, who were last assessed at 6 to 21 months of age, and found high variability among them. None had cardiomyopathy or cardiac rhythm disturbances. CK, AST, and ALT were increased in 8 patients (40%), and urinary Glc4 was increased in 2 (10%). All patients showed postural and kinematic concerns, and 3 of 18 had AIMS scores below the 10th percentile. Speech-language pathology assessments were normal in all patients, and 9 (45%) had mild feeding and swallowing abnormalities. The authors concluded that careful physical therapy evaluation is necessary to monitor for subtle musculoskeletal signs that may reflect early muscle involvement. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Angelini et al. (1972) reported abnormalities in fibroblasts cultured from patients with adult acid maltase deficiency.

Using a sensitive fluorometric assay, Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found residual acid maltase activity in muscle biopsies from 15 patients with late-onset acid maltase deficiency (mean, 6.91%; range, 2.4 to 12.2%). They did not find this activity in muscle from 3 patients with the infantile form. Electrophoresis, kinetic studies, and subcellular fractionation indicated that the residual activity was lysosomal acid maltase and not a contaminating neutral maltase isozyme.

Beratis et al. (1978) purified acid alpha-glucosidase from fibroblasts of a patient with the adult form of the deficiency. The enzyme had the same pH optimum, Km, electrophoretic mobility, thermal denaturation, and inhibitor sensitivity as the normal enzyme. Immune complexes from the adult-form cells had enzyme activity, but the amount of cross-reacting material (CRM) appeared to be significantly lower than in normal cells or cells from the infantile form.

Beratis et al. (1983) found that fibroblasts with phenotype 2 of acid alpha-glucosidase, which is considered a normal variant, had less enzyme protein and a reduced ability to cleave glycogen, but normal catalytic activity toward maltose. The authors stated that it remained unknown whether the deficiency in homozygotes for isozyme 2 is enough to cause glycogen accumulation and a muscular dystrophy-like disease later in life.

In their patients with the childhood form of acid maltase deficiency, Matsuishi et al. (1984) found the highest neutral maltase activity in the muscle biopsy of the patient with the least severe weakness. They suggested that this enzyme may help reduce muscle involvement.

In 18 Israeli patients with alpha-glucosidase deficiency, 1 of whom had the juvenile type, Bashan et al. (1988) found muscle glycogen content of 4 to 17% wet weight. Muscle alpha-glucosidase activity was zero in 10 of the 17 patients examined. In the 7 with residual activity, the highest value was 18% of normal.

### Inheritance

Late-onset Pompe disease is an autosomal recessive disorder. Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 affected sons of healthy, nonconsanguineous parents. Wokke et al. (1995) found that all of their patients with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency were compound heterozygotes for mutations in the GAA gene, and Laforet et al. (2000) found compound heterozygous or homozygous GAA mutations in their patients. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Mehler and DiMauro (1977) suggested that late-onset and infantile acid maltase deficiency are genetically distinct. Their reasons were that acid maltase activity was present in muscle, liver, and, to a greater extent, leukocytes in the late-onset form but not in the infantile form, and that the two forms did not occur in the same family.

Beratis et al. (1983) found extensive genetic heterogeneity in acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency, with molecular differences between the clinical forms and within both the infantile and adult forms. Of 9 infantile-form fibroblast strains, 8 were CRM-negative and 1 was CRM-positive. Two adult-form strains had reduced enzyme protein in proportion to their reduced activity. A third adult-form strain had activity within the infantile range and no detectable CRM.

Byrne et al. (1986) reported a family in which 7 members over 3 generations had a cardioskeletal myopathy with lysosomal glycogen storage in skeletal and cardiac muscle, but normal acid maltase levels. The authors noted that this syndrome was unusual among glycogenoses because it showed dominant inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Wokke et al. (1995) found that all were compound heterozygotes for mutations in the GAA gene. Every patient carried the IVS1-13T-G transversion (c.-32-13T-G) on one allele. The second allele carried a 1-bp deletion of T at position 525 in exon 2 in 7 probands (64%) or a deletion of exon 18 in 1 proband (9%).

In 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Laforet et al. (2000) found that all but 1 were compound heterozygotes. Seventeen patients carried the IVS1-13T-G transversion in the GAA gene, and 1 of them was homozygous for it. Both mutant alleles were identified in 10 cases, and 13 different GAA mutations were detected.

In a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II, Lam et al. (2003) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, P361L and R437C. The patient's asymptomatic 13-year-old brother was also compound heterozygous for both mutations.

In a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement, Anneser et al. (2005) identified 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, A237V and G293R.

Montalvo et al. (2006) performed complete molecular analysis of the GAA gene in 40 Italian patients with late-onset glycogen storage disease type II and identified 12 novel alleles. The c.-32-13T-G mutation was the most frequent and was present in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of the patients. In 9 of the 40 patients it occurred together with c.2237G-A (W746X). The authors functionally characterized the missense mutations by measuring enzyme activity and protein processing in a human GAA-deficient cell line. They studied the splicing mutations by RT-PCR and in silico analysis, and they identified a complex allele carrying 3 different alterations in cis.

Herbert et al. (2019) found compound heterozygous GAA mutations in 4 Caucasian patients with LOPD whose symptoms began in the first 2 years of life. Each patient carried c.-32-13T-G. The second allele was c.525delT (Glu176ArgfsTer45) in patient 1, c.1827delC in patient 2, c.2501_2502delCA (Thr834fs) in patient 3, and c.118C-T in patient 4.

Among 20 infants with LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found that 4 were homozygous for the c.-32-13T-G splice site variant in the GAA gene, 14 were compound heterozygous for it, and 2 did not carry it. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found no correlation between the amount of residual acid maltase activity and either clinical severity or glycogen accumulation in late-onset acid maltase deficiency.

Wokke et al. (1995) concluded that the adult form of acid maltase deficiency appears to be clinically and genetically rather homogeneous. They proposed that reduced alpha-glucosidase activity is the final common pathway that destroys muscle fibers and causes muscle weakness to progress over years.

Laforet et al. (2000) found no clear correlation between the type of GAA mutation and phenotype in French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. They suggested that the physiopathology is complex.

Because the asymptomatic brother of their proband carried the same 2 GAA mutations, Lam et al. (2003) concluded that intrafamilial phenotypic variation in juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II occurs across ethnic groups. They suggested that other genes contribute to the phenotypic variability.

Montalvo et al. (2006) discussed genotype-phenotype correlations in late-onset glycogen storage disease type II, with particular emphasis on the subgroup with the c.-32-13T-G/c.2237G-A genotype.

In 38 patients with late-onset Pompe disease, aged 44.6 +/- 19.8 years, de Filippi et al. (2010) found that the ACE insertion/deletion polymorphism was distributed as in the general population (16% II, 37% DD). The DD genotype was associated with earlier onset of disease, higher creatine kinase levels at diagnosis, presence of muscle pain, and a more severe rate of disease progression. The authors suggested that the ACE polymorphism may modulate the phenotype and prognosis. The 3 ACE genotype groups did not differ in mean age, disease duration, Walton score, or other disease severity scores.

Forsha et al. (2011) found no significant difference in the frequency of cardiovascular abnormalities between patients with disease duration of less than 15 years and those with 15 years or more. Three (60%) of the patients whose symptoms began before 10 years of age had 1 or more ECG or echocardiographic abnormalities.

Herbert et al. (2019) noted that the c.525delT and c.1827delC variants carried by 2 of their early-onset patients were each also present in 12 other patients in their LOPD cohort, all with adult-onset disease. They suggested that these genotype combinations with c.-32-13T-G are not sufficient to cause the early-onset phenotype. The ACE D/D genotype was not present in the 2 patients tested.

### Pathogenesis

Askanas et al. (1976) established muscle cultures from the biopsy of a patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. The cultured muscle reproduced the abnormalities of the biopsied muscle: vacuoles containing acid phosphatase-positive material, glycogen stored in secondary lysosomes, undetectable acid maltase, and increased acid phosphatase. The authors said this supported the idea that the disorder is a biochemically distinct primary myopathy.

Beratis et al. (1978) concluded that an infantile case resulted from a structural gene mutation producing a catalytically inactive, CRM-positive enzyme. In contrast, the mutation in the adult form appeared to reduce the amount of enzyme protein in the cells.

Slonim et al. (1983) found that circulating branched-chain amino acids fell rapidly after protein ingestion in a child with acid maltase deficiency. They suggested that increased net muscle protein catabolism may play a part in the pathogenesis of the disorder. Margolis and Hill (1986) likewise based their dietary approach on evidence that muscle catabolism may contribute to muscle weakness in acid maltase deficiency.

Makos et al. (1987) suggested that the basilar artery aneurysms in their sibship may have been a consequence of alpha-glucosidase deficiency. At postmortem they found PAS-positive, diastase-sensitive material accumulated in vascular smooth muscle. Glycogen deposition in vascular smooth muscle had been described in the disorder before but had not been clinically significant.

In a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency, Kretzschmar et al. (1990) used electron microscopy to find lysosomal and free cytoplasmic glycogen in smooth muscle cells of the intima of large cerebral arteries and in pericytes of arterioles and capillaries. The glycogen accumulation was associated with severe cellular damage and necrosis. The authors thought this damage led to the formation of numerous small intracerebral aneurysms.

### Diagnosis

Trend et al. (1985) found characteristic glycogen vacuoles in peripheral blood lymphocytes in all 5 of their adult patients, whereas the muscle biopsy was characteristic in only 1 of 4 cases. The diagnosis was confirmed by enzyme assay in muscle and cultured fibroblasts.

Bashan et al. (1988) found leukocyte alpha-glucosidase activity to be highly variable and concluded that leukocytes are unfit for enzymatic diagnosis of the disease. They also found marked heterogeneity in the pH profiles of muscle and leukocyte alpha-glucosidase activity.

Wokke et al. (1995) found that serum creatine kinase activity and alpha-glucosidase activity in peripheral blood cells or muscle were helpful for diagnosis but had no prognostic value.

In a study of 18 patients with adult-onset glycogen storage disease II, Ausems et al. (1999) found that elevated creatine kinase was a sensitive marker of the disease. When acid alpha-glucosidase activity in leukocytes was clearly deficient, measuring the activity in muscle and examining muscle tissue under the microscope appeared to add nothing. The authors formulated a diagnostic protocol.

Kallwass et al. (2007) showed that a fluorometric assay of GAA activity in dried blood spots efficiently detected patients with late-onset Pompe disease. The assay uses acarbose, a highly selective alpha-glucosidase inhibitor, to eliminate isoenzyme interference. The authors noted that these patients are often misdiagnosed by conventional methods because of residual GAA activity in other tissue types.

Bembi et al. (2008) noted that a clinical diagnosis of glycogenosis type II is confirmed by GAA enzyme activity in blood samples, cultured fibroblasts, or muscle biopsies. Activity is virtually absent in infants and markedly reduced in juveniles and adults. They also reviewed the use of muscle imaging to evaluate muscle pathology.

For an ACMG work group, Wang et al. (2011) developed expert-opinion educational guidelines for several lysosomal storage diseases, including Pompe disease. The guidelines cover confirmatory diagnostic testing and management of presymptomatic individuals identified by newborn screening, by family-based testing after a proband is identified, or by carrier testing. ⚠︎

Forsha et al. (2011) found that LVH by ECG criteria was not specific for increased left ventricular mass in late-onset Pompe disease. Of 10 patients with LVH on ECG, only 1 had an elevated left ventricular mass index on echocardiography.

Jones et al. (2021) assessed tongue structure and function by manual and quantitative muscle testing and by ultrasound in 10 untreated LOPD patients, 30 patients with other acquired or hereditary myopathies, and 30 neuropathic controls. LOPD patients had significantly lower quantitative tongue strength and sonographic tongue thickness than patients with other myopathies. The authors concluded that quantitative tongue weakness and/or atrophy may help distinguish LOPD from other myopathies. They found ultrasound to be an effective and efficient method for quantitative assessment of the tongue muscles at the point of care.

### Clinical Management

Sivak et al. (1981) found that exercise stress testing was needed to define the limits of exercise tolerance in their patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency and diaphragmatic paralysis.

Slonim et al. (1983) treated a child with acid maltase deficiency with a high-protein diet for 12 months. The weakness and wasting reversed, and muscle function, exercise tolerance, and growth improved.

Trend et al. (1985) reported that long-term home ventilatory support (25 patient-years), using a rocking bed or intermittent positive pressure ventilation via tracheostomy, allowed all 5 of their patients to return to work. Their clinical condition remained stable.

Margolis and Hill (1986) treated a 55-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency and severe ventilatory muscle weakness with a high-protein (1.6 g/kg), weight-reducing diet for 27 months. The diet was associated with striking improvement in respiratory function, including measures of ventilatory muscle strength and endurance. Isaacs et al. (1986) tried several forms of therapy in an adult with acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency and reported a favorable response to a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet.

Hagemans et al. (2005) found a subgroup of patients under 15 years of age whose disease was more severe and progressed faster. They concluded that these patients need closer follow-up and early intervention, before irreversible damage occurs. ⚠︎

Bembi et al. (2008) reviewed the management of glycogenosis type II as a multisystem disorder. They recommended a multidisciplinary team of specialists covering cardiology, pulmonology, neuromuscular disease, rehabilitation, nutrition, and genetic counseling.

Forsha et al. (2011) performed a post hoc analysis of a 78-week double-blind trial in which patients were randomized 2:1 to recombinant human acid alpha-glucosidase (20 mg/kg every 2 weeks) or placebo. ECG and echocardiographic parameters did not change significantly with enzyme replacement therapy, and there were no significant cardiovascular safety concerns. One patient with known Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome had an episode of supraventricular tachycardia that was classified as possibly related to treatment.

Herbert et al. (2019) observed that all 4 of their early-onset patients improved on standardized motor assessments after starting ERT but remained below age level. One patient had persistent weakness even at 40 mg/kg/week. Antibody titers remained low in the 3 patients tested, so the residual deficits were not attributed to high antibody titers.

### Population Genetics

Bashan et al. (1988) noted a high prevalence of glycogen storage disease type II in the Arab population of Israel. All but 2 of their 18 patients were Palestinian Arabs; the other 2 were sibs from a Jewish Iraqi family.

Kroos et al. (1995) studied 121 patients with glycogen storage disease type II, looking at the frequency of 3 common mutant alleles and the clinical phenotypes associated with them. Martiniuk et al. (1998) estimated the carrier frequency of the disease in New York and, from it, the number of affected individuals born with the disease.

In 40 Italian patients with late-onset glycogen storage disease type II, Montalvo et al. (2006) found that c.-32-13T-G was the most frequent GAA mutation, as in other Caucasian late-onset populations. It was present in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of patients, with an allele frequency of 42.3%.

### Animal Model

Bijvoet et al. (1998) generated a mouse model of glycogen storage disease II by targeted disruption of the Gaa gene in embryonic stem cells. Homozygous knockout mice lacked Gaa mRNA and had virtually complete acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Glycogen-containing lysosomes appeared soon after birth in liver, heart, and skeletal muscle, and large focal glycogen deposits had formed by 13 weeks of age. Vacuolar spaces stained positive for acid phosphatase. Male and female knockout mice were fertile. At 9 months of age the mice still had no overt clinical symptoms, but the heart was typically enlarged and the electrocardiogram was abnormal.

Raben et al. (1998) generated mice homozygous for targeted disruption of the Gaa gene (6neo/6neo). The mice lacked enzyme activity, accumulated glycogen in cardiac and skeletal muscle lysosomes by 3 weeks of age, and had markedly reduced mobility and strength by 3.5 weeks. They grew normally, reached adulthood, and remained fertile. As in the human adult disease, older mice accumulated glycogen in the diaphragm, and by 8 to 9 months they developed obvious muscle wasting and a weak, waddling gait. In a second model, mice with deletion of exon 6 (Delta6/Delta6) had unimpaired strength and mobility up to 6.5 months of age, although their biochemical and pathologic changes were indistinguishable from those of the 6neo/6neo mice. The authors suggested that the genetic background of the mouse strains contributed to the differences between the models.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, IVS1AS, T-G, -13 [c.-32-13T>G]

In 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Wokke et al. (1995) found the IVS1-13T-G transversion (c.-32-13T-G) in the GAA gene on one allele in every patient. All patients were compound heterozygotes, with a second mutation such as a 1-bp deletion (525delT) or a deletion of exon 18.

Laforet et al. (2000) found the IVS1-13T-G transversion in 17 of 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. One of the 17 was homozygous for the mutation.

In 40 Italian patients with late-onset glycogen storage disease type II, Montalvo et al. (2006) found that c.-32-13T-G was the most frequent GAA mutation. It was present in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of patients (allele frequency 42.3%) and occurred with W746X in 9 of the 40 patients.

Herbert et al. (2019) found c.-32-13T-G in the compound heterozygous state in 4 Caucasian patients with LOPD whose symptoms began in the first 2 years of life. Each patient carried a different second GAA mutation: c.525delT, c.1827delC, c.2501_2502delCA, or c.118C-T.

Among 20 infants with LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found 4 homozygous and 14 compound heterozygous for the c.-32-13T-G splice site variant.
