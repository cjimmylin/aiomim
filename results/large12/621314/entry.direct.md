### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the gene encoding acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA).

### Description

Late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) is an autosomal recessive, progressive metabolic myopathy caused by partial deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA), which leads to lysosomal glycogen accumulation. It can present any time from the first year of life to adulthood, as late as the sixth decade. Weakness mainly affects the trunk, proximal lower limb, and respiratory muscles. Classic infantile Pompe disease has residual enzyme activity below 1% and causes hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and early death. In contrast, the late-onset (juvenile and adult) form follows a more insidious course, and significant ventricular hypertrophy is not a typical feature (Forsha et al., 2011; Herbert et al., 2019; Jones et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hudgson et al. (1968) described adult myopathy from glycogen storage disease due to acid maltase deficiency. Swaiman et al. (1968) reported late infantile acid maltase deficiency.

Engel (1970) studied 4 adults with acid maltase deficiency and described the condition as a syndrome that may mimic muscular dystrophy or other myopathies.

Sivak et al. (1981) reported a 51-year-old man with a 6-year history of gradually progressive exertional dyspnea, which was first attributed to increasing age. Evaluation eventually showed diaphragmatic paralysis. Neurologic examination found only minimal weakness of the limb muscles. Electromyographic studies suggested acid maltase deficiency, which was confirmed by electron microscopic and biochemical studies of a muscle biopsy. Exercise stress testing was needed to define the limits of the patient's exercise tolerance.

Francesconi and Auff (1982) reported cardiac arrhythmias in the adult form of type II glycogenosis.

Matsuishi et al. (1984) studied 3 children with the childhood form of acid maltase deficiency: 2 sibs and 1 sporadic patient. The patients had delayed developmental milestones and mild generalized muscle weakness. The authors considered nasal vocalization and an electromyographic pattern of abnormal insertion voltage followed by pseudomyotonic discharge to be characteristic diagnostic findings for the childhood form. The common histochemical findings in affected muscles were selective type 2A fiber atrophy and type 2B fiber deficiency.

Trend et al. (1985) described 5 patients with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All developed their first pelvic girdle symptoms late in the second or early in the third decade, followed some years later by signs of respiratory insufficiency. Typically the patients were tall and had weak, wasted paraspinal and gluteal muscles with lower limb weakness. All were orthopneic with marked diaphragmatic weakness.

Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 sons of healthy, nonconsanguineous parents who developed progressive proximal muscle weakness due to alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Each developed a fusiform basilar artery aneurysm. The aneurysm ruptured fatally in 2 of the brothers and was complicated by cerebellar infarction in the third. At postmortem, there was severe vacuolation of skeletal muscle, liver, and vascular smooth muscle, with accumulation of periodic acid-Schiff (PAS)-positive, diastase-sensitive material. In the surviving brother, similar glycogen deposits were found in the smooth muscle of the superficial temporal artery.

Kretzschmar et al. (1990) reported a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency and marked involvement of the liver and skeletal muscles. Unlike other adult cases, the patient had CNS pathology. CT and MRI showed hypodense and hyperintense white matter areas, and myelin changes ranged from focal demyelination to necrosis. The vascular pathology consisted of vacuolar degeneration of small and large arteries, with lysosomal and free cytoplasmic glycogen in smooth muscle cells and pericytes. The patient had numerous small aneurysms of the intracerebral arteries.

Chancellor et al. (1991) reported a 68-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency who presented with a myopathy and exercise-induced urinary incontinence.

Wokke et al. (1995) studied 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Worsening handicap was related to age, and the decline in vital capacity was related to the duration of the symptomatic stage. Respiratory insufficiency was never the first manifestation. The authors concluded that the adult form is clinically and genetically fairly homogeneous.

Laforêt et al. (2000) studied 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Progressive muscle weakness began in adulthood in all but 1 patient. However, 16 patients had mild, nonprogressive muscle symptoms that began in childhood. Eighteen patients shared a clinical pattern of pelvic girdle weakness, most marked in the glutei and thigh adductors. Eight patients had restrictive respiratory insufficiency with a vital capacity below 60%, and respiratory failure was the first manifestation in 2 patients.

Lam et al. (2003) reported a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II. His 13-year-old brother carried the same GAA genotype but was asymptomatic.

Anneser et al. (2005) described a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement that resembled the atherosclerotic angiopathy of the elderly.

Hagemans et al. (2005) collected questionnaire data on 255 children and adults with Pompe disease. Disease severity was associated with disease duration, not age, and earlier onset meant earlier dependence on a wheelchair or ventilator. Patients younger than 15 years included a subgroup with a more severe and rapid course.

Amartino et al. (2006) reported 2 clinical forms of glycogen storage disease type II in 2 generations of the same family.

Groen et al. (2006) reported ptosis as a feature of late-onset glycogenosis type II.

Forsha et al. (2011) evaluated cardiovascular findings in 87 patients with late-onset Pompe disease enrolled in a randomized trial of enzyme replacement therapy (median age 44 years; 51% men). At baseline, 46% of patients had at least 1 electrocardiographic or echocardiographic abnormality. ECG showed left ventricular hypertrophy in 12% and a short PR interval (90 to 120 ms) in 10%. On echocardiography, 7% had a left ventricular ejection fraction below 55% (range, 41 to 54%) and 5% had an elevated left ventricular mass index, all in the mild range. Bundle branch block and atrial enlargement were each found in 4%. Two patients had a history of Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. There were no significant differences between patients with disease duration under 15 years and those with 15 years or more. Three of the patients whose symptoms began before age 10 years (60%) had at least 1 abnormality. The authors noted that the significant ventricular hypertrophy classically seen in infantile Pompe disease was absent, and that the abnormalities might be related to Pompe disease or to comorbid conditions. ⚠︎

Herbert et al. (2019) reviewed the charts of 84 patients with LOPD carrying the c.-32-13T-G variant. Four clinically diagnosed patients (4.8%), 2 male and 2 female, had symptom onset between 10 days and 20 months of age. Initial symptoms included delayed gross motor milestones, signs of proximal muscle weakness, swallowing and feeding difficulties, and sleep apnea. All patients had early, characteristic changes in posture and movement. Diagnosis was made between 10 and 26 months of age, a delay of 6 to 18 months. Additional findings included elevated creatine kinase (CK), ALT, and AST levels, PAS-positive vacuoles on muscle biopsy, myopathic facies, generalized or truncal hypotonia, hypernasality, Gowers sign, and myotonic discharges on EMG. Despite enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), musculoskeletal involvement progressed and residual muscle weakness persisted in all patients. This was shown by ptosis, myopathic facies, scoliosis, lumbar lordosis, scapular winging, and weakness of the trunk and lower extremities. One patient needed surgery for progressive thoracolumbar scoliosis at age 12 years. Echocardiograms and ECGs were normal in the patients described.

Korlimarla et al. (2020) performed brain MRI and developmental assessments in 14 children aged 6 to 18 years with Pompe disease who were receiving ERT. Many children with infantile Pompe disease had white matter hyperintense foci. However, the 2 children with LOPD had no white matter hyperintense foci and scored high on most developmental assessments.

Jones et al. (2021) assessed tongue structure and function in 10 treatment-naive patients with LOPD, 30 patients with other acquired or hereditary myopathies, and 30 controls with neuropathy. Compared with neuropathic controls, patients with LOPD differed significantly in tongue strength, ultrasound appearance, echointensity, and thickness. Compared with patients with other myopathies, they had significantly lower quantitative tongue strength and thinner tongue muscle on ultrasound. The authors interpreted these findings as tongue weakness with sonographic changes suggesting fibrofatty replacement and atrophy.

Huggins et al. (2022) systematically evaluated 20 infants aged 6 to 21 months who had LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening. None had evidence of cardiomyopathy or cardiac rhythm disturbances. CK, AST, and ALT were elevated in 8 patients (40%), and urinary glucose tetrasaccharide (Glc4) was elevated in 2 (10%). All patients showed postural and kinematic concerns. Three patients (17%) scored below the 10th percentile on the Alberta Infant Motor Scale. Speech-language assessments were normal in all patients, and 9 (45%) had mild feeding or swallowing abnormalities. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Angelini et al. (1972) found abnormalities in fibroblasts cultured from patients with adult acid maltase deficiency.

Askanas et al. (1976) established muscle cultures from a biopsy of a patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. The newly grown cultured fibers showed the same abnormalities as the biopsied muscle. These included multiple vacuoles filled with acid phosphatase-positive material and abnormal glycogen accumulation in membrane-bound sacs (secondary lysosomes). Acid maltase measured at pH 4.0 was undetectable in both cultured and biopsied muscle, whereas acid phosphatase was increased in both.

Using a sensitive fluorometric assay, Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found residual acid maltase activity in muscle biopsies from 15 patients with late-onset acid maltase deficiency (mean, 6.91%; range, 2.4 to 12.2). No residual activity was found in muscle from 3 patients with the infantile form. Electrophoresis, kinetic characteristics, and subcellular fractionation showed that the residual activity was lysosomal acid maltase rather than neutral maltase. The amount of residual activity did not correlate with clinical severity or with glycogen accumulation. Residual activity was also present in liver and, to a greater extent, in leukocytes from late-onset patients.

Beratis et al. (1978) found that acid alpha-glucosidase purified from fibroblasts of a patient with the adult form had normal properties. These included pH optimum, Michaelis constant, electrophoretic mobility, thermal denaturation, and inhibitor sensitivity. The amount of cross-reacting material (CRM) was significantly lower than in normal cells or in cells from a patient with the infantile form. The authors concluded that the adult-form mutation reduced the amount of enzyme protein in the cells.

Beratis et al. (1983) found that the amount of enzyme protein in 2 fibroblast strains from patients with the adult form was reduced in direct proportion to the reduction in enzyme activity. In a third adult-form strain, activity was within the range of the infantile form and no CRM was detected. The authors concluded that acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency is genetically heterogeneous, both between and within the clinical forms.

Matsuishi et al. (1984) found the highest neutral maltase activity in muscle from the least weak of 3 children with the childhood form. They suggested that this enzyme may help to reduce muscle involvement.

In a study of 18 Israeli patients with alpha-glucosidase deficiency, 1 of whom had the juvenile type, Bashan et al. (1988) found highly variable leukocyte alpha-glucosidase activity. They concluded that leukocytes are unsuitable for enzymatic diagnosis. The pH profiles of muscle and leukocyte activity were markedly heterogeneous.

### Inheritance

Late-onset Pompe disease is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern (Laforêt et al., 2000; Anneser et al., 2005; Montalvo et al., 2006; Jones et al., 2021). Affected individuals are compound heterozygous or homozygous for GAA mutations (Wokke et al., 1995; Laforêt et al., 2000; Huggins et al., 2022). Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 affected sons of healthy, nonconsanguineous parents.

### Molecular Genetics

Wokke et al. (1995) studied 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All were compound heterozygous for mutations in the GAA gene, with the IVS1 (-13T-G) transversion on 1 allele. The second allele carried either a deletion of T at position 525 in exon 2 (7 probands, 64%) or a deletion of exon 18 (1 proband, 9%).

Kroos et al. (1995) studied the frequency of 3 common mutant GAA alleles, and their associated clinical phenotypes, in 121 patients with glycogen storage disease type II.

In 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Laforêt et al. (2000) found that all but 1 patient were compound heterozygous. Seventeen patients carried the IVS1 (-13T-G) transversion, and 1 was homozygous for it. Both mutant alleles were identified in 10 patients, with 13 different GAA mutations detected. The authors concluded that juvenile and adult acid maltase deficiency is genetically highly heterogeneous in the French population.

Lam et al. (2003) found compound heterozygosity for 2 novel GAA mutations, pro361-to-leu (P361L) and arg437-to-cys (R437C), in a 16-year-old Chinese boy with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II. His asymptomatic 13-year-old brother was also compound heterozygous for both mutations. ⚠︎

Anneser et al. (2005) identified 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, ala237-to-val (A237V) and gly293-to-arg (G293R), in a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and an unusual vascular phenotype.

Montalvo et al. (2006) carried out complete molecular analysis of the GAA gene in 40 Italian patients with late-onset glycogen storage disease type II and identified 12 novel alleles. Novel missense mutations were characterized functionally by enzyme activity and protein processing in a human GAA-deficient cell line. Splicing mutations were studied by RT-PCR and in silico analysis. One complex allele carried 3 different changes in cis. The most frequent mutation was c.-32-13T-G, carried in the compound heterozygous state by 85% of patients (allele frequency 42.3%). In 9 of the 40 patients it was combined with c.2237G-A (W746X).

Herbert et al. (2019) reported 4 patients with early-onset symptoms of LOPD. Each was compound heterozygous for the c.-32-13T-G variant and a second pathogenic GAA variant: c.525delT (p.Glu176Argfs*45), c.1827delC, or c.2501_2502delCA (p.T834fs). The diagnosis was made by GAA enzyme assay and confirmed by variant analysis. ⚠︎

Among 20 infants with LOPD identified by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found that 4 were homozygous for the c.-32-13T-G splice site variant and 14 were compound heterozygous for it. The remaining 2 did not carry the variant.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Residual acid maltase activity was present in late-onset but not in infantile acid maltase deficiency, and the 2 forms did not occur in the same family. On this basis, Mehler and DiMauro (1977) suggested that the 2 forms are genetically distinct. In contrast, Amartino et al. (2006) reported 2 clinical forms of glycogen storage disease type II in 2 generations of the same family.

Wokke et al. (1995) found that serum CK and alpha-glucosidase activity in blood cells or muscle were helpful for diagnosis but had no prognostic value. They concluded that reduced alpha-glucosidase activity is the final common pathway leading to muscle fiber destruction and progressive weakness. Laforêt et al. (2000) found no clear correlation between mutation type and phenotype in French patients. Montalvo et al. (2006) discussed genotype-phenotype correlations, with particular emphasis on Italian patients with the c.-32-13T-G/c.2237G-A genotype.

Lam et al. (2003) found the same GAA genotype in a symptomatic 16-year-old Chinese patient and his asymptomatic 13-year-old brother. They suggested that other genes contribute to the phenotypic variability of the disease.

De Filippi et al. (2010) studied the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) insertion/deletion polymorphism in 38 patients with late-onset Pompe disease aged 44.6 +/- 19.8 years. The DD genotype was associated with earlier disease onset (P = 0.041), higher CK levels at diagnosis (P = 0.024), muscle pain (P = 0.014), and faster disease progression (P = 0.037). The authors suggested that the ACE polymorphism may modify the Pompe disease phenotype.

Herbert et al. (2019) noted that the c.-32-13T-G variant has traditionally been thought to cause a mild, adult-onset phenotype because it allows production of low levels of normal GAA. However, 4 of 84 patients with this variant (4.8%) had symptoms in the first 2 years of life. These early-onset patients appeared to have more rapid and severe disease progression, with persistent residual muscle deficits that improved partially with higher doses of ERT. Huggins et al. (2022) found high variability among children with LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening.

### Diagnosis

Trend et al. (1985) found characteristic glycogen vacuoles in peripheral blood lymphocytes of all 5 of their adult patients. Muscle biopsy was characteristic in only 1 of 4 patients and showed minimal or nonspecific features in the other 3. The diagnosis was confirmed by enzyme assay in muscle and cultured fibroblasts.

Ausems et al. (1999) studied 18 patients with adult-onset glycogen storage disease type II. When leukocyte GAA activity was clearly deficient, measuring GAA activity in muscle and examining muscle histopathology added no diagnostic value. Elevated CK was a sensitive marker of the disease, and the authors formulated a diagnostic protocol.

Kallwass et al. (2007) showed that a fluorometric assay of GAA activity in dried blood spots, using acarbose to eliminate isoenzyme interference, efficiently detected patients with late-onset Pompe disease. Such patients are often misdiagnosed by conventional methods because of residual GAA activity in other tissues.

Bembi et al. (2008) stated that the clinical diagnosis is confirmed by markedly reduced GAA activity in juveniles and adults (compared with virtual absence in infants) in blood samples, cultured fibroblasts, or muscle biopsies. They also reviewed differential diagnosis and the use of muscle imaging. Wang et al. (2011) developed guidelines for diagnostic confirmation and management of presymptomatic individuals with Pompe disease, including those identified by newborn screening.

Herbert et al. (2019) noted that patients with LOPD are often misdiagnosed. As young children they may be diagnosed with nonspecific developmental delay or hypotonia, and when older with conditions such as limb-girdle muscular dystrophy or polymyositis. The diagnostic delay can be up to 12 years. Jones et al. (2021) found that quantitative tongue weakness and/or atrophy may help distinguish LOPD from other acquired or hereditary myopathies.

### Clinical Management

Slonim et al. (1983) treated a child with acid maltase deficiency with a high-protein diet for 12 months. Weakness and wasting reversed, with improved muscle function, exercise tolerance, and growth.

Trend et al. (1985) reported that long-term home ventilatory support (25 patient-years), using a rocking bed or intermittent positive pressure respiration via tracheostomy, allowed all 5 of their adult patients to return to work while their clinical condition remained stable.

Margolis and Hill (1986) treated a 55-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency and severe ventilatory muscle weakness with a high-protein (1.6 g/kg), weight-reducing diet for 27 months. Respiratory function improved strikingly, including ventilatory muscle strength and endurance. Isaacs et al. (1986) reported a favorable response to a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet in an adult patient.

Bembi et al. (2008) recommended management by a multidisciplinary team and reviewed functional assessment, rehabilitation, nutrition, genetic counseling, prenatal diagnosis, screening, and emerging therapies.

In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of ERT with recombinant human GAA (20 mg/kg every other week for 78 weeks), Forsha et al. (2011) found no change in cardiovascular status with ERT and no significant cardiovascular safety concerns. One patient with known Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome had an episode of supraventricular tachycardia that was classified as serious and possibly related to study medication.

Herbert et al. (2019) found that in patients with early-onset LOPD, residual muscle deficits persisted despite ERT and improved partially with higher doses. Huggins et al. (2022) recommended careful physical therapy evaluation and close monitoring for progression in children diagnosed by newborn screening.

### Population Genetics

Bashan et al. (1988) reported 18 patients with alpha-glucosidase deficiency diagnosed in Israel over 15 years. All were Palestinian Arabs except 2 sibs from a Jewish Iraqi family, and only 1 patient had the juvenile type.

Martiniuk et al. (1998) estimated the carrier frequency for glycogen storage disease type II in New York and the number of affected individuals born with the disease. ⚠︎

In 40 Italian patients with late-onset disease, Montalvo et al. (2006) found an allele frequency of 42.3% for the c.-32-13T-G mutation. Herbert et al. (2019) noted that this variant is found on at least 1 allele in 68 to 90% of Caucasian patients with LOPD.

### Animal Model

Bijvoet et al. (1998) generated Gaa knockout mice. Homozygous mice lacked Gaa mRNA and had virtually complete acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency, with glycogen-containing lysosomes in liver, heart, and skeletal muscle soon after birth. At 9 months, the mice had no overt clinical symptoms, but the heart was typically enlarged and the electrocardiogram was abnormal.

Raben et al. (1998) found that mice homozygous for disruption of exon 6 of Gaa (6neo/6neo) lacked enzyme activity and accumulated glycogen in cardiac and skeletal muscle lysosomes from 3 weeks of age. By 3.5 weeks they had markedly reduced mobility and strength, but they reached adulthood. Older mice accumulated glycogen in the diaphragm and by 8 to 9 months developed muscle wasting and a weak, waddling gait, features of the adult human disease. In contrast, mice with deletion of exon 6 (delta6/delta6) had unimpaired strength and mobility up to 6.5 months of age. The authors suggested that genetic background contributed to these differences. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, IVS1, T-G, -13 [c.-32-13T>G; IVS1(-13T>G)]

Wokke et al. (1995) found the IVS1 (-13T-G) transversion in the GAA gene on 1 allele in all 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All patients were compound heterozygous.

Laforêt et al. (2000) found the IVS1 (-13T-G) transversion in 17 of 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- or adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. One patient was homozygous for it.

Montalvo et al. (2006) found the c.-32-13T-G mutation in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of 40 Italian patients with late-onset disease (allele frequency 42.3%).

Herbert et al. (2019) reported 4 patients with LOPD and symptom onset in the first 2 years of life who were compound heterozygous for the c.-32-13T-G splice variant and a second pathogenic GAA variant. They noted that this variant allows production of low levels of normal GAA.

Huggins et al. (2022) found 4 infants homozygous and 14 compound heterozygous for c.-32-13T-G among 20 infants with LOPD identified by newborn screening.

**.0002 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, 1-BP DEL, 525T [c.525delT; p.Glu176Argfs*45]

Wokke et al. (1995) found deletion of a T at position 525 in exon 2 of the GAA gene on the second allele in 7 of 11 probands (64%) with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. These patients were compound heterozygous with the IVS1 (-13T-G) transversion.

Herbert et al. (2019) found compound heterozygosity for c.525delT (p.Glu176Argfs*45) and c.-32-13T-G in a Caucasian boy with early-onset LOPD. He presented at 5 months with delayed motor development and feeding difficulties, and fibroblast GAA activity was 4.35% of normal.

**.0003 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, EX18DEL

Wokke et al. (1995) found deletion of exon 18 of the GAA gene on the second allele in 1 of 11 probands (9%) with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. The proband was compound heterozygous with the IVS1 (-13T-G) transversion.

**.0004 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, PRO361LEU [p.Pro361Leu]

Lam et al. (2003) found a novel pro361-to-leu (P361L) mutation in the GAA gene in a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II, who was compound heterozygous with R437C. His asymptomatic 13-year-old brother carried the same 2 mutations.

**.0005 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, ARG437CYS [p.Arg437Cys]

Lam et al. (2003) found a novel arg437-to-cys (R437C) mutation in the GAA gene in the compound heterozygous state with P361L in a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II and in his asymptomatic 13-year-old brother.

**.0006 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, ALA237VAL [p.Ala237Val]

Anneser et al. (2005) identified a novel ala237-to-val (A237V) mutation in the GAA gene, together with a second novel mutation (G293R), in a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement resembling the atherosclerotic angiopathy of the elderly.

**.0007 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, GLY293ARG [p.Gly293Arg]

Anneser et al. (2005) identified a novel gly293-to-arg (G293R) mutation in the GAA gene, together with A237V, in a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and an unusual vascular phenotype.

**.0008 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, TRP746TER [c.2237G>A; p.Trp746Ter]

Montalvo et al. (2006) found a c.2237G-A transition in the GAA gene, resulting in a trp746-to-ter (W746X) substitution, in combination with c.-32-13T-G in 9 of 40 Italian patients with late-onset glycogen storage disease type II.

**.0009 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, 1-BP DEL, 1827C [c.1827delC]

Herbert et al. (2019) found compound heterozygosity for c.1827delC and c.-32-13T-G in the GAA gene in a Caucasian girl, one of fraternal twins. She presented at day 10 of life with poor feeding, lethargy, and vomiting, and later developed hypotonia, delayed motor development, and apneic episodes during sleep.

**.0010 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, 2-BP DEL, 2501CA [c.2501_2502delCA; p.T834fs]

Herbert et al. (2019) found c.2501_2502delCA (p.T834fs) together with c.-32-13T-G in the GAA gene in a Caucasian boy diagnosed with Pompe disease at age 2 years. At 6 months he had difficulty sitting unsupported and holding his head upright. He later developed proximal muscle weakness, Gowers sign, lordosis, scapular winging, and scoliosis.
