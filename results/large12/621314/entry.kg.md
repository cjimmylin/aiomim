### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the gene encoding acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA; 606800) on chromosome 17q25.3.

### Description

Bembi et al. (2008) described glycogenosis type II as a progressive, debilitating, and often fatal neuromuscular disorder that occurs as a continuum of clinical phenotypes. These phenotypes vary in organ involvement, age at onset, and severity. The late-onset (juvenile and adult) forms are characterized by progressive proximal and respiratory muscle weakness (Trend et al., 1985; Laforêt et al., 2000). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Swaiman et al. (1968) reported on late infantile acid maltase deficiency.

Hudgson et al. (1968) described adult myopathy caused by glycogen storage disease due to acid maltase deficiency.

Engel (1970) reported studies in 4 adults with acid maltase deficiency. In these patients the syndrome could mimic muscular dystrophy or other myopathies.

Askanas et al. (1976) reported a patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency in whom light microscopy of biopsied muscle showed multiple vacuoles filled with acid phosphatase-positive material. Electron microscopy showed abnormal accumulation of glycogen in membrane-bound sacs (secondary lysosomes), some of which also contained dark membranous or homogeneous material. Newly grown fibers of cultured muscle showed the same abnormalities.

Sivak et al. (1981) reported a 51-year-old man with a 6-year history of gradually progressive exertional dyspnea. This was at first attributed to increasing age, but evaluation eventually showed diaphragmatic paralysis. Neurologic examination found only minimal weakness of the limb muscles, and exercise stress testing was needed to define the limits of his exercise tolerance.

Francesconi and Auff (1982) reported cardiac arrhythmias in association with the adult form of type II glycogenosis.

Matsuishi et al. (1984) studied 3 children with the childhood form of acid maltase deficiency, including 2 sibs and 1 sporadic patient. Features included delayed developmental milestones and mild generalized muscle weakness. The authors considered nasal vocalization and an EMG finding of abnormal insertion voltage followed by pseudomyotonic discharge to be characteristic diagnostic findings for the childhood form. Selective type 2A fiber atrophy and type 2B fiber deficiency were common histochemical findings in affected muscles.

Trend et al. (1985) described 5 patients with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency whose initial pelvic girdle symptoms began late in the second or early in the third decade, followed some years later by signs of respiratory insufficiency. Typically the patients were tall, with weak and wasted paraspinal and gluteal muscles and lower limb weakness; all were orthopneic with marked diaphragmatic weakness.

Margolis and Hill (1986) described a 55-year-old man with adult acid maltase deficiency and severe ventilatory muscle weakness. The authors noted that the adult disorder is associated with progressive ventilatory muscle weakness and death from respiratory failure.

Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 brothers, sons of healthy nonconsanguineous parents, who developed progressive proximal muscle weakness due to alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Each developed a fusiform basilar artery aneurysm; the aneurysm ruptured fatally in 2 of the brothers and was complicated by cerebellar infarction in the third. Postmortem examination showed severe vacuolation of skeletal muscle, liver, and vascular smooth muscle with accumulation of PAS-positive, diastase-sensitive material; in the surviving brother, similar glycogen deposition was shown in smooth muscle of the superficial temporal artery. The authors suggested that basilar artery aneurysm formation may be a consequence of alpha-glucosidase deficiency.

Kretzschmar et al. (1990) reported a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency who had marked liver and skeletal muscle involvement. Unlike other adult cases, the patient had CNS pathology: white matter areas that were hypodense on CT and hyperintense on MRI, and myelin changes ranging from focal demyelination to necrosis.

Chancellor et al. (1991) reported a 68-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency who presented with a myopathy and urinary incontinence brought on by exercise.

In 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Wokke et al. (1995) found that worsening of handicap was related to age, and the decline in vital capacity was related to the duration of symptoms. Respiratory insufficiency was never the first manifestation. Muscle weakness progressed over a period of years.

Laforêt et al. (2000) studied 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Progressive muscle weakness began in adulthood in all but 1 patient, but 16 patients had mild, nonprogressive muscular symptoms that started in childhood. Eighteen patients had a similar pattern of pelvic girdle weakness, mostly affecting the glutei and thigh adductors. Eight patients had restrictive respiratory insufficiency with vital capacity below 60%, and in 2 patients respiratory failure was the first sign of disease.

Lam et al. (2003) described a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II. His 13-year-old brother was asymptomatic despite carrying the same genotype. The authors concluded that intrafamilial phenotypic variation of juvenile-onset disease is ethnically diverse and suggested that other genes contribute to the phenotypic variability. ⚠︎

Anneser et al. (2005) described a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease who had vascular involvement resembling the atherosclerotic angiopathy of the elderly, which the authors considered an unusual phenotype.

Hagemans et al. (2005) used a questionnaire to collect information on 255 children and adults with Pompe disease. Disease severity was associated with disease duration, not with age. Earlier disease manifestation meant that patients became dependent on a wheelchair or ventilator earlier.

Amartino et al. (2006) reported 2 clinical forms of glycogen storage disease type II in 2 generations of the same family.

Groen et al. (2006) reported ptosis as a feature of late-onset glycogenosis type II.

Forsha et al. (2011) evaluated baseline ECGs and echocardiograms in 87 patients with late-onset Pompe disease (median age 44 years, 51% male, 94% Caucasian; median disease duration 15 years) enrolled in a randomized trial. Overall, 46% had 1 or more ECG or echocardiographic abnormalities: the most common ECG findings were left ventricular hypertrophy (12%) and short PR interval (10%, 90-120 ms); 7% had decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (41-54%), and 5% had elevated left ventricular mass index on echocardiogram, all in the mild range. Other findings included bundle branch block (4%), atrial enlargement (4%), and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (2 patients). Severe ventricular hypertrophy classically associated with infantile Pompe disease was not observed, and ECG-defined left ventricular hypertrophy was not specific for increased left ventricular mass: of 10 patients with LVH on ECG, only 1 had elevated LV mass index on echocardiography. No differences were seen between patients with disease duration under versus at least 15 years; 3 of 5 (60%) patients with symptom onset before age 10 had abnormalities. The authors suggested that the abnormalities may be related to Pompe disease or to comorbid conditions.

Herbert et al. (2019) reviewed the records of 84 patients with LOPD carrying the c.-32-13T-G variant in the GAA gene who were followed at Duke University Medical Center. Four patients (4.8%; 2 males and 2 females) were diagnosed clinically and had early-onset symptoms. Symptom onset ranged from 10 days to 20 months and age at diagnosis from 10 to 26 months. The most common early features were delayed motor milestones with characteristic patterns of posture and movement (4 of 4), feeding difficulty (2 of 4), and sleep apnea (2 of 4). Cardiac involvement was absent.

Patient 1 of Herbert et al. (2019), a Caucasian boy, presented at 5 months with delayed motor development, feeding and swallowing difficulty, easy fatigability, and snoring with likely apneic episodes. CK, ALT, and AST were elevated. Muscle biopsy at 9 months showed PAS-positive vacuoles, and GAA activity in fibroblasts was 4.35% of normal. At 15 months he had generalized hypotonia, head lag, myopathic facies, and hypertrophied calves. EMG showed myotonic discharges, barium swallow showed penetration and aspiration, and polysomnography showed obstructive and central apneas. ECG and echocardiography were normal. He later developed coxa valga, mild ptosis, and progressive thoracolumbar scoliosis that required surgery at 12 years.

Patient 2 of Herbert et al. (2019), a Caucasian girl and one of fraternal twins, presented on day 10 of life with poor feeding, lethargy, and vomiting. By 2 months she had breathing difficulty and sleep apneic episodes, as well as hypotonia and developmental delay. Muscle biopsy at 14 months showed PAS-positive glycogen vacuoles, and blood GAA activity was low. She had failure to thrive, myopathic facies, scapular winging, and neck flexor weakness, and walked at 17 months. Later problems included dysphagia requiring a gastrostomy tube, gastroesophageal reflux, urinary retention, constipation, hypernasal voice, and recurrent suspected aspiration pneumonia. Echocardiography and ECG were normal. ⚠︎

Patient 3 of Herbert et al. (2019), a Caucasian boy, could not sit unsupported or hold his head upright at 6 months. At 12 months he had an unsteady wide-based gait and frequent falls. At 23 months he had a weak cry, truncal hypotonia, proximal weakness, lordosis, scapular winging, scoliosis, a positive Gowers sign, and elevated CK, AST, and ALT. Muscle biopsy showed PAS-positive glycogen storage. He later developed mild ptosis, tongue weakness, scoliosis with a 40-degree Cobb angle, pectus excavatum, cramping, vomiting, and diarrhea. Pulmonary function tests, ECG, and echocardiography were normal. ⚠︎

Patient 4 of Herbert et al. (2019), a Caucasian girl born preterm at 36 weeks, needed brief CPAP for apnea and bradycardia. Her motor milestones were delayed: she stood unsupported at 20 months and walked at 22 months. She had proximal weakness with a positive Gowers sign and a waddling gait; spinal muscular atrophy testing was negative. Blood GAA activity was low and she was diagnosed at 26 months. She also had lumbar lordosis, winged scapulae, leg pain, snoring, apneic pauses, and mild reversible obstructive airway disease. Cardiology evaluations were normal.

Korlimarla et al. (2020) assessed CNS involvement by brain MRI and developmental assessments in 14 children aged 6 to 18 years with Pompe disease on enzyme replacement therapy, 12 with infantile Pompe disease and 2 with late-onset Pompe disease. The 2 children with late-onset disease had no white matter hyperintense foci and had high scores on most developmental assessments, whereas 10 of the 12 children with infantile disease had mild to severe white matter hyperintense foci.

Jones et al. (2021) assessed tongue structure and function in 10 treatment-naive patients with LOPD, compared with 30 patients with other acquired or hereditary myopathies and 30 controls with neuropathy, using manual and quantitative muscle testing and tongue ultrasound. Compared with the neuropathic controls, LOPD patients had significantly different tongue strength, qualitative appearance, echointensity, and thickness. The authors concluded that tongue involvement in LOPD consists of weakness and sonographic abnormalities suggestive of fibrofatty replacement and atrophy.

Huggins et al. (2022) systematically evaluated 20 infants aged 6 to 21 months with LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening. None had evidence of cardiomyopathy or cardiac rhythm disturbances. CK, AST, and ALT were elevated in 8 patients (40%), and Glc4 was elevated in 2 (10%). All patients showed postural and kinematic concerns, and 3 (17%) scored below the 10th percentile on the Alberta Infant Motor Scale. Speech-language assessments were normal in all patients, and 9 (45%) had mild feeding or swallowing abnormalities. The authors concluded that the phenotype varied widely.

### Biochemical Features

Angelini et al. (1972) reported abnormalities in fibroblasts cultured from patients with adult acid maltase deficiency.

Askanas et al. (1976) established muscle tissue cultures from the biopsy of a patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Acid maltase (pH 4.0) activity, measured by maltose hydrolysis, was undetectable in both the cultured and the biopsied muscle, whereas acid phosphatase, another lysosomal enzyme, was increased in both.

Using a sensitive fluorometric assay, Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found residual acid maltase activity in muscle biopsies from 15 patients with late-onset acid maltase deficiency (mean 6.91% of normal; range 2.4 to 12.2%), but not in biopsy or autopsy muscle from 3 patients with the infantile form. Electrophoresis, kinetic characteristics, and subcellular fractionation indicated that the residual activity was lysosomal acid maltase and not a contaminating neutral maltase isozyme.

Beratis et al. (1978) purified acid alpha-glucosidase from normal fibroblasts and from fibroblasts of a patient with the adult form of the deficiency and found that the pH optimum, Michaelis constant, electrophoretic mobility in starch, thermal denaturation at pH 4.0 and 7.0, and inhibition by turanose, alpha-methylglucoside, and trehalose were the same in the mutant and normal enzyme.

Beratis et al. (1983) found that fibroblasts with phenotype 2 of acid alpha-glucosidase, considered a normal variant, had less enzyme protein and a reduced ability to cleave glycogen, but normal catalytic activity toward maltose. The authors stated that it remained unknown whether isozyme 2 homozygotes have enough enzyme deficiency to cause glycogen accumulation and a muscular dystrophy-like disease later in life.

Matsuishi et al. (1984) found that neutral maltase activity was highest in the muscle biopsy from the patient with the least severe weakness among 3 children with childhood acid maltase deficiency, and suggested that this enzyme may play a role in reducing muscle involvement.

Wokke et al. (1995) found that serum creatine kinase activity and alpha-glucosidase activity in peripheral blood cells or muscle were helpful for diagnosis but did not have prognostic value.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of late-onset Pompe disease is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 affected brothers born to healthy nonconsanguineous parents. Wokke et al. (1995) and Laforêt et al. (2000) found that affected individuals were compound heterozygous for GAA mutations, with 1 homozygous patient reported by Laforêt et al. (2000), and Huggins et al. (2022) found both homozygous and compound heterozygous infants identified by newborn screening. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found acid maltase activity in muscle, liver, and especially leukocytes in late-onset but not infantile acid maltase deficiency, and the 2 forms did not occur together in the same family. They suggested that the late-onset and infantile forms are genetically distinct.

Beratis et al. (1978) observed competition for antibody binding sites between normal enzyme and both infantile and adult mutant enzymes. They suggested that the infantile and adult forms of acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency have different molecular defects: an inactive CRM-positive protein in the infantile form and a reduced amount of enzyme protein in the adult form.

Beratis et al. (1983) studied cultured fibroblasts from 3 patients with the adult form of acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency. In 2 strains, rocket immunoelectrophoresis showed less enzyme protein, and the reduction was directly proportional to the reduction in enzyme activity. The third adult-form strain had activity within the range of the infantile form, and no crossreacting material (CRM) could be identified. Of 9 fibroblast strains from patients with the infantile form, 8 were CRM-negative and 1 was CRM-positive, with no difference in apparent enzyme activity between them. The authors concluded that there was molecular heterogeneity within the adult form as well as between the clinical forms. ⚠︎

Byrne et al. (1986) described a family in which 7 members over 3 generations had a cardioskeletal myopathy with a vacuolar myopathy and excess free and intralysosomal glycogen storage in skeletal and cardiac muscle, but normal acid maltase activity. Phosphorylase, debrancher enzyme, lysosomal enzyme screens, and glycolytic enzyme levels were also normal. Postmortem studies in several patients showed congestive cardiomyopathy with myocardial fibrosis. The authors called this the third report of lysosomal glycogen storage disease without acid maltase deficiency and noted its dominant inheritance pattern. ⚠︎

Laforêt et al. (2000) found both mutant GAA alleles in 10 of 21 French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency and detected 13 different mutations in total. They concluded that the disorder is genetically highly heterogeneous in the French population.

### Molecular Genetics

Wokke et al. (1995) studied 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency and found that all were compound heterozygotes carrying the IVS1(-13T-G) transversion in the GAA gene on one allele. On the second allele, 7 of the 11 probands (64%) had a deletion of a T at position 525 in exon 2, and 1 proband (9%) had a deletion of exon 18.

Of 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, Laforêt et al. (2000) found that 17 carried the IVS1-13T-G transversion in the GAA gene; 1 was homozygous for it and the others carried it on one allele. All patients except 1 were compound heterozygotes. Both mutant alleles were identified in 10 of the 21 patients, and 13 different mutations were detected in total.

In a 16-year-old Chinese boy with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II, Lam et al. (2003) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, P361L and R437C. His asymptomatic 13-year-old brother was also a compound heterozygote for the 2 mutations. ⚠︎

Montalvo et al. (2006) performed complete molecular analysis of the GAA gene in 40 Italian patients with late-onset glycogen storage disease type II and identified 12 novel alleles. Missense mutations were functionally characterized by enzyme activity and protein processing in a human GAA-deficient cell line, and splicing mutations were studied by RT-PCR and in silico analysis. A complex allele carrying 3 different alterations in cis was also identified. The c.-32-13T-G mutation was the most frequent, present in compound heterozygous state in 85% of patients (allele frequency 42.3%).

Herbert et al. (2019) found that 4 patients with early-onset symptoms among 84 patients with LOPD carrying the c.-32-13T-G GAA variant were compound heterozygous for c.-32-13T-G and a second pathogenic variant: c.525delT (patient 1), c.1827delC (patient 2), c.2501_2502delCA (patient 3), and c.118C-T (patient 4).

Among 20 infants with LOPD identified by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found 4 who were homozygous for the IVS1 splice site variant c.-32-13T-G in the GAA gene, 14 who were compound heterozygous for this variant, and 2 who did not carry it.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found no correlation between the amount of residual acid maltase activity in muscle and either clinical severity or the amount of glycogen accumulation in patients with late-onset acid maltase deficiency.

Wokke et al. (1995) concluded that the adult form of acid maltase deficiency appears to be fairly homogeneous both clinically and genetically, and suggested that reduced alpha-glucosidase activity is the final common pathway leading to destruction of muscle fibers and progressive muscle weakness.

Laforêt et al. (2000) found no clear correlation between type of GAA mutation and phenotype in juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, and suggested that this pointed to a complex disease mechanism.

Hagemans et al. (2005) found that patients under 15 years of age included a subgroup whose disease was more severe and progressed more rapidly, and suggested that these patients need more intensive follow-up and early intervention before irreversible damage occurs.

Montalvo et al. (2006) found the c.-32-13T-G mutation in association with the c.2237G-A (W746X) nonsense mutation in 9 of 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSDII, and discussed genotype-phenotype correlations with emphasis on this subgroup.

De Filippi et al. (2010) studied the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE; 106180) insertion/deletion polymorphism in 38 patients with late-onset Pompe disease (mean age 44.6 years). Genotype frequencies were similar to those of the general population: 16% II, 37% DD, and the rest ID. The 3 genotype groups did not differ in mean age, disease duration, Walton score, or other severity scores. However, the DD genotype was associated with earlier disease onset (P = 0.041), higher creatine kinase levels at diagnosis (P = 0.024), muscle pain (P = 0.014), and faster disease progression (P = 0.037). The authors suggested that the ACE polymorphism may modify the phenotype and prognosis of Pompe disease.

Herbert et al. (2019) noted that the second GAA variants in their 4 early-onset patients were nonsense or frameshift variants. However, c.525delT and c.1827delC were each found in 12 other patients in their LOPD cohort, all with adult-onset disease, and the authors suggested that these genotype combinations are not sufficient to cause the early-onset phenotype. In patients 1 and 4, whose ACE genotypes were available, the unfavorable D/D genotype was absent. The authors proposed that other genetic and epigenetic modifiers might be involved.

### Pathogenesis

Askanas et al. (1976) concluded that the reproduction of morphologic and biochemical abnormalities in cultured muscle fibers supported the concept that adult-onset acid maltase deficiency is a biochemically distinct primary myopathy.

Using monospecific antibodies against placental acid alpha-glucosidase, Beratis et al. (1978) found that cross-reacting material in adult-form fibroblasts appeared significantly reduced compared with normal or infantile-form cells, and that immune complexes from adult-form cells retained enzyme activity, whereas those from infantile-form cells did not. They concluded that in the adult form the mutation reduces the amount of enzyme protein, whereas the infantile form results from a structural gene mutation producing a catalytically inactive, CRM-positive protein.

Slonim et al. (1983) observed a rapid fall in circulating branched-chain amino acids after protein ingestion in a child with acid maltase deficiency, suggesting that increased net muscle protein catabolism may contribute to progressive muscle weakness, in addition to intralysosomal glycogen accumulation and altered myofibrillar morphology.

Isaacs et al. (1986) discussed how acid maltase deficiency relates to muscle weakness and to glycogenosome formation, with or without accumulation of glycogen inside muscle fibers.

In a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency, Kretzschmar et al. (1990) found vacuolar degeneration of small and large cerebral arteries, with vacuoles in pericytes and arterial smooth muscle cells partly filled with PAS-positive material. Electron microscopy showed lysosomal and free cytoplasmic glycogen in intimal smooth muscle cells of large cerebral arteries and in pericytes of arterioles and capillaries, and local glycogen accumulation was associated with severe cell damage and necrosis. The authors thought that this vessel wall damage caused the many small intracerebral aneurysms and suggested that the vascular pathology underlay the neuropathologic changes. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

In the patient reported by Sivak et al. (1981), electromyographic studies suggested acid maltase deficiency, and electron microscopic and biochemical studies of a muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis.

Trend et al. (1985) found characteristic glycogen vacuoles in peripheral blood lymphocytes in all 5 of their patients, whereas muscle biopsy was characteristic in only 1 of 4 cases and showed minimal or nonspecific features in 3. Diagnosis was confirmed by acid maltase assay in muscle and cultured fibroblasts.

In a clinical, biochemical, and genetic study of 18 patients with adult-onset GSD II, Ausems et al. (1999) found that measurement of GAA activity in muscle and muscle histopathology appeared to add no value when GAA activity in leukocytes was clearly deficient, and that creatine kinase elevation was a sensitive marker of the disease. They formulated a diagnostic protocol.

Kallwass et al. (2007) showed that a fluorometric assay of GAA activity in dried blood spots, using the selective alpha-glucosidase inhibitor acarbose to remove interference from isoenzymes, efficiently detected patients with late-onset Pompe disease. They noted that late-onset patients are often misdiagnosed by conventional methods because other tissue types retain some GAA activity.

In a review, Bembi et al. (2008) stated that a clinical diagnosis of glycogenosis type II is confirmed by measuring GAA activity in blood samples, cultured fibroblasts, and muscle biopsies; activity is virtually absent in infants and markedly reduced in juveniles and adults. They also reviewed the use of muscle imaging.

Wang et al. (2011), an ACMG work group, developed educational guidelines for diagnostic confirmation and management of presymptomatic individuals with lysosomal storage diseases, including Pompe disease, identified by newborn screening, family-based testing, or carrier testing in at-risk populations. They noted that the evidence was limited, so the recommendations should be considered expert opinion. ⚠︎

Jones et al. (2021) found significantly lower quantitative tongue strength and significantly reduced tongue thickness on ultrasound in LOPD patients than in patients with other acquired or hereditary myopathies, and suggested that quantitative tongue weakness and/or atrophy may help distinguish LOPD from other myopathies, with ultrasound serving as an effective point-of-care method. ⚠︎

Because of the high phenotypic variability in children with LOPD diagnosed through newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) recommended careful physical therapy evaluation and close monitoring for symptom progression.

### Clinical Management

Slonim et al. (1983) treated a child with acid maltase deficiency and progressive muscle weakness with a high-protein diet for 12 months, which reversed the weakness and wasting, with improvement in muscle function, exercise tolerance, and growth.

Trend et al. (1985) reported that long-term domiciliary ventilatory support (25 patient-years) using a rocking bed or intermittent positive pressure respiration via tracheostomy allowed all 5 of their patients to return to work, with clinical condition remaining stable.

Margolis and Hill (1986) treated a 55-year-old man with adult acid maltase deficiency and severe ventilatory muscle weakness with a high-protein (1.6 g/kg), weight-reducing diet for 27 months. The therapy was associated with striking improvement in respiratory function, including measures of ventilatory muscle strength and endurance.

Isaacs et al. (1986) reported that an adult patient with lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency responded favorably to a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet after trials of several forms of therapy. ⚠︎

Bembi et al. (2008) reviewed management of glycogenosis type II as a multisystem disorder requiring a multidisciplinary team, and discussed functional assessment, rehabilitation, nutritional management, genetic counseling, prenatal diagnosis, screening, and emerging therapeutic options.

In a double-blind trial in which patients with late-onset Pompe disease were randomized 2:1 to biweekly 20 mg/kg recombinant human acid alpha-glucosidase or placebo for 78 weeks, Forsha et al. (2011) found no significant ECG or echocardiographic changes associated with enzyme replacement therapy and no significant cardiovascular safety concerns. One patient with known Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome had an episode of supraventricular tachycardia after treatment, classified as serious and possibly related to the study medication.

Herbert et al. (2019) found that all 4 of their early-onset LOPD patients improved on enzyme replacement therapy with alglucosidase alfa (median age at start, 23.5 months), but gross motor function stayed below age level and all had residual weakness, including ptosis, lordosis, scoliosis, scapular winging, and trunk and limb weakness. Patient 1 had only partial improvement even at 40 mg/kg/week. Antibody titers stayed low in the 3 patients tested. The authors suggested that patients with early onset have more rapid and severe progression. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Bashan et al. (1988) reported 18 patients with alpha-glucosidase deficiency diagnosed in Israel over 15 years, all Palestinian Arabs except 2 sibs from a Jewish Iraqi family; all had the infantile type except 1 patient with the juvenile type. They noted a high prevalence of alpha-glucosidase deficiency in the Arab population of Israel, which has a high rate of consanguinity.

Kroos et al. (1995) studied the frequency of 3 common mutant alleles in 121 patients with glycogen storage disease type II and their associated clinical phenotypes.

Martiniuk et al. (1998) determined the carrier frequency for glycogen storage disease type II in New York and estimated the number of affected individuals born with the disease.

Montalvo et al. (2006) found that c.-32-13T-G was the most frequent GAA mutation among 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSDII (allele frequency 42.3%), as described in other late-onset Caucasian populations.

### Animal Model

Bijvoet et al. (1998) generated a mouse model of Pompe disease by targeted disruption of the Gaa gene in embryonic stem cells. Homozygous knockout mice lacked Gaa mRNA and had virtually complete acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Glycogen-containing lysosomes were detected soon after birth in liver, heart, and skeletal muscle, and by 13 weeks large focal glycogen deposits had formed, with vacuolar spaces staining positive for acid phosphatase. Male and female knockout mice were fertile. At 9 months, the oldest mice still had no overt clinical symptoms, but their hearts were typically enlarged and their electrocardiograms abnormal.

Raben et al. (1998) disrupted the Gaa gene in mice. Homozygous 6(neo)/6(neo) mice had no enzyme activity; glycogen began to accumulate in cardiac and skeletal muscle lysosomes by 3 weeks of age and increased progressively, and by 3.5 weeks the mice had markedly reduced mobility and strength. The mice grew normally, reached adulthood, and remained fertile. As in the human adult disease, older mice accumulated glycogen in the diaphragm, and by 8 to 9 months they developed obvious muscle wasting and a weak, waddling gait. A second model with deletion of exon 6 (Delta6/Delta6), like the exon 13 knockout, kept normal strength and mobility up to 6.5 months despite biochemical and pathologic changes indistinguishable from those of 6(neo)/6(neo) mice. The authors concluded that the model recapitulates critical features of both infantile and adult forms of the disease and suggested that genetic background contributes to the differences among the 3 models.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 POMPE DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
GAA, IVS1, T-G, -13 (c.-32-13T-G) [c.-32-13T>G]

Wokke et al. (1995) found that all 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency were compound heterozygotes carrying the IVS1(-13T-G) transversion in the GAA gene on one allele. The second allele carried a 1-bp deletion of T at position 525 in 7 probands and a deletion of exon 18 in 1 proband.

Laforêt et al. (2000) found the IVS1-13T-G transversion in 17 of 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency; 1 patient was homozygous and the others were heterozygous for it.

Montalvo et al. (2006) found the c.-32-13T-G mutation in compound heterozygous state in 85% of 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSDII (allele frequency 42.3%); in 9 patients it occurred with the W746X nonsense mutation.

Herbert et al. (2019) found c.-32-13T-G in compound heterozygosity with a second pathogenic GAA variant in 4 Caucasian patients with early-onset symptoms among 84 LOPD patients carrying this variant.

Among 20 infants with LOPD identified by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found 4 homozygous and 14 compound heterozygous for c.-32-13T-G.
