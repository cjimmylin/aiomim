### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the GAA gene (606800) on chromosome 17q25.3.

### Description

Glycogenosis type II (Pompe disease) is a progressive, debilitating and often fatal neuromuscular disorder. Its clinical phenotypes form a continuum that varies in which organs are involved, age at onset and severity (Bembi et al., 2008). The late-onset form, which includes juvenile- and adult-onset cases, is marked by slowly progressive proximal muscle weakness that mainly affects the pelvic girdle, together with respiratory insufficiency due to diaphragmatic weakness (Trend et al., 1985; Laforêt et al., 2000). Residual acid maltase activity is found in muscle from patients with the late-onset form (Mehler and DiMauro, 1977). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Swaiman et al. (1968) reported a late-infantile form of acid maltase deficiency. Hudgson et al. (1968) described adult myopathy due to glycogen storage disease caused by acid maltase deficiency.

Engel (1970) studied 4 adults with acid maltase deficiency and described the condition as a syndrome that can mimic muscular dystrophy or other myopathies.

Sivak et al. (1981) reported a 51-year-old man with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency who presented with a 6-year history of gradually progressive exertional dyspnea. His complaint was first attributed to increasing age, but evaluation led to a diagnosis of diaphragmatic paralysis. Neurologic examination showed only minimal weakness of limb muscles. Electromyography suggested acid maltase deficiency, and electron microscopic and biochemical studies of a muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis.

Francesconi and Auff (1982) reported cardiac arrhythmias in association with the adult form of type II glycogenosis.

Matsuishi et al. (1984) studied 3 children with the childhood form of acid maltase deficiency: 2 sibs and 1 sporadic patient. The patients had delayed developmental milestones and mild generalized muscle weakness. The authors considered nasal vocalization and an EMG finding of abnormal insertion voltage followed by pseudomyotonic discharge to be characteristic diagnostic findings for this form. The common histochemical findings in affected muscle were selective type 2A fiber atrophy and type 2B fiber deficiency.

Trend et al. (1985) described 5 patients with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All had developed their first pelvic girdle symptoms late in the second or early in the third decade, and some years later they developed signs of respiratory insufficiency. Typically they were tall and had weak, wasted paraspinal and gluteal muscles with lower limb weakness. All were orthopneic, with marked diaphragmatic weakness.

Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 brothers, sons of healthy nonconsanguineous parents, who developed progressive proximal muscle weakness secondary to alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Each developed a fusiform basilar artery aneurysm. The aneurysm ruptured fatally in 2 of the brothers and was complicated by cerebellar infarction in the third. Skeletal muscle showed severe vacuolation with PAS-positive, diastase-sensitive material, and the liver showed vacuolation with glycogen accumulation. In the surviving brother, similar glycogen deposition was found in the smooth muscle of the superficial temporal artery. ⚠︎

Kretzschmar et al. (1990) reported a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency with pronounced involvement of the liver and skeletal muscles. CT and MRI scans showed hypodense and hyperintense white matter areas, and myelin changes ranged from focal demyelination to necrosis. The patient had vacuolar degeneration of the cerebral arteries and many small aneurysms of the intracerebral arteries.

Chancellor et al. (1991) reported a 68-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency who presented with a myopathy and exercise-induced urinary incontinence.

Wokke et al. (1995) studied 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. The patients had progressive muscle weakness, decreased vital capacity and elevated serum creatine kinase. Increasing handicap was related to age, and decreasing vital capacity was related to how long the patient had had symptoms. Respiratory insufficiency was never the first manifestation. ⚠︎

Laforêt et al. (2000) studied 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. Progressive muscle weakness began in adulthood in all but 1 patient, but 16 patients had mild nonprogressive muscular symptoms in childhood. Eighteen patients shared a clinical pattern of pelvic girdle weakness predominating in the glutei and thigh adductors. Eight had restrictive respiratory insufficiency with a vital capacity of less than 60%, and in 2 patients respiratory failure was the first manifestation.

Lam et al. (2003) reported a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset glycogen storage disease type II. His 13-year-old brother had the same genotype but was asymptomatic.

Anneser et al. (2005) described a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement resembling the atherosclerotic angiopathy of the elderly.

Hagemans et al. (2005) collected questionnaire information on 255 children and adults with Pompe disease. Disease severity was associated with disease duration and not with age. Patients whose disease began earlier became dependent on a wheelchair or ventilator earlier.

Amartino et al. (2006) reported a family in which two clinical forms of glycogen storage disease type II occurred in two generations. Groen et al. (2006) reported ptosis as a feature of late-onset glycogenosis type II.

Forsha et al. (2011) evaluated baseline ECGs and echocardiograms in 87 adults with late-onset Pompe disease enrolled in a randomized trial of enzyme replacement therapy. There were 44 males and 43 females with a median age of 44 years, and 82 were Caucasian. Forty-six percent had 1 or more abnormalities. The most frequent were left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) by ECG (12%), a short PR interval (10%) and decreased ejection fraction (7%). Six patients had a left ventricular ejection fraction of less than 55% (range, 41 to 54%). Only 4 patients (5%) had an elevated left ventricular mass index on echocardiogram, all in the mild range. The severe ventricular hypertrophy typical of infantile Pompe disease was not seen. Other findings included sinus tachycardia, prolonged QTc interval, bundle branch block, atrial enlargement, and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome in 2 patients. No patient had atrioventricular block, right ventricular hypertrophy or ventricular tachycardia. The authors noted that the abnormalities might be due to Pompe disease or to comorbid conditions.

Herbert et al. (2019) found that 4 of 84 patients (4.8%) followed at their center for late-onset Pompe disease with the c.-32-13T-G GAA variant had been clinically diagnosed with symptoms beginning in the first 2 years of life (10 days to 20 months). Initial symptoms included delayed gross motor milestones, proximal weakness, swallowing and feeding difficulties, and sleep apnea. All 4 patients were Caucasian. Patient 1, a boy, presented at 5 months with delayed motor development and feeding difficulties, and had elevated CK, ALT and AST. He had generalized hypotonia, head lag when pulled to sit, myopathic facies and hypertrophied calf muscles. He later had swallowing difficulty with aspiration, obstructive and central apneas, motor regression, myotonic discharges on EMG, progressive scoliosis requiring surgery, and mild ptosis of the left eye. Patient 2, a girl and one of fraternal twins, was born at term and presented on day 10 of life with poor feeding, lethargy and vomiting. She later had difficulty swallowing saliva, persistent gastroesophageal reflux requiring a gastrostomy tube, urinary retention, chronic constipation, a hypernasal voice, aspiration pneumonia and apneic episodes during sleep. Patient 3, a boy, had difficulty sitting unsupported and holding his head upright at 6 months, and at 12 months had an unsteady wide-based gait and frequent falls. He later had a Gowers sign, scapular winging, scoliosis with a Cobb angle of 40 degrees, hypernasality, lingual weakness, hyporeflexia and mild ptosis. Patient 4, a girl born preterm at 36 weeks, needed brief CPAP in the newborn period for apnea and bradycardia. She had delayed motor milestones, leg pain, a Gowers sign, a waddling gait, lumbar lordosis, bilateral winged scapulae, and snoring with apneic pauses. Cardiac evaluations, including ECG and echocardiography, were normal in all 4 patients. Despite enzyme replacement therapy, all had residual muscle weakness. ⚠︎

Korlimarla et al. (2020) used brain MRI to study 14 children aged 6 to 18 years with Pompe disease who were receiving enzyme replacement therapy. Ten of the 12 children with infantile Pompe disease had mild to severe white matter hyperintense foci. The 2 children with late-onset disease had no such foci and scored high on most developmental assessments.

Jones et al. (2021) studied 10 patients with LOPD who had not received treatment. They found tongue weakness, together with sonographic abnormalities suggesting fibrofatty replacement and atrophy of the tongue.

Huggins et al. (2022) studied 20 infants with LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening, last assessed at 6 to 21 months of age. None had cardiomyopathy or cardiac rhythm disturbances. CK, AST and ALT were elevated in 8 patients (40%), and urinary Glc4 was elevated in 2 (10%). All patients showed postural and kinematic concerns, and 3 of 18 had motor scores below the 10th percentile. Speech-language assessments were normal in all patients, and 9 (45%) had mild feeding or swallowing abnormalities. The authors found high variability among the patients. They concluded that careful physical therapy evaluation is needed to monitor for subtle musculoskeletal signs that may reflect early muscle involvement. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Angelini et al. (1972) reported abnormalities in fibroblasts cultured from patients with adult acid maltase deficiency.

Using a sensitive fluorometric assay, Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found residual acid maltase activity in muscle biopsies from 15 patients with late-onset acid maltase deficiency (mean, 6.91%; range, 2.4 to 12.2%). They did not find it in muscle from 3 patients with the infantile form. Electrophoresis, kinetic studies and subcellular fractionation indicated that the residual activity was lysosomal acid maltase and not a contaminating neutral maltase isozyme.

Beratis et al. (1978) purified acid alpha-glucosidase from adult-form deficiency fibroblasts. The enzyme had the same pH optimum, Km, electrophoretic mobility, thermal denaturation and inhibitor sensitivity as the normal enzyme. Immune complexes from the adult-form cells showed enzyme activity, but the amount of cross-reacting material (CRM) appeared to be significantly lower than in normal cells or in cells from the infantile form.

Beratis et al. (1983) found that fibroblasts with phenotype 2 of acid alpha-glucosidase, which is considered a normal variant, had less enzyme protein and a reduced ability to cleave glycogen, but normal catalytic activity toward maltose. The authors stated that it remained unknown whether the deficiency in homozygotes for isozyme 2 is enough to cause glycogen accumulation and a muscular dystrophy-like disease later in life.

In their 3 children with the childhood form of acid maltase deficiency, Matsuishi et al. (1984) found the highest neutral maltase activity in the muscle biopsy from the patient with the least severe weakness. They suggested that this enzyme may help reduce muscle involvement.

### Inheritance

Late-onset Pompe disease is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Makos et al. (1987) reported 3 affected brothers born to healthy nonconsanguineous parents. Wokke et al. (1995) found that all 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency were compound heterozygotes for GAA mutations. Lam et al. (2003) found that a Chinese proband with juvenile-onset disease and his brother were both compound heterozygous for the same 2 GAA mutations. Laforêt et al. (2000) and Huggins et al. (2022) reported patients who were homozygous for the c.-32-13T-G (IVS1-13T-G) GAA variant. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Mehler and DiMauro (1977) suggested that late-onset and infantile acid maltase deficiency are genetically distinct. Acid maltase activity was present in muscle, liver and, to a greater extent, leukocytes in the late-onset form but not in the infantile form, and the two forms did not occur in the same family.

Beratis et al. (1983) found extensive genetic heterogeneity in acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency, with molecular differences between the clinical forms and within both the infantile and adult forms. Of 9 infantile-form fibroblast strains, 8 were CRM-negative and 1 was CRM-positive. In 2 adult-form strains, enzyme protein was reduced in direct proportion to the reduction in enzyme activity. A third adult-form strain had activity in the infantile range and no detectable CRM.

### Molecular Genetics

Wokke et al. (1995) studied 16 patients from 11 families with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All were compound heterozygotes in the GAA gene and carried the IVS1(-13T-G) transversion on one allele. The second allele carried either a deletion of a T at position 525 in exon 2 (7 probands, 64%) or a deletion of exon 18 (1 proband, 9%).

Laforêt et al. (2000) studied 21 unrelated French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. All but 1 were compound heterozygotes. Seventeen carried the IVS1(-13T-G) transversion in the GAA gene, 1 of them in the homozygous state. Both mutant alleles were identified in 10 cases, and 13 different GAA mutations were found.

In a 16-year-old Chinese patient with juvenile-onset GSD II, Lam et al. (2003) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, P361L and R437C. His asymptomatic 13-year-old brother was also compound heterozygous for both mutations.

In a 30-year-old woman with late-onset Pompe disease and vascular involvement, Anneser et al. (2005) identified 2 novel mutations in the GAA gene, ala237-to-val (A237V) and gly293-to-arg (G293R).

Montalvo et al. (2006) performed complete molecular analysis of the GAA gene in 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSD II and identified 12 novel alleles. The c.-32-13T-G mutation was the most frequent and was present in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of patients. It occurred together with c.2237G-A (W746X) in 9 of the 40 patients. The authors characterized missense mutations by enzyme activity and protein processing in a human GAA-deficient cell line, and studied splicing mutations by RT-PCR and in silico analysis. They also identified a complex allele carrying 3 different alterations in cis.

Herbert et al. (2019) studied 4 Caucasian children with late-onset Pompe disease whose symptoms began in the first 2 years of life. Each was compound heterozygous for the c.-32-13T-G variant in the GAA gene and a second variant: c.525delT (p.Glu176Argfs*45) in patient 1, c.1827delC in patient 2, c.2501_2502delCA (p.T834fs) in patient 3, and c.118C-T in patient 4.

Among 20 infants with LOPD diagnosed by newborn screening, Huggins et al. (2022) found that 4 were homozygous for the IVS1 splice site variant c.-32-13T-G in the GAA gene, 14 were compound heterozygous for it, and 2 did not carry it.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Mehler and DiMauro (1977) found no correlation between the amount of residual acid maltase activity and either clinical severity or glycogen accumulation in late-onset acid maltase deficiency.

Wokke et al. (1995) concluded that the adult form of acid maltase deficiency appears to be both clinically and genetically rather homogeneous. They proposed that reduced alpha-glucosidase activity is the final common pathway that destroys muscle fibers and causes muscle weakness to progress over years.

Laforêt et al. (2000) found no clear correlation between the type of GAA mutation and phenotype in French patients with juvenile- and adult-onset acid maltase deficiency, suggesting a complex physiopathology.

Lam et al. (2003) found that the asymptomatic 13-year-old brother of a Chinese proband with juvenile-onset GSD II was also compound heterozygous for the P361L and R437C GAA mutations. They concluded that intrafamilial phenotypic variation in juvenile-onset GSD II occurs across ethnic groups, and suggested that other genes contribute to the phenotypic variability of GSD II.

Montalvo et al. (2006) discussed genotype-phenotype correlations in late-onset GSD II, with particular emphasis on the subgroup carrying the c.-32-13T-G/c.2237G-A genotype.

In 38 patients with late-onset Pompe disease (aged 44.6 +/- 19.8 years), de Filippi et al. (2010) found that the ACE insertion/deletion polymorphism was distributed as in the general population (16% II, 37% DD). The DD genotype was associated with earlier onset of disease, higher creatine kinase levels at diagnosis, muscle pain, and a more severe rate of disease progression. The authors suggested that the ACE polymorphism may modulate phenotype and prognosis. The 3 ACE genotype groups did not differ in mean age, disease duration, Walton score or other disease severity scores.

Forsha et al. (2011) found no significant difference in the frequency of cardiovascular abnormalities between adults with late-onset Pompe disease of less than 15 years' duration and those with disease of 15 years or more. Three (60%) of the patients whose symptoms began before 10 years of age had 1 or more ECG or echocardiographic abnormalities.

Herbert et al. (2019) noted that the c.525delT and c.1827delC variants found in 2 of their early-onset patients were each present in 12 other patients in their LOPD cohort, all with adult-onset disease. They suggested that these variants combined with c.-32-13T-G are not enough to cause the early-onset phenotype. The ACE D/D genotype was not present in the 2 early-onset patients tested.

### Pathogenesis

Askanas et al. (1976) established muscle cultures from the biopsy of a patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency. The cultured muscle had the same abnormalities as the biopsied muscle: acid phosphatase-positive vacuoles, glycogen stored in membrane-bound secondary lysosomes, undetectable acid maltase and raised acid phosphatase. The authors said this supported the idea that the disorder is a biochemically distinct primary myopathy.

Beratis et al. (1978) concluded that an infantile case resulted from a structural gene mutation producing a catalytically inactive, CRM-positive enzyme. In contrast, the mutation in the adult form appeared to reduce the amount of enzyme protein in the cells.

Slonim et al. (1983) found that circulating branched-chain amino acids fell rapidly after protein ingestion in a child with acid maltase deficiency. They suggested that increased net muscle protein catabolism may play a part in the pathogenesis of the disorder. Margolis and Hill (1986) likewise based their dietary approach on evidence that muscle catabolism may contribute to muscle weakness in acid maltase deficiency.

Makos et al. (1987) suggested that basilar artery aneurysm formation in their sibship may have been a consequence of alpha-glucosidase deficiency. At postmortem, they found PAS-positive, diastase-sensitive material accumulated in vascular smooth muscle. Glycogen deposition in vascular smooth muscle had previously been described in the disorder but had not been clinically significant.

In a patient with late-onset acid maltase deficiency, Kretzschmar et al. (1990) used electron microscopy to find lysosomal and free cytoplasmic glycogen in smooth muscle cells of the intima of large cerebral arteries and in pericytes of arterioles and capillaries. The glycogen accumulation was associated with severe cellular damage and necrosis, which the authors thought resulted in the formation of numerous small intracerebral aneurysms.

### Diagnosis

Trend et al. (1985) found characteristic glycogen vacuoles in peripheral blood lymphocytes in all 5 of their adults with acid maltase deficiency, whereas muscle biopsy was characteristic in only 1 of 4 cases. The diagnosis was confirmed by enzyme assay in muscle and cultured fibroblasts.

Bashan et al. (1988) found leukocyte alpha-glucosidase activity to be highly variable in Israeli patients with alpha-glucosidase deficiency and concluded that leukocytes are unfit for enzymatic diagnosis of the disease. They also found marked heterogeneity in the pH profiles of alpha-glucosidase activity in muscle and leukocytes.

Wokke et al. (1995) found that serum creatine kinase activity and alpha-glucosidase activity in peripheral blood cells or muscle were helpful for diagnosis but had no prognostic value.

In a study of 18 patients with adult-onset GSD II, Ausems et al. (1999) found that measuring GAA activity in muscle and examining muscle tissue microscopically appeared to add nothing when GAA activity in leukocytes was clearly deficient. They found that elevated creatine kinase was a sensitive marker of GSD II, and they formulated a diagnostic protocol.

Kallwass et al. (2007) showed that a fluorometric assay of GAA activity in dried blood spots efficiently detected patients with late-onset Pompe disease. The assay uses acarbose, a highly selective alpha-glucosidase inhibitor, to eliminate isoenzyme interference. They noted that these patients are frequently misdiagnosed by conventional methods because of residual GAA activity in other tissue types.

Bembi et al. (2008) noted that a clinical diagnosis of glycogenosis type II is confirmed by GAA enzyme activity in blood samples, cultured fibroblasts or muscle biopsies. Activity is virtually absent in infants and markedly reduced in juveniles and adults. They also reviewed the use of muscle imaging to evaluate muscle pathology.

For an ACMG work group, Wang et al. (2011) developed expert-opinion educational guidelines on several lysosomal storage diseases, including glycogen storage disease type II (Pompe disease). The guidelines cover confirmatory diagnostic testing and management of presymptomatic individuals identified by newborn screening, by family-based testing after a proband is found, or by carrier testing. ⚠︎

Forsha et al. (2011) found that LVH by ECG criteria was not specific for increased left ventricular mass in late-onset Pompe disease. Of 10 patients with LVH on ECG, only 1 had an elevated left ventricular mass index on echocardiography.

Jones et al. (2021) assessed tongue structure and function by manual and quantitative muscle testing and ultrasound. They studied 10 untreated LOPD patients, 30 patients with other acquired or hereditary myopathies, and 30 neuropathic controls. LOPD patients had significantly lower quantitative tongue strength and sonographic tongue thickness than patients with other myopathies. The authors concluded that quantitative tongue weakness and/or atrophy may help distinguish LOPD from other myopathies. They also found ultrasound to be an effective, efficient method for quantitative assessment of the tongue muscles at the point of care.

### Clinical Management

Sivak et al. (1981) found that exercise stress testing was necessary to define the limits of exercise tolerance in their patient with adult-onset acid maltase deficiency and diaphragmatic paralysis.

Slonim et al. (1983) treated a child with acid maltase deficiency with a high-protein diet for 12 months. The weakness and wasting reversed, and muscle function, exercise tolerance and growth improved.

Trend et al. (1985) reported that long-term home ventilatory support (25 patient-years), using a rocking bed or intermittent positive pressure ventilation via tracheostomy, allowed all 5 of their adult patients to return to work, and their clinical condition remained stable.

Margolis and Hill (1986) treated a 55-year-old man with acid maltase deficiency and severe ventilatory muscle weakness with a high-protein (1.6 g/kg), weight-reducing diet for 27 months. The diet was associated with striking improvement in respiratory function, including measures of ventilatory muscle strength and endurance. The authors suggested that high-protein dietary therapy may be useful in adults with acid maltase deficiency and respiratory insufficiency. Isaacs et al. (1986) tried several forms of therapy in an adult with acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency and reported a favorable response to a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet.

Hagemans et al. (2005) found that patients younger than 15 years included a subgroup whose disease was more severe and progressed faster. They concluded that these patients need closer follow-up and early intervention, before irreversible damage occurs.

Bembi et al. (2008) reviewed the management of glycogenosis type II as a multisystem disorder. They recommended a multidisciplinary team covering cardiology, pulmonology, neuromuscular disease, rehabilitation, nutrition and genetic counseling, and they reviewed treatment, including emerging therapeutic options.

Forsha et al. (2011) performed a post hoc analysis of a 78-week double-blind trial in which adults with late-onset Pompe disease were randomized 2:1 to recombinant human acid alpha-glucosidase (20 mg/kg every 2 weeks) or placebo. ECG and echocardiographic parameters did not change significantly with enzyme replacement therapy, and there were no significant cardiovascular safety concerns. One patient with known Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome had an episode of supraventricular tachycardia that was classified as possibly related to treatment. ⚠︎

Herbert et al. (2019) observed that all 4 of their early-onset patients improved on standardized motor assessments after starting enzyme replacement therapy but remained below age level. One patient had persistent weakness even at 40 mg/kg/week. Antibody titers remained low in the 3 patients tested, so the residual deficits were not attributed to high antibody titers.

### Population Genetics

Kroos et al. (1995) studied 121 patients with glycogen storage disease type II, examining how common 3 mutant alleles were and which clinical phenotypes went with them.

Martiniuk et al. (1998) estimated the carrier frequency for glycogen storage disease type II in New York and, from it, the number of affected individuals born with the disease. ⚠︎

Montalvo et al. (2006) found that c.-32-13T-G was the most frequent GAA mutation in 40 Italian patients with late-onset GSD II, present in the compound heterozygous state in 85% of patients (allele frequency 42.3%), as in other Caucasian late-onset populations.

### Animal Model

Bijvoet et al. (1998) generated a mouse model of GSD II by targeted disruption of the Gaa gene in embryonic stem cells. Homozygous knockout mice lacked Gaa mRNA and had virtually complete acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Glycogen-containing lysosomes appeared in liver, heart and skeletal muscle soon after birth, and large focal glycogen deposits had formed by 13 weeks of age. Vacuolar spaces stained positive for acid phosphatase. Male and female knockout mice were fertile. At 9 months of age the mice still had no overt clinical symptoms, but the heart was typically enlarged and the electrocardiogram was abnormal.

Raben et al. (1998) generated mice homozygous for a targeted disruption of the Gaa gene (6neo/6neo). The mice lacked enzyme activity and accumulated glycogen in cardiac and skeletal muscle lysosomes by 3 weeks of age, and their mobility and strength were markedly reduced by 3.5 weeks. They grew normally, reached adulthood and remained fertile. As in the human adult disease, older mice accumulated glycogen in the diaphragm, and by 8 to 9 months they had obvious muscle wasting and a weak, waddling gait. A second model, mice with deletion of exon 6 (Delta6/Delta6), had unimpaired strength and mobility up to 6.5 months of age, although their biochemical and pathologic changes were indistinguishable from those of the 6neo/6neo mice. The authors suggested that the genetic background of the mouse strains contributed to the differences between the models.
