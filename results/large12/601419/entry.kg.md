### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-1 (MFM1) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the desmin gene (DES; 125660) on chromosome 2q35.

### Description

Goldfarb et al. (1998) described desmin-related myopathy as a familial disorder. Its features were skeletal muscle weakness with cardiac conduction blocks, arrhythmias, and restrictive heart failure, together with intracytoplasmic deposits reactive for desmin in cardiac and skeletal muscle cells. Park et al. (2000) noted that desmin myopathy may be hereditary or sporadic. van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) described desmin-related myopathy as an autosomally inherited skeletal and cardiac myopathy caused mainly by dominant mutations in the DES gene. Gudkova et al. (2013) noted that desmin cardiomyopathy is a rare cause of congestive heart failure that can present as hypertrophic, restrictive, or arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, and that adult-onset cases are often associated with conduction disorders and a neuromuscular phenotype.

### Nomenclature

Greenberg et al. (2012) noted that the disorder they studied was called LGMD1D in OMIM nomenclature and LGMD1E by the Human Gene Nomenclature Committee.

Straub et al. (2018) reported on the 229th ENMC international workshop, held in Naarden, the Netherlands, in March 2017, which addressed the nomenclature and a reformed classification of the limb-girdle muscular dystrophies.

### Clinical Features

Porte et al. (1980) described 3 brothers with progressive myocardial deficiency. Most of their cardiac muscle cells showed massive storage of PAS-negative proteinaceous material. On electron microscopy this material consisted of sinuous filaments 7 to 10 nm in diameter, similar to intermediate filaments. Filament storage coincided with disintegration of neighboring myofibrils. Z-band changes gave rise to rod-like bodies, and Z-band material associated with sarcoplasmic reticulum tubules formed more complex structures. Filament storage and myofibrillar disintegration always occurred in areas of extensive development and involution of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, with relatively high glycogen accumulation. A few skeletal muscle fibers also showed discrete sarcoplasmic reticulum proliferation, glycogen overload, and filament deposits. ⚠︎

Stoeckel et al. (1981) described an unusual familial cardiomyopathy characterized by aberrant accumulations of desmin-type intermediate filaments.

Wolburg et al. (1982) reported 2 unrelated 32-year-old patients with a congenital, slowly progressive skeletal myopathy. One patient (case 2) also had congenital paresis of the pupillary dilator muscle and cardiomyopathy. Skeletal muscle function became severely impaired in case 1 but not in case 2. Muscle biopsies from both patients showed abnormal variation in fiber caliber, and in case 1 atrophic fibers were grouped in places. Inclusions with dense granular and fine filamentous components, consistent with cytoplasmic bodies, were present in about one-third of fibers in case 1 and about one-tenth in case 2. The biopsies also showed Z-disk streaming or disintegration and subsarcolemmal pure filamentous bodies.

Chapon et al. (1989) described 3 patients from 3 successive generations of a family with cytoplasmic body myopathy who presented with respiratory insufficiency. The patients were 72, 45, and 18 years old. Onset was at about 40 years of age in the 2 older patients and at 18 years in the youngest. Serum muscle enzymes and leukocyte maltase activity were normal. Muscle biopsy in all 3 patients showed numerous cytoplasmic (spheroid) bodies within type I muscle fibers.

Abe et al. (1993) described a Japanese kindred in which 12 patients across 4 successive generations had a progressive myopathy with diffuse cytoplasmic bodies. Patients had weakness and atrophy of the muscles of the distal extremities, neck, thorax, and shoulder girdles. Mean age at onset was 42 years, and patients became disabled after 5 to 10 years because of chronic respiratory failure. Serum creatine kinase was normal or slightly elevated. EMG showed predominantly myopathic changes with slight neurogenic change. Males were affected more often than females (2:1). Autopsy of 2 affected members showed numerous cytoplasmic bodies in skeletal muscle, mainly in type I fibers, and smooth and cardiac muscle were also affected. Electron microscopy showed dense central cores of myofilaments surrounded by radiating filaments.

Vajsar et al. (1993) reported 2 sibs who developed cardiomyopathy several years before the onset of slowly progressive muscle weakness. Skeletal muscle biopsy showed subsarcolemmal crescents of dark eosinophilic material in both type I and type II fibers. This material stained for desmin and ubiquitin but for no other cytoskeletal proteins. On electron microscopy the deposits were aggregates of granular and filamentous material arising from Z-bands. Follow-up biopsies 6 years later showed more fibers with desmin- and ubiquitin-positive subsarcolemmal aggregates. The authors concluded that these features characterized a rare familial myopathy with an unusual distribution of desmin intermediate filament proteins in skeletal and probably also cardiac muscle.

Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994) described a large kindred with autosomal dominant distal myopathy. Onset was in early to middle adulthood with gait disturbance due to distal leg weakness. Over 5 to 10 years the disease spread to all extremities and to the bulbar, respiratory, and facial muscles. Cardiac involvement was frequent, with arrhythmias, conduction blocks, and congestive heart failure, and led to premature death. EMG showed prominent spontaneous activity, short-duration motor unit potentials, and polyphasia. Muscle biopsies from several family members at different stages of the disease showed desmin storage and autophagocytosis. The authors noted that this distal myopathy differed from phenotypically similar disorders in its rapid progression, fatal course, and pathologic features.

Ariza et al. (1995) described a 28-year-old man with desmin myopathy who had generalized muscular atrophy, cardiomyopathy, and intestinal malabsorption and pseudo-obstruction. Abundant granular and filamentous sarcoplasmic aggregates that stained strongly for desmin were present throughout the skeletal muscle, the myocardium, and the smooth muscle of the intestine. On electron microscopy the aggregates were continuous with Z lines or dense bodies. Axonal spheroids that stained for neurofilament were also found in the spinal cord and roots. The authors stated that this was the first report of intestinal smooth muscle involvement in desmin myopathy, and they called the combination of intermediate filament pathologies in the neuromuscular system extremely rare.

Messina et al. (1997) characterized an autosomal dominant disorder made up of dilated cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction-system disease, and adult-onset limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, which they called FDC-CDM (familial dilated cardiomyopathy, conduction disease, and myopathy).

Goldfarb et al. (1998) reported 2 families with desmin-related cardioskeletal myopathy. In one family, skeletal myopathy began in adulthood and cardiac involvement was mild. In the other, cardiac and skeletal myopathy began in childhood and followed an aggressive course.

Muñoz-Mármol et al. (1998) reported a patient with severe generalized myopathy whose muscle showed abnormal accumulation of desmin intermediate filaments within the sarcoplasm.

Sjöberg et al. (1999) studied a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy of distal onset.

Melberg et al. (1999) studied a Swedish family with myopathy and cardiomyopathy. They examined 21 family members neurologically and cardiologically and reviewed the charts of 2 deceased affected patients. Twelve patients had myopathy. Weakness was axial in mildly affected patients, axial and predominantly distal in moderately affected patients, and generalized in severely affected patients. EMG showed myopathic signs in 10 patients. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic changes, rimmed vacuoles, and accumulation of desmin, dystrophin, and other proteins, and electron microscopy showed granulofilamentous changes and disorganized myofibrils. Several family members had episodes of chest pain or palpitations. Three men had arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), with documented nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, atrial flutter, and ventricular dilatation affecting mainly the right ventricle. Two of the 3 men received pacemakers for atrioventricular block and sick sinus syndrome. ⚠︎

Saavedra-Matiz et al. (2000) studied a large family with autosomal dominant hereditary distal myopathy. The myopathy began in early adulthood in the distal leg muscles, progressed slowly, and caused a gait disturbance. Most affected members also had cardiomyopathy, presenting as conduction block or congestive heart failure. Histology showed accumulation of desmin in the subsarcolemmal spaces of myofibers.

Kaminska et al. (2004) studied 3 families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy caused by small in-frame deletions in the DES gene. They described desminopathy as a disease characterized by progressive limb muscle weakness and by desmin-reactive granular aggregates that accumulate in the myofibers.

Selcen et al. (2004) reviewed 63 unrelated patients diagnosed with myofibrillar myopathy (MFM) at the Mayo Clinic between 1977 and 2003. Mean age at onset was 54 +/- 16 years. Weakness was both proximal and distal in 77% of patients and proximal only in 13%, and cardiomyopathy was diagnosed in 16%. EMG showed a myopathic pattern with abnormal electrical irritability. Thirteen patients had abnormal nerve conduction studies, but 4 of them had long-standing diabetes. Trichrome-stained sections showed abnormal fibers containing amorphous, granular, or pleomorphic hyaline structures that were strongly congophilic, and vacuoles containing membranous material. Electron microscopy showed progressive myofibrillar degeneration beginning at the Z-disk, accumulation of degraded filamentous material, and dislocated membranous organelles trapped in autophagic vacuoles.

Bergman et al. (2007) reported 2 distantly related Dutch families in which 15 members had autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy. The clinical picture was highly heterogeneous, ranging from isolated dilated cardiomyopathy to a more generalized skeletal myopathy with mild respiratory problems. Desmin and p62 staining of muscle biopsies showed intracytoplasmic desmin aggregates.

Pica et al. (2008) described a Chinese man with desmin myopathy, complete heart block, and mild weakness of the proximal and distal limbs. Family members who carried the mutation had a similar or milder phenotype.

Piñol-Ripoll et al. (2009) reported a girl who was the only child of consanguineous (uncle-niece) parents. She had recurrent syncope from age 6 months, initially attributed to an accessory conduction pathway (Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome), and progressive syncopal, presyncopal, and cyanotic attacks from age 3 years. At age 15 she had a long narrow face, bilateral facial weakness, ptosis, and symmetric scapular winging. She had atrophy of the masseter, temporalis, sternocleidomastoid, and trunk muscles, as well as pes cavus, mild shoulder girdle and distal limb weakness, and hypoactive reflexes. Creatine kinase was elevated 2-fold. EMG showed complex repetitive discharges, some myotonic discharges, and a myopathic pattern. Holter monitoring showed monomorphic and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, QT prolongation, and second-degree AV block, and she required an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator and a pacemaker. At 18 years she had recurrent ventricular tachyarrhythmias, mild left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF 45%), right ventricular dilatation, markedly enlarged atria, and restrictive cardiomyopathy. Skeletal weakness progressed rapidly, and diarrhea, vomiting, and weight loss suggested involvement of intestinal smooth muscle. She died of cardiac failure before age 21. Deltoid muscle biopsy showed variation in fiber size, and more than 90% of fibers had peripheral deposits of homogeneous eosinophilic material that was Congo red positive and strongly desmin-immunoreactive. Some rimmed vacuoles were present, and electron microscopy showed granulofilamentous material, consistent with myofibrillar myopathy. The inclusions differed from the amorphous patches seen in most patients with dominant or de novo desmin mutations. ⚠︎

van Tintelen et al. (2009) studied 27 carriers or obligate carriers of the DES S13F founder mutation from 5 families. The phenotype was fully penetrant but variable. All patients had cardiac involvement, characterized by high-grade AV block at young ages and important right ventricular involvement. Ten patients had right bundle branch block, sometimes as the first manifestation, and 6 had right ventricular heart failure, including 2 who fulfilled diagnostic criteria for ARVC. The authors concluded that the phenotype was predominantly cardiologic, with early conduction disease, right bundle branch block and/or right ventricular tachycardias, and ARVC phenocopies.

Otten et al. (2010) evaluated 2 families with different DES mutations. One family was diagnosed with desmin-related myopathy, with an ARVC-like phenotype in 1 patient. The other family had severe biventricular cardiomyopathy. The authors concluded that an ARVC-like phenotype or a severe cardiomyopathy with right ventricular involvement are possible but infrequent cardiac phenotypes in desmin-related myopathy.

In a meta-analysis of 159 reported DES mutation carriers with 40 different mutations, van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found neurologic signs in 74% and cardiologic signs in 74%. Both were present in 49% of carriers, isolated neurologic signs in 22%, and isolated cardiologic signs in 22%. More than 70% had myopathy or muscle weakness, and creatine kinase was normal in one-third of these. Up to 50% had cardiomyopathy, and around 60% had cardiac conduction disease or arrhythmias, with atrioventricular block an important hallmark. Symptoms generally began in the fourth decade. A quarter of carriers died, at a mean age of 49 years. Two patients with a pacemaker died suddenly, suggesting ventricular tachyarrhythmia as the cause of death.

Gudkova et al. (2013) reported what they described as the first case of desmin cardiomyopathy beginning in adolescence. Over time the patient's phenotype passed through several types of cardiomyopathy, which the authors noted made correct diagnosis and treatment at an early stage difficult.

Cetin et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous family in which 2 members had autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD2), marked by progressive weakness of the proximal shoulder and pelvic girdle muscles. In contrast to autosomal dominant desminopathies, the patients' skeletal muscle did not show ultrastructural changes such as disrupted myofibrillar organization, myofibrillar degradation products, or dislocated or aggregated membranous organelles. ⚠︎

Riley et al. (2019) described 2 sisters who presented in their twenties with slowly progressive limb-girdle weakness, severe systolic dysfunction, and progressive, severe respiratory weakness. Muscle biopsy showed variation in fiber size with more central nuclei. Electron microscopy showed extensive myofibrillar disarray and duplication of the basal lamina, but no inclusions or aggregates.

### Inheritance

Chapon et al. (1989) reported cytoplasmic body myopathy in 3 successive generations of a family, noting that familial occurrence had rarely been reported. Abe et al. (1993) observed autosomal dominant inheritance through 4 successive generations of a Japanese kindred. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also reported by Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994), Messina et al. (1997), Sjöberg et al. (1999), and Saavedra-Matiz et al. (2000). Melberg et al. (1999) concluded that the disorder in a Swedish family was autosomal dominant, with variable onset and severity of both skeletal and cardiac involvement. Bergman et al. (2007) reported autosomal dominant transmission in 2 Dutch families.

Goldfarb et al. (1998) found compound heterozygosity for 2 DES mutations in a family with childhood-onset disease. Piñol-Ripoll et al. (2009) stated that their patient was the first exhaustively proven case of autosomal recessive desminopathy, because her heterozygous carrier parents were unaffected. They noted that about 6% of patients with desminopathy show autosomal recessive inheritance, with onset in childhood at an average age of 5 years. Cetin et al. (2013) reported autosomal recessive LGMD2 in a consanguineous family with a homozygous DES mutation. Riley et al. (2019) noted that desminopathy is usually autosomal dominant but that a few autosomal recessive cases had been reported. They concluded that their family widened the phenotypic range of recessive DES cardio/myopathy to include disease without myofibrillar aggregates.

### Mapping

In families with FDC-CDM, Messina et al. (1997) carried out a genomewide scan and found significant linkage to chromosome 6q (maximum lod score of 4.99 at theta = 0.00 for D6S262). Haplotype analysis refined the interval to 3 cM between D6S1705 and D6S1656. This excluded several genes in the region that are expressed in striated muscle, including laminin alpha-2, laminin alpha-4, triadin, and phospholamban. Greenberg et al. (2012) later showed that this disorder, LGMD1D/1E, was caused by a mutation in the DES gene on chromosome 2q35 (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

In a Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and ARVC, Melberg et al. (1999) carried out linkage analysis of candidate regions. They obtained a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.76 at marker D10S1752 on 10q and a multipoint peak lod score of 3.06 between markers D10S605 and D10S215, and suggested that 10q22.3 might harbor the defect. Kuhl et al. (2008) re-examined this family with a high-density SNP panel (Affymetrix Human Mapping 10K Array). They confirmed linkage to 10q22.3, found no other region with significant evidence of linkage, and narrowed the critical interval from 10.6 Mb to 4.27 Mb between D10S1645 and D10S1786. The narrowed region contained 18 candidate genes, 17 of which (RAI17, PPIF, C10ORF56, SFTPA1, SFTPA2, SFTPA1B, SFTPA2B, SFTPD, C10ORF57, PLAC9, ANXA11, MAT1A, DYDC1, DYDC2, C10ORF58, TSPAN14, and SH2D4B) lie in the CMD1C region as well. No disease-causing mutation was found in the coding regions of these genes. Sequence analysis also excluded metavinculin (VCL) and ZASP/cypher (LDB3), which flank the linked region. To search for submicroscopic and intragenic deletions, the authors generated hybrid cell lines that carried only the affected or only the normal chromosome 10 homolog. All sequence-tagged sites and exons were present on both homologs. They speculated that regulatory mutations in 1 of the 18 genes from 10q22.3 might cause a heterogeneous spectrum of myodegenerative disorders. Hedberg et al. (2012) noted that no causative mutation had been found in the 10q22.3 region and that the causative mutation in this family lay instead in the DES gene on chromosome 2q35.

In a large family with autosomal dominant hereditary distal myopathy with desmin accumulation, Saavedra-Matiz et al. (2000) mapped the disease gene by linkage analysis to chromosome 2q33. The gene lay within a 17-cM segment bounded by D2S2248 and D2S401, and the authors considered desmin the best candidate gene.

Cetin et al. (2013) used genomewide homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous family and mapped the LGMD2 phenotype to chromosome 2q35-q36.3.

### Heterogeneity

Messina et al. (1997) used linkage to exclude genomic regions known to be linked to dilated cardiomyopathy and muscular dystrophy phenotypes, which confirmed genetic heterogeneity and identified FDC-CDM as genetically distinct. ⚠︎

Goldfarb et al. (1998) noted that the DES gene had been excluded in 3 families diagnosed with desmin-related myopathy.

Ferreiro et al. (2004) noted that desmin-related myopathies are a heterogeneous group of muscle disorders defined by intrasarcoplasmic desmin aggregates, and that mutations in the desmin and alpha-B crystallin genes account for about one-third of cases. In the original German family with early-onset recessive desmin-related myopathy with Mallory body-like inclusions, they found linkage to the SEPN1 locus on 1p36. Affected patients were homozygous for a 92-nucleotide deletion in SEPN1 (del 92 nucleotide -19/+73). The authors reported that this disorder had clinical features identical to SEPN-related myopathy and proposed that it be classified as such. They concluded that these findings substantiated the molecular heterogeneity of desmin-related myopathy.

Selcen et al. (2004) found mutations in desmin or alpha-B-crystallin in only 6 of 63 patients with MFM. They concluded that MFM is morphologically distinct but genetically heterogeneous.

Otten et al. (2010) screened the DES gene in 50 patients with ARVC or ARVC-like phenotypes and found no additional DES mutations, indicating that DES mutations are an infrequent cause of ARVC. ⚠︎

Hedberg et al. (2012) concluded that some dominantly inherited muscle diseases are difficult to diagnose because they can vary widely in clinical and morphologic features, even within a single family.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement, Goldfarb et al. (1998) identified a heterozygous A337P mutation in the DES gene, located in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain. In a family with childhood-onset aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, they found compound heterozygosity for 2 DES mutations in the carboxy-terminal end of the rod domain, A360P and N393I.

In muscle from a patient with severe generalized myopathy, Muñoz-Mármol et al. (1998) found a DES mutation that encoded a deletion of 7 amino acids within the coiled-coil segment of desmin. The wildtype desmin allele was absent from the patient's muscle. Recombinant desmin carrying the deletion did not form proper desmin intermediate filament networks in cultured cells and did not assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro.

In affected members of a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant distal desmin-related myopathy, Sjöberg et al. (1999) identified a heterozygous L345P missense mutation in the DES gene that cosegregated with the disease. It was the first point mutation in desmin reported to cosegregate with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy. The mutation affects an evolutionarily highly conserved position in the coiled-coil rod domain that is important for dimer formation. ⚠︎

Park et al. (2000) sequenced cDNA and genomic DNA from a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy. They identified a heterozygous de novo A-to-G change at the +3 position of the intron 3 splice donor site of the DES gene (IVS3+3A-G). In a second, unrelated patient with a similar myopathy, they identified a G-to-A transition at the -1 position of the intron 2 splice acceptor site (IVS2-1G-A). When genomic DNA fragments carrying either mutation were expressed, exon 3 was deleted.

In patients with desmin-related myopathy, Kaminska et al. (2004) identified 2 new in-frame deletions in the DES gene, E359_S361del and N366del, both of which change the heptad periodicity of the 2B coiled-coil segment. In expression studies in 2 cell lines, desmin carrying either deletion could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network.

Selcen et al. (2004) screened 63 unrelated patients with MFM for mutations in desmin, alpha-B-crystallin, telethonin, and syncoilin. Four patients carried missense mutations in the head or tail region of desmin. Two carried truncating mutations in the C-terminal domain of alpha-B-crystallin (CRYAB), and no patient had a mutation in syncoilin or telethonin.

Bär et al. (2007) reported 3 novel mutations in the non-alpha-helical carboxy-terminal tail domain of desmin in patients with desmin-related myofibrillar myopathy: T442I, R454W, and S460I. They studied these and 4 previously described tail mutations (E413K, K449T, I451M, and V469M) by in vitro filament assembly and in transfected C2C12 myoblasts. All mutants except E413K and R454W assembled into intermediate filaments in vitro, and all except E413K were incorporated into filament arrays in cells. Viscometric assays, however, showed that the properties of the mutant filaments differed significantly from those of wildtype desmin. When the mutants were coassembled with wildtype desmin, filament formation and filament-filament interactions were severely disturbed. ⚠︎

In affected members of 2 distantly related Dutch families with desmin-related myopathy, Bergman et al. (2007) identified a novel heterozygous missense mutation in the head domain of desmin, S13F, that cosegregated with the disease phenotype.

Pica et al. (2008) found a heterozygous S13F mutation in the DES gene in a Chinese man with desmin myopathy and complete heart block. Family members who carried the mutation were also affected.

In a girl with infantile-onset cardiomyopathy and myofibrillar myopathy born to consanguineous parents, Piñol-Ripoll et al. (2009) identified a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in the DES gene. The deletion was predicted to remove 7 amino acids (RARVDVE; R173_E179del), a full heptad of the 1B helix domain, in frame. Both parents were heterozygous and unaffected.

van Tintelen et al. (2009) identified the S13F mutation in the head domain of desmin in 27 carriers or obligate carriers from 5 families with desmin-related myopathy. It was a founder mutation and was fully penetrant.

Otten et al. (2010) identified a DES missense mutation, N342D, in a family with desmin-related myopathy in which 1 patient had an ARVC-like phenotype. They identified the R454W mutation in a second family, which had severe biventricular cardiomyopathy.

Greenberg et al. (2012) sequenced a patient with LGMD1D/1E and family members and found a splice donor site mutation in the DES gene (IVS3+3A-G). LGMD1D/1E had previously been linked to chromosome 6q23.

In the Swedish family with autosomal dominant myofibrillar myopathy and ARVC originally reported by Melberg et al. (1999), Hedberg et al. (2012) used exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.1255C-T transition in the DES gene, resulting in a pro419-to-ser (P419S) substitution. Sanger sequencing of 17 family members found the mutation in 7 of the 10 members who had been classified as affected in the earlier study. ⚠︎

In the 2 affected members of a consanguineous family with autosomal recessive LGMD2, Cetin et al. (2013) sequenced the candidate gene DES and found a homozygous splice site mutation (c.1289-2A-G). The mutation added 16 amino acids to the tail domain of desmin, a region suggested to interact with lamin B.

Ojrzyńska et al. (2017) reported restrictive cardiomyopathy caused by a novel mutation in the desmin gene.

In 2 sisters with recessive DES cardio/myopathy without myofibrillar aggregates, Riley et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygous variants in the DES gene. One was a missense variant, L190P, inherited from the father. The other was a deep intronic variant in intron 7, inherited from the mother, that was predicted to create a cryptic splice site. The disease segregated with the variants, and the father and brothers, who carried only the L190P variant, were clinically unaffected. RNA sequencing and analysis of muscle cDNA showed that the maternal variant caused aberrant splicing, with insertion of an artificial exon/intronic fragment that shifted the reading frame and created a premature termination codon. Nonsense-mediated decay was strong: 92% of DES transcripts were paternal and 8% were maternal. Western blot showed a 60 to 75% reduction in desmin, which likely consisted only of the L190P mutant protein.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In a meta-analysis of 159 DES mutation carriers, van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found that most DES mutations were missense mutations, located mostly in the 2B domain. Mutations in the 2B domain predominated in patients with an isolated neurologic phenotype. Mutations in the head and tail domains predominated in patients with an isolated cardiologic phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Porte et al. (1980) suggested that a disturbance of desmin metabolism might be involved in the cardiomyopathy of the 3 brothers they studied. They described desmin as the protein subunit of intermediate filaments and a fundamental component of Z bands. Because the sarcoplasmic reticulum abnormalities were marked, they also suggested that a chronic defect in calcium regulation might play a role.

Wolburg et al. (1982) proposed a basic defect in myofilament biosynthesis, presumably triggered by a neurogenic factor affecting the trophic influence of the neuron on the muscle cell.

In a review, Goebel (1995) stated that desmin pathology in human neuromuscular disorders always takes the form of increased amounts of desmin, either diffuse or focal. Focal accumulation occurs in 2 forms: with inclusion bodies (cytoplasmic and spheroid bodies), and as patchier granulofilamentous material. Both lesions had been found in families with a myopathy and/or cardiomyopathy. Other proteins, such as dystrophin, vimentin, actin, ubiquitin, and alpha-B crystallin, may also be overexpressed. Goebel (1995) suggested that desmin pathology may be genetically regulated or may simply reflect severely impaired metabolism of several proteins within muscle fibers.

Muñoz-Mármol et al. (1998) concluded that their findings were direct evidence that a desmin mutation can cause human myopathy. They noted that desmin-null mice form muscle fibers with Z disks and normal sarcomeres, but that these muscles are mechanically fragile and degenerate with repeated contraction.

Sjöberg et al. (1999) found that L345P desmin could not form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells. They concluded that the mutation causes myopathy by interfering in a dominant-negative manner with the dimerization-polymerization process of intermediate filament assembly.

Park et al. (2000) found that both of the splice mutations they identified caused skipping of exon 3, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 32 codons. The mutant desmin was predicted to lack 32 amino acids from the 1B segment of the alpha-helical rod. When it was expressed in vimentin-deficient SW13 cells, abnormal coarse clumps of desmin-positive material aggregated throughout the cytoplasm.

Kaminska et al. (2004) concluded that small in-frame deletions in the 2B coiled-coil segment of desmin change the coiled-coil geometry in specific ways. They proposed that this fatally damages desmin filament assembly, leading to breakdown of muscle cells and to skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy.

Bär et al. (2007) suggested that the different clinical phenotypes caused by desmin tail mutations may reflect altered interactions of the tail domain with different components of the myoblast cytoskeleton. They proposed that this may reduce the biomechanical properties and/or alter the metabolism of the individual myocyte.

Because the disorder overlaps clinically with desmosomal cardiomyopathies, van Tintelen et al. (2009) studied intercalated disks in the hearts of S13F carriers. Major desmosomal proteins were present in normal amounts, but the intercalated disks were more convoluted and elongated, with a zigzag appearance. The authors suggested that a localized effect of desmin on the structure of the cardiac intercalated disk might contribute to pathogenesis.

Otten et al. (2010) performed immunohistochemistry on myocardial tissue from 2 carriers of the R454W mutation. They found desmin aggregates and decreased desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk, suggesting a link between desmosomal cardiomyopathies and cardiomyopathies caused by DES mutations.

Greenberg et al. (2012) used laser capture microdissection to isolate cytoplasmic inclusions from the skeletal muscle of a patient with LGMD1D/1E. Mass spectrometry-based proteomics and bioinformatic analysis showed that desmin was the major constituent of the inclusions.

Cetin et al. (2013) found by immunofluorescence and Western blot analysis that mutant desmin was still expressed and still formed a cytoskeletal network in the patients' skeletal muscle fibers. Confocal laser scanning microscopy showed a specific disruption of the interaction between desmin and lamin B. The authors concluded that autosomal recessive DES mutations cause an LGMD2 phenotype without features of myofibrillar myopathy.

### Diagnosis

In their 63 patients with MFM, Selcen et al. (2004) used semiquantitative immunostaining to examine abnormal fibers. On average, 90% of abnormal fibers abnormally expressed myotilin, 75% desmin, 75% alpha-B-crystallin, 70% dystrophin, and 70% beta-amyloid precursor protein. The authors concluded that immunostains for these proteins, especially myotilin, are useful aids in the diagnosis of MFM.

Bergman et al. (2007) stressed the importance of analyzing the desmin gene in patients with familial cardiac conduction disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, and/or progressive skeletal myopathy resembling limb-girdle muscular dystrophy.

### Clinical Management

Piñol-Ripoll et al. (2009) concluded that patients with desmin-related cardiomyopathy are at high risk of ventricular arrhythmia and should be considered for an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator even before ventricular dysfunction becomes evident.

### Population Genetics

van Tintelen et al. (2009) reported that the DES S13F mutation shared by 5 families with 27 carriers or obligate carriers was a founder mutation.

### Animal Model

Shelton et al. (2004) described a 1-year-old male neutered Australian Shepherd dog with chronic lameness, contractures, and exercise intolerance. The dog had elevated serum creatine kinase, complex repetitive discharges on EMG, and clinically identified cardiomyopathy. Light and electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry of muscle biopsies showed a myofibrillar myopathy with accumulation of desmin and other proteins. The authors stated that this was the first report of myofibrillar myopathy in the dog.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ALA337PRO [p.A337P]

In a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement, Goldfarb et al. (1998) identified a heterozygous ala337-to-pro (A337P) mutation in the DES gene. The mutation lies in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain.

**.0002 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ALA360PRO [p.A360P]

In a family with childhood-onset aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, Goldfarb et al. (1998) found compound heterozygosity for 2 DES mutations in the carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain: an ala360-to-pro (A360P) substitution and an asn393-to-ile (N393I) substitution.

**.0003 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ASN393ILE [p.N393I]

For discussion of the asn393-to-ile (N393I) mutation in the DES gene, which Goldfarb et al. (1998) found in compound heterozygous state with A360P in a family with childhood-onset aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, see ALA360PRO.

**.0004 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 7-AA DEL

In muscle from a patient with severe generalized myopathy and sarcoplasmic accumulation of desmin intermediate filaments, Muñoz-Mármol et al. (1998) found a DES mutation that encoded a deletion of 7 amino acids within the coiled-coil segment of desmin. The wildtype desmin allele was absent from the patient's muscle. Recombinant desmin carrying the deletion did not form proper intermediate filament networks in cultured cells and did not assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro.

**.0005 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, LEU345PRO [p.L345P]

In affected members of a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy of distal onset, Sjöberg et al. (1999) identified a heterozygous leu345-to-pro (L345P) mutation in the DES gene that cosegregated with the disease. The mutation lies at an evolutionarily highly conserved position in the coiled-coil rod domain that is important for dimer formation. L345P desmin could not form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells. The authors concluded that the mutation interferes in a dominant-negative manner with the dimerization-polymerization process of intermediate filament assembly. ⚠︎

**.0006 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, IVS3DS, A-G, +3

In a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy, Park et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous de novo A-to-G change at the +3 position of the intron 3 splice donor site of the DES gene (IVS3+3A-G). They found it by sequencing cDNA and genomic DNA. Expression of genomic fragments carrying the mutation resulted in skipping of exon 3, an in-frame deletion of 32 codons predicted to remove 32 amino acids from the 1B segment of the alpha-helical rod. Mutant desmin expressed in vimentin-deficient SW13 cells formed abnormal coarse clumps of desmin-positive material throughout the cytoplasm. ⚠︎

Greenberg et al. (2012) sequenced a patient with LGMD1D/1E, a skeletal and cardiac myopathy previously linked to chromosome 6q23, together with family members, and identified the IVS3+3A-G splice donor site mutation in the DES gene. Laser capture microdissection proteomics showed that desmin was the major constituent of the cytoplasmic inclusions in the patient's skeletal muscle.

**.0007 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, IVS2AS, G-A, -1

In a patient with skeletal and cardiac myopathy, Park et al. (2000) identified a G-to-A transition at the -1 position of the intron 2 splice acceptor site of the DES gene (IVS2-1G-A). Expression of genomic DNA fragments carrying the mutation resulted in deletion of exon 3, an in-frame deletion of 32 codons. Mutant desmin expressed in vimentin-deficient SW13 cells formed abnormal coarse cytoplasmic aggregates. ⚠︎

**.0008 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 3-AA DEL, GLU359_SER361DEL [p.E359]

In patients with desmin-related myopathy, Kaminska et al. (2004) identified an in-frame deletion in the DES gene, E359_S361del, that changes the heptad periodicity of the 2B coiled-coil segment. Structural analysis showed that the deletion adds a second stutter immediately downstream of the naturally occurring stutter, doubling the extent of local coiled-coil unwinding. In 2 cell lines, the mutant desmin could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network.

**.0009 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 1-AA DEL, ASN366DEL [p.N366del]

In patients with desmin-related myopathy, Kaminska et al. (2004) identified an in-frame deletion in the DES gene, N366del. It changes the heptad periodicity of the 2B coiled-coil segment and turns the wildtype stutter into a stammer, which the authors expected to cause extra overwinding of the coiled coil. In 2 cell lines, the mutant desmin could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network.

**.0010 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, GLU413LYS [c.1237G>A; p.E413K]

Bär et al. (2007) studied a previously described c.1237G-A mutation in the tail domain of desmin, resulting in a glu413-to-lys (E413K) substitution. Unlike the other tail mutants tested, E413K desmin did not assemble into intermediate filaments in vitro and was not incorporated into filament arrays in transfected C2C12 myoblasts. Coassembly of tail mutants with wildtype desmin severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions. ⚠︎

**.0011 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, THR442ILE [c.1325C>T; p.T442I]

In patients with desmin-related myofibrillar myopathy, Bär et al. (2007) identified a c.1325C-T mutation in the non-alpha-helical carboxy-terminal tail domain of desmin, resulting in a thr442-to-ile (T442I) substitution. The mutant assembled into intermediate filaments in vitro and was incorporated into filament arrays in transfected C2C12 myoblasts. Viscometric assays, however, showed filament properties that differed significantly from wildtype, and coassembly with wildtype desmin severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions. ⚠︎

**.0012 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, LYS449THR [c.1346A>C; p.K449T]

Bär et al. (2007) studied a previously described c.1346A-C mutation in the tail domain of desmin, resulting in a lys449-to-thr (K449T) substitution. Its filament properties on viscometric assays, however, differed significantly from those of wildtype desmin, and coassembly with wildtype desmin severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions.

**.0013 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ILE451MET [c.1353C>G; p.I451M]

**.0014 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ARG454TRP [c.1360C>T; p.R454W]

In patients with desmin-related myofibrillar myopathy, Bär et al. (2007) identified a c.1360C-T mutation in the tail domain of desmin, resulting in an arg454-to-trp (R454W) substitution. The R454W mutant did not assemble into intermediate filaments in vitro, but it was incorporated into filament arrays in transfected C2C12 myoblasts. Coassembly of tail mutants with wildtype desmin severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions. ⚠︎

Otten et al. (2010) identified the R454W mutation in a family with severe biventricular cardiomyopathy. Immunohistochemistry of myocardial tissue from 2 mutation carriers showed desmin aggregates and decreased desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk.

**.0015 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, SER460ILE [c.1379G>T; p.S460I]

In patients with desmin-related myofibrillar myopathy, Bär et al. (2007) identified a c.1379G-T mutation in the tail domain of desmin, resulting in a ser460-to-ile (S460I) substitution. The mutant assembled into intermediate filaments in vitro and was incorporated into filament arrays in transfected C2C12 myoblasts. Viscometric assays, however, showed filament properties that differed significantly from wildtype, and coassembly with wildtype desmin severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions. ⚠︎

**.0016 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, VAL469MET [c.1405G>A; p.V469M]

Bär et al. (2007) studied a previously described c.1405G-A mutation in the tail domain of desmin, resulting in a val469-to-met (V469M) substitution. The mutant assembled into intermediate filaments in vitro and was incorporated into filament arrays in transfected C2C12 myoblasts. Its filament properties on viscometric assays, however, differed significantly from those of wildtype desmin, and coassembly with wildtype desmin severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions.

**.0017 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, SER13PHE [p.S13F]

In affected members of 2 distantly related Dutch families with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy, Bergman et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous ser13-to-phe (S13F) mutation in the head domain of desmin that cosegregated with the disease. The 15 affected family members ranged from isolated dilated cardiomyopathy to generalized skeletal myopathy.

Pica et al. (2008) found a heterozygous S13F mutation in a Chinese man with desmin myopathy and complete heart block, and in family members with a similar or milder phenotype. The mutation lies at a protein kinase C phosphorylation site within a highly conserved nonapeptide sequence in the head domain of desmin. When the mutant cDNA was expressed in cell lines, it induced large desmin accumulations while a filamentous network was preserved.

van Tintelen et al. (2009) identified S13F as a fully penetrant founder mutation in 27 carriers or obligate carriers from 5 families. All had cardiac involvement with high-grade AV block at young ages and important right ventricular involvement. Hearts of carriers had normal amounts of major desmosomal proteins, but the intercalated disks were more convoluted and elongated, with a zigzag appearance. ⚠︎

**.0018 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 21-BP DEL, ARG173_GLU179DEL [p.R173]

In a girl born to consanguineous (uncle-niece) parents, Piñol-Ripoll et al. (2009) identified a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in the DES gene. The patient had infantile-onset cardiomyopathy with arrhythmias, myofibrillar myopathy, and death from cardiac failure before age 21. The deletion was predicted to remove 7 amino acids (RARVDVE; R173_E179del), a full heptad of the 1B helix domain, in frame. Both parents were heterozygous and unaffected. The authors noted that the mutation prevents desmin filament assembly in transfected MCF-7 cells, and that the same homozygous deletion had previously been reported by Muñoz-Mármol et al. in a severely affected boy.

**.0019 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ASN342ASP [p.N342D]

Otten et al. (2010) identified an asn342-to-asp (N342D) mutation in the DES gene in a family with desmin-related myopathy in which 1 patient had an ARVC-like phenotype.

**.0020 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, PRO419SER [c.1255C>T; p.P419S]

In a Swedish family with autosomal dominant myofibrillar myopathy and ARVC, Hedberg et al. (2012) used exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.1255C-T transition in the DES gene, resulting in a pro419-to-ser (P419S) substitution. The family had previously shown linkage to chromosome 10q22.3. Sanger sequencing of 17 family members found the mutation in 7 of the 10 members who had been classified as affected in an earlier study.

**.0021 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 1289-2A-G [c.1289-2A>G]

In 2 members of a consanguineous family with autosomal recessive LGMD2, Cetin et al. (2013) identified a homozygous splice site mutation in the DES gene (c.1289-2A-G). The mutation added 16 amino acids to the desmin tail domain. Mutant desmin was still expressed and formed a cytoskeletal network in patient muscle fibers, but the interaction between desmin and lamin B was specifically disrupted. Patient muscle lacked the ultrastructural features of myofibrillar myopathy.

**.0022 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, LEU190PRO [p.L190P]

In 2 sisters with slowly progressive limb-girdle weakness, severe systolic dysfunction, and progressive respiratory weakness without myofibrillar aggregates, Riley et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygous variants in the DES gene. One was a leu190-to-pro (L190P) missense variant inherited from the father. The other was a deep intronic variant in intron 7 inherited from the mother. The father and brothers, who carried only L190P, were clinically unaffected. Because the maternal allele underwent strong nonsense-mediated decay, the desmin present in muscle, which was reduced by 60 to 75%, likely consisted only of the L190P mutant protein.

**.0023 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, IVS7, DEEP INTRONIC VARIANT

In 2 sisters with recessive DES cardio/myopathy, Riley et al. (2019) identified a maternally inherited deep intronic variant in intron 7 of the DES gene, in compound heterozygous state with L190P. RNA sequencing and analysis of muscle cDNA showed that the variant created aberrant splicing, inserting an artificial exon/intronic fragment that shifted the reading frame and created a premature termination codon. Nonsense-mediated decay was strong, so that 92% of DES transcripts were paternal and 8% maternal, and Western blot showed a 60 to 75% reduction in desmin.
