### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-1 (MFM1), also known as desmin-related myopathy, is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding desmin (DES) on chromosome 2q35. ⚠︎

### Description

Desmin-related myopathy is a familial disorder characterized by skeletal muscle weakness associated with cardiac conduction blocks, arrhythmias, and restrictive heart failure, and by intracytoplasmic accumulation of desmin-reactive deposits in cardiac and skeletal muscle cells (Goldfarb et al., 1998). van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) described it as an autosomally inherited skeletal and cardiac myopathy mainly caused by dominant mutations in the DES gene; Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) noted that autosomal recessive mutations are rare. Selcen et al. (2004) stated that the term myofibrillar myopathy (MFM) was proposed in 1996 as a noncommittal term for a pathologic pattern of myofibrillar dissolution with accumulation of myofibrillar degradation products and ectopic expression of multiple proteins, and that MFM is morphologically distinct but genetically heterogeneous.

### Nomenclature

Messina et al. (1997) designated the disorder they linked to chromosome 6q23 as familial dilated cardiomyopathy with conduction defect and muscular dystrophy (FDC-CDM). Greenberg et al. (2012) referred to this 6q23-linked disorder as limb-girdle muscular dystrophy 1D/1E (OMIM nomenclature LGMD1D; Human Gene Nomenclature Committee LGMD1E) and showed that it is caused by a DES mutation. Kuhl et al. (2008) referred to myofibrillar myopathy with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy in a Swedish family as ARVC7; Hedberg et al. (2012) showed that this disorder is also caused by a DES mutation. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Porte et al. (1980) reported 3 brothers with progressive myocardial deficiency. Cardiac muscle cells showed massive storage of PAS-negative proteinaceous material, which by electron microscopy consisted of sinuous filaments 7 to 10 nm in diameter, similar to intermediate filaments. Filament storage coincided with disintegration of neighboring myofibrils, with Z-band changes giving rise to rod-like bodies. Filament deposits were also observed in a few skeletal muscle fibers. The authors suggested that a disturbance in the metabolism of desmin might be involved.

Stoeckel et al. (1981) reported an unusual familial cardiomyopathy characterized by aberrant accumulations of desmin-type intermediate filaments.

Wolburg et al. (1982) reported 2 patients from unrelated families, both 32 years old, with congenital involvement of the skeletal muscles; one also had congenital paresis of the dilator muscle of the pupil and cardiomyopathy. Muscle biopsies showed inclusions consistent with cytoplasmic bodies in about a third and about a tenth of the fibers, respectively, with Z-disc streaming or disintegration and subsarcolemmal filamentous bodies.

Chapon et al. (1989) reported 3 patients, aged 72, 45, and 18 years, from 3 successive generations of a family, who presented with respiratory insufficiency. Onset was at about 40 years of age in the 2 older patients and at 18 years in the youngest. Serum muscle enzymes were normal. Muscle biopsy in all 3 showed numerous cytoplasmic (spheroid) bodies within type I fibers.

Abe et al. (1993) described a Japanese kindred with an autosomal dominant progressive myopathy with diffuse cytoplasmic bodies. Twelve patients in 4 successive generations had weakness and atrophy of muscles of the distal extremities, neck, thorax, and shoulder girdles. Mean age at onset was 42 years, and patients became disabled after 5 to 10 years due to chronic respiratory failure. Serum creatine kinase was normal or slightly elevated, and EMG showed predominantly myopathic changes with slight neurogenic change. Autopsy of 2 cases showed numerous cytoplasmic bodies, predominantly in type I fibers, and smooth and cardiac muscles were also affected. Males were affected more frequently than females (2:1).

Vajsar et al. (1993) reported 2 sibs who developed cardiomyopathy several years before slowly progressive muscle weakness. Skeletal muscle biopsies showed subsarcolemmal crescents of dark eosinophilic material in type I and type II fibers that stained for desmin and ubiquitin and ultrastructurally consisted of granular and filamentous aggregates arising from Z bands. Follow-up biopsies 6 years later showed an increased number of affected fibers.

Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994) described a large family with autosomal dominant distal myopathy beginning in early to middle adulthood with gait disturbance due to distal leg weakness and progressing over 5 to 10 years to involve all extremities as well as bulbar, respiratory, and facial muscles. Cardiac involvement, manifest as arrhythmias, conduction blocks, and congestive failure, was frequent and resulted in premature death. EMG showed prominent spontaneous activity, short-duration motor unit potentials, and polyphasia, and muscle biopsies showed desmin storage and autophagocytosis.

Ariza et al. (1995) studied a 28-year-old man with generalized muscular atrophy, cardiomyopathy, and intestinal malabsorption and pseudo-obstruction. Granular and filamentous aggregates with intense desmin immunostaining were present throughout the skeletal musculature, myocardium, and intestinal smooth muscle. Neurofilament-immunoreactive axonal spheroids were also identified in the spinal cord and roots.

Messina et al. (1997) characterized an autosomal dominant disorder involving dilated cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction-system disease, and adult-onset limb-girdle muscular dystrophy.

Melberg et al. (1999) examined 21 members of a Swedish family with myopathy and cardiomyopathy and reviewed the charts of 2 deceased affected members. Twelve patients had myopathy, with weakness that was axial in mildly affected, axial and predominantly distal in moderately affected, and generalized in severely affected patients. EMG showed myopathy in 10 patients. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic changes, rimmed vacuoles, and accumulation of desmin, dystrophin, and other proteins, and electron microscopy showed granulofilamentous changes and myofibrillar disorganization. Three men had arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, with nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, atrial flutter, and ventricular dilatation mainly affecting the right ventricle; 2 had pacemakers implanted because of atrioventricular block and sick sinus syndrome.

Selcen et al. (2004) reviewed 63 unrelated patients diagnosed with myofibrillar myopathy at the Mayo Clinic between 1977 and 2003. Age at onset was 54 +/- 16 years. Weakness was both proximal and distal in 77% and proximal only in 13%, and cardiomyopathy was diagnosed in 16%. EMG showed a myopathic pattern with abnormal electrical irritability. Abnormal fibers contained amorphous, granular, or pleomorphic hyaline structures, which were strongly congophilic, and vacuoles containing membranous material; electron microscopy showed myofibrillar degeneration commencing at the Z disc.

Bergman et al. (2007) reported 2 distantly related Dutch families with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy affecting 15 family members. The clinical picture was highly heterogeneous, varying from isolated dilated cardiomyopathy to a more generalized skeletal myopathy and mild respiratory problems. Muscle biopsies showed intracytoplasmic aggregates staining for desmin and p62.

Pica et al. (2008) reported a Chinese man with complete heart block and mild proximal and distal limb weakness; family members carrying the same DES mutation showed a similar or milder phenotype.

Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) reported a girl, the only child of consanguineous (uncle-niece) parents, who had recurrent syncope from 6 months of age, initially attributed to Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. At age 15 years she had bilateral facial weakness, ptosis, symmetric scapular winging, atrophy of masseter, temporalis, sternocleidomastoid, and trunk muscles, pes cavus, and mild weakness of the shoulder girdle and distal muscles. Creatine kinase was twice normal, and EMG showed complex repetitive discharges, some myotonic discharges, and a myopathic recruitment pattern. Holter monitoring showed ventricular tachycardia, QT prolongation, and second-degree AV block, and a defibrillator and pacemaker were implanted. At age 18 years she had episodes of ventricular tachyarrhythmia, and echocardiography indicated restrictive cardiomyopathy. Diarrhea, vomiting, and weight loss suggested intestinal smooth muscle involvement, and she died from cardiac failure before her 21st birthday. More than 90% of muscle fibers contained subsarcolemmal desmin-immunoreactive, Congo red-positive deposits, and electron microscopy showed granulofilamentous material. ⚠︎

van Tintelen et al. (2009) studied 27 carriers or obligate carriers of the same DES mutation from 5 families. The phenotype was fully penetrant but variable. All patients had cardiac involvement characterized by high-grade AV block at young ages and important right ventricular involvement: right bundle branch block was present in 10 patients and right ventricular heart failure in 6, including 2 who fulfilled diagnostic criteria for arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy.

Otten et al. (2010) evaluated 2 families with different DES mutations: one family had desmin-related myopathy with an arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy-like phenotype in 1 patient, and the other presented with severe biventricular cardiomyopathy. They concluded that an ARVC-like phenotype or severe cardiomyopathy with right ventricular involvement are possible but infrequent cardiac phenotypes of desmin-related myopathy.

In a meta-analysis of 159 DES mutation carriers with 40 different mutations, van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found neurologic signs in 74% and cardiologic signs in 74% (both in 49%, isolated neurologic signs in 22%, and isolated cardiologic signs in 22%). More than 70% had myopathy or muscle weakness, with normal creatine kinase in one-third of them. Up to 50% had cardiomyopathy and around 60% had cardiac conduction disease or arrhythmias, with atrioventricular block as an important hallmark. Symptoms generally began in the thirties, and a quarter of carriers died at a mean age of 49 years. Sudden cardiac death occurred in 2 patients with a pacemaker, suggesting ventricular tachyarrhythmia as the cause.

Gudkova et al. (2013) described a patient with desmin cardiomyopathy with early manifestation in adolescence and transformation of several clinical phenotypes over time, which made correct clinical diagnosis difficult at an early stage.

Cetin et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous family with autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy in 2 affected individuals. Unlike autosomal dominant desminopathies, ultrastructural alterations such as myofibrillar disorganization, myofibrillar degradation products, and aggregation of membranous organelles were not present, and the authors concluded that their patients had an LGMD2 phenotype without features of myofibrillar myopathy. ⚠︎

Ojrzynska et al. (2017) reported restrictive cardiomyopathy due to a novel desmin gene mutation.

Riley et al. (2019) reported 2 sisters who presented in their twenties with slowly progressive limb-girdle weakness, severe systolic dysfunction, and progressive, severe respiratory weakness. Muscle biopsy showed fiber size variation with increased central nuclei, and electron microscopy showed extensive myofibrillar disarray and duplication of the basal lamina but no inclusions or aggregates.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance was reported in the families described by Abe et al. (1993), Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994), Messina et al. (1997), Melberg et al. (1999), Sjoberg et al. (1999), and Bergman et al. (2007). Melberg et al. (1999) noted variable onset and severity of skeletal muscle and cardiac involvement. Park et al. (2000) found a de novo mutation in a patient with sporadic disease.

Autosomal recessive inheritance was demonstrated by compound heterozygosity in a family reported by Goldfarb et al. (1998) and by homozygous mutations in patients from consanguineous families reported by Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) and Cetin et al. (2013). Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) stated that inheritance was autosomal recessive in about 6% of patients with desminopathy and that such patients develop disease in childhood. Riley et al. (2019) reported autosomal recessive inheritance in sibs with compound heterozygous DES variants. ⚠︎

### Mapping

In a family with dilated cardiomyopathy, conduction-system disease, and limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, Messina et al. (1997) performed a genomewide scan and found significant linkage to chromosome 6q (maximum lod score of 4.99 at theta = 0.00 for D6S262). Haplotype analysis refined the interval to 3 cM between D6S1705 and D6S1656, excluding several striated muscle-expressed genes in the region. Greenberg et al. (2012) later found that the disorder in this 6q23-linked family was due to a DES mutation (see MOLECULAR GENETICS). ⚠︎

In a Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, Melberg et al. (1999) found a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.76 for marker D10S1752 and a multipoint peak lod score of 3.06 between D10S605 and D10S215, suggesting linkage to chromosome 10q22.3. Using a high-density SNP panel, Kuhl et al. (2008) confirmed the linkage, found no other linked regions, and narrowed the critical interval to 4.27 Mbp between D10S1645 and D10S1786, containing 18 candidate genes; no disease-causing mutation was found in their coding regions, and VCL and LDB3 were also excluded. Hedberg et al. (2012) subsequently found a DES mutation in this family (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

Saavedra-Matiz et al. (2000) mapped the gene causing autosomal dominant hereditary distal myopathy with desmin accumulation in a large family to chromosome 2q33, within a 17-cM segment bounded by D2S2248 and D2S401, and considered desmin the best candidate gene.

### Heterogeneity

Goldfarb et al. (1998) noted that involvement of the DES gene had been excluded in 3 families diagnosed with desmin-related myopathy. Selcen et al. (2004) found truncating mutations in the C-terminal domain of alpha-B-crystallin (CRYAB) in 2 of 63 patients with myofibrillar myopathy and desmin mutations in 4, and concluded that MFM is genetically heterogeneous. Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) stated that myofibrillar myopathy is caused by mutations in the DES, CRYAB, MYOT, ZASP, FLNC, and BAG3 genes.

Ferreiro et al. (2004) found that the early-onset recessive form of desmin-related myopathy with Mallory body-like inclusions, originally described in 5 related German patients, was caused by a homozygous deletion in the SEPN1 gene, and proposed that it be categorized as SEPN1-related myopathy.

### Molecular Genetics

Goldfarb et al. (1998) identified mutations in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain in 2 families with desmin-related cardioskeletal myopathy. A heterozygous A337P mutation was found in a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement. Compound heterozygosity for A360P and N393I mutations was found in a second family characterized by childhood onset and an aggressive course of cardiac and skeletal myopathy.

In a patient with severe generalized myopathy and aberrant intrasarcoplasmic accumulation of desmin, Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) found that muscle tissue lacked the wild-type desmin allele and carried a DES mutation encoding a 7-amino acid deletion within the coiled-coil segment. Recombinant mutant desmin could not form proper filament networks in cultured cells or assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro.

In a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant distal desmin-related myopathy, Sjoberg et al. (1999) identified a L345P mutation in the desmin coiled-coil rod domain that cosegregated with the disorder. The mutant protein was unable to form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells, and the authors concluded that it acts in a dominant-negative manner on intermediate filament assembly.

Park et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous de novo IVS3+3A-G mutation in the DES gene in a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy, and an IVS2-1G-A mutation in an unrelated patient with a similar phenotype. Expression studies showed that both mutations caused deletion of exon 3, leading to in-frame loss of 32 amino acids from the 1B segment of the rod domain; mutant desmin formed coarse clumps of desmin-positive material in SW13 cells.

Kaminska et al. (2004) studied 3 families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy caused by small in-frame deletions in the DES gene, including the newly identified E359_S361del and N366del, which alter heptad periodicity in the 2B coiled-coil segment. Expression studies in 2 cell lines showed that the mutant proteins could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network.

Among 63 unrelated patients with myofibrillar myopathy, Selcen et al. (2004) found 4 who carried missense mutations in the head or tail region of desmin; none carried mutations in syncoilin or telethonin.

Bar et al. (2007) reported 3 novel mutations in the non-alpha-helical carboxy-terminal tail domain of desmin, c.1325C-T (T442I), c.1360C-T (R454W), and c.1379G-T (S460I), and studied these with 4 previously described tail mutations. All but 2 mutants (E413K and R454W) assembled into intermediate filaments in vitro, but when coassembled with wildtype desmin the mutants severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions.

In 2 distantly related Dutch families with desmin-related myopathy, Bergman et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous S13F missense mutation in the head domain of desmin that cosegregated with the disease.

Pica et al. (2008) identified a heterozygous S13F mutation in the DES gene in a Chinese man with desmin myopathy and complete heart block; family members carrying the mutation had a similar or milder phenotype. The mutation is located at a protein kinase C phosphorylation site in the head domain, and expression of mutant desmin in cell lines induced large desmin accumulations with preservation of a filamentous network.

In the girl with infantile-onset cardiomyopathy and myopathy born to consanguineous parents, Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) identified a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in the DES gene, predicting in-frame loss of 7 amino acids (RARVDVE) from arg173 through glu179 (R173_E179del) in the 1B helix domain. Each parent was heterozygous for the mutation and unaffected. The authors noted that an identical homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion had been reported by Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998).

van Tintelen et al. (2009) described 27 carriers or obligate carriers of the S13F DES founder mutation from 5 families.

Otten et al. (2010) identified the DES mutations N342D and R454W in a family with desmin-related myopathy and a family with severe biventricular cardiomyopathy, respectively. Screening of the DES gene in 50 patients with ARVC or ARVC-like phenotypes identified no additional mutations.

Using laser capture microdissection and mass spectrometry-based proteomics of cytoplasmic inclusions from skeletal muscle of a patient with LGMD1D/1E, Greenberg et al. (2012) identified desmin as the major constituent. Sequencing in the patient and family members identified an IVS3+3A-G splice donor site mutation in the DES gene as the cause of the previously reported 6q23-linked disorder (Messina et al., 1997). ⚠︎

By exome sequencing in the Swedish family with autosomal dominant myofibrillar myopathy with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy previously linked to 10q22.3 (Melberg et al., 1999), Hedberg et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.1255C-T transition in the DES gene, resulting in a pro419-to-ser (P419S) substitution. Sanger sequencing of 17 family members confirmed the mutation in 7 of the 10 members who had been classified as affected in the previous study.

In a consanguineous family with autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, Cetin et al. (2013) used genomewide homozygosity mapping to map the disorder to chromosome 2q35-q36.3 and identified a homozygous splice site mutation (c.1289-2A-G) in the DES gene in the 2 affected members. The mutation results in addition of 16 amino acids within the tail domain; expression and cytoskeletal network formation of mutant desmin were preserved in skeletal muscle, but desmin-lamin B interaction was specifically disrupted.

In 2 sisters with myopathy and cardiac involvement, Riley et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygous DES variants: a paternal missense L190P variant and a maternal deep intronic variant in intron 7 predicted to create a cryptic splice site, which segregated with disease. The father and brothers carrying L190P were clinically unaffected. RNA sequencing and muscle cDNA studies showed that the intronic variant caused aberrant inclusion of an artificial exon/intronic fragment encoding a premature termination codon, with nonsense-mediated decay (92% paternal and 8% maternal DES transcripts). Western blot showed a 60 to 75% reduction in desmin levels. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In their meta-analysis of DES mutation carriers, van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found that most DES mutations were missense mutations located mainly in the 2B domain. Mutations in the 2B domain predominated in patients with an isolated neurologic phenotype, whereas head and tail domain mutations predominated in patients with an isolated cardiologic phenotype.

van Tintelen et al. (2009) found that patients with the head domain S13F mutation showed a variable but predominantly cardiologic phenotype, characterized by conduction disease at an early age and right ventricular involvement.

### Pathogenesis

Goebel (1995) noted that focal accretion of desmin may occur in conjunction with cytoplasmic and spheroid bodies or, more patchily, as granulofilamentous material, and that both lesions had been found in families with myopathy and/or cardiomyopathy.

In myocardium from patients with the S13F mutation, van Tintelen et al. (2009) found normal amounts of the major desmosomal proteins, but intercalated disks were more convoluted and elongated with a zigzag appearance. Otten et al. (2010) found decreased desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk in R454W mutation carriers, suggesting a link between desmosomal cardiomyopathies and those caused by DES mutations.

### Diagnosis

Selcen et al. (2004) found that among abnormal fibers in myofibrillar myopathy, an average of 90, 75, 75, 70, and 70% abnormally expressed myotilin, desmin, alpha-B-crystallin, dystrophin, and beta-amyloid precursor protein, respectively, and suggested that immunostains for these proteins, especially myotilin, are useful adjuncts in diagnosis. Bergman et al. (2007) emphasized the importance of analyzing the DES gene in patients with familial cardiac conduction disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, and/or progressive skeletal myopathy resembling limb-girdle muscular dystrophy.

### Clinical Management

Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) concluded that patients with desmin-related cardiomyopathy are at high risk of ventricular arrhythmia and should be considered candidates for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation even before ventricular dysfunction becomes evident.

### Animal Model

Shelton et al. (2004) reported a 1-year-old male Australian Shepherd dog with chronic lameness, contractures, exercise intolerance, elevated serum creatine kinase, complex repetitive discharges on EMG, and cardiomyopathy. Muscle biopsies showed myofibrillar myopathy with accumulation of desmin and other proteins.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ALA337PRO [A337P]

In a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement, Goldfarb et al. (1998) identified a heterozygous ala337-to-pro (A337P) mutation in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain.

**.0002 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ALA360PRO [A360P]

In a family with childhood-onset, aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, Goldfarb et al. (1998) found compound heterozygosity for an ala360-to-pro (A360P) mutation and an asn393-to-ile (N393I) mutation in the DES gene.

**.0003 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ASN393ILE [N393I]

For discussion of the asn393-to-ile (N393I) mutation in the DES gene, found in compound heterozygous state with A360P in a family with childhood-onset, aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, see Goldfarb et al. (1998).

**.0004 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 21-BP DEL, ARG173_GLU179DEL [p.R173_E179del; p.Arg173_Glu179del]

In a patient with severe generalized myopathy, Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) found that muscle tissue lacked the wild-type desmin allele and carried a DES mutation encoding a 7-amino acid deletion within the coiled-coil segment; the mutant protein could not form proper filament networks in cultured cells or assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro.

In a girl, born to consanguineous parents, with infantile-onset arrhythmia, restrictive cardiomyopathy, and progressive skeletal and smooth muscle involvement who died before age 21 years, Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) identified a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in the DES gene predicting in-frame loss of 7 amino acids from arg173 through glu179 (R173_E179del) in the 1B domain. Both unaffected parents were heterozygous. The authors stated that the same homozygous deletion had been reported by Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998).

**.0005 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, LEU345PRO [L345P]

In a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant distal desmin-related myopathy, Sjoberg et al. (1999) identified a leu345-to-pro (L345P) mutation in the desmin coiled-coil rod domain that cosegregated with disease. The mutant could not form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells and was thought to act in a dominant-negative manner.

**.0006 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, IVS3DS, A-G, +3 [IVS3+3A>G]

Park et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous de novo A-to-G change at position +3 of the splice donor site of intron 3 (IVS3+3A-G) in a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy. Expression studies showed that the mutation caused deletion of exon 3, resulting in in-frame loss of 32 amino acids from the 1B segment.

Greenberg et al. (2012) identified the IVS3+3A-G mutation in the DES gene as the cause of LGMD1D/1E in the family previously linked to chromosome 6q23.

**.0007 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, IVS2AS, G-A, -1 [IVS2-1G>A]

Park et al. (2000) identified a G-to-A transition at the -1 position of intron 2 (IVS2-1G-A), affecting the splice acceptor site, in a patient with skeletal and cardiac myopathy. Expression of genomic fragments confirmed that the mutation causes deletion of exon 3.

**.0008 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 3-AA DEL, GLU359_SER361DEL [E359_S361del]

Kaminska et al. (2004) identified an in-frame deletion, E359_S361del, in the DES gene in families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy. Structural analysis indicated that the deletion introduces a second stutter in the 2B coiled-coil segment, and expression studies showed failure to polymerize into a functional filamentous network. ⚠︎

**.0009 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, 1-AA DEL, ASN366DEL [N366del]

Kaminska et al. (2004) identified an in-frame deletion, N366del, in the DES gene in families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy. The deletion converts the wildtype stutter into a stammer in the 2B coiled-coil segment, and the mutant could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network. ⚠︎

**.0010 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, ARG454TRP [c.1360C>T; p.R454W]

Bar et al. (2007) reported a novel c.1360C-T mutation in the tail domain of desmin, resulting in an arg454-to-trp (R454W) substitution. The mutant did not assemble into intermediate filaments in vitro.

Otten et al. (2010) identified the R454W mutation in a family with severe biventricular cardiomyopathy; myocardium from mutation carriers showed desmin aggregates and decreased desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk.

**.0011 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1**  
DES, SER13PHE [S13F; p.S13F]

In 2 distantly related Dutch families with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy affecting 15 members, Bergman et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous ser13-to-phe (S13F) mutation in the head domain of desmin that cosegregated with disease.

Pica et al. (2008) identified a heterozygous S13F mutation in a Chinese man with desmin myopathy and complete heart block; family members carrying the mutation had a similar or milder phenotype.

**.0015 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY 1, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**
