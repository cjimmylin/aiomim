### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-1 (MFM1) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the desmin gene (DES; 125660) on chromosome 2q35.

### Description

Goldfarb et al. (1998) described desmin-related myopathy as a familial disorder in which skeletal muscle weakness occurs with cardiac conduction blocks, arrhythmias, and restrictive heart failure, and with intracytoplasmic accumulation of desmin-reactive deposits in cardiac and skeletal muscle cells. Vajsar et al. (1993) associated this rare familial myopathy with an unusual distribution of desmin intermediate filament proteins in skeletal muscle and probably also in cardiac muscle. van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) described desmin-related myopathy as an autosomally inherited skeletal and cardiac myopathy, caused mainly by dominant mutations in the DES gene.

### Nomenclature

Greenberg et al. (2012) noted that the disorder they studied was designated limb-girdle muscular dystrophy 1D (LGMD1D) in OMIM nomenclature and LGMD1E by the Human Gene Nomenclature Committee. Straub et al. (2018) reported the results of the 229th ENMC international workshop, held in Naarden, the Netherlands, in March 2017, on the nomenclature and reformed classification of the limb-girdle muscular dystrophies.

### Clinical Features

Porte et al. (1980) reported 3 brothers with progressive myocardial deficiency. Cardiac muscle cells stored PAS-negative proteinaceous material. On electron microscopy this material consisted of sinuous filaments, 7 to 10 nm in diameter, similar to the intermediate filaments normally present in cardiac muscle cells. Cardiac muscle cells also showed myofibrillar disintegration with Z-band changes and rod-like bodies, sarcoplasmic reticulum proliferation, and glycogen accumulation. Discrete sarcoplasmic reticulum proliferation, glycogen overload, and filament deposits were seen in a few skeletal muscle fibers.

Stoeckel et al. (1981) reported a family with an unusual cardiomyopathy characterized by aberrant accumulations of desmin-type intermediate filaments.

Wolburg et al. (1982) reported 2 patients, both 32 years old, with a congenital disorder of the skeletal muscles. One patient (case 2) also had congenital paresis of the dilator muscle of the pupil and cardiomyopathy. Muscle biopsies showed variation in fiber caliber, with grouped atrophic fibers in case 1. Cytoplasmic bodies were present in about a third of fibers in case 1 and about a tenth of fibers in case 2, together with Z-disk streaming and subsarcolemmal filamentous bodies. The inclusions consisted of dense granular and fine filamentous components consistent with cytoplasmic bodies. The authors noted that cytoplasmic bodies are a nonspecific type of inclusion but were the outstanding structural anomaly in both patients. The course ended in severe impairment of skeletal muscle function in case 1 but not in case 2. ⚠︎

Chapon et al. (1989) reported 3 patients from 3 successive generations of a family, aged 72, 45, and 18 years, who presented with respiratory insufficiency. Onset was at about 40 years of age in the 2 older patients and at 18 years in the youngest. Serum enzymes of muscle origin and leukocyte maltase activity were normal. Muscle biopsy in all 3 patients showed numerous cytoplasmic (spheroid) bodies within type I fibers. The authors noted that familial cytoplasmic body myopathy had rarely been reported, and even more rarely had it been revealed by respiratory insufficiency in adult life.

Abe et al. (1993) described a Japanese kindred with an autosomal dominant progressive myopathy with diffuse cytoplasmic bodies. Across 4 successive generations, 12 patients had weakness and atrophy of the muscles of the distal extremities, neck, thorax, and shoulder girdles. Mean age at onset was 42 years, and patients became disabled 5 to 10 years after onset because of chronic respiratory failure. Males were affected about twice as often as females. Serum creatine kinase was normal or slightly elevated, and electromyography was myopathic with slight neurogenic change. Autopsy of 2 patients showed numerous cytoplasmic bodies in skeletal muscle, predominantly in type I fibers, and smooth and cardiac muscle were also affected. Electron microscopy showed dense central cores of myofilaments with radiating filaments.

Vajsar et al. (1993) reported 2 sibs who developed cardiomyopathy several years before slowly progressive muscle weakness. Skeletal muscle biopsies showed subsarcolemmal crescents of dark eosinophilic material in both type I and type II fibers. This material stained for desmin and ubiquitin but for no other cytoskeletal proteins. Ultrastructurally it consisted of aggregates of granular and filamentous material arising from Z bands. Follow-up biopsies 6 years later showed more fibers containing these aggregates, indicating that the pathology progressed over time.

Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994) described a large family with autosomal dominant distal myopathy with desmin storage. The disease began in early to middle adulthood with gait disturbance due to distal leg weakness. Over 5 to 10 years it progressed to involve all extremities and the bulbar, respiratory, and facial muscles. Cardiac involvement was frequent, with arrhythmias, conduction blocks, and congestive heart failure, and led to premature death. Muscle biopsies from multiple family members at different stages of disease showed desmin storage and autophagocytosis. Electromyography showed prominent spontaneous activity, short-duration motor unit potentials, and polyphasia.

Ariza et al. (1995) reported a 28-year-old man with generalized muscular atrophy, cardiomyopathy, intestinal malabsorption, and intestinal pseudo-obstruction. Abundant sarcoplasmic granular and filamentous aggregates that were continuous with Z lines or dense bodies and stained intensely for desmin were present throughout the skeletal musculature, the myocardium, and the smooth muscle of the intestine. Neurofilament-immunoreactive axonal spheroids were also found in the spinal cord and roots. The authors concluded that desmin myopathy is a multisystem disorder. They described intestinal smooth muscle involvement for the first time and considered the coexisting neurofilament aggregates an extremely rare combination of intermediate filament pathology in the neuromuscular system.

Messina et al. (1997) characterized a kindred with an autosomal dominant disorder involving dilated cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction-system disease, and adult-onset limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (FDC-CDM). ⚠︎

Goldfarb et al. (1998) reported a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement, and a second family with a childhood-onset, aggressive course of cardiac and skeletal myopathy.

Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) reported a patient with severe generalized myopathy and aberrant intrasarcoplasmic accumulation of desmin intermediate filaments.

Melberg et al. (1999) investigated 21 members of a Swedish family with myopathy and cardiomyopathy. Twelve patients had myopathy, and electromyography was myopathic in 10. Weakness was axial in mildly affected patients, axial and mainly distal in moderately affected patients, and generalized in severely affected patients. Muscle biopsies showed rimmed vacuoles and accumulation of desmin, dystrophin, and other proteins, and electron microscopy showed granulofilamentous changes and myofibrillar disorganization. Some patients had episodes of chest pain and palpitations. Three men had arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, with nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, atrial flutter, and dilatation of the ventricles mainly affecting the right ventricle. Two of the 3 had atrioventricular block requiring a pacemaker and 2 had sick sinus syndrome. ⚠︎

Saavedra-Matiz et al. (2000) studied a large family with autosomal dominant hereditary distal myopathy. The slowly progressive myopathy began in early adulthood in the distal leg muscles and caused a gait disturbance. Most affected family members also had cardiomyopathy, manifesting as conduction block or congestive heart failure. Histologically, desmin accumulated in the subsarcolemmal spaces of myofibers.

Park et al. (2000) reported a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy and an unrelated patient with a similar phenotype.

Kaminska et al. (2004) studied 3 families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy.

Selcen et al. (2004) reviewed 63 unrelated patients diagnosed with myofibrillar myopathy at the Mayo Clinic between 1977 and 2003, 4 of whom carried desmin mutations. In the whole series, mean age at onset was 54 +/- 16 years. Weakness was both proximal and distal in 77% of patients and proximal only in 13%, and cardiomyopathy was diagnosed in 16%. Electromyography showed a myopathic pattern with abnormal electrical irritability.

Bergman et al. (2007) reported 2 distantly related Dutch families with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy affecting 15 members. The clinical picture was highly heterogeneous, ranging from isolated dilated cardiomyopathy to a more generalized skeletal myopathy with mild respiratory problems. Muscle biopsies showed intracytoplasmic aggregates that stained for desmin and p62.

Pica et al. (2008) reported a Chinese man with desmin myopathy who had complete heart block and mild proximal and distal limb weakness. Family members carrying the same mutation had a similar or milder phenotype.

Kuhl et al. (2008) re-examined the Swedish family with autosomal dominant myofibrillar myopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC7) originally reported by Melberg et al. (1999).

Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) reported a girl, the only child of consanguineous parents (uncle and niece), who had recurrent syncope from the age of 6 months. The episodes were first attributed to a right posteroseptal accessory conduction pathway and later diagnosed as Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. She also had cyanotic attacks. On examination she had a long narrow face, bilateral facial weakness, ptosis, scapular winging, and atrophy of the masseter, temporalis, sternocleidomastoid, and trunk muscles. She also had pes cavus, shoulder girdle weakness, distal weakness of the upper and lower extremities, and hypoactive deep tendon reflexes. Sensation was normal and there was no clinical myotonia. Creatine kinase was twice normal. Electromyography showed low-amplitude motor potentials, complex repetitive and myotonic discharges, and a myopathic pattern. Cardiac findings included monomorphic and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, QT prolongation, second-degree AV block, ventricular fibrillation, mild left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF 45%), right ventricular dilatation, marked atrial enlargement, and restrictive cardiomyopathy. Diarrhea, vomiting, and weight loss suggested involvement of intestinal smooth muscle. Brain MRI was normal. Muscle biopsy showed variation in fiber size, subsarcolemmal desmin-reactive aggregates, and rimmed vacuoles, with granulofilamentous material on electron microscopy. She died of cardiac failure. The authors considered this the first reported case of infantile-onset desmin-associated cardiomyopathy. ⚠︎

van Tintelen et al. (2009) studied 27 patients from 5 families who all carried the same desmin head-domain mutation. All 27 carriers or obligate carriers were affected, with a fully penetrant but variable phenotype that was mainly cardiac. Features included high-grade atrioventricular block at a young age and right ventricular predominance. Ten patients had right bundle branch block, sometimes as the first manifestation. Six had right ventricular heart failure, including 2 who fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, and some had right ventricular tachycardias.

Otten et al. (2010) reported 2 families. One was diagnosed with desmin-related myopathy, and one patient in that family had an ARVC-like phenotype. The other family presented with a severe biventricular cardiomyopathy with desmin aggregates in the myocardium.

In a meta-analysis of 159 reported carriers of 40 different DES mutations, van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found neurologic signs in 74% and cardiologic signs in 74%. Both types of signs occurred in 49% of carriers, isolated neurologic signs in 22%, and isolated cardiologic signs in 22%. More than 70% had myopathy or muscle weakness, and one-third of these had normal creatine kinase levels. Up to 50% of carriers had cardiomyopathy and about 60% had cardiac conduction disease or arrhythmias, with atrioventricular block as an important hallmark. Symptoms generally began in the thirties, and a quarter of carriers died, at a mean age of 49 years. Two patients with pacemakers died suddenly, suggesting ventricular tachyarrhythmia as the cause of death.

Gudkova et al. (2013) noted that desmin cardiomyopathy can produce a range of clinical phenotypes, including hypertrophic, restrictive, and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. They described a patient whose desmin cardiomyopathy began in adolescence and changed through several clinical phenotypes over time, which made early diagnosis and treatment difficult.

Cetin et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous family with 2 members affected by an LGMD2 phenotype of progressive proximal muscle weakness of the shoulder and pelvic girdles. Unlike in autosomal dominant desminopathies, there was no ultrastructural disruption of myofibrillar organization, no formation of myofibrillar degradation products, and no dislocation or aggregation of membranous organelles. ⚠︎

Riley et al. (2019) reported 2 sisters who presented in their twenties with slowly progressive limb-girdle weakness, severe systolic dysfunction, and progressive, severe respiratory weakness. Muscle biopsy showed fiber size variation with increased central nuclei. Electron microscopy showed extensive myofibrillar disarray and duplication of the basal lamina but no inclusions or aggregates.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance was reported in a Japanese kindred by Abe et al. (1993) and in a large family by Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994). Melberg et al. (1999) concluded that the disorder in a Swedish family was inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, with variable onset and severity of skeletal muscle and cardiac involvement. Bergman et al. (2007) reported autosomal dominant inheritance in 2 Dutch families, in which a heterozygous DES mutation cosegregated with the disease.

Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) reported a patient with a homozygous DES mutation whose consanguineous parents were heterozygous and unaffected. They considered her the first case exhaustively proven to be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Autosomal recessive inheritance was also reported by Cetin et al. (2013) in a consanguineous family with a homozygous DES mutation and by Riley et al. (2019) in sisters who were compound heterozygous for DES variants. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Messina et al. (1997) performed a genomewide scan in a family with autosomal dominant dilated cardiomyopathy, conduction-system disease, and adult-onset limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (FDC-CDM). They found linkage of the myopathy to chromosome 6q, with a maximum lod score of 4.99 at theta = 0.00 for D6S262. Haplotype analysis narrowed the region to a 3-cM interval between D6S1705 and D6S1656 and excluded the striated muscle-expressed candidate genes laminin alpha-2, laminin alpha-4, triadin, and phospholamban. ⚠︎

In a Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, Melberg et al. (1999) found a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.76 at marker D10S1752 and a multipoint peak lod score of 3.06 between D10S605 and D10S215. These results suggested linkage to chromosome 10q22.3. Kuhl et al. (2008) re-examined this family with a high-density SNP array and confirmed linkage to 10q22.3, with no other region showing significant linkage. They narrowed the critical interval to 4.27 Mbp between D10S1645 and D10S1786, a region containing 18 candidate genes, 17 of them shared with the CMD1C region.

By linkage analysis in a large family with autosomal dominant distal myopathy with desmin accumulation and cardiomyopathy, Saavedra-Matiz et al. (2000) mapped the disease gene to chromosome 2q33, within a 17-cM segment bounded by markers D2S2248 and D2S401. They considered desmin the best candidate gene.

Greenberg et al. (2012) found that the LGMD1D/1E family previously linked to chromosome 6q23 carried a splice donor site mutation in the desmin gene on chromosome 2q35, which reassigned the genetic basis of that disorder. Hedberg et al. (2012) noted that earlier studies of the Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and ARVC had shown linkage to 10q22.3 without finding a causative mutation there. Exome sequencing instead identified a mutation in the desmin gene on 2q35.

By genomewide homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous family with autosomal recessive LGMD, Cetin et al. (2013) mapped the phenotype to chromosome 2q35-q36.3.

### Heterogeneity

Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994) concluded that the distal myopathy in their family differed from other phenotypically similar distal myopathies in its rapid progression, fatal course, and pathologic features. They stated that the role and specificity of excessive desmin accumulation in this and other myopathic and cardiac disorders were unknown.

Messina et al. (1997) used linkage analysis to exclude, in their family, the genomic regions already known to be linked to dilated cardiomyopathy and muscular dystrophy phenotypes, and concluded that these disorders are genetically heterogeneous.

Goldfarb et al. (1998) noted that involvement of the DES gene had been excluded in 3 families diagnosed with desmin-related myopathy.

Selcen et al. (2004) screened 63 unrelated patients with myofibrillar myopathy for mutations in the desmin, alphaB-crystallin, telethonin, and syncoilin genes. Four patients had missense mutations in the head or tail region of desmin and 2 had truncating mutations in the C-terminal domain of alphaB-crystallin. None had a mutation in syncoilin or telethonin. The authors concluded that myofibrillar myopathy is morphologically distinct but genetically heterogeneous.

Ferreiro et al. (2004) noted that mutations in the desmin and alpha-B crystallin genes account for about one-third of cases of desmin-related myopathy. In the original German family with early-onset recessive desmin-related myopathy with Mallory body-like inclusions, they found linkage to the SEPN1 locus on 1p36 and a homozygous 92-nucleotide deletion in SEPN1. This showed that desmin-related myopathy is molecularly heterogeneous.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement, Goldfarb et al. (1998) identified a heterozygous A337P mutation in the DES gene. In a second family with a childhood-onset, aggressive course of cardiac and skeletal myopathy, they found compound heterozygosity for 2 other DES mutations, A360P and N393I. The mutations lay in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain.

In a patient with severe generalized myopathy, Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) identified a DES mutation encoding a 7-amino acid deletion within the coiled-coil segment of the protein. No wild-type desmin allele was found in the patient's muscle tissue. Recombinant mutant desmin could not form proper desmin intermediate filament networks in cultured cells and could not assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro. The authors concluded that this was direct evidence that a desmin mutation can cause human myopathies.

In a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant distal myopathy, Sjoberg et al. (1999) identified a heterozygous L345P missense mutation in the DES gene that cosegregated with the disease. They described it as the first point mutation in desmin found to cosegregate with an autosomal dominant form of desmin-related myopathy. The mutation lay at an evolutionarily highly conserved position of the coiled-coil rod domain that is important for dimer formation. ⚠︎

By sequencing cDNA and genomic DNA, Park et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous de novo A-to-G change at the +3 position of the intron 3 splice donor site of the DES gene (IVS3+3A-G) in a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy. In an unrelated patient with a similar phenotype, they found a G-to-A transition at the -1 position of intron 2 affecting the splice acceptor site (IVS2-1G-A). Expression of genomic DNA fragments showed that both mutations caused skipping of exon 3. This resulted in an in-frame deletion of 32 codons, predicted to remove 32 amino acids from the 1B segment of the alpha-helical rod.

In 3 families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy, Kaminska et al. (2004) identified small in-frame deletions in the DES gene, including the newly identified E359_S361del and N366del, which alter heptad periodicity within the 2B coiled-coil segment. Structural analysis showed that the E359_S361 deletion introduced a second stutter immediately downstream of the naturally occurring stutter, doubling the extent of local coiled-coil unwinding. The N366del mutation converted the wildtype stutter into a stammer, which was expected to cause extra overwinding of the coiled coil.

Among 63 unrelated patients with myofibrillar myopathy, Selcen et al. (2004) found 4 with missense mutations in the head or tail region of desmin.

Bar et al. (2007) reported 3 novel mutations in the non-alpha-helical carboxy-terminal tail domain of desmin in patients with myopathies: T442I, R454W, and S460I. ⚠︎

In 2 distantly related Dutch families with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy, Bergman et al. (2007) identified the same novel heterozygous missense mutation, S13F, in the head domain of the DES gene. It cosegregated with the disease phenotype.

In a Chinese man with desmin myopathy and complete heart block, Pica et al. (2008) identified a heterozygous S13F mutation in the DES gene, which was also carried by affected family members. The mutation lay at a protein kinase C phosphorylation site within a highly conserved nonapeptide sequence in the desmin head domain. Expression of the mutant cDNA in cell lines caused large desmin accumulations, although a filamentous network was still preserved.

In the Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and ARVC7, Kuhl et al. (2008) found no disease-causing mutation in the coding regions of candidate genes in the linked 10q22.3 region and excluded VCL and LDB3 by sequencing. Using hybrid cell lines carrying only the affected or the normal chromosome 10 homolog, they found no submicroscopic or intragenic deletions. They speculated that regulatory mutations in one of the 18 genes in the region were responsible.

In a girl with infantile-onset cardiomyopathy and skeletal myopathy born to consanguineous parents, Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) identified a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in the DES gene. It predicted the in-frame loss of 7 amino acids (RARVDVE) from arg173 through glu179 (p.R173_E179del) in the 1B helix domain. Both unaffected parents were heterozygous. The deletion removes a full heptad at the start of the 1B domain, and in transiently transfected MCF-7 cells it prevented desmin filament assembly and compromised intermediate filament network formation.

van Tintelen et al. (2009) identified the DES p.S13F mutation as a founder mutation in 27 carriers or obligate carriers from 5 families, all of whom were affected.

Otten et al. (2010) identified a DES N342D mutation in a family with desmin-related myopathy, one member of which had an ARVC-like phenotype. They found a DES R454W mutation in a family with severe biventricular cardiomyopathy. Screening of DES in 50 patients with ARVC or an ARVC-like phenotype found no further mutations. The authors concluded that an ARVC-like phenotype or severe cardiomyopathy with right ventricular involvement is a possible but infrequent cardiac phenotype of desmin-related myopathy.

Greenberg et al. (2012) used laser capture microdissection and proteomics to identify desmin as the major constituent of skeletal muscle inclusions from a patient in the LGMD1D/1E family. Sequencing in the patient and family members then identified an intron splice donor site mutation (IVS3+3A-G) in the DES gene.

By exome sequencing in the Swedish family with autosomal dominant myofibrillar myopathy and ARVC, Hedberg et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.1255C-T transition in the DES gene, resulting in a pro419-to-ser (P419S) substitution. Sanger sequencing of DNA from 17 family members confirmed the mutation in 7 of the 10 members who had been classified as affected in the previous study.

In 2 affected members of a consanguineous family with LGMD2, Cetin et al. (2013) identified a homozygous splice site mutation (c.1289-2A-G) in the DES gene, a candidate gene in the region found by homozygosity mapping. The mutation added 16 amino acids within the desmin tail domain. Expression and cytoskeletal network formation of the mutant desmin were preserved in patient skeletal muscle, but confocal microscopy showed specific disruption of the desmin-lamin B interaction.

In 2 sisters with recessive myopathy with cardiac involvement, Riley et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygous DES variants. The paternal variant was a missense change, p.(leu190-to-pro) (L190P), and was also carried by the clinically unaffected father and brothers. The maternal variant was a deep intronic variant in intron 7, which segregated with the disease. RNA sequencing and muscle cDNA studies showed that the intron 7 variant caused inclusion of an artificial exon/intronic fragment encoding a premature termination codon. This potently activated nonsense-mediated decay, so that 92% of DES transcripts were paternal and 8% maternal. Western blot showed a 60 to 75% reduction in desmin, likely comprised only of L190P desmin.

Ojrzynska et al. (2017) reported restrictive cardiomyopathy caused by a novel mutation in the desmin gene.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found that most DES mutations were missense mutations, mostly located in the 2B domain. Mutations in the 2B domain predominated in patients with an isolated neurologic phenotype, whereas mutations in the head and tail domains predominated in patients with an isolated cardiologic phenotype.

Cetin et al. (2013) concluded that, unlike autosomal dominant desminopathies, autosomal recessive DES mutations cause an LGMD2 phenotype without features of myofibrillar myopathy.

### Pathogenesis

Porte et al. (1980) suggested that a disturbance in the metabolism of desmin, the protein subunit of intermediate filaments and a fundamental component of Z bands, might be involved in the cardiomyopathy in their family. Given the marked sarcoplasmic reticulum abnormalities, they also proposed a possible role for a chronic defect in calcium regulation.

Wolburg et al. (1982) considered that the disorder in their patients was caused by a basic defect of myofilament biosynthesis. They suggested that the defect was presumably evoked by a neurogenic factor affecting the trophic influence of the neuron on the muscle cell.

Goebel (1995) reviewed desmin-related neuromuscular disorders and noted that desmin pathology was always marked by increased amounts of desmin, either diffusely or focally. Focal desmin accretion occurred with cytoplasmic and spheroid bodies or as patchy granulofilamentous material, and both lesions had been found in certain families with myopathy and/or cardiomyopathy. Other proteins, such as dystrophin, vimentin, actin, ubiquitin, and alpha-B crystallin, may also be overexpressed. Goebel suggested that desmin pathology may be genetically regulated or may merely reflect profoundly impaired metabolism of several proteins within myofibers.

Sjoberg et al. (1999) showed that L345P desmin could not form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells. They concluded that the mutation causes myopathy by interfering in a dominant-negative manner with the dimerization-polymerization process of intermediate filament assembly.

Park et al. (2000) found that expression of exon 3-deleted mutant desmin in vimentin-deficient SW13 cells resulted in abnormal coarse clumps of desmin-positive material throughout the cytoplasm.

Kaminska et al. (2004) showed in 2 cell lines that desmin molecules carrying the E359_S361del or N366del in-frame deletions could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network, consistent with fatal damage to desmin filament assembly.

On electron microscopy, Selcen et al. (2004) observed progressive myofibrillar degeneration starting at the Z disk in myofibrillar myopathy. Degraded filamentous material and dislocated membranous organelles accumulated and were trapped in autophagic vacuoles. ⚠︎

Bar et al. (2007) studied 7 desmin tail mutations (T442I, R454W, S460I, E413K, K449T, I451M, and V469M). Two mutants, E413K and R454W, failed to assemble into intermediate filaments in vitro. All except E413K were incorporated into filament arrays in transfected C2C12 cells, but their filament properties still differed significantly from wildtype in viscometric assembly assays. When coassembled with wildtype desmin, the tail mutants severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions, indicating that mutant and wildtype protein are inherently incompatible in forming mixed filaments. The authors suggested that the different clinical phenotypes may reflect altered interactions of the tail domain with different cytoskeletal components.

In cardiac tissue from DES S13F carriers, van Tintelen et al. (2009) found normal amounts of the major desmosomal proteins. However, the intercalated disks were more convoluted and elongated, with a zigzag appearance. The authors suggested that a localized effect of desmin on intercalated disk structure might contribute to pathogenesis.

By immunohistochemistry of myocardial tissue from 2 carriers of the DES R454W mutation, Otten et al. (2010) found desmin aggregates and less desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk. They suggested a link between desmosomal cardiomyopathies and cardiomyopathies caused by DES mutations.

### Diagnosis

Selcen et al. (2004) found that, on average, 90% of abnormal muscle fibers in myofibrillar myopathy abnormally expressed myotilin, compared with 75% for desmin and alphaB-crystallin and 70% for dystrophin and beta-amyloid precursor protein. They concluded that immunostains for these proteins, especially myotilin, are useful adjuncts in diagnosis. Abnormal fibers were best identified in trichrome-stained sections by their congophilic hyaline structures. ⚠︎

Bar et al. (2007) proposed their in vitro assembly regimen as a sensitive tool for detecting whether a particular desmin mutation can cause filament abnormalities.

Bergman et al. (2007) stressed that the desmin gene should be analyzed in patients with (familial) cardiac conduction disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, and/or a progressive skeletal myopathy resembling limb-girdle muscular dystrophy.

Greenberg et al. (2012) showed that laser capture microdissection of skeletal muscle inclusions, followed by mass spectrometry-based proteomics and bioinformatic analysis, identified desmin as the major inclusion constituent and so pointed to the causative gene. Hedberg et al. (2012) concluded that their findings showed the usefulness of next-generation sequencing. They also highlighted how hard some dominantly inherited muscle diseases are to diagnose, because clinical and morphologic features can vary widely even within one family.

Riley et al. (2019) emphasized the continuing importance of muscle biopsy for functional genomic investigation of difficult variants in neuromuscular conditions. They noted that the paternal L190P variant in their family was at first considered unlikely to be causative because heterozygous carriers were unaffected.

### Clinical Management

Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) concluded that patients with desmin-related cardiomyopathy are at high risk of ventricular arrhythmia and should be considered for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement even before ventricular dysfunction becomes evident.

### Population Genetics

van Tintelen et al. (2009) identified the DES S13F head-domain mutation as a founder mutation shared by 5 families.

### Animal Model

Shelton et al. (2004) reported a 1-year-old male Australian Shepherd dog with chronic lameness, contractures, exercise intolerance, elevated serum creatine kinase, complex repetitive discharges on electromyography, and clinically identified cardiomyopathy. Light and electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry of muscle biopsies showed a myofibrillar myopathy with accumulation of desmin and other proteins. The authors stated that this was the first report of myofibrillar myopathy in the dog.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, ALA337PRO [p.A337P]

In a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement, Goldfarb et al. (1998) identified a heterozygous ala337-to-pro (A337P) mutation in the DES gene. The mutation lay in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain.

**.0002 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, ALA360PRO [p.A360P]

In a family with a childhood-onset, aggressive course of cardiac and skeletal myopathy, Goldfarb et al. (1998) found compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the DES gene: an ala360-to-pro (A360P) substitution and an asn393-to-ile (N393I) substitution. Both lay in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain.

**.0003 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, ASN393ILE [p.N393I]

For discussion of the asn393-to-ile (N393I) mutation in the DES gene, which Goldfarb et al. (1998) found in compound heterozygous state with A360P in a family with childhood-onset, aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, see the A360P mutation.

**.0004 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, 7-AA DEL

In a patient with severe generalized myopathy and aberrant intrasarcoplasmic accumulation of desmin intermediate filaments, Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) identified a DES mutation encoding a 7-amino acid deletion within the coiled-coil segment of desmin. The patient's muscle tissue lacked the wild-type desmin allele. Recombinant mutant desmin could not form proper intermediate filament networks in cultured cells and could not assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro.

**.0005 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, LEU345PRO [p.L345P]

In a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant distal myopathy, Sjoberg et al. (1999) identified a heterozygous leu345-to-pro (L345P) mutation in the DES gene that cosegregated with the disease. The mutation lay at an evolutionarily highly conserved position of the coiled-coil rod domain that is important for dimer formation. L345P desmin could not form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells, and the authors concluded that it acts in a dominant-negative manner on intermediate filament assembly. ⚠︎

**.0006 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, IVS3DS, A-G, +3

In a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy, Park et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous de novo A-to-G change at the +3 position of the intron 3 splice donor site of the DES gene (IVS3+3A-G). Expression of genomic DNA fragments showed skipping of exon 3, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 32 codons predicted to remove 32 amino acids from the 1B segment of the alpha-helical rod. In vimentin-deficient SW13 cells, the exon 3-deleted mutant desmin formed abnormal coarse clumps of desmin-positive material throughout the cytoplasm.

**.0007 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, IVS2AS, G-A, -1

In a patient with skeletal and cardiac myopathy, Park et al. (2000) identified a G-to-A transition at the highly conserved -1 position of the intron 2 splice acceptor site of the DES gene (IVS2-1G-A). Expression of genomic DNA fragments showed that the mutation caused skipping of exon 3, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 32 codons in the 1B segment of the alpha-helical rod. In vimentin-deficient SW13 cells, the exon 3-deleted mutant desmin formed coarse cytoplasmic clumps of desmin-positive material.

**.0008 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, GLU359_SER361DEL [p.E359]

Kaminska et al. (2004) identified the in-frame E359_S361del mutation in the DES gene in a study of 3 families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy caused by small in-frame desmin deletions. Structural analysis showed that the deletion introduced a second stutter immediately downstream of the naturally occurring stutter in the 2B coiled-coil segment, doubling the extent of local coiled-coil unwinding. In expression studies in 2 cell lines, the mutant desmin could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network.

**.0009 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, ASN366DEL [p.N366del]

Kaminska et al. (2004) identified the in-frame N366del mutation in the DES gene in a study of 3 families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy caused by small in-frame desmin deletions. Structural analysis showed that the deletion converted the wildtype stutter of the 2B coiled-coil segment into a stammer, which was expected to cause extra overwinding of the coiled coil. In expression studies in 2 cell lines, the mutant desmin could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network.

**.0010 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, SER13PHE [p.S13F]

In 2 distantly related Dutch families with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy affecting 15 members, Bergman et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous ser13-to-phe (S13F) mutation in the head domain of the DES gene. The mutation cosegregated with the disease. The phenotype ranged from isolated dilated cardiomyopathy to generalized skeletal myopathy with mild respiratory problems, and muscle biopsies showed intracytoplasmic aggregates staining for desmin and p62.

Pica et al. (2008) identified a heterozygous S13F mutation in the DES gene in a Chinese man with complete heart block and mild proximal and distal limb weakness. Family members carrying the mutation had a similar or milder phenotype. The mutation lay at a protein kinase C phosphorylation site within a highly conserved nonapeptide sequence of the head domain. Expression of the mutant cDNA in cell lines caused large desmin accumulations, although a filamentous network was still preserved.

van Tintelen et al. (2009) identified S13F as a founder mutation in 27 carriers or obligate carriers from 5 families, all of whom were affected. The phenotype was variable but mainly cardiac, with high-grade AV block at a young age, right bundle branch block in 10 patients, and right ventricular heart failure in 6, including 2 who met the criteria for arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. Cardiac tissue showed normal amounts of the major desmosomal proteins, but the intercalated disks were convoluted and elongated, with a zigzag appearance.

**.0011 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, ARG454TRP [p.R454W]

Bar et al. (2007) reported an arg454-to-trp (R454W) mutation in the tail domain of desmin in a patient with myopathy. The R454W mutant failed to assemble into intermediate filaments in vitro, and when coassembled with wildtype desmin, it disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions, as the other tail mutants studied did. ⚠︎

Otten et al. (2010) identified the R454W mutation in the DES gene in a family with severe biventricular cardiomyopathy. Myocardial tissue from 2 mutation carriers showed desmin aggregates and less desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk.

**.0012 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, 21-BP DEL [p.R173]

In a girl with infantile-onset cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, skeletal myopathy, and suspected intestinal smooth muscle involvement, born to consanguineous parents (uncle and niece), Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) identified a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in the DES gene. It predicted the in-frame loss of 7 amino acids (RARVDVE) from arg173 through glu179 (p.R173_E179del) in the 1B helix domain. Both unaffected parents were heterozygous. In transiently transfected MCF-7 cells, the mutation prevented desmin filament assembly and compromised intermediate filament network formation.

**.0013 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, ASN342ASP [p.N342D]

Otten et al. (2010) identified an asn342-to-asp (N342D) mutation in the DES gene in a family diagnosed with desmin-related myopathy, in which one patient had an ARVC-like phenotype.

**.0014 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, IVS3DS, A-G, +3

Greenberg et al. (2012) identified an intron 3 splice donor site mutation (IVS3+3A-G) in the DES gene as the cause of skeletal and cardiac myopathy in the LGMD1D/1E family previously linked to chromosome 6q23. Laser capture microdissection proteomics had shown that desmin was the major constituent of the skeletal muscle inclusions in an affected patient. The same change had been reported de novo by Park et al. (2000).

**.0015 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, PRO419SER [c.1255C>T; p.P419S]

By exome sequencing in the Swedish family with autosomal dominant myofibrillar myopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, Hedberg et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.1255C-T transition in the DES gene, resulting in a pro419-to-ser (P419S) substitution. Sanger sequencing of 17 family members confirmed the mutation in 7 of the 10 members previously classified as affected.

**.0016 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, 1289-2A-G [c.1289-2A>G]

In 2 affected members of a consanguineous family with LGMD2, Cetin et al. (2013) identified a homozygous splice site mutation (c.1289-2A-G) in the DES gene. The mutation added 16 amino acids within the desmin tail domain. Mutant desmin was expressed and formed a cytoskeletal network in patient skeletal muscle, but confocal microscopy showed specific disruption of the desmin-lamin B interaction. Ultrastructural features of myofibrillar myopathy were absent.

**.0017 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, LEU190PRO [p.L190P]

In 2 sisters with slowly progressive limb-girdle weakness, severe systolic dysfunction, and severe respiratory weakness, Riley et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygous DES variants. One was a paternally inherited leu190-to-pro (L190P) missense variant, which was also carried by the clinically unaffected father and brothers. The other was a maternal deep intronic variant in intron 7. Because the intron 7 variant activated nonsense-mediated decay of the maternal transcript, desmin was reduced by 60 to 75% and likely consisted only of L190P desmin.

**.0018 MYOFIBRILLAR MYOPATHY-1**  
DES, IVS7, DEEP INTRONIC VARIANT

In 2 sisters with recessive myopathy with cardiac involvement, Riley et al. (2019) identified a maternally inherited deep intronic variant in intron 7 of the DES gene. It was in compound heterozygous state with the paternal L190P variant and segregated with disease. RNA sequencing and muscle cDNA studies showed that the variant caused inclusion of an artificial exon/intronic fragment encoding a premature termination codon. This potently activated nonsense-mediated decay, so that 92% of DES transcripts were paternal and 8% maternal.
