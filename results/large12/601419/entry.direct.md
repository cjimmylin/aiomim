### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-1 (MFM1) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding desmin (DES) on chromosome 2q35. ⚠︎

### Description

Desmin-related myopathy is a familial skeletal muscle weakness that occurs with cardiac conduction blocks, arrhythmias, and restrictive heart failure. In both cardiac and skeletal muscle cells, desmin-reactive deposits build up inside the cytoplasm (Goldfarb et al., 1998). The clinical picture varies. It includes myopathies, cardiomyopathies, conduction disease, and combinations of these (van Tintelen et al., 2009). In a meta-analysis of 159 DES mutation carriers, van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found that symptoms usually began in the 30s. Atrioventricular block was an important hallmark, and most mutations were dominant. ⚠︎

Myofibrillar myopathy (MFM) is defined by its pathology. Myofibrils dissolve, the breakdown products accumulate, and multiple proteins are expressed ectopically, including desmin, alpha-B-crystallin, and dystrophin, along with congophilic amyloid material. MFM is genetically heterogeneous (Selcen et al., 2004).

### Nomenclature

According to Selcen et al. (2004), the term 'myofibrillar myopathy' was proposed in 1996 as a noncommittal name for a pathologic pattern of myofibrillar dissolution with accumulation of myofibrillar degradation products and ectopic expression of multiple proteins.

Greenberg et al. (2012) noted that the disorder linked to chromosome 6q23 by Messina et al. (1997) had been called limb-girdle muscular dystrophy 1D (LGMD1D) in OMIM nomenclature and LGMD1E by the Human Gene Nomenclature Committee. ⚠︎

The disorder in a Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, initially mapped to chromosome 10q22.3, was designated ARVC7 (Kuhl et al., 2008). Hedberg et al. (2012) later found that it was caused by a DES mutation.

### Clinical Features

Porte et al. (1980) reported 3 brothers with progressive myocardial deficiency. On histology, most cardiac muscle cells held massive stores of PAS-negative proteinaceous material. On electron microscopy, this material consisted of sinuous filaments 7 to 10 nm in diameter, similar to the intermediate filaments normally found in cardiac muscle cells. Where filaments were stored, the neighboring myofibrils were disintegrating, with Z-band changes that formed rod-like bodies and more complex structures. These changes were associated with extensive development and involution of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, together with relatively high glycogen accumulation. A few skeletal muscle fibers showed sarcoplasmic reticulum proliferation, glycogen overload, and filament deposits. The authors suggested that a disturbance in desmin metabolism might be involved. Stoeckel et al. (1981) further described this unusual familial cardiomyopathy as having aberrant accumulations of desmin-type intermediate filaments.

Wolburg et al. (1982) reported 2 unrelated patients, both 32 years old, with a congenital disorder of skeletal muscle. One patient also had congenital paresis of the pupillary dilator muscle and cardiomyopathy. In both patients, muscle biopsy showed abnormal variation in fiber caliber and inclusions consistent with cytoplasmic bodies. Streaming or disintegration of Z disks was seen around or apart from the inclusions, and both patients had purely filamentous bodies beneath the sarcolemma.

Chapon et al. (1989) reported 3 patients from 3 successive generations, aged 72, 45, and 18 years, who presented with respiratory insufficiency. Onset was at about 40 years of age in the 2 older patients and at 18 years in the youngest. Serum muscle enzymes and leukocyte maltase activity were normal. In all 3 patients, muscle biopsy showed numerous cytoplasmic (spheroid) bodies within type I muscle fibers.

Abe et al. (1993) described a Japanese kindred with autosomal dominant progressive myopathy with diffuse cytoplasmic bodies. Over 4 successive generations, 12 patients had weakness and atrophy of muscles of the distal extremities, neck, thorax, and shoulder girdles. Mean age at onset was 42 years, and patients became disabled 5 to 10 years later from chronic respiratory failure. Serum creatine kinase was normal or slightly elevated. Electromyography showed mainly myopathic changes with slight neurogenic changes. Autopsy in 2 cases showed numerous cytoplasmic bodies in skeletal muscle, mostly in type I fibers, and smooth and cardiac muscle were also affected. Males were affected more often than females (2:1).

Vajsar et al. (1993) reported 2 sibs who developed cardiomyopathy several years before slowly progressive muscle weakness. Skeletal muscle biopsies showed subsarcolemmal crescents of dark eosinophilic material in both type I and type II fibers. This material stained for desmin and ubiquitin but for no other cytoskeletal proteins. Ultrastructurally, it consisted of granular and filamentous aggregates arising from Z bands. Follow-up biopsies 6 years later showed more fibers containing these aggregates.

Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994) described a large family with autosomal dominant distal myopathy. Onset was in early to middle adulthood, with gait disturbance from distal leg weakness. Over 5 to 10 years the disease spread to all extremities and to bulbar, respiratory, and facial muscles. Cardiac involvement was frequent, with arrhythmias, conduction blocks, and congestive failure, and led to premature death. Electromyography showed prominent spontaneous activity, short-duration motor unit potentials, and polyphasia. Muscle biopsies from multiple family members showed desmin storage and autophagocytosis.

Goebel (1995) reviewed desmin-related neuromuscular disorders. He noted that focal accretion of desmin takes 2 forms: with cytoplasmic and spheroid bodies, or as more patchy granulofilamentous material. Both lesions had been found in families with myopathy and/or cardiomyopathy. Other proteins, such as dystrophin, vimentin, actin, ubiquitin, and alpha-B-crystallin, could also be overexpressed.

Ariza et al. (1995) reported a 28-year-old man with generalized muscular atrophy, cardiomyopathy, and intestinal malabsorption and pseudo-obstruction. Sarcoplasmic granular and filamentous aggregates that stained intensely for desmin were present throughout the skeletal muscles, the myocardium, and the intestinal smooth muscle. Ultrastructurally, the aggregates were continuous with Z lines or dense bodies. Axonal spheroids immunoreactive for neurofilament were also found in the spinal cord and nerve roots.

Messina et al. (1997) characterized a family with an autosomal dominant disorder of dilated cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction-system disease, and adult-onset limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, which they called FDC-CDM. ⚠︎

Melberg et al. (1999) studied 21 members of a Swedish family with myopathy and cardiomyopathy and reviewed the records of 2 affected deceased patients. Twelve patients had myopathy. Weakness was axial in mildly affected patients, axial and mainly distal in moderately affected patients, and generalized in severely affected patients. Electromyography showed myopathic signs in 10 patients. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic changes, rimmed vacuoles, and accumulation of desmin, dystrophin, and other proteins. Electron microscopy showed granulofilamentous changes and disorganized myofibrils. Several patients had episodes of chest pain or palpitations. Three men had arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, with nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, atrial flutter, and ventricular dilatation affecting mainly the right ventricle. Two of them received pacemakers for atrioventricular block and sick sinus syndrome. Onset and severity of skeletal and cardiac involvement varied.

Selcen et al. (2004) reviewed 63 unrelated patients diagnosed with MFM at the Mayo Clinic between 1977 and 2003. Age at onset was 54 +/- 16 years (mean +/- SD). Weakness was both proximal and distal in 77% and proximal only in 13%, and 16% had cardiomyopathy. Electromyography showed a myopathic pattern with abnormal electrical irritability. In trichrome-stained sections, abnormal fibers contained amorphous, granular, or pleomorphic hyaline structures, which were strongly congophilic, and vacuoles with membranous material. Among abnormal fibers, an average of 90%, 75%, 75%, 70%, and 70% abnormally expressed myotilin, desmin, alpha-B-crystallin, dystrophin, and beta-amyloid precursor protein, respectively. Electron microscopy showed progressive myofibrillar degeneration beginning at the Z disk.

Bergman et al. (2007) reported 2 distantly related Dutch families in which 15 members had autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy. The clinical picture ranged from isolated dilated cardiomyopathy to a more generalized skeletal myopathy with mild respiratory problems. Muscle biopsies showed intracytoplasmic aggregates that stained for desmin and p62.

Pica et al. (2008) reported a Chinese man with complete heart block and mild proximal and distal limb weakness. Family members who carried the same mutation had a similar or milder phenotype.

Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) reported a girl who was the only child of consanguineous parents (uncle and niece). She had recurrent syncope from 6 months of age, which was first attributed to Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. From 3 years of age she had progressive syncopal, presyncopal, and cyanotic attacks. At 15 years, she had a long, narrow face with bilateral facial weakness, ptosis, symmetric scapular winging, and poor muscle bulk. She also had atrophy of the masseter, temporalis, sternocleidomastoid, and trunk muscles, bilateral pes cavus, mild weakness of the shoulder girdle and distal limb muscles, and hypoactive deep tendon reflexes. Creatine kinase was twice normal. Electromyography showed complex repetitive discharges, some myotonic discharges, and a myopathic recruitment pattern. Holter monitoring showed monomorphic and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, occasional QT prolongation, and second-degree AV block, and a defibrillator and pacemaker were implanted. At 18 years, she had several episodes of ventricular tachyarrhythmia. Echocardiography then showed mild left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF 45%), slight right ventricular dilatation, markedly enlarged atria, and filling consistent with restrictive cardiomyopathy. Diarrhea, vomiting, and weight loss suggested involvement of intestinal smooth muscle. She died of cardiac failure before age 21. On muscle biopsy, more than 90% of fibers had subsarcolemmal deposits of eosinophilic, Congo red-positive material that was strongly desmin-immunoreactive. On electron microscopy, the deposits formed a granulofilamentous matrix. The authors described her as the youngest known patient with desminopathy. ⚠︎

van Tintelen et al. (2009) studied 27 patients from 5 families who carried the same DES mutation. The phenotype was fully penetrant but variable. All patients had cardiac involvement, with high-grade AV block at young ages and important right ventricular involvement. Ten patients had right bundle branch block, sometimes as the first manifestation. Six had right ventricular heart failure, and 2 of these fulfilled diagnostic criteria for arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. Intercalated disks had normal amounts of the major desmosomal proteins but were more convoluted and elongated, with a zigzag appearance.

Otten et al. (2010) evaluated 2 families with different DES mutations. One family had desmin-related myopathy, and 1 patient in that family had an ARVC-like phenotype. The other family had severe biventricular cardiomyopathy. In addition to desmin aggregates, myocardium from 2 patients in the second family showed reduced desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk.

van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) performed a meta-analysis of 159 DES mutation carriers with 40 different mutations. Neurologic signs were present in 74% and cardiologic signs in 74%. Both were present in 49%, isolated neurologic signs in 22%, and isolated cardiologic signs in 22%. More than 70% had myopathy or muscle weakness, and one-third of these had normal creatine kinase levels. Up to 50% had cardiomyopathy, and around 60% had cardiac conduction disease or arrhythmias. Symptoms generally began in the 30s, and a quarter of carriers died, at a mean age of 49 years. Two patients with pacemakers died suddenly, suggesting ventricular tachyarrhythmia as the cause of death.

Gudkova et al. (2013) reported a patient whose desmin cardiomyopathy appeared early, in adolescence, and passed through several clinical phenotypes over time, which made correct early diagnosis and treatment difficult.

Cetin et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous family with autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy and 2 affected members. Unlike in autosomal dominant desminopathies, skeletal muscle showed no ultrastructural signs of myofibrillar myopathy: no disrupted myofibrillar organization, no myofibrillar degradation products, and no dislocation or aggregation of membranous organelles. Desmin expression and the cytoskeletal network were well preserved.

Ojrzynska et al. (2017) reported restrictive cardiomyopathy caused by a novel desmin mutation.

Riley et al. (2019) reported 2 sisters who presented in their twenties with slowly progressive limb-girdle weakness, severe systolic dysfunction, and progressive, severe respiratory weakness. Muscle biopsy showed variation in fiber size and increased central nuclei. Electron microscopy showed extensive myofibrillar disarray and duplication of the basal lamina, but no inclusions or aggregates.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance was described in a Japanese kindred by Abe et al. (1993), in a large family by Horowitz and Schmalbruch (1994), in a Swedish family by Melberg et al. (1999), and in 2 Dutch families by Bergman et al. (2007). Park et al. (2000) found a de novo heterozygous DES mutation in a sporadic patient. ⚠︎

Autosomal recessive inheritance was shown by compound heterozygous DES mutations in a family reported by Goldfarb et al. (1998) and by a homozygous DES deletion in the child of consanguineous parents reported by Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009). The heterozygous parents of that child were unaffected. Recessive inheritance was also shown by a homozygous splice site mutation in a consanguineous family (Cetin et al., 2013) and by compound heterozygous variants in 2 sisters (Riley et al., 2019). Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) stated that inheritance was autosomal recessive in about 6% of patients with desminopathy, and that these patients developed disease in childhood (average 5 years). ⚠︎

### Mapping

In a family with dilated cardiomyopathy, conduction-system disease, and limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, Messina et al. (1997) carried out a genomewide scan. They found linkage to chromosome 6q23 (maximum lod score of 4.99 at theta = 0.00 for D6S262). Haplotype analysis narrowed the interval to 3 cM between D6S1705 and D6S1656 and excluded laminin alpha-2, laminin alpha-4, triadin, and phospholamban. Greenberg et al. (2012) later found a DES mutation, on chromosome 2q35, in this 6q23-linked disorder. ⚠︎

In a Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, Melberg et al. (1999) obtained a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.76 for marker D10S1752 and a multipoint peak lod score of 3.06 between D10S605 and D10S215. These results suggested linkage to chromosome 10q22.3. Kuhl et al. (2008) restudied the family with a high-density SNP panel and confirmed the linkage, with no other regions showing significant evidence of linkage. They narrowed the critical interval to 4.27 Mbp between D10S1645 and D10S1786. No disease-causing mutations were found in the coding regions of 17 candidate genes in this interval, and VCL and LDB3 were excluded by sequencing. Hedberg et al. (2012) later identified a DES mutation on chromosome 2q35 in this family. ⚠︎

Saavedra-Matiz et al. (2000) performed linkage analysis in a large family with autosomal dominant distal myopathy with desmin accumulation. They mapped the disorder to chromosome 2q33, within a 17-cM segment bounded by D2S2248 and D2S401, and named desmin as the best candidate gene.

Cetin et al. (2013) used genomewide homozygosity mapping to map the autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy phenotype in a consanguineous family to chromosome 2q35-q36.3.

### Heterogeneity

Goldfarb et al. (1998) noted that DES involvement had been excluded in 3 families diagnosed with desmin-related myopathy. Of 63 unrelated patients with MFM, Selcen et al. (2004) found desmin mutations in only 4 and alpha-B-crystallin mutations in 2, and concluded that MFM is genetically heterogeneous.

Ferreiro et al. (2004) found that an early-onset recessive form of desmin-related myopathy with Mallory body-like inclusions, first described in 5 related German patients, was caused by a homozygous deletion in the SEPN1 gene. They stated that mutations in the desmin and alpha-B-crystallin genes account for about one-third of desmin-related myopathy cases.

### Molecular Genetics

Goldfarb et al. (1998) reported 2 families with desmin-related cardioskeletal myopathy and mutations at the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain. A family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement carried a heterozygous A337P mutation in the DES gene. A second family, with childhood-onset, aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, was compound heterozygous for 2 other mutations, A360P and N393I.

Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) reported a patient with severe generalized myopathy and abnormal accumulation of desmin intermediate filaments in the sarcoplasm. The patient's muscle tissue lacked the wild-type desmin allele and carried a DES mutation encoding a 7-amino acid deletion within the coiled-coil segment. Recombinant desmin with this deletion could not form proper desmin intermediate filament networks in cultured cells and could not assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro.

In a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy, Sjoberg et al. (1999) found that an L345P missense mutation in the desmin coiled-coil rod domain cosegregated with disease. L345P desmin could not form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells. The authors concluded that the mutation interferes with filament assembly in a dominant-negative manner.

Park et al. (2000) sequenced cDNA and genomic DNA from a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy and found a de novo heterozygous splice donor site mutation in intron 3 of the DES gene (IVS3+3A-G). In an unrelated patient with a similar phenotype, they found a splice acceptor site mutation in intron 2 (IVS2-1G-A). Expression studies showed that both mutations caused deletion of exon 3. This was predicted to give an in-frame loss of 32 amino acids from the 1B segment of the rod. In SW13 cells, the mutant desmin formed coarse aggregated clumps.

Kaminska et al. (2004) studied 3 families with skeletal or cardioskeletal myopathy caused by small in-frame deletions in the DES gene. The newly identified deletions, E359_S361del and N366del, alter heptad periodicity within the 2B coiled-coil segment. In 2 cell lines, the mutant desmin could not polymerize into a functional filamentous network.

Among 63 unrelated patients with MFM, Selcen et al. (2004) found 4 with missense mutations in the head or tail region of desmin.

Bar et al. (2007) reported 3 novel mutations in the non-alpha-helical carboxy-terminal tail domain of desmin: T442I, R454W, and S460I. They studied these and 4 previously described tail mutations by in vitro filament assembly and in transfected C2C12 myoblasts. All mutants except E413K and R454W assembled into intermediate filaments in vitro, but their filament properties differed significantly from wild type. When coassembled with wild-type desmin, the mutants severely disturbed filament formation and filament-filament interactions.

In 2 distantly related Dutch families with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy, Bergman et al. (2007) found the same novel heterozygous missense mutation, S13F, in the head domain of desmin. The mutation cosegregated with disease.

In a Chinese family with desmin myopathy and heart block, Pica et al. (2008) found a heterozygous S13F mutation in the DES gene. The mutation lies at a protein kinase C phosphorylation site within a highly conserved nonapeptide of the head domain. Expression of the mutant cDNA in cell lines produced large desmin accumulations, but a filamentous network was preserved.

In a patient with infantile-onset severe cardiomyopathy and skeletal myopathy, the child of consanguineous parents, Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) sequenced the DES and alpha-B-crystallin genes. They found a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in DES predicting in-frame loss of 7 amino acids (RARVDVE) from arg173 to glu179 (R173_E179del) in the 1B helix domain. Both unaffected parents were heterozygous for the deletion. The authors stated that the case reported by Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) carried an identical homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion, and that this deletion prevented desmin filament assembly in transfected MCF-7 cells.

van Tintelen et al. (2009) reported 27 carriers or obligate carriers from 5 families of a p.S13F founder mutation in the head domain of desmin.

Otten et al. (2010) found the DES mutation N342D in a family with desmin-related myopathy and R454W in a family with severe biventricular cardiomyopathy. In 50 patients with ARVC or an ARVC-like phenotype, they found no additional DES mutations.

Greenberg et al. (2012) used laser capture microdissection and mass spectrometry-based proteomics on cytoplasmic inclusions from the skeletal muscle of a patient with the 6q23-linked disorder (LGMD1D/1E) reported by Messina et al. (1997). They identified desmin as the main constituent. Sequencing of the patient and family members then identified a splice donor site mutation (IVS3+3A-G) in the DES gene on chromosome 2q35. ⚠︎

Using exome sequencing in the Swedish family with myofibrillar myopathy and ARVC reported by Melberg et al. (1999), Hedberg et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.1255C-T transition in the DES gene, resulting in a pro419-to-ser (P419S) substitution. Sanger sequencing of 17 family members confirmed the mutation in 7 of the 10 members who had been classified as affected in the earlier study. The authors noted that the earlier linkage to chromosome 10q22.3 had not revealed a causative mutation, and that the disease varied widely in clinical and morphologic features within the family. ⚠︎

In 2 affected members of a consanguineous family with autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, Cetin et al. (2013) found a homozygous splice site mutation (c.1289-2A-G) in the DES gene. The mutation added 16 amino acids within the tail domain of desmin. Confocal microscopy showed a specific disruption of the interaction between desmin and lamin B in the patient's skeletal muscle.

In 2 sisters with myopathy and cardiac involvement, Riley et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygous DES variants. The paternal variant was a missense change, p.(Leu190Pro). Their father and brothers carried it and were clinically unaffected. The maternal variant was a deep intronic variant in intron 7, predicted to create a cryptic splice site, and it segregated with disease. RNA sequencing and studies of muscle cDNA showed that the intronic variant caused an artificial exon or intronic fragment to be spliced into maternal transcripts. This introduced a premature termination codon and triggered nonsense-mediated decay, so that 92% of DES transcripts were paternal and 8% maternal. Western blot showed a 60 to 75% reduction in desmin, which likely consisted only of L190P desmin.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In a meta-analysis of 159 DES mutation carriers, van Spaendonck-Zwarts et al. (2011) found that most DES mutations were missense mutations, mostly in the 2B domain. Patients with an isolated neurologic phenotype mostly had 2B-domain mutations, whereas those with an isolated cardiologic phenotype mostly had head- or tail-domain mutations.

Cetin et al. (2013) and Riley et al. (2019) found that some patients with autosomal recessive DES mutations had myopathy without the myofibrillar aggregates or other ultrastructural features of myofibrillar myopathy.

### Pathogenesis

Kaminska et al. (2004) performed structural analysis of the in-frame deletions. The E359_S361 deletion adds a second stutter immediately downstream of the naturally occurring stutter, doubling the length of local coiled-coil unwinding. The N366 deletion converts the wild-type stutter into a stammer, which is expected to cause extra overwinding of the coiled-coil.

van Tintelen et al. (2009) found that intercalated disks in S13F carriers were more convoluted and elongated, with a zigzag appearance. They suggested that a localized effect of desmin on intercalated disk structure might contribute to pathogenesis. Otten et al. (2010) found reduced desmoplakin and plakophilin-2 at the intercalated disk in R454W carriers, suggesting a link between desmosomal cardiomyopathies and cardiomyopathies caused by DES mutations.

### Animal Model

Shelton et al. (2004) reported a 1-year-old male Australian Shepherd dog with chronic lameness, contractures, exercise intolerance, elevated serum creatine kinase, complex repetitive discharges on electromyography, and clinically identified cardiomyopathy. Muscle biopsies showed a myofibrillar myopathy with accumulation of desmin and other proteins.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MYOPATHY, MYOFIBRILLAR, 1**  
DES, ALA337PRO [A337P]

In a family with adult-onset skeletal myopathy and mild cardiac involvement, Goldfarb et al. (1998) identified a heterozygous ala337-to-pro (A337P) mutation in the highly conserved carboxy-terminal end of the desmin rod domain.

**.0002 MYOPATHY, MYOFIBRILLAR, 1**  
DES, ALA360PRO [A360P]

In a family with childhood-onset, aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy, Goldfarb et al. (1998) found compound heterozygosity for an ala360-to-pro (A360P) mutation and an asn393-to-ile (N393I) mutation in the DES gene.

**.0003 MYOPATHY, MYOFIBRILLAR, 1**  
DES, ASN393ILE [N393I]

For discussion of the asn393-to-ile (N393I) mutation in the DES gene, found in compound heterozygous state in a family with childhood-onset, aggressive cardiac and skeletal myopathy by Goldfarb et al. (1998), see DES, ALA360PRO.

**.0004 MYOPATHY, MYOFIBRILLAR, 1**  
DES, 21-BP DEL [p.R173_E179del; p.Arg173_Glu179del]

In a patient with severe generalized myopathy, Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) found that muscle tissue lacked the wild-type desmin allele and carried a DES mutation encoding a 7-amino acid deletion within the coiled-coil segment. The mutant recombinant desmin could not form intermediate filament networks in cultured cells or assemble into 10-nm filaments in vitro. ⚠︎

In a girl with infantile-onset cardiac arrhythmia, restrictive cardiomyopathy, and skeletal and probable smooth muscle involvement, born to consanguineous parents, Pinol-Ripoll et al. (2009) found a homozygous 21-nucleotide deletion in the DES gene. The deletion predicts in-frame loss of 7 amino acids (RARVDVE) from arg173 to glu179 (R173_E179del) in the 1B helix domain. Both unaffected parents were heterozygous. The authors stated that this deletion was identical to the one reported by Munoz-Marmol et al. (1998) and that it prevented filament assembly in transfected MCF-7 cells.

**.0005 MYOPATHY, MYOFIBRILLAR, 1**  
DES, LEU345PRO [L345P]

In a large 6-generation Ashkenazi Jewish family with autosomal dominant desmin-related myopathy, Sjoberg et al. (1999) identified a leu345-to-pro (L345P) mutation that cosegregated with disease. It lies at an evolutionarily highly conserved position in the coiled-coil rod domain that is important for dimer formation. The mutant desmin could not form filamentous networks in transfected HeLa and SW13 cells, consistent with a dominant-negative effect.

**.0006 MYOPATHY, MYOFIBRILLAR, 1**  
DES, IVS3DS, A-G, +3 [IVS3+3A>G]

In a patient with sporadic skeletal and cardiac myopathy, Park et al. (2000) identified a de novo heterozygous A-to-G change at position +3 of the splice donor site of intron 3 of the DES gene (IVS3+3A-G). Expression studies showed deletion of exon 3, predicted to give an in-frame loss of 32 amino acids from the 1B segment of the rod. The mutant desmin formed coarse aggregated clumps in SW13 cells.

Greenberg et al. (2012) identified the IVS3+3A-G mutation in a patient and family members with the 6q23-linked limb-girdle muscular dystrophy 1D/1E reported by Messina et al. (1997).
