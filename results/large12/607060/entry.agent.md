### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding leucine-rich repeat kinase-2 (LRRK2) on chromosome 12q12. ⚠︎

### Description

Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is an autosomal dominant, usually late-onset parkinsonism that resembles idiopathic Parkinson disease (PD). Features include bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor and impaired postural reflexes, with a good response to levodopa (Wszolek et al., 1995; Zimprich et al., 2004). The neuropathology is pleomorphic. Brainstem dopaminergic degeneration has been accompanied by Lewy body PD, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, or progressive supranuclear palsy-like pathology (Zimprich et al., 2004).

### Nomenclature

Funayama et al. (2002) assigned the symbol PARK8 to the locus they mapped on chromosome 12 in a Japanese family. Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) named the protein encoded by the PARK8 gene 'dardarin', from the Basque word 'dardara', meaning tremor. They chose the name because of the tremor seen in PD and because several of their families were of Basque descent. Alcalay et al. (2009) noted that the protein encoded by LRRK2 was named dardarin.

### Clinical Features

Wszolek et al. (1995) studied a family (family D) from western Nebraska whose ancestors probably came to the United States from England. The pedigree had 188 individuals across 6 generations, 18 of them affected, with autosomal dominant inheritance. Affected members developed typical levodopa-responsive PD with bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor and impaired postural reflexes. There were no eye movement abnormalities, pyramidal or cerebellar signs, sensory disturbances, or orthostatic blood pressure changes, and the disease progressed slowly. PET with 18F-6-fluoro-L-dopa (FD) in 1 affected person showed decreased FD uptake in a pattern consistent with PD. Autopsy of another showed neuronal and pigmentary loss, gliosis and Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra pars compacta.

Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) reported autosomal dominant PD in 5 generations of a family living in Sagamihara City, Japan. The clinical features did not differ from common PD, but the neuropathology did. In autopsied cases, loss of melanin-containing cells was mild to moderate, the number of locus ceruleus neurons was maintained, and no Lewy bodies were found in any region where they frequently appear in PD. Funayama et al. (2005) later stated that the Sagamihara family was the family used to define the PARK8 locus. They described its pathology as pure nigral degeneration without Lewy bodies. ⚠︎

Funayama et al. (2002) studied a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism whose clinical features were compatible with common PD. The disease-associated haplotype was shared by all patients and by some unaffected carriers, which suggested incomplete penetrance.

In follow-up of a parkinsonian kindred first reported in 1995, Wszolek et al. (2004) noted that 4 more individuals had become affected, with a mean age at onset of 65 years. All 4 had the cardinal signs of PD and a good response to levodopa. Four autopsies showed neuronal loss and gliosis in the substantia nigra. Lewy bodies were limited to brainstem nuclei in 1 case and diffuse in a second. The third case had neurofibrillary tangles without Lewy bodies, and the fourth had neither.

In affected LRRK2 mutation carriers from families A and D, Zimprich et al. (2004) reported 6 postmortem diagnoses. All showed brainstem dopaminergic degeneration, with strikingly diverse accompanying pathology: Lewy body PD, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, and progressive supranuclear palsy-like pathology. Clinical diagnoses of parkinsonism with dementia, amyotrophy, or both were also noted.

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) described 4 Basque families with autosomal dominant PD linked to the PARK8 locus. The clinical features were those of typical PD, including rigidity, akinesia and a good response to levodopa. Mean age at onset was 55 years.

Nichols et al. (2005) found that individuals carrying the G2019S mutation had typical clinical findings of idiopathic PD. Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found a broad phenotype in families with this mutation, with both early and late disease onset.

Ross et al. (2006) reported that Lewy body disease was the most common neuropathology of G2019S-associated PD.

Giasson et al. (2006) identified 3 autopsied patients with the G2019S mutation. Two had classic PD with Lewy bodies, and 1 of these also had pathologic changes consistent with Alzheimer disease. The third had parkinsonism without Lewy bodies, but with dystrophic neurites in the substantia nigra that stained intensely for Lrrk2.

Rajput et al. (2006) described family SK, in which the LRRK2 G2019S mutation segregated with slowly progressive parkinsonism. The affected proband had tau-immunopositive neurofibrillary tangle pathology. The authors suggested that alpha-synucleinopathy and tauopathy may be alternative outcomes of the same genetic cause.

Chen-Plotkin et al. (2008) screened 434 individuals for G2019S and several other reported LRRK2 mutations: 254 patients from neurodegenerative disease clinics and 180 autopsy cases from a brain bank. Eight cases carried a LRRK2 mutation. Two of them presented with cognitive deficits and were clinically diagnosed with corticobasal syndrome and primary progressive aphasia. The authors concluded that LRRK2-associated disease may be clinically more heterogeneous than previously assumed.

Silveira-Moriyama et al. (2008) tested smell with the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) in 19 parkinsonian and 2 asymptomatic G2019S carriers. The mean UPSIT score in parkinsonian carriers was lower than in healthy controls and similar to that in patients with PD. The 2 asymptomatic carriers scored in the normal range. Four parkinsonian carriers were examined postmortem, and all had alpha-synuclein deposition with Lewy bodies in the olfactory pathways (rhinencephalon).

Nandhagopal et al. (2008) performed multitracer PET of presynaptic dopaminergic integrity in 13 members of family D, a kindred carrying the LRRK2 R1441C mutation. Five subjects were rescanned 2 to 3 years later. Two mutation carriers (subjects 8 and 9) and 1 genealogically at-risk subject (subject 13) showed a decline in PET markers significantly greater than the expected decline in healthy controls. Reduced dopamine transporter binding was the earliest sign of subclinical dopaminergic dysfunction. Progression to clinical disease generally coincided with the appearance of abnormal fluorodopa uptake.

Alcalay et al. (2009) studied 925 patients with early-onset PD (onset before age 51 years) from 13 movement disorders centers. Thirty-four (3.7%) carried G2019S, and 1 of these, of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, was homozygous. Carriers were more likely than noncarriers to be Ashkenazi Jewish (55.9% vs 11.9%) but did not differ in age at onset, disease duration or UPDRS scores. Carriers had a lower tremor score and were more likely to have the postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) phenotype (92.3% vs 58.9%). The association with PIGD held after adjustment for disease duration and Ashkenazi ancestry (odds ratio, 17.7). ⚠︎

In 600 Ashkenazi PD patients, Gan-Or et al. (2010) found that LRRK2 G2019S carriers more often reported muscle stiffness and balance disturbance as initial symptoms. Carriers of GBA mutations more often reported slowness.

Saunders-Pullman et al. (2011) gave the UPSIT to 31 patients with G2019S-related PD, 30 PD patients without mutations, 28 nonmanifesting carrier relatives, and 46 controls. Olfaction was impaired in G2019S-related PD, but less than in PD without mutations. It was also impaired in a subset of nonmanifesting carriers, whose mean score was lower than that of controls.

Marras et al. (2011) found that tremor was more often a presenting feature in 25 individuals with LRRK2 G2019S-associated PD than in 84 with idiopathic PD. Compared with idiopathic PD, LRRK2-PD patients had better olfactory identification, higher Beck Depression Inventory scores, and higher error scores on the Farnsworth-Munsell 100-Hue test of color discrimination. Postural or action tremor was more common in 29 nonmanifesting carriers than in 53 noncarrier relatives. Alcalay et al. (2009) found lower tremor scores in early-onset G2019S carriers.

Among 490 genotyped Jewish PD patients, Inzelberg et al. (2012) found 79 (16.1%) with the G2019S mutation. A non-skin cancer had been diagnosed in 18 (23%) carriers versus 49 (12%) noncarriers (odds ratio, 2.18). Among Ashkenazi patients, the age-adjusted odds ratio for carriers was 3.38.

### Biochemical Features

Aasly et al. (2012) measured CSF amyloid-beta-42, total tau and phosphorylated tau in 26 LRRK2 mutation carriers: 18 asymptomatic and 8 with a phenotype resembling sporadic PD. All also had PET with 3 dopaminergic tracers. Lower CSF amyloid-beta-42 and tau levels correlated with lower striatal dopaminergic function on all 3 tracers, and the association between amyloid-beta-42 and fluorodopa uptake was significant. When the analysis was restricted to G2019S carriers, the tau correlations were also significant.

### Inheritance

Wszolek et al. (1995) found autosomal dominant inheritance in family D. Funayama et al. (2002) observed incomplete penetrance in the original Japanese PARK8 family. Ross et al. (2006) found the G2019S mutation in 1 control subject and 1 patient with Alzheimer disease, which they took to reflect reduced penetrance.

### Mapping

Funayama et al. (2002) carried out genomewide linkage analysis in a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism. Parametric 2-point analysis gave a maximum lod score of 4.32 at D12S345 (12p11.21), and multipoint analysis gave a maximum of 4.71 at D12S85 (12q12). Obligate recombinations at D12S1631 and D12S339 defined a 13.6-cM disease-associated haplotype in 12p11.2-q13.1. Nonparametric 2-point and multipoint analyses gave maximum lod scores of 14.2 and 24.9 at D12S345, supporting the locus at 12p11.23-q13.11.

Zimprich et al. (2004) tested 21 white families with apparently autosomal dominant PD for linkage to the PARK8 region, using 29 markers in 167 family members (84 affected). Assuming heterogeneity, the maximum multipoint lod score in the total sample was 2.01, with an estimated 0.32 of families linked. Two families reached significant linkage on their own: family A (German Canadian) and family D (from western Nebraska). Their combined maximum multipoint lod score was 3.33 under an affecteds-only model and 3.92 under a penetrance-dependent model. The 1-lod support interval lay between a CA-repeat polymorphism on genomic clone AC025253 (44.5 Mb) and marker D12S1701 (47.7 Mb).

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported that genetic studies of their kindred suggested linkage to the PARK8 locus on chromosome 12.

In 4 Basque families with autosomal dominant PD, Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) found linkage to the PARK8 locus, with a maximum 2-point lod score of 3.21 (theta = 0.00) at D12S345.

### Molecular Genetics

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) cloned a novel gene containing missense mutations that segregated with PARK8-linked PD in 5 families from England and Spain. They named the encoded protein dardarin.

Zimprich et al. (2004) used high-resolution recombination mapping and candidate gene sequencing in 46 families with autosomal dominant, late-onset parkinsonism linked to 12p11.2-q13.1. They found 6 disease-segregating mutations (5 missense and 1 putative splice site mutation) in the LRRK2 gene. LRRK2 encodes a large, multifunctional protein of the ROCO family that includes a MAPKKK-class protein kinase domain. ⚠︎

In the original Japanese PARK8 family (the Sagamihara family), Funayama et al. (2005) found a missense mutation in the kinase domain of LRRK2 in members with autosomal dominant PD. Neither this mutation nor other known LRRK2 mutations were found in Japanese patients with sporadic PD.

Nichols et al. (2005) screened 767 affected individuals from 358 multiplex PD families for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. Thirty-five (5%) carried it: 34 were heterozygous and 1 was homozygous.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) sequenced the LRRK2 coding region and found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 4 of 61 (6.6%) unrelated families with autosomal dominant PD. The families came from Italy, Portugal and Brazil.

Gilks et al. (2005) directly sequenced LRRK2 exon 41 in 482 patients with idiopathic PD, 263 of them pathologically confirmed. They found the heterozygous 2877510G-A mutation, which results in a gly2019-to-ser substitution (G2019S), in 8 (1.6%) patients.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) sequenced all 51 LRRK2 coding exons in 23 probands from families with dominantly inherited PD. Three probands had heterozygous mutations: 2 carried the known G2019S mutation and 1 carried a novel ile1371-to-val (I1371V) substitution. Mutation frequencies were assessed in 180 neurologically normal controls. In a large family, G2019S segregated completely with PD. Four common coding polymorphisms showed no association with PD in 121 patients and 250 controls.

Farrer et al. (2005) studied 786 PD probands, 32 affected sibs, 1,044 unaffected sibs and 278 unrelated controls with allelic discrimination assays for 9 LRRK2 mutations. Mutations were found in 6 probands, 1 affected sib, 1 unaffected sib and 1 unrelated control. The authors concluded that LRRK2 mutations only rarely cause idiopathic PD.

Lesage et al. (2009) sequenced all 51 LRRK2 coding exons in index cases from 226 families with PD compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance, mostly from France (182) and North Africa (14). They found 79 sequence variants, 29 of them novel. Eight potentially or proven pathogenic mutations were present in 22 probands (9.7%). G2019S was found in 13 probands (5.8%), 6 of them from North Africa (43%). The known heterozygous R1441H and I1371V mutations were each found in 2 probands. Four novel substitutions were considered potentially pathogenic (S52F, N363S, I810V and R1325Q).

Skipper et al. (2005) examined common genetic variation in LRRK2 with tagging SNPs in an epidemiologically matched sporadic case-control series of 932 Chinese individuals. They found a haplotype that markedly increased PD risk when present in 2 copies (OR = 5.5).

In a genomewide association study and 2 replication studies totaling 2,011 cases and 18,381 controls from Japan, Satake et al. (2009) found a strong association between PD and LRRK2 on 12q12 (P = 2.72 x 10(-8)). In 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects, Tan et al. (2010) found that SNPs at the PARK8 locus were associated with increased risk of PD.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ishihara et al. (2006) described patients with familial PD who were homozygous for the LRRK2 6055G-A (G2019S) mutation: 23 Tunisians, 2 Algerians, 2 US patients, 1 Canadian and 1 Moroccan. They compared them with published heterozygous patients and found no observable phenotypic differences. They concluded that the similarity did not support a gene dosage effect.

### Pathogenesis

Giasson et al. (2006) found that Lrrk2 is mainly a soluble cytoplasmic protein of about 250 kD, expressed throughout the brain and in many other organs. Lrrk2 accumulations were seen only in their patient with parkinsonism without Lewy bodies, and Lrrk2 was not detected in other types of pathologic inclusions. In contrast, Zhu et al. (2006) reported that LRRK2 protein is a component of Lewy bodies.

Rajput et al. (2006) found no evidence of a direct interaction of tau or alpha-synuclein with Lrrk2.

Gehrke et al. (2010) found that pathogenic LRRK2 interacts with the microRNA pathway to regulate protein synthesis. In Drosophila, e2f1 and dp mRNAs are translationally repressed by miR-184* and let-7, respectively. Pathogenic LRRK2 antagonized these microRNAs, causing overproduction of E2F1/DP, which the authors showed is critical for LRRK2 pathogenesis. LRRK2 associated with Drosophila Argonaute-1 and human Argonaute-2 of the RNA-induced silencing complex. Pathogenic LRRK2 promoted the binding of phospho-4E-BP1 to human Argonaute-2. Introducing kinase-inactivating mutations into G2019S or I2020T LRRK2 abolished its effect on let-7 activity, indicating dependence on kinase activity. LRRK2 with the R1441G mutation had no effect on let-7 function, which suggested a different mechanism for that mutation.

### Diagnosis

Nichols et al. (2005) recommended including screening for the G2019S mutation in genetic testing for PD. Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) suggested that testing symptomatic patients may help establish the diagnosis. They considered screening asymptomatic individuals of questionable value until penetrance and age-dependent risk are better known. Alcalay et al. (2010) suggested that patients with early-onset PD of Jewish or Hispanic ancestry, with onset at or before 30 years, or with an affected first-degree relative may benefit from genetic counseling. Because penetrance is unknown, they saw little or no role for genetic testing in family planning, predictive or prenatal testing. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Lesage et al. (2005) reported haplotype analyses of LRRK2 in European and North African families with PD that indicated a common founder for the G2019S mutation dating from the 13th century.

Ross et al. (2006) found the G2019S mutation in about 2% (8) of 405 cases with PD or Lewy body disease.

In Jewish Israeli patients with PD, Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) found G2019S in 14.8% of Ashkenazi and 2.7% of non-Ashkenazi patients. It was present in 26% of familial and 10.6% of apparently sporadic Ashkenazi cases. Carrier frequencies were 2.4% in Ashkenazi and 0.4% in non-Ashkenazi controls. They tested for the I2012T, I2020T and R1441G/C/H mutations and found none. Nearly all carriers tested shared a common haplotype. Women and patients with a positive family history were significantly over-represented among carriers, and age at onset was similar in carriers and noncarriers.

Choi et al. (2008) studied 72 unrelated Korean patients with early-onset PD (onset at or before 50 years). None had the G2019S mutation, and 9 (12.5%) were heterozygous for the LRRK2 G2385R polymorphism, which the authors described as a susceptibility allele in Asian PD.

Among 953 individuals with early-onset PD (onset before 51 years), Alcalay et al. (2010) found 35 (3.6%) carrying LRRK2 G2019S. In the results, 31 were described as G2019S carriers (30 heterozygotes and 1 homozygote), and 4 further G2019S carriers also carried a PRKN or GBA mutation. G2019S carriers were more likely than noncarriers to report Jewish ancestry and to have the PIGD motor phenotype.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2008) made transgenic Drosophila expressing wildtype human LRRK2 or LRRK2 G2019S. Expression of either in photoreceptor cells caused retinal degeneration. Neuronal expression caused adult-onset selective loss of dopaminergic neurons, locomotor dysfunction and early mortality, and G2019S caused a more severe parkinsonism-like phenotype than equivalent levels of wildtype LRRK2. L-DOPA improved locomotor impairment but did not prevent loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons.

Lee et al. (2010) found that overexpression of LRRK2 G2019S in primary cortical neurons caused neurite shortening and cell death, whereas kinase-dead versions were not toxic. Kinase inhibitors that act on LRRK2 (GW5074, indirubin-3-prime-monooxime and sorafenib) protected against this toxicity. Delivery of LRRK2 G2019S into mouse striatum with a herpes simplex virus amplicon caused significant loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive nigral neurons after 3 weeks. Kinase-dead G2019S/D1994A LRRK2 caused no neuronal loss. Twice-daily intraperitoneal GW5074 or indirubin-3-prime-monooxime reduced the neuronal loss.

Gehrke et al. (2010) found that Drosophila expressing pathogenic dLRRK (I1915T) or human LRRK2 G2019S lost dopaminergic neurons. The loss was suppressed by removing 1 copy of e2f1 or dp, by overexpressing RBF, or by increasing let-7 or miR-184* levels. Blocking let-7 or miR-184* function in wildtype flies reproduced the toxic effects of pathogenic LRRK2.
