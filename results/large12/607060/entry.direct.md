### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the LRRK2 gene on chromosome 12q12.

### Description

PARK8 is an autosomal dominant form of Parkinson disease with incomplete penetrance. It usually presents as typical levodopa-responsive parkinsonism with bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired postural reflexes, and a clinical picture that closely resembles idiopathic Parkinson disease (Wszolek et al., 1995; Funayama et al., 2002). The neuropathology varies widely, even within a single family. Reported findings include Lewy body Parkinson disease, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, and pathology resembling progressive supranuclear palsy (Zimprich et al., 2004). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Funayama et al. (2002) assigned the name PARK8 to the locus they mapped on chromosome 12 in a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism. Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) named the protein encoded by the PARK8 gene dardarin, from the Basque word 'dardara', meaning tremor. They chose the name because of the tremor seen in Parkinson disease and because several of their families were of Basque descent. Zimprich et al. (2004) called the gene LRRK2 (leucine-rich repeat kinase-2).

### Clinical Features

Wszolek et al. (1995) studied a family (family D) whose ancestors probably came to the United States from England. The pedigree contained 188 individuals over 6 generations, 18 of whom were affected. Affected members developed typical levodopa-responsive Parkinson disease with bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired postural reflexes. Eye movement abnormalities, pyramidal and cerebellar signs, sensory disturbances, and orthostatic blood pressure changes did not occur. The disease progressed slowly. PET with (18F)-6-fluoro-L-dopa in 1 affected individual showed decreased fluorodopa uptake in a pattern consistent with Parkinson disease. Autopsy of another affected individual showed neuronal and pigmentary loss, gliosis, and Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra pars compacta.

Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) reported autosomal dominant Parkinson disease over 5 generations of a family living in Sagamihara City, Japan. The clinical features did not differ from those of common Parkinson disease, but the neuropathology did. In autopsied cases, loss of melanin-containing cells was mild to moderate, neurons of the locus ceruleus were preserved, and no Lewy bodies were found in any region where they are commonly seen in Parkinson disease.

Wszolek et al. (2004) followed up family D. Since the original 1995 report, 4 more individuals had become affected, with a mean age at onset of 65 years. All 4 had the cardinal signs of Parkinson disease and responded well to levodopa. Four autopsies all showed neuronal loss and gliosis in the substantia nigra, but the inclusion pathology differed between cases: Lewy bodies limited to brainstem nuclei in 1, diffuse Lewy bodies in a second, neurofibrillary tangles without Lewy bodies in a third, and neither tangles nor Lewy bodies in the fourth.

Zimprich et al. (2004) reported 6 postmortem diagnoses in affected mutation carriers from families A and D. All showed brainstem dopaminergic degeneration, accompanied by strikingly diverse pathology: changes consistent with Lewy body Parkinson disease, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, and pathology resembling progressive supranuclear palsy. Clinical diagnoses of parkinsonism with dementia, amyotrophy, or both were also noted.

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) described 4 Basque families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease linked to the PARK8 locus. The clinical features were those of typical Parkinson disease, including good response to levodopa, rigidity, and akinesia. Mean age at onset was 55 years.

Silveira-Moriyama et al. (2008) tested odor identification with the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) in 19 parkinsonian and 2 asymptomatic carriers of the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. The mean UPSIT score of the parkinsonian carriers was lower than that of healthy controls (p less than 0.001) and similar to that of patients with Parkinson disease. The 2 asymptomatic carriers scored in the normal range. Postmortem examination of 4 parkinsonian G2019S carriers, including 1 who had tested hyposmic, showed alpha-synuclein deposition with Lewy bodies in the olfactory pathways in all 4.

Chen-Plotkin et al. (2008) screened 434 individuals for LRRK2 mutations: 254 patients from neurodegenerative disease clinics and 180 autopsy cases from a brain bank. Of the 8 cases with a LRRK2 mutation, 2 presented with cognitive deficits and were clinically diagnosed with corticobasal syndrome and primary progressive aphasia, respectively. The authors concluded that LRRK2-associated disease may be more clinically heterogeneous than previously assumed.

Alcalay et al. (2009) studied 925 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before age 51 years) from 13 movement disorder centers. Of these, 34 (3.7%) carried the G2019S mutation, 1 of them homozygous. Compared with noncarriers, carriers had a lower tremor score (P = 0.03) and were more likely to have the postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) motor phenotype (92.3% vs 58.9%; P = 0.003). The association remained after adjustment for disease duration and Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry (odds ratio, 17.7; P less than 0.001). Carriers and noncarriers had similar age at onset, disease duration, motor scores on the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), and Mini-Mental State Examination scores.

Gan-Or et al. (2010) compared initial symptoms in 600 Ashkenazi Parkinson disease patients screened for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation and for 8 GBA mutations. G2019S carriers more often reported muscle stiffness (rigidity; p = 0.007) and balance disturbances (p = 0.008) as initial symptoms, whereas GBA mutation carriers more often reported slowness (bradykinesia).

Saunders-Pullman et al. (2011) gave the UPSIT to 31 Parkinson disease patients carrying the LRRK2 G2019S mutation, 30 Parkinson disease patients without mutations, 28 nonmanifesting carrier family members, and 46 controls. Compared with controls, mean scores were lower in nonmanifesting carriers (difference = -3.518, p = 0.006), in affected carriers (difference = -7.677, p less than 0.0001), and in patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease (difference = -13.810, p less than 0.0001). Affected carriers had better olfaction than noncarrier patients (difference = 6.13, p = 0.0012). Impairment was present in only a subset of the nonmanifesting carriers.

Marras et al. (2011) used a family study design to examine G2019S probands and their first-degree relatives. Tremor was more often a presenting feature in 25 individuals with LRRK2-associated Parkinson disease than in 84 with idiopathic Parkinson disease. Compared with the idiopathic patients, the LRRK2 patients had better olfactory identification, higher Beck Depression Inventory scores, and higher error scores on the Farnsworth-Munsell 100-Hue test of color discrimination. Postural or action tremor was more common in 29 nonmanifesting carriers than in 53 noncarrier family members. The tremor finding contrasts with the lower tremor scores in G2019S carriers reported by Alcalay et al. (2009). ⚠︎

Inzelberg et al. (2012) genotyped 490 Jewish Parkinson disease patients and found the G2019S mutation in 79 (16.1%). A non-skin cancer had been diagnosed in 18 (23%) carriers and 49 (12%) noncarriers (p = 0.01; odds ratio, 2.18). Among Ashkenazi patients, the odds ratio for carriers adjusted for age was 3.38 (95% CI 1.64-6.97, p = 0.0009).

### Other Features

Ross et al. (2006) found the G2019S mutation in about 2% (n = 8) of 405 cases with Parkinson disease or Lewy body disease. Lewy body disease was the most common neuropathology in G2019S-associated Parkinson disease. The mutation was also found in 1 control subject and 1 patient with Alzheimer disease, which the authors attributed to reduced penetrance.

Giasson et al. (2006) identified 3 autopsied patients with the G2019S mutation. Two had classic Parkinson disease with Lewy bodies, and 1 of these also had pathologic changes consistent with Alzheimer disease. The third had parkinsonism without Lewy bodies but had dystrophic neurites in the substantia nigra that stained intensely for Lrrk2. These Lrrk2 accumulations were seen only in this patient, and Lrrk2 was not detected in other types of pathologic inclusions. Biochemically, Lrrk2 was a predominantly soluble cytoplasmic protein of about 250 kD, expressed throughout the brain and in many other organs.

Zhu et al. (2006) reported that LRRK2 protein is a component of Lewy bodies.

Rajput et al. (2006) described a family (family SK) in which the G2019S mutation segregated with slowly progressive parkinsonism. The affected proband had tau-immunopositive neurofibrillary tangle pathology. The authors found no evidence of direct interaction of either tau or alpha-synuclein with Lrrk2.

Nandhagopal et al. (2008) performed multitracer PET imaging of presynaptic dopaminergic integrity in 13 members of family D, a family carrying the R1441C mutation. Five subjects were rescanned 2 to 3 years later. In 2 mutation carriers and 1 genealogically at-risk subject, PET markers declined significantly faster than expected in healthy controls. Reduced dopamine transporter binding was the earliest sign of subclinical dopaminergic dysfunction, and progression to clinical disease was generally associated with the emergence of abnormal fluorodopa uptake.

Aasly et al. (2012) measured CSF amyloid beta-42, total tau, and phosphorylated tau in 26 LRRK2 mutation carriers, of whom 18 were asymptomatic and 8 had a phenotype resembling sporadic Parkinson disease. All also had PET scans of striatal dopaminergic function. Lower CSF amyloid beta-42 and tau levels correlated with lower striatal dopaminergic function, with a significant association between amyloid beta-42 and fluorodopa uptake. When the analysis was restricted to G2019S carriers, significant correlations were also seen for tau.

### Inheritance

Wszolek et al. (1995) observed autosomal dominant inheritance of Parkinson disease in family D. Funayama et al. (2002) found that the disease-associated haplotype in a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism was shared by all patients and also by some unaffected carriers, indicating incomplete penetrance. Ross et al. (2006) also found the G2019S mutation in unaffected individuals, reflecting reduced penetrance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Funayama et al. (2002) performed a genomewide linkage analysis of a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism. The highest parametric 2-point lod score was 4.32 at D12S345 (12p11.21), and the maximum multipoint lod score was 4.71 at D12S85 (12q12). Haplotype analysis detected obligate recombinations at D12S1631 and D12S339, defining a 13.6-cM disease interval in 12p11.2-q13.1. Nonparametric 2-point and multipoint analyses gave maximum lod scores of 14.2 and 24.9, respectively, at D12S345.

Zimprich et al. (2004) tested 21 white families with Parkinson disease consistent with autosomal dominant transmission (167 members: 84 affected and 83 unaffected) for linkage to the PARK8 region. Under heterogeneity, the maximum multipoint lod score was 2.01, with an estimated proportion of linked families of 0.32. Two families, family A (German Canadian) and family D (from western Nebraska), reached significant linkage on their own. Their combined maximum multipoint lod score was 3.33 under an affecteds-only model and 3.92 under a penetrance-dependent model. The 1-lod support interval placed the disease locus between a CA repeat on genomic clone AC025253 (44.5 Mb) and marker D12S1701 (47.7 Mb). Wszolek et al. (2004) also reported that genetic studies of family D suggested linkage to the PARK8 locus.

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) found linkage of autosomal dominant Parkinson disease to the PARK8 locus in 4 Basque families, with a maximum 2-point lod score of 3.21 (theta = 0.00) at D12S345.

### Molecular Genetics

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) cloned a novel gene, which they named dardarin, and found missense mutations in it that segregated with PARK8-linked Parkinson disease in 5 families from England and Spain. ⚠︎

Zimprich et al. (2004) used high-resolution recombination mapping and candidate gene sequencing in 46 families with autosomal dominant, late-onset parkinsonism. They found 6 disease-segregating mutations, 5 missense and 1 putative splice site, in the LRRK2 gene. LRRK2 encodes a large multifunctional protein of the ROCO family that contains a protein kinase domain of the MAPKKK class and several other major functional domains.

Nichols et al. (2005) screened for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 358 multiplex families with Parkinson disease, which included 767 affected individuals, as well as in controls. Thirty-five (5%) carried the mutation, 34 heterozygous and 1 homozygous, and all had typical clinical findings of idiopathic Parkinson disease.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) sequenced the coding region of LRRK2 and found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 4 of 61 (6.6%) unrelated families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease. The families came from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil, and the associated phenotype was broad, including both early and late onset.

Gilks et al. (2005) sequenced LRRK2 exon 41 in 482 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease, 263 of whom had pathologically confirmed disease. They found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 8 (1.6%) patients and concluded that a common single mendelian mutation is implicated in sporadic Parkinson disease.

Funayama et al. (2005) found a missense mutation in the kinase domain of LRRK2 in members of the Sagamihara family with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease. This was the Japanese family originally used to define the PARK8 locus (Funayama et al., 2002). They found neither this mutation nor other known LRRK2 mutations in Japanese patients with sporadic Parkinson disease. ⚠︎

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in 23 probands from families with dominantly inherited Parkinson disease. Three probands carried heterozygous mutations: 2 had G2019S and 1 had a novel I1371V substitution. In a large family, G2019S segregated completely with Parkinson disease. The disease in all 3 families had a phenotype indistinguishable from typical Parkinson disease but a very variable age at onset, which APOE genotype did not explain. Four common coding LRRK2 polymorphisms showed no association with Parkinson disease in 121 patients and 250 controls.

Farrer et al. (2005) genotyped 786 Parkinson disease probands, 32 affected sibs, 1,044 unaffected sibs, and 278 unrelated controls for 9 LRRK2 mutations. They found mutations in 6 probands, 1 affected sib, 1 unaffected sib, and 1 unrelated control, and concluded that LRRK2 mutations only rarely cause idiopathic Parkinson disease.

Lesage et al. (2009) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in index cases from 226 families with Parkinson disease compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance, mostly from France (182) and North Africa (14). They identified 79 sequence variants, 29 of them novel. Eight potentially or proven pathogenic mutations were found in 22 probands (9.7%). G2019S was present in 13 probands (5.8%), 6 of them from North Africa (43%). The known heterozygous R1441H and I1371V mutations were each found in 2 probands, and E334K in 1 patient. Four novel substitutions were considered potentially pathogenic (S52F, N363S, I810V, R1325Q), whereas 2 others (H1216R, T1410M) were probably not causative.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ishihara et al. (2006) described Parkinson disease patients homozygous for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation: 23 Tunisians, 2 Algerians, 2 US patients, 1 Canadian, and 1 Moroccan. They saw no differences between the homozygous and previously described heterozygous phenotypes, and concluded that the findings did not support a gene dosage effect.

### Pathogenesis

Gehrke et al. (2010) found that pathogenic LRRK2 interferes with microRNA (miRNA)-mediated translational repression in Drosophila. Pathogenic Drosophila Lrrk (I1915T) and human LRRK2 (G2019S) antagonized the miRNAs let-7 and miR-184*, which repress translation of the dp and e2f1 mRNAs, respectively, and this led to overproduction of E2F1/DP. In HEK293T cells, LRRK2 G2019S and I2020T relieved let-7 repression of a reporter, and this effect depended on kinase activity. LRRK2 with the R1441G mutation had no such effect. LRRK2 associated with Drosophila Argonaute-1 and human Argonaute-2, and in aged fly brain pathogenic LRRK2 reduced Argonaute-1 protein levels. Pathogenic LRRK2 also promoted association of phosphorylated 4E-BP1 with human Argonaute-2. Removing 1 copy of e2f1 or dp suppressed the loss of dopaminergic neurons caused by pathogenic Lrrk, and increasing let-7 or miR-184* levels attenuated the effects of pathogenic LRRK2. Conversely, blocking let-7 or miR-184* function, or protecting dp mRNA from let-7, reproduced the dopaminergic neuron loss.

Lee et al. (2010) identified compounds that inhibit LRRK2 kinase activity. Overexpression of LRRK2 G2019S caused neurite shortening and cell death in primary cortical neurons, whereas kinase-dead LRRK2 (D1994A) was not toxic. The inhibitors GW5074, indirubin-3-prime-monooxime, and sorafenib protected against G2019S toxicity, whereas compounds that did not inhibit LRRK2 kinase failed to protect. In mice, herpes simplex virus amplicon-mediated delivery of LRRK2 G2019S caused loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons in the substantia nigra; kinase-dead G2019S/D1994A did not. Twice-daily intraperitoneal GW5074 or indirubin-3-prime-monooxime attenuated this neuronal loss. Lee et al. (2010) concluded that LRRK2-induced neurodegeneration in vivo is kinase dependent.

### Diagnosis

Nichols et al. (2005) suggested that screening for the G2019S mutation should be part of genetic testing for Parkinson disease, and Gilks et al. (2005) suggested that testing for this mutation would be important in management and genetic counseling. Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) suggested that testing symptomatic patients may help establish the diagnosis. They considered the value of screening asymptomatic individuals questionable until the penetrance and age-dependent risk of the mutation are better defined. In a review, Dächsel and Farrer (2010) noted that predisposing mutations are diagnostically useful in early or atypical presentations.

### Population Genetics

Lesage et al. (2005) performed LRRK2 haplotype analyses in European and North African families with Parkinson disease and reported a common founder for the G2019S mutation dating from the 13th century.

Skipper et al. (2005) evaluated common variation across LRRK2 using tagging SNPs in a sporadic Parkinson disease case-control series of 932 individuals from the Chinese population. They identified a haplotype that markedly increased disease risk when present in 2 copies (OR = 5.5, 95% CI = 2.1-14.0, P = 0.0001).

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) studied Jewish Israeli patients with Parkinson disease. G2019S was the only LRRK2 mutation found, present in 14.8% of Ashkenazi patients and 2.7% of non-Ashkenazi patients. Among Ashkenazi patients, it was present in 26% of familial cases and 10.6% of apparently sporadic cases. Carrier frequencies in Ashkenazi and non-Ashkenazi controls were 2.4% and 0.4%, respectively. A common shared haplotype was found in nearly all carriers tested. Women and patients with a positive family history were significantly overrepresented among carriers, and age at onset was similar in carriers and noncarriers.

Choi et al. (2008) analyzed 72 unrelated Korean patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset at or before 50 years). Nine (12.5%) were heterozygous for the LRRK2 G2385R variant, previously identified as a susceptibility allele in Asian Parkinson disease, and none carried G2019S.

In a genomewide association study of 2,011 cases and 18,381 controls from Japan, Satake et al. (2009) found a strong association of Parkinson disease with LRRK2 on 12q12 (P = 2.72 x 10(-8)). Comparison with a study of individuals of European ancestry identified LRRK2 as a risk locus shared between the populations. Tan et al. (2010) studied 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects and found that PARK8 SNPs were associated with an increased risk of Parkinson disease. A pooled analysis of the Chinese cohorts and 2 Japanese replication cohorts (1,366 cases and 16,669 controls) confirmed the association.

Alcalay et al. (2010) studied 953 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before 51 years) and found the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 35 (3.6%). G2019S carriers were more likely than noncarriers to report Jewish ancestry.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2008) generated transgenic Drosophila expressing either wildtype human LRRK2 or LRRK2 G2019S. Expression of either form in photoreceptor cells caused retinal degeneration. Expression in neurons caused adult-onset selective loss of dopaminergic neurons, locomotor dysfunction, and early mortality, and G2019S produced a more severe phenotype than equivalent levels of wildtype LRRK2. Treatment with L-DOPA improved locomotor impairment but did not prevent loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 8, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
LRRK2, ARG1441CYS [p.Arg1441Cys]

Zimprich et al. (2004) identified disease-segregating LRRK2 mutations in families with autosomal dominant parkinsonism, including family D from western Nebraska, originally described by Wszolek et al. (1995). Nandhagopal et al. (2008) reported that family D carries the arg1441-to-cys (R1441C) mutation. PET imaging of presymptomatic mutation carriers in this family showed dopaminergic dysfunction that progressed over time.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE 8, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
LRRK2, GLY2019SER [c.6055G>A; p.Gly2019Ser]

Nichols et al. (2005) found the gly2019-to-ser (G2019S) mutation in 35 (5%) of 767 affected individuals from 358 multiplex families with Parkinson disease. Thirty-four were heterozygous and 1 was homozygous, and all had typical clinical findings of idiopathic Parkinson disease.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 4 of 61 (6.6%) unrelated families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil. ⚠︎

Gilks et al. (2005) found the heterozygous G2019S mutation, caused by a 2877510G-A change, in 8 (1.6%) of 482 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 2 of 23 probands with dominantly inherited Parkinson disease and showed that it segregated completely with the disease in a large family.

Ishihara et al. (2006) described 29 Parkinson disease patients homozygous for the 6055G-A (G2019S) mutation, from Tunisia, Algeria, the US, Canada, and Morocco. Their phenotype was indistinguishable from that of heterozygous patients.

Lesage et al. (2005) reported a common founder for the G2019S mutation dating from the 13th century in European and North African families. Lesage et al. (2009) found G2019S in 13 (5.8%) of 226 probands from families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease, including 6 from North Africa (43%).

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE 8, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
LRRK2, ILE1371VAL [p.Ile1371Val]

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) identified a novel heterozygous ile1371-to-val (I1371V) substitution in LRRK2 in 1 of 23 probands from families with dominantly inherited Parkinson disease. Mutation frequencies were evaluated in 180 neurologically normal controls. Lesage et al. (2009) found the heterozygous I1371V mutation in 2 probands from families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
LRRK2, GLY2385ARG [p.Gly2385Arg]

Choi et al. (2008) found the heterozygous gly2385-to-arg (G2385R) variant in LRRK2, described as a susceptibility allele for Parkinson disease in Asians, in 9 (12.5%) of 72 unrelated Korean patients with early-onset Parkinson disease.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE 8, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
LRRK2, ARG1441HIS [p.Arg1441His]

Lesage et al. (2009) found the known heterozygous arg1441-to-his (R1441H) mutation in LRRK2 in 2 probands from a series of 226 families with Parkinson disease compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance, mostly from France and North Africa.
