## PARKINSON DISEASE 8, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT; PARK8

### Phenotype-Gene Relationships

| 12q12 | Parkinson disease 8, autosomal dominant | 607060 | AD | 3 | LRRK2 | 609007 |

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the LRRK2 gene (609007) on chromosome 12q12. ⚠︎

### Description

Gehrke et al. (2010) stated that gain-of-function mutations in the leucine-rich repeat kinase-2 gene (LRRK2) cause familial and sporadic Parkinson disease (PD), characterized by age-dependent degeneration of dopaminergic neurons. Lee et al. (2010) noted that LRRK2 mutations are a common cause of PD, and that patients with LRRK2 mutations cannot be distinguished clinically or neurochemically from patients with idiopathic PD. Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) noted that PARK8 appears to be a common cause of familial PD.

### Nomenclature

Funayama et al. (2002) named the parkinsonism locus on chromosome 12p11.2-q13.1 PARK8.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) named the protein encoded by the PARK8 gene 'dardarin', from the Basque word 'dardara', meaning tremor. They chose the name because tremor is a feature of Parkinson disease and because several of the families they studied were of Basque descent.

### Clinical Features

Wszolek et al. (1995) described a large family (family D) from western Nebraska whose ancestors had probably immigrated to the United States from England. The family included 188 individuals spanning 6 generations, 18 of whom were affected. Affected members had typical levodopa-responsive Parkinson disease with bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired postural reflexes. Eye movement abnormalities, pyramidal and cerebellar signs, sensory disturbances, and orthostatic blood pressure changes did not occur. The disease progressed slowly. In one affected member, PET with [18F]-6-fluoro-L-dopa showed decreased fluorodopa uptake in a pattern consistent with PD. Autopsy of another affected member showed neuronal and pigmentary loss, gliosis, and Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra pars compacta. The authors concluded that the disorder closely resembled, if it was not identical to, idiopathic PD.

Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) reported a family from Sagamihara City, Japan, with Parkinson disease in 5 generations. Clinically, the disease did not differ from common PD. Onset was at an older age, and the response to levodopa therapy was good. The neuropathology in autopsied family members differed from that of typical PD. Loss of melanin-containing cells was mild to moderate, and the number of locus ceruleus neurons was maintained. No Lewy bodies were found in any of the regions where Lewy bodies are often seen in PD.

Funayama et al. (2002) described a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism. The clinical features were compatible with those of common Parkinson disease. Funayama et al. (2005) later noted that this family, the Sagamihara family, had distinctive pathology: pure nigral degeneration without Lewy bodies. ⚠︎

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported follow-up of the parkinsonian kindred first described in 1995. Since that report, 4 more individuals had become affected, with a mean age at onset of 65 years. All 4 had the cardinal signs of Parkinson disease and responded well to levodopa. All 4 autopsies in the kindred showed neuronal loss and gliosis in the substantia nigra, but the other pathology varied. One case had Lewy bodies confined to brainstem nuclei, and a second had diffuse Lewy bodies. A third had neurofibrillary tangles without Lewy bodies, and the fourth had neither. ⚠︎

Zimprich et al. (2004) reported 6 postmortem examinations of affected LRRK2 mutation carriers from families A and D. All showed brainstem dopaminergic degeneration, but the accompanying pathologies were strikingly diverse. They included Lewy body Parkinson disease, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, and pathology resembling progressive supranuclear palsy. Some carriers had clinical diagnoses of parkinsonism with dementia, amyotrophy, or both. ⚠︎

Nichols et al. (2005) reported that individuals with the LRRK2 G2019S mutation had clinical findings typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease. Di Fonzo et al. (2005) reported a broad phenotype in G2019S families, with both early and late disease onset.

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) reported that affected members of 4 Basque families with PARK8-linked autosomal dominant Parkinson disease had the clinical features of typical PD, including rigidity, akinesia, and a good response to levodopa. The mean age at onset was 55 years.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) reported that the disease in 3 families with LRRK2 mutations could not be distinguished from typical Parkinson disease. Age at onset varied widely in all 3 families, and APOE genotypes did not explain this variation.

Ross et al. (2006) reported that Lewy body disease was the most common neuropathology in Parkinson disease associated with LRRK2 G2019S.

Giasson et al. (2006) identified 3 autopsied patients with the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. Two had classic Parkinson disease with Lewy bodies, and one of these also had concurrent pathologic changes consistent with Alzheimer disease. The third patient had parkinsonism without Lewy bodies, but dystrophic neurites in the substantia nigra stained intensely for Lrrk2. These Lrrk2 accumulations were found only in this patient.

Rajput et al. (2006) described a family (Family SK) with slowly progressive parkinsonism. The affected proband had tau-immunopositive neurofibrillary tangle pathology rather than the alpha-synuclein-immunopositive Lewy body pathology mainly linked to LRRK2 G2019S. ⚠︎

Chen-Plotkin et al. (2008) screened 434 individuals for LRRK2 mutations: 254 patients from neurodegenerative disease clinics and 180 neurodegenerative disease autopsy cases. Eight carried a LRRK2 mutation. Two of the 8 carriers presented first with cognitive deficits and were clinically diagnosed with corticobasal syndrome and primary progressive aphasia, respectively. The authors concluded that LRRK2-associated neurodegenerative disease may be more clinically varied than previously assumed.

Silveira-Moriyama et al. (2008) evaluated olfaction with the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) in 19 parkinsonian and 2 asymptomatic carriers of the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. Mean UPSIT scores in parkinsonian carriers were lower than in healthy controls (p less than 0.001) and similar to those of patients with Parkinson disease (p greater than 0.999). The 2 asymptomatic carriers scored in the normal range. The authors also examined the rhinencephalon postmortem in 4 parkinsonian G2019S carriers, including 1 with clinical hyposmia. All 4 had alpha-synuclein deposition with Lewy bodies in the olfactory pathways.

Nandhagopal et al. (2008) performed multitracer PET of presynaptic dopaminergic integrity in 13 members of family D, a parkinsonian kindred from western Nebraska carrying the LRRK2 R1441C mutation. Five subjects were rescanned 2 to 3 years later. In 2 mutation carriers and 1 genealogically at-risk subject, PET markers declined significantly faster than expected in healthy controls. Reduced dopamine transporter binding was the earliest sign of subclinical dopaminergic dysfunction. Progression to clinical disease generally coincided with the emergence of abnormal fluorodopa uptake. The authors concluded that presymptomatic members of the kindred had dopaminergic dysfunction that progressed over time.

Among probands with early-onset Parkinson disease, Alcalay et al. (2009) found that LRRK2 G2019S carriers and noncarriers were similar in age at onset (range, 13 to 50 years), disease duration, age at examination, UPDRS-III score, MMSE score, and levodopa dose. Of carriers with complete UPDRS data, 88.5% had asymmetric onset and 44.0% presented with rest tremor. In the cohort as a whole, the postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) phenotype was associated with more severe disease than the tremor-dominant phenotype, but carriers and noncarriers did not differ on these measures of severity.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that LRRK2 G2019S carriers with early-onset Parkinson disease were more likely than noncarriers to have the PIGD motor phenotype: 96% (25 carriers) versus 58% of noncarriers. Carriers had a mean age at onset of 42 years, similar to that of noncarriers. The presenting symptom did not differ significantly between mutation groups. ⚠︎

Saunders-Pullman et al. (2011) gave the UPSIT to 31 patients with Parkinson disease associated with LRRK2 G2019S (manifesting carriers), 30 patients with PD who did not carry the mutation, 28 carrier relatives without PD (nonmanifesting carriers), and 46 controls. Compared with controls, mean scores were lower in nonmanifesting carriers (difference = -3.518, p = 0.006), manifesting carriers (difference = -7.677), and noncarrier PD patients (difference = -13.810). Olfaction was better in manifesting carriers than in non-LRRK2 PD patients (difference = 6.13, p = 0.0012). The authors concluded that olfaction is impaired in G2019S-related PD, but less overall than in other PD. Olfaction was also impaired in a subset of the nonmanifesting carriers. The authors suggested that olfaction may be a marker for the development of PD in this group and that longitudinal studies are needed.

Marras et al. (2011) used a family study design, with patients recruited from 4 movement disorders centers, to compare 25 individuals with LRRK2 G2019S-associated Parkinson disease with 84 individuals with idiopathic PD. Tremor was more often a presenting feature in LRRK2-PD. Patients with LRRK2-PD also had better olfactory identification, higher Beck Depression Inventory scores, and worse color discrimination on the Farnsworth-Munsell 100-Hue test. The authors concluded that the phenotype largely overlapped with that of idiopathic PD. Postural or action tremor was more common in 29 nonmanifesting G2019S carriers than in 53 noncarriers from the same families, and the authors suggested that it may be an early sign. Nonparkinsonian family members had higher UPDRS motor scores, more constipation, and worse color discrimination than controls, whether or not they carried the mutation.

### Other Features

Inzelberg et al. (2012) genotyped 490 Jewish patients with Parkinson disease for LRRK2 G2019S and found 79 carriers (16.1%). Non-skin cancer was more common in carriers than in noncarriers: 18 of 79 carriers (23%) versus 49 noncarriers (12%) (p = 0.01; OR, 2.18; 95% CI, 1.19-3.99). Among Ashkenazi patients, the age-adjusted OR for carriers was 3.38 (95% CI, 1.64-6.97; p = 0.0009). The authors concluded that Ashkenazi Jewish patients with PD who carry G2019S are more likely than noncarriers to have a concomitant non-skin cancer. Bandmann and Cookson (2012) discussed whether this link between LRRK2-associated PD and cancer is causal or only an association.

### Biochemical Features

Aasly et al. (2012) measured CSF amyloid beta-42 (Abeta42), total tau, and phosphorylated tau in 26 LRRK2 mutation carriers, 18 asymptomatic and 8 with a phenotype resembling sporadic Parkinson disease. They compared these levels with PET measures of striatal dopaminergic function using 18F-6-fluoro-L-dopa (FD), 11C-dihydrotetrabenazine, and 11C-d-threo-methylphenidate. Lower CSF Abeta42 and tau levels correlated with lower striatal dopaminergic function on all 3 tracers, and the association between Abeta42 and FD uptake was significant. When the analysis was limited to carriers of G2019S, the most common LRRK2 variant in the cohort, the correlations with tau were also significant. The authors concluded that how amyloid beta and tau are handled is likely important in both LRRK2-related and sporadic PD, even early in the disease. They suggested that these CSF proteins might serve as biomarkers of early or presymptomatic disease. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Wszolek et al. (1995) found autosomal dominant inheritance of parkinsonism in family D from western Nebraska. Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) reported autosomal dominant inheritance of Parkinson disease through 5 generations of the Japanese Sagamihara family.

In the Japanese family used to map PARK8, Funayama et al. (2002) found that all patients shared the disease-associated haplotype, but some unaffected individuals also carried it. They suggested that penetrance in this family was incomplete and that environmental or other genetic factors modify disease expression.

Ross et al. (2006) found LRRK2 G2019S in 1 control subject and 1 patient with Alzheimer disease, and attributed this to reduced penetrance.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that, although LRRK2-related Parkinson disease is autosomal dominant, PRKN carriers were more likely than LRRK2 carriers to report a family history of PD. G2019S was carried by 3.6% of patients with a family history of PD and 3.1% of those without one. The authors suggested that incomplete penetrance of G2019S could be one explanation, and noted that the penetrance of these mutations remains unknown.

### Mapping

Funayama et al. (2002) performed genomewide linkage analysis in a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism. Parametric 2-point analysis gave a maximum lod score of 4.32 at D12S345 (12p11.21), and multipoint analysis gave a maximum lod score of 4.71 at D12S85 (12q12). Haplotype analysis identified obligate recombinations at D12S1631 and D12S339, which defined a 13.6-cM disease-associated interval in 12p11.2-q13.1. Nonparametric 2-point and multipoint analyses gave maximum lod scores of 14.2 and 24.9 at D12S345.

Zimprich et al. (2004) used 29 markers to test 21 white families for linkage to the PARK8 region on centromeric chromosome 12. Each family had Parkinson disease in a pattern compatible with autosomal dominant transmission, with at least 3 affected individuals in more than 1 generation. In total, 167 family members were tested (84 affected and 83 unaffected). Assuming heterogeneity and using an affecteds-only model, the authors obtained a maximum multipoint lod score of 2.01 (P = 0.0047) in the whole sample, with an estimated 0.32 proportion of linked families. Two families each showed significant linkage to PARK8: family A (German Canadian) and family D (from western Nebraska). Their combined maximum multipoint lod score was 3.33 under an affecteds-only model and 3.92 under a penetrance-dependent model. The 1-lod support interval placed the disease locus between a CA repeat on genomic clone AC025253 (44.5 Mb) and marker D12S1701 (47.7 Mb).

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported that genetic studies suggested linkage of the autosomal dominant parkinsonism in their kindred to the PARK8 locus on chromosome 12.

Zimprich et al. (2004) refined the PARK8 region on chromosome 12p11.2-q13.1 by high-resolution recombination mapping in families with autosomal dominant, late-onset parkinsonism.

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) found evidence for linkage to the PARK8 locus in 4 Basque families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease, with a maximum 2-point lod score of 3.21 (theta = 0.00) at marker D12S345.

### Heterogeneity

Funayama et al. (2002) noted that the chromosome 12 locus they mapped differed from every locus then known for hereditary parkinsonism.

Zimprich et al. (2004) concluded that PARK8 causes disease in a subset of white families with autosomal dominant parkinsonism, with an estimated 0.32 proportion of linked families. They suggested that PARK8 could be a relatively common locus.

In a Japanese genome-wide association study, Satake et al. (2009) found that BST1 and MAPT showed population differences compared with a European study, suggesting that population differences contribute to genetic heterogeneity in Parkinson disease.

### Molecular Genetics

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) cloned a novel gene in the PARK8 region. They found missense mutations in the gene that segregated with PARK8-linked Parkinson disease in 5 families from England and Spain.

Zimprich et al. (2004) studied 46 families with autosomal dominant, late-onset parkinsonism that had been linked to chromosome 12p11.2-q13.1 (PARK8). Using high-resolution recombination mapping and candidate gene sequencing, they identified 6 disease-segregating mutations in the LRRK2 gene: 5 missense mutations and 1 putative splice site mutation. They noted that LRRK2 belongs to the ROCO protein family and contains a MAPKKK-class protein kinase domain along with several other major functional domains. ⚠︎

Nichols et al. (2005) screened genomic DNA from 767 affected individuals in 358 multiplex families with familial Parkinson disease, as well as controls, for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. Thirty-five affected individuals (5%) carried the mutation: 34 were heterozygous and 1 was homozygous. The authors suggested that this single LRRK2 mutation causes PD in 5% of individuals with familial disease.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) sequenced the coding region of LRRK2 in 61 unrelated families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease. They found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 4 families (6.6%), from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil, which showed that the mutation occurs in different populations. ⚠︎

Gilks et al. (2005) directly sequenced exon 41 of LRRK2 in 482 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease, 263 of whom had pathologically confirmed disease. Eight patients (1.6%) were heterozygous for the G2019S mutation. The authors concluded that a common single Mendelian mutation is implicated in sporadic PD.

Funayama et al. (2005) found a missense mutation in the kinase domain of LRRK2 in affected members of the Japanese Sagamihara family, the family on which the PARK8 locus was originally defined. They did not find this mutation, or other known LRRK2 mutations, in Japanese patients with sporadic Parkinson disease.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in 23 probands from families with dominantly inherited Parkinson disease. Three probands had heterozygous LRRK2 mutations. Two had the known G2019S substitution, and 1 had a novel I1371V substitution. Mutation frequencies were assessed in 180 neurologically normal controls. In a large family, G2019S segregated completely with PD. In a separate case-control study of 121 patients with PD and 250 controls, mostly white Canadians, none of 4 coding LRRK2 polymorphisms was associated with PD at the allelic or genotypic level (p greater than 0.17). In the patient group, 84% had onset before age 50 years and 42% had a positive family history. Neither these genotypes nor APOE genotypes modified age at onset (p greater than 0.20). ⚠︎

Farrer et al. (2005) used allelic discrimination assays to screen 786 probands with Parkinson disease, 32 affected sibs, 1,044 unaffected sibs, and 278 unrelated controls for 9 LRRK2 mutations. Mutations were found in 6 probands, 1 affected sib, 1 unaffected sib, and 1 unrelated control. The authors concluded that LRRK2 mutations rarely cause idiopathic PD.

Skipper et al. (2005) evaluated common variation across LRRK2 with tagging SNPs in an epidemiologically matched case-control series of 932 Chinese individuals, either patients with sporadic Parkinson disease or controls. Single-tSNP and haplotype analyses both gave significant evidence of disease association. Carrying 2 copies of one haplotype sharply increased disease risk (OR = 5.5; 95% CI, 2.1-14.0; P = 0.0001). The authors concluded that common LRRK2 variation contributes to the risk of sporadic PD in the Chinese population.

Ross et al. (2006) found the LRRK2 G2019S substitution in about 2% (8) of 405 cases of Parkinson disease or Lewy body disease.

In Family SK, Rajput et al. (2006) found that LRRK2 G2019S segregated with slowly progressive parkinsonism, although the proband had tau rather than alpha-synuclein pathology. The authors suggested that alpha-synucleinopathy and tauopathy may be alternate outcomes of the same genetic cause. ⚠︎

Choi et al. (2008) sequenced SNCA, PARKIN, DJ-1, PINK1, and LRRK2 and analyzed gene dosage by MLPA in 72 unrelated Korean patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset at or before age 50 years), recruited from 10 movement disorder clinics in South Korea. Nine patients (12.5%) were heterozygous for LRRK2 G2385R, a known Asian susceptibility allele. None carried G2019S.

Lesage et al. (2009) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in index cases from 226 families with Parkinson disease compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance, most from France (182) and North Africa (14). They found 79 sequence variants, 29 of them novel. Eight potentially or proven pathogenic mutations were found in 22 probands (9.7%), most of them in the functional domains of the LRRK2 protein. The known heterozygous mutations R1441H and I1371V were each found in 2 probands. Four novel amino acid substitutions were considered potentially pathogenic: S52F, N363S, I810V, and R1325Q. Two other novel variants, H1216R and T1410M, were considered probably not causative, and the E334K variant was found in a single patient. The authors concluded that LRRK2 mutations are a common cause of autosomal dominant PD in Europe and North Africa.

Satake et al. (2009) carried out a genome-wide association study and 2 replication studies in a total of 2,011 Japanese patients with Parkinson disease and 18,381 controls. They found strong association of PD with variants at LRRK2 on 12q12 (P = 2.72 x 10(-8)) and at SNCA on 4q22 (P = 7.35 x 10(-17)). Comparison with a European GWAS identified LRRK2 as a risk locus shared between populations. The authors concluded that autosomal dominant parkinsonism loci are involved in typical PD.

Alcalay et al. (2009) genotyped 925 probands with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset at 50 years or younger), from 13 movement disorders centers, for the LRRK2 mutations G2019S, I2020T, R1441C, and Y1699C. Thirty-four probands (3.7%) carried G2019S, 14 of whom had been reported previously. One Ashkenazi Jewish carrier was homozygous. No other mutations were found. Carriers were more likely than noncarriers to be Ashkenazi Jewish (55.9% vs 11.9%), but were not more likely to have a first-degree relative with PD.

Tan et al. (2010) analyzed 13 SNPs at loci linked to Parkinson disease by genome-wide association studies (PARK16, PARK8, PARK1, and BST1) in 2 case-control cohorts totaling 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects. PARK8 SNPs were associated with increased risk of PD. In a pooled analysis that added 2 Japanese replication cohorts (1,366 PD patients and 16,669 controls), association with the PARK8 locus was robust, and its strength was similar in Chinese and Japanese subjects. ⚠︎

Among patients with early-onset Parkinson disease, Alcalay et al. (2010) identified 4 who carried LRRK2 G2019S together with a mutation in another gene. A Jewish man also carried a heterozygous exon 5-6 deletion in PRKN (602544) and had onset at age 41. A non-Jewish man also carried GBA1 (606463) L444P and had onset at age 31. Two Jewish patients also carried GBA1 N370S, with onset at ages 47 and 49. None of the 4 patients reported PD in a first-degree relative.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ishihara et al. (2006) described 29 patients with familial Parkinson disease who were homozygous for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation, gathered by an international consortium: 23 Tunisians, 2 Algerians, 2 US patients, 1 Canadian, and 1 Moroccan. The homozygous phenotype showed no observable differences from previously published descriptions of heterozygous patients. The authors concluded that LRRK2-related PD has typical clinical features and that the similarities do not support a gene dosage effect.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) found that women and patients with a positive family history of Parkinson disease were significantly overrepresented among Jewish Israeli carriers of LRRK2 G2019S. Age at onset was similar in carriers and noncarriers. The authors concluded that sex affects the frequency of the mutation among patients.

Lesage et al. (2008) examined whether LRRK2 G2019S is related to dyskinesias in North African patients with Parkinson disease.

Alcalay et al. (2009) found that G2019S carriers with early-onset Parkinson disease had lower UPDRS tremor scores than noncarriers (P = 0.03). Carriers were also more likely to have the postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) phenotype (92.3% vs 58.9%). Only 3.8% of carriers had the tremor-dominant phenotype, compared with 27.4% of noncarriers. The association with PIGD persisted after adjustment for disease duration and Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry (OR 17.7), and held when Ashkenazi and non-Ashkenazi probands were analyzed separately. The authors noted that this contrasted with earlier reports linking LRRK2 to tremor-dominant disease.

Gan-Or et al. (2010) screened 600 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with Parkinson disease for LRRK2 G2019S and 8 GBA mutations, and compared the initial symptoms reported by GBA mutation carriers, G2019S carriers, and noncarriers. Muscle stiffness (rigidity; p = 0.007) and balance disturbances (p = 0.008) were more often reported as initial symptoms by G2019S carriers. Slowness (bradykinesia; p = 0.021) was more often reported by GBA mutation carriers. The authors suggested that LRRK2 and GBA mutations have distinct effects on the initial presentation of PD.

### Pathogenesis

Zimprich et al. (2004) suggested that LRRK2 may be central to the pathogenesis of several major neurodegenerative disorders associated with parkinsonism.

Using a Lrrk2-specific antibody, Giasson et al. (2006) found that Lrrk2 is mainly a soluble cytoplasmic protein of about 250 kD, expressed throughout the brain and in many other organs, and that it was not detected in other types of pathologic inclusions. They suggested that LRRK2 mutations may prime select neuronal populations to cellular insults that can lead to aberrant protein aggregation. Zhu et al. (2006) reported that LRRK2 protein is a component of Lewy bodies. Rajput et al. (2006) found no evidence that either tau or alpha-synuclein interacts directly with LRRK2.

Lee et al. (2010) found that overexpression of either wildtype or G2019S LRRK2 injured primary cortical neurons, as measured by neurite shortening. G2019S overexpression also caused cell death, as measured by TUNEL. Kinase-dead versions of wildtype LRRK2 (D1994A) and of G2019S LRRK2 (G2019S plus D1994A) were not toxic. The LRRK2 kinase inhibitors GW5074, indirubin-3-prime-monooxime, and sorafenib (each 0.5 microM) attenuated or completely prevented G2019S-induced injury and death. ZM336372 and indirubin, which do not inhibit LRRK2 or LRRK1 kinase activity, did not protect. The authors concluded that the protection came from inhibition of LRRK2 kinase activity rather than inhibition of Raf, cyclin-dependent kinase, or GSK3-beta.

Gehrke et al. (2010) proposed that pathogenic LRRK2 impairs microRNA (miRNA)-mediated translational repression. In flies, ubiquitous or dopaminergic neuron-specific expression of pathogenic Drosophila dLRRK I1915T or human LRRK2 G2019S stimulated expression of an in vivo reporter carrying let-7-binding sites more than the wildtype proteins did. dLRRK RNAi decreased reporter expression, and kinase-dead dLRRK had no effect. Reporter mRNA levels were unchanged. In HEK293T cells, LRRK2 I2020T or G2019S efficiently suppressed let-7 inhibition of a luciferase reporter, and adding kinase-inactivating mutations abolished this effect. By coimmunoprecipitation, LRRK2 associated with Argonaute proteins of the RNA-induced silencing complex (human Ago2 and Drosophila Ago1), apparently directly rather than through RNA. Pathogenic LRRK2 did not change let-7 levels. The authors concluded that LRRK2 antagonizes let-7 function at the translational level in a kinase-dependent manner.

Gehrke et al. (2010) found that 4E-BP1 associated with human Ago2, and that dLRRK I1915T and LRRK2 G2019S promoted this association more effectively than the wildtype proteins, presumably through increased phosphorylation of 4E-BP1. Phosphomimetic 4E-BP attenuated let-7 effects and was toxic to dopaminergic neurons. Nonphosphorylatable 4E-BP blocked the ability of LRRK2 G2019S to antagonize let-7. Translational profiling of fly heads identified e2f1 and dp as targets whose translation is upregulated by pathogenic LRRK2, through a miR-184*-binding site in the e2f1 3-prime UTR and a let-7-binding site in the dp 3-prime UTR. E2F1 and DP protein levels were increased by pathogenic dLRRK or LRRK2 and decreased in dLRRK-null mutants, without changes in mRNA level. Removing 1 copy of e2f1 or dp, or overexpressing the negative regulator RBF, suppressed the dopaminergic neuron phenotypes and oxidative stress sensitivity of dLRRK I1915T animals. Pathogenic dLRRK did not affect the activities of the bantam and miR-14 miRNAs in the eye, suggesting miRNA- or tissue-specific regulation. The authors concluded that pathogenic LRRK2 antagonizes let-7 and miR-184*, causing overproduction of E2F1/DP, which in postmitotic neurons may lead to abortive cell division and cell death. Because LRRK2 effects appeared only in aged animals, they suggested that LRRK2 is particularly important for miRNA regulation under stress.

Gehrke et al. (2010) reported that LRRK2 carrying the pathogenic R1441G mutation had no effect on let-7 function. Transgenic flies expressing dLRRK with the equivalent R1069G mutation showed no dopaminergic neuron loss. The authors suggested that this mutation acts through a different mechanism from G2019S and I2020T.

Koch et al. (2026) noted that gain of function of LRRK2 increases Parkinson disease risk, whereas heterozygous predicted loss-of-function variants of LRRK2 have not been associated with any phenotype, including PD.

### Diagnosis

Nichols et al. (2005) recommended including screening for LRRK2 G2019S in genetic testing for Parkinson disease. Gilks et al. (2005) suggested that testing for G2019S will be important in the management and genetic counseling of patients with PD.

Dächsel and Farrer (2010) noted that mutations predisposing to Parkinson disease are diagnostically useful in early or atypical presentations. They also noted that affected and asymptomatic LRRK2 carriers offer an opportunity to define the natural history of PD, including penetrance, motor symptoms, nonmotor comorbidities, and associated biomarkers.

### Clinical Management

Lee et al. (2010) screened 84 kinase and phosphatase inhibitors at 16 microM against LRRK2 autophosphorylation and LRRK2-mediated phosphorylation of myelin basic protein. Eight compounds substantially inhibited LRRK2 kinase activity: the indolinones staurosporine, GF 109203X, Ro 31-8220, 5-iodotubercidin, GW5074, and indirubin-3-prime-monooxime, and the anthracenes SP 600125 and damnacanthal. None enhanced LRRK2 activity. All except indirubin-3-prime-monooxime had relatively similar potency against wildtype and G2019S LRRK2 autophosphorylation; indirubin-3-prime-monooxime inhibited G2019S autophosphorylation more potently. All 8 had a similar inhibitory profile against LRRK1. Among Raf kinase inhibitors, GW5074 inhibited both LRRK2 and LRRK1, sorafenib and Raf inhibitor IV were relatively selective for LRRK2, and ZM336372 had minimal or no effect on either kinase. GW5074 and indirubin-3-prime-monooxime, but not ZM336372 or indirubin, also inhibited LRRK2-mediated phosphorylation of 4E-BP1, a putative physiologic substrate.

Lee et al. (2010) concluded that LRRK2-induced neuronal degeneration in vivo is kinase dependent, and that pharmacologic inhibition of LRRK2 kinase is a potentially promising neuroprotective therapy for Parkinson disease. They cautioned that an authentic LRRK2 substrate had not yet been identified, which raised concerns about the physiologic relevance of screens based on autophosphorylation and artificial substrates.

### Population Genetics

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) concluded that LRRK2 G2019S is a common mutation in dominantly inherited Parkinson disease, present in 6.6% of the unrelated autosomal dominant families they studied.

From LRRK2 haplotype analyses in European and North African families with Parkinson disease, Lesage et al. (2005) concluded that G2019S arose from a common founder dating from the 13th century.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) screened Jewish Israeli patients with Parkinson disease for the LRRK2 mutations G2019S, I2012T, I2020T, and R1441G/C/H, and found only G2019S. It was present in 14.8% of Ashkenazi and 2.7% of non-Ashkenazi patients, and in 26% of Ashkenazi familial cases and 10.6% of apparently sporadic Ashkenazi cases. Carrier frequencies were 2.4% in Ashkenazi controls and 0.4% in non-Ashkenazi controls. Haplotypes were determined with 6 microsatellite markers spanning LRRK2 in 76 G2019S carriers and 50 Ashkenazi noncarrier patients. All non-Ashkenazi and half-Ashkenazi carriers shared a common haplotype, as did all but 2 of the full-Ashkenazi carriers tested.

Choi et al. (2008) found no LRRK2 G2019S mutation among 72 Korean patients with early-onset Parkinson disease, whereas 12.5% were heterozygous for the G2385R risk variant. Overall, variants in PARKIN, PINK1, LRRK2, and SNCA accounted for causes or genetic risk factors in 25% of Korean early-onset PD. PARKIN mutations were the most common genetic cause.

Lesage et al. (2009) identified LRRK2 G2019S in 13 of 226 probands (5.8%) with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease. Six of these probands were from North Africa, accounting for 43% of the North African probands.

In the CORE-PD study, Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset at 50 years or younger) from 13 movement disorders centers, recruited without regard to family history. Thirty-five (3.6%) carried LRRK2 G2019S. Of the 31 G2019S carriers with no mutation in another gene, 30 were heterozygous and 1 was homozygous. One additional patient carried LRRK2 G2385R. No other pathogenic LRRK2 mutations (R1441C, I2020T, Y1699C) were found. Of the G2019S-only carriers, 55% were Jewish, compared with 12% of noncarriers. Among patients with Jewish ancestry, G2019S was carried by 13.6% of those with a family history of PD and 12.2% of those without one. Grandparents' country of origin was known for 11 of the 15 non-Jewish carriers. Four reported Southern European descent (Italy or Portugal), 4 mixed European descent, 1 Eastern European ancestry, and 2 Puerto Rican ancestry.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2008) used the GAL4/UAS system to generate transgenic Drosophila expressing wildtype human LRRK2 or LRRK2 G2019S. Expression of either form in photoreceptor cells caused retinal degeneration. Neuronal expression of either form caused adult-onset selective loss of dopaminergic neurons, locomotor dysfunction, and early death. At equivalent expression levels, G2019S caused a more severe parkinsonism-like phenotype than wildtype LRRK2. L-DOPA treatment improved the locomotor impairment but did not prevent loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons. The authors considered this, to their knowledge, the first in vivo gain-of-function model to recapitulate several key features of LRRK2-linked human parkinsonism.

In Drosophila, Gehrke et al. (2010) found that pathogenic human LRRK2 significantly reduced dAgo1 protein in aged but not young flies, and that dAgo1 was increased in dLRRK mutants. Ago1 heterozygosity worsened the climbing defect and dopaminergic neuron loss in pathogenic dLRRK or LRRK2 transgenics. Dicer1 overexpression suppressed dLRRK I1915T toxicity, and Dicer1 RNAi worsened it. Overexpression or injection of let-7 or miR-184* lowered DP or E2F1 levels, respectively, and partially rescued dLRRK I1915T phenotypes. Loss of miRNA function phenocopied pathogenic LRRK2. Let-7 mutants had reduced locomotor activity and a specific reduction in dopaminergic neurons. Antagomirs against let-7 or miR-184* raised DP or E2F1 levels and caused dopaminergic neuron loss and reduced climbing, effects attenuated by e2f1 or dp heterozygosity. E2F1 overexpression alone was enough to cause dopaminergic neuron degeneration. Disrupting the let-7/dp interaction, either with a dp target protector or with genomic dp transgenes lacking functional let-7-binding sites, caused DP accumulation, dopaminergic neuron loss, and locomotor defects.

Lee et al. (2010) developed a mouse model of LRRK2 dopaminergic neurotoxicity based on herpes simplex virus (HSV) amplicons. Three weeks after stereotaxic injection into the striatum, HSV-delivered LRRK2 G2019S caused significant loss of ipsilateral tyrosine hydroxylase-positive substantia nigra neurons compared with wildtype LRRK2 and eGFP control viruses. Kinase-dead LRRK2 G2019S/D1994A caused no neuronal loss, despite equivalent overexpression. Twice-daily intraperitoneal injections of the LRRK2 kinase inhibitors GW5074 or indirubin-3-prime-monooxime (2.5 mg/kg) attenuated the neuronal loss compared with controls. GW5074 also rescued the reduced density of TH-positive fibers and prevented the increase in isolectin B4-positive inflammatory cells in the striatum and substantia nigra compacta.

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 8, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**

Nichols et al. (2005) screened 767 affected individuals from 358 multiplex families with familial Parkinson disease for the gly2019-to-ser (G2019S) mutation in LRRK2. Thirty-five affected individuals (5%) carried the mutation: 34 were heterozygous and 1 was homozygous.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 4 of 61 unrelated families with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease (6.6%), from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil.

Gilks et al. (2005) found a heterozygous 2877510G-A transition in exon 41 of LRRK2, resulting in a G2019S substitution, in 8 (1.6%) of 482 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease.

Using haplotype analyses in European and North African families with Parkinson disease, Lesage et al. (2005) concluded that G2019S arose from a common founder dating from the 13th century.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) found heterozygous G2019S in 2 of 23 probands from families with dominantly inherited Parkinson disease. In a large family, the mutation segregated completely with PD.

Ross et al. (2006) found G2019S in 8 of 405 cases of Parkinson disease or Lewy body disease. Lewy body disease was the most common neuropathology. The mutation was also found in 1 control and 1 patient with Alzheimer disease, which the authors attributed to reduced penetrance.

Giasson et al. (2006) studied 3 autopsied G2019S carriers. Two had classic Parkinson disease with Lewy bodies. The third had parkinsonism without Lewy bodies, with Lrrk2-positive dystrophic neurites in the substantia nigra.

Ishihara et al. (2006) described 29 patients with familial Parkinson disease from Tunisia, Algeria, the US, Canada, and Morocco who were homozygous for the 6055G-A (G2019S) mutation. Their phenotype did not differ observably from that of heterozygous patients.

Rajput et al. (2006) found that G2019S segregated with slowly progressive parkinsonism in Family SK. The proband had tau-immunopositive neurofibrillary tangle pathology rather than Lewy body pathology.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) found G2019S in 14.8% of Ashkenazi and 2.7% of non-Ashkenazi Jewish Israeli patients with Parkinson disease, compared with 2.4% and 0.4% of the respective controls. Nearly all carriers shared a common haplotype.

Liu et al. (2008) found that transgenic Drosophila expressing human LRRK2 G2019S in neurons developed a more severe parkinsonism-like phenotype than flies expressing equivalent levels of wildtype LRRK2. The phenotype included adult-onset loss of dopaminergic neurons, locomotor dysfunction, and early death.

Lesage et al. (2009) identified G2019S in 13 of 226 probands (5.8%) with autosomal dominant Parkinson disease, including 6 North African probands (43% of the North African probands).

Alcalay et al. (2009) found G2019S in 34 of 925 probands (3.7%) with early-onset Parkinson disease. One Ashkenazi Jewish carrier was homozygous. Carriers were more likely than noncarriers to have the postural instability and gait difficulty phenotype.

Gehrke et al. (2010) showed that LRRK2 G2019S antagonized let-7 microRNA-mediated translational repression in Drosophila and in HEK293T cells, and that this effect depended on kinase activity.
