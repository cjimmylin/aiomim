### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the LRRK2 gene (609007) on chromosome 12q12. ⚠︎

### Description

Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is an autosomal dominant form of parkinsonism. Its clinical features closely resemble those of idiopathic Parkinson disease (PD): levodopa-responsive bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired postural reflexes, with slow progression. The neuropathology varies, even within a single family. Affected individuals consistently show nigral degeneration, which may be accompanied by brainstem or diffuse Lewy bodies, by tau-positive neurofibrillary tangles, or by neither. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) named the PARK8 protein dardarin, from the Basque word dardara, meaning tremor. They chose the name because of the tremor seen in PD and because several of their families were of Basque descent.

### Clinical Features

Wszolek et al. (1995) described family D, a western Nebraska kindred whose ancestors probably came from England. The pedigree spanned 6 generations and contained 188 individuals, 18 of them affected. Affected members developed typical levodopa-responsive PD with bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired postural reflexes. Eye movement abnormalities, pyramidal and cerebellar signs, sensory disturbances, and orthostatic blood pressure changes did not occur, and the disease progressed slowly. In 1 affected individual, [18F]-6-fluoro-L-dopa PET showed decreased uptake in a pattern consistent with PD. Autopsy of another affected individual showed neuronal and pigment loss, gliosis, and Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra pars compacta. The authors concluded that the disorder closely resembled, if it was not identical to, idiopathic PD.

Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) reported autosomal dominant PD across 5 generations of a family living in Sagamihara City, Japan. Clinically, the disorder did not differ from common PD. In autopsied cases, however, the loss of melanin-containing cells was only mild to moderate, the number of locus ceruleus neurons was maintained, and no Lewy bodies were found in any region where they frequently appear in PD.

Funayama et al. (2002) studied a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism whose clinical features were compatible with common PD. Funayama et al. (2005) stated that the Sagamihara family had served as the basis for the original definition of the PARK8 locus. They described its pathology as pure nigral degeneration without Lewy bodies.

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported that, since the original 1995 report of a parkinsonian kindred, 4 further individuals had become affected, with a mean age at onset of 65 years. All 4 had the cardinal signs of PD and responded well to levodopa. Neuropathologic findings in this kindred are described under PATHOGENESIS.

Zimprich et al. (2004) described late-onset parkinsonism in the affected LRRK2 mutation carriers of families A and D. Clinical diagnoses in these carriers included parkinsonism with dementia, parkinsonism with amyotrophy, or both. ⚠︎

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) described 4 Basque families with autosomal dominant PD. The clinical features were those of typical PD, including good response to levodopa therapy, rigidity, and akinesia, and the mean age at disease onset was 55 years.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found that the phenotype in 4 families with autosomal dominant PD carrying the LRRK2 G2019S mutation was broad, with both early and late disease onset. The families came from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) found that the disease in 3 families with heterozygous LRRK2 mutations had a phenotype indistinguishable from typical PD. All 3 families showed a very variable age at onset that APOE genotypes did not explain.

Ishihara et al. (2006) reported the clinical characteristics of 29 patients with familial PD who were homozygous for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. Twenty-three were Tunisian, 2 Algerian, 2 from the US, 1 Canadian, and 1 Moroccan. The patients had typical clinical features of PD.

Rajput et al. (2006) described family SK, in which the LRRK2 G2019S mutation segregated with slowly progressive parkinsonism.

Chen-Plotkin et al. (2008) screened 434 individuals with a range of neurodegenerative diseases for G2019S and other previously reported LRRK2 mutations: 254 clinic patients and 180 brain bank autopsy cases. They found a mutation in 8 cases. Two of these presented with cognitive deficits and received clinical diagnoses of corticobasal syndrome and primary progressive aphasia. The authors concluded that the clinical presentation of LRRK2-associated neurodegenerative disease may be more heterogeneous than previously thought.

Silveira-Moriyama et al. (2008) tested the sense of smell with the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) in 19 parkinsonian and 2 asymptomatic carriers of the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. In the parkinsonian carriers, the mean UPSIT score was lower than in healthy controls (p less than 0.001) and similar to that of patients with PD. The 2 asymptomatic carriers scored in the normal range.

Nandhagopal et al. (2008) performed multitracer PET of presynaptic dopaminergic integrity in 13 members of family D, which carries the LRRK2 R1441C mutation, and rescanned 5 of them 2 to 3 years later. In 2 mutation carriers and 1 genealogically at-risk subject, the PET markers declined significantly faster than the rate expected in healthy controls.

Alcalay et al. (2009) identified the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 34 of 925 probands with early-onset PD (onset before age 51). Thirty-three were heterozygous and 1, who was Ashkenazi Jewish, was homozygous. In carriers with complete UPDRS data, the mean age at onset was 42.9 years and the mean age at examination was 52.7 years. Among the carriers, 24 of 26 had the postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) motor phenotype and 1 of 26 had the tremor-dominant phenotype. Onset was asymmetric in 23 of 26, rest tremor was the presenting symptom in 11 of 25, and hallucinations occurred in 1 of 26. Five of 24 had a first-degree relative with PD.

Gan-Or et al. (2010) screened 600 Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients. Compared with other patients, LRRK2 G2019S carriers more often reported muscle stiffness (rigidity, p = 0.007) and balance disturbances (p = 0.008) as initial symptoms.

Alcalay et al. (2010) studied 31 patients with early-onset PD who carried the LRRK2 G2019S mutation without a mutation in another tested gene: 30 heterozygotes and 1 homozygote, 17 female and 14 male. Seventeen were Jewish and 2 Hispanic, and the others were of Southern, mixed, or Eastern European descent. Mean age at onset was 42 years and mean age at examination was 54 years. Twenty-five (96%) of the carriers assessed had the PIGD motor phenotype. ⚠︎

Saunders-Pullman et al. (2011) used the UPSIT to compare 31 PD patients carrying LRRK2 G2019S with 30 PD patients without the mutation and 46 controls. Olfaction in the G2019S PD patients was impaired compared with controls but less impaired than in non-LRRK2 PD patients (difference = 6.13, p = 0.0012). Twenty-eight nonmanifesting G2019S carriers also had lower mean olfactory scores than controls (difference = -3.518, p = 0.006). Because the impairment affected only a subset of the nonmanifesting carriers, the authors suggested that olfaction might mark those who will develop PD.

Marras et al. (2011) compared 25 individuals with LRRK2 G2019S-associated PD with 84 individuals with idiopathic PD. Tremor was more often a presenting feature in the LRRK2 group. LRRK2-PD patients also had better olfactory identification, higher Beck Depression Inventory scores, and higher error scores on the Farnsworth-Munsell 100-Hue test of color discrimination. Overall, however, the phenotype largely overlapped with that of idiopathic PD. Postural or action tremor was more common in 29 nonmanifesting G2019S carriers than in 53 noncarrier family members, suggesting that it may be an early sign. Regardless of mutation status, nonparkinsonian family members had higher UPDRS motor scores, more constipation, and worse color discrimination than controls.

Inzelberg et al. (2012) conducted a cross-sectional study of 490 Jewish patients with PD and found that 79 (16.1%) carried the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. A non-skin cancer had occurred in 18 (23%) carriers, compared with 49 (12%) noncarriers (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.19-3.99, p = 0.01). Among Ashkenazi Jewish patients, the age-adjusted odds ratio for a concomitant non-skin cancer in carriers was 3.38 (95% CI 1.64-6.97, p = 0.0009).

### Biochemical Features

Aasly et al. (2012) measured CSF amyloid beta-42, total tau, and phosphorylated tau in 26 LRRK2 mutation carriers, 18 asymptomatic and 8 with a phenotype resembling sporadic PD. They compared these levels with striatal dopaminergic function on PET using FD, DTBZ, and MP tracers. Lower CSF amyloid beta-42 and tau levels correlated with lower striatal dopaminergic function, and the association between amyloid beta-42 and FD uptake was significant. When the analysis was restricted to G2019S carriers, the correlations with tau were also significant.

### Inheritance

Wszolek et al. (1995) found autosomal dominant inheritance in family D, which spanned 6 generations. Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) reported autosomal dominant PD across 5 generations of the Sagamihara family.

In the Japanese family used to map PARK8, Funayama et al. (2002) found that all patients and some unaffected family members shared the disease-associated haplotype. They took this as evidence of incomplete penetrance and proposed that environmental or other genetic factors modify expression of the disease.

Ross et al. (2006) found the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 1 control subject and 1 patient with Alzheimer disease, which they attributed to reduced penetrance. Alcalay et al. (2010) found that PRKN carriers were more likely than LRRK2 carriers to report a family history of PD, even though LRRK2 disease is autosomal dominant. They suggested that this might partly reflect incomplete penetrance of the G2019S mutation.

### Mapping

Funayama et al. (2002) performed a genomewide linkage analysis in a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism. The highest 2-point lod score was 4.32 at D12S345 (12p11.21), and multipoint analysis gave a maximum lod score of 4.71 at D12S85 (12q12). Haplotype analysis placed the disease-associated haplotype in a 13.6-cM interval in 12p11.2-q13.1, bounded by recombinations at D12S1631 and D12S339. Nonparametric (penetrance-independent) analyses gave maximum lod scores of 14.2 (2-point) and 24.9 (multipoint) at D12S345. Together these results supported a locus in 12p11.23-q13.11, a region that did not overlap any known hereditary parkinsonism locus. The new locus was designated PARK8.

Zimprich et al. (2004) studied 21 white families with PD compatible with autosomal dominant transmission, comprising 84 affected and 83 unaffected members, using 29 markers in the PARK8 region on chromosome 12. Under heterogeneity with an affecteds-only model, the maximum multipoint lod score was 2.01 (P = 0.0047), with an estimated proportion of linked families of 0.32. Two families reached significant linkage on their own: a German Canadian family (family A) and a family from western Nebraska (family D). Each had a lod score of 1.67 under the affecteds-only model, for a combined multipoint lod score of 3.33. Under a penetrance-dependent model, the scores were 1.68 for family A and 2.24 for family D, combining to 3.92. The 1-lod support interval placed the most likely disease location between a CA repeat on genomic clone AC025253 (44.5 Mb) and marker D12S1701 (47.7 Mb).

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported that genetic studies of their kindred with autosomal dominant parkinsonism suggested linkage to the PARK8 locus on chromosome 12.

In 4 Basque families with autosomal dominant PD, Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) found evidence for linkage to the PARK8 locus, with a maximum 2-point lod score of 3.21 (theta = 0.00) at marker D12S345.

### Heterogeneity

Zimprich et al. (2004) concluded that the PARK8 locus causes disease in a subset of white families with autosomal dominant parkinsonism and suggested that it could be a more common locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) cloned a novel gene in the PARK8 region. In 5 families from England and Spain with PARK8-linked PD, several of Basque descent, they identified missense mutations in this gene that segregated with the disease.

Zimprich et al. (2004) studied 46 families with autosomal dominant, late-onset parkinsonism linked to chromosome 12p11.2-q13.1 (PARK8). Using high-resolution recombination mapping and candidate gene sequencing, they identified 6 disease-segregating mutations in the LRRK2 gene (609007): 5 missense mutations and 1 putative splice site mutation. Affected carriers included members of families A and D. The authors noted that LRRK2 belongs to the ROCO protein family and contains a protein kinase domain of the MAPKKK class along with several other major functional domains. ⚠︎

Nichols et al. (2005) screened 767 affected individuals from 358 multiplex PD families for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. They found it in 35 individuals (5%): 34 heterozygotes and 1 homozygote, all with typical clinical findings of idiopathic PD. The authors concluded that this single mutation causes PD in 5% of individuals with familial disease. ⚠︎

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) identified the heterozygous LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 4 of 61 (6.6%) unrelated families with autosomal dominant PD, originating from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil. They identified G2019S as a common mutation in dominantly inherited PD. ⚠︎

Gilks et al. (2005) sequenced exon 41 of LRRK2 in 482 patients with idiopathic PD, 263 of whom had pathologically confirmed disease. They found a heterozygous 2877510G-A transition, resulting in a gly2019-to-ser (G2019S) substitution, in 8 patients (1.6%). They concluded that a common single mendelian mutation is implicated in sporadic PD.

In affected members of the Sagamihara family, the Japanese family on which the PARK8 locus was originally defined, Funayama et al. (2005) detected a missense mutation in the kinase domain of LRRK2. They did not detect this mutation or other known LRRK2 mutations in Japanese patients with sporadic PD.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in 23 probands of families with dominantly inherited PD. Three probands carried heterozygous mutations: 2 had G2019S and 1 had a novel I1371V substitution. In a large family, G2019S segregated completely with PD. Mutation frequencies were evaluated in 180 neurologically normal controls.

Farrer et al. (2005) used allelic discrimination assays for 9 LRRK2 mutations and identified mutations in 6 probands with PD and 1 affected sib, as well as in 1 unaffected sib and 1 unrelated control.

Skipper et al. (2005) characterized linkage disequilibrium across LRRK2 with densely spaced SNPs and selected tagging SNPs. In an epidemiologically matched case-control series of 932 Chinese individuals with sporadic PD or controls, both single tagging-SNP and haplotype analyses gave significant evidence for association. One haplotype greatly increased disease risk when present in 2 copies (OR = 5.5, 95% CI 2.1-14.0, P = 0.0001).

Ross et al. (2006) found the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in about 2% (8 of 405) of autopsied cases of PD or Lewy body disease. ⚠︎

Lesage et al. (2009) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in index cases from 226 PD families compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance, most of them from France (182) or North Africa (14). They found 8 potentially or proven pathogenic mutations in 22 probands (9.7%), most located in functional domains of the LRRK2 protein. The common G2019S mutation was present in 13 probands, and the known heterozygous R1441H and I1371V mutations were each found in 2 probands. An E334K variant was found in a single patient. Four novel amino acid substitutions were considered potentially pathogenic: S52F, N363S, I810V, and R1325Q. Of 79 LRRK2 sequence variants identified, 29 were novel. Two of the novel variants, H1216R and T1410M, were considered probably not causative.

Choi et al. (2008) sequenced 35 exons of SNCA, PARKIN, DJ-1, PINK1, and LRRK2 in 72 unrelated Korean patients with early-onset PD (onset at or before age 50). Nine patients (12.5%) carried the LRRK2 G2385R variant, which the authors termed a polymorphism, in the heterozygous state, and none carried G2019S. Overall, 25% of the patients had variants in PARKIN, PINK1, LRRK2, or SNCA that the authors considered either causes or genetic risk factors. PARKIN mutations were the most common genetic cause.

Satake et al. (2009) carried out a genomewide association study and 2 replication studies, totaling 2,011 Japanese patients with PD and 18,381 controls. They found a strong association at LRRK2 on 12q12 (P = 2.72 x 10(-8)), a risk locus also shared with populations of European ancestry. The authors concluded that loci for autosomal dominant parkinsonism are involved in typical PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with early-onset PD for known mutations. Four LRRK2 G2019S heterozygotes also carried a heterozygous variant in a second gene. These were a Jewish man with onset at age 41 who also carried a PRKN (602544) exon 5-6 deletion; a non-Jewish man with onset at age 31 who also carried the GBA1 (606463) L444P variant; and a Jewish man with onset at age 47 and a Jewish woman with onset at age 49, each of whom also carried the GBA1 N370S variant. One additional subject carried the LRRK2 G2385R variant, and no other pathogenic LRRK2 mutations were found. ⚠︎

Tan et al. (2010) studied 13 SNPs at PD loci identified by genomewide association studies (PARK16, PARK8, PARK1, and BST1) in 2 case-control cohorts totaling 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects. SNPs at PARK8 and PARK1 were associated with increased risk of PD, SNPs at PARK16 were associated with decreased risk, and BST1 was not associated. They pooled these cohorts with 2 Japanese replication cohorts, totaling 1,366 PD patients and 16,669 controls. In the pooled analysis, the association with PARK16, PARK8, and PARK1 was robust, BST1 was also associated, and the strength of association was similar in Chinese and Japanese subjects.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ishihara et al. (2006) found no observable differences between the phenotypes of PD patients homozygous for LRRK2 G2019S and those of previously published heterozygous patients. They concluded that LRRK2-related PD has typical clinical features and that the similarity between homozygotes and heterozygotes does not support a gene dosage effect.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) found that women and patients with a positive family history of PD were significantly overrepresented among G2019S carriers. Age at onset was similar in carriers and noncarriers. The authors suggested that gender affects the frequency of the mutation among patients.

Lesage et al. (2008) examined whether the LRRK2 G2019S mutation is related to dyskinesias in North African patients with PD.

Among 925 probands with early-onset PD (onset before age 51), Alcalay et al. (2009) found that LRRK2 G2019S carriers had a lower tremor score than noncarriers and were more likely to have the PIGD phenotype (92.3% vs 58.9%). The association remained after adjustment for disease duration and Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry (odds ratio 17.7). It was present in Ashkenazi Jewish and non-Ashkenazi probands analyzed separately and was seen mainly in the shortest disease-duration tertile. Nearly all carriers had PIGD whatever their disease duration, whereas in noncarriers the prevalence of PIGD rose with disease duration. Carriers did not differ from noncarriers in age at onset, disease duration, UPDRS-III score, levodopa dose, or MMSE score.

Gan-Or et al. (2010) screened 600 Ashkenazi PD patients for LRRK2 G2019S and for 8 GBA mutations. LRRK2 G2019S carriers more often reported muscle stiffness (rigidity, p = 0.007) and balance disturbances (p = 0.008) as initial symptoms, whereas GBA mutation carriers more often reported slowness (bradykinesia, p = 0.021). The authors suggested that LRRK2 and GBA mutations have distinct effects on the initial symptoms of PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that patients with early-onset PD carrying LRRK2 G2019S were more likely than noncarriers to have the PIGD motor phenotype (96% vs 58%).

### Pathogenesis

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported autopsies of 4 affected members of a parkinsonian kindred linked to PARK8. All 4 had neuronal loss and gliosis in the substantia nigra, but the other findings varied. One case had Lewy bodies limited to brainstem nuclei and another had diffuse Lewy bodies. A third had neurofibrillary tangles without Lewy bodies, and the fourth had neither.

Zimprich et al. (2004) reported 6 postmortem examinations of affected LRRK2 mutation carriers in families A and D. All showed brainstem dopaminergic degeneration, accompanied by strikingly diverse pathologies: Lewy body PD, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, and progressive supranuclear palsy-like pathology. The authors suggested that LRRK2 may be central to the pathogenesis of several major neurodegenerative disorders associated with parkinsonism.

Ross et al. (2006) reported that Lewy body disease was the most common neuropathology in PD associated with LRRK2 G2019S.

Giasson et al. (2006) showed biochemically that Lrrk2 is mainly a soluble cytoplasmic protein of about 250 kD, expressed throughout the brain and in many other organs. They examined 3 patients with the G2019S mutation. Two had classic PD with Lewy bodies, and one of these also had concurrent pathologic changes consistent with Alzheimer disease. The third had parkinsonism without Lewy bodies but had dystrophic neurites in the substantia nigra that stained intensely for Lrrk2. Lrrk2 accumulations were found only in this patient, and Lrrk2 was not detected in other types of pathologic inclusions. The authors suggested that LRRK2 mutations may make select neuronal populations more vulnerable to cellular insults that can lead to aberrant protein aggregation.

Zhu et al. (2006) reported that LRRK2 protein is a component of Lewy bodies.

In family SK, Rajput et al. (2006) found tau-immunopositive neurofibrillary tangle pathology in the affected proband carrying LRRK2 G2019S. They found no evidence that tau or alpha-synuclein interacts directly with LRRK2. They suggested that alpha-synucleinopathy and tauopathy, the main pathologies associated with parkinsonism, may be alternate outcomes of the same underlying genetic cause, the G2019S mutation.

Silveira-Moriyama et al. (2008) examined 4 parkinsonian carriers of LRRK2 G2019S postmortem and found alpha-synuclein accumulation with Lewy bodies in the rhinencephalon of all 4.

Aasly et al. (2012) concluded that the disposition of amyloid beta and tau is likely important in both LRRK2-related and sporadic PD, even in the early phases of the disease, and that these proteins may be useful biomarkers. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Nichols et al. (2005) recommended that screening for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation be included in genetic testing for PD. Gilks et al. (2005) suggested that testing for this mutation would be important in the management and genetic counseling of patients with PD.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) concluded that testing symptomatic patients may help establish the diagnosis of PD. They considered screening of asymptomatic individuals to be of questionable value until the penetrance and age-dependent risk of G2019S are better known.

In presymptomatic LRRK2 mutation carriers from family D, Nandhagopal et al. (2008) found that reduced dopamine transporter binding was the earliest sign of subclinical dopaminergic dysfunction. Progression to clinical disease was generally associated with the appearance of abnormal fluorodopa uptake.

In a review, Dächsel and Farrer (2010) noted that mutations predisposing to PD, including those in LRRK2, are diagnostically useful in early or atypical presentations. They added that affected and asymptomatic LRRK2 carriers alike offer a chance to define the natural history of PD, including penetrance, motor and nonmotor features, and related biomarkers.

### Clinical Management

Ross et al. (2006) suggested that treatments aimed at modulating LRRK2 kinase activity may be important for patients with genetically defined familial PD or typical sporadic PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) concluded that the penetrance of known PD mutations remains unknown. They stated that genetic testing for PD currently has little or no role in family planning or in predictive or prenatal testing. However, patients with early-onset PD who are of Jewish or Hispanic ancestry, who had onset at or before age 30, or who have an affected first-degree relative may benefit from genetic counseling.

In the Pakistan Genome Resource (173,303 exomes and genomes), Koch et al. (2026) identified 3 individuals homozygous for LRRK2 loss-of-function variants who had reduced kidney function. The first was a 42-year-old homozygous for a splice donor variant (c.706+1G-T) with an eGFR of 68 ml/min. The second was a 25-year-old with chronic kidney disease (eGFR 14 ml/min) homozygous for a frameshift (Arg1639GlyfsTer15). The third, found by recall-by-genotype, was a 49-year-old woman homozygous for c.1418+1G-T. Her eGFR of 51 ml/min was lower than that of all 13 of her heterozygous and noncarrier relatives who were recruited. The authors noted that heterozygous loss of function in LRRK2 had not been associated with any phenotype, including PD. Because these renal findings support those in LRRK2-knockout rodents and in preclinical studies of LRRK2 inhibitors, they recommended monitoring kidney function during long-term LRRK2 inhibition for PD, while noting that the number of carriers was small. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Lesage et al. (2005) performed LRRK2 haplotype analyses in European and North African families with PD and reported a common founder for the G2019S mutation dating from the 13th century.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) studied 121 PD patients and 250 controls. The patients were predominantly white Canadians, and 84% had onset before age 50. The authors found no association between PD and 4 common coding LRRK2 polymorphisms. Neither these genotypes nor APOE genotypes modified age at onset, although a modest risk effect could not be excluded.

Farrer et al. (2005) screened 786 PD probands, 32 affected sibs, 1,044 unaffected sibs, and 278 unrelated controls for 9 LRRK2 mutations. They concluded that LRRK2 mutations only rarely cause idiopathic PD.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) screened Jewish Israeli patients with PD for LRRK2 mutations and found only G2019S, which they identified in 76 patients. The mutation was present in 14.8% of Ashkenazi and 2.7% of non-Ashkenazi patients. Among Ashkenazi patients, it occurred in 26% of familial cases and 10.6% of apparently sporadic cases. Carrier frequencies were 2.4% in Ashkenazi controls and 0.4% in non-Ashkenazi controls. Typing of 6 microsatellite markers spanning LRRK2 showed a common shared haplotype in all non-Ashkenazi and half-Ashkenazi G2019S carriers and in all but 2 of the full-Ashkenazi carriers tested.

Choi et al. (2008) found the LRRK2 G2385R variant in the heterozygous state in 9 of 72 (12.5%) unrelated Korean patients with early-onset PD. None of the patients carried G2019S.

Lesage et al. (2009) found LRRK2 G2019S in 13 of 226 probands (5.8%) with autosomal dominant PD. Six of the carriers were from North Africa, amounting to 43% of the North African probands. They concluded that LRRK2 mutations are a common cause of autosomal dominant PD in Europe and North Africa.

Alcalay et al. (2009) identified LRRK2 G2019S in 34 (3.7%) of 925 probands with early-onset PD from 13 centers and found no I2020T, R1441C, or Y1699C mutations. Carriers were more likely than noncarriers to be Ashkenazi Jewish (55.9% vs 11.9%), but they were not more likely to have a first-degree relative with PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with early-onset PD (onset at or before age 50) from 13 movement disorders centers and found that 35 (3.6%) carried LRRK2 G2019S. G2019S carriers were more often of Jewish ancestry: 17 of the 31 carriers of G2019S alone were Jewish, compared with 12% of noncarriers. Of the 15 G2019S carriers who did not report Jewish ancestry, 11 gave their grandparents' country of origin. Four reported Southern European descent, 4 mixed European descent, 1 Eastern European ancestry, and 2 Puerto Rican ancestry.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2008) used the GAL4/UAS system to generate transgenic Drosophila expressing wildtype human LRRK2 or LRRK2 G2019S. Expression of either form in photoreceptor cells caused retinal degeneration. Neuronal expression caused adult-onset selective loss of dopaminergic neurons, locomotor dysfunction, and early mortality. The G2019S mutant produced a more severe parkinsonism-like phenotype than equivalent levels of wildtype LRRK2. L-DOPA treatment improved the locomotor impairment caused by mutant LRRK2 but did not prevent loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons. The authors stated that, to their knowledge, this was the first in vivo gain-of-function model to recapitulate several key features of LRRK2-linked parkinsonism.
