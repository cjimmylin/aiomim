### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the LRRK2 gene (609007) on chromosome 12q12. ⚠︎

### Description

Parkinson disease-8 (PARK8) is an autosomal dominant form of parkinsonism. Clinically it closely resembles idiopathic Parkinson disease (PD), with bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired postural reflexes that respond to levodopa, and a slowly progressive course (Wszolek et al., 1995). The neuropathology varies, even within a single family, and includes brainstem dopaminergic degeneration with Lewy body PD, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, and progressive supranuclear palsy-like pathology (Zimprich et al., 2004). Penetrance is incomplete (Funayama et al., 2002). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) named the PARK8 protein dardarin, from the Basque word dardara, meaning tremor. They chose the name because of the tremor seen in PD and because several of the families they studied were of Basque descent.

### Clinical Features

Wszolek et al. (1995) described family D, a western Nebraska kindred whose ancestors probably came from England. The pedigree contained 188 individuals spanning 6 generations, 18 of whom were affected, and inheritance was autosomal dominant. Affected members developed typical levodopa-responsive PD with bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and impaired postural reflexes, and the disease progressed slowly. Eye movement abnormalities, pyramidal and cerebellar signs, sensory disturbances, and orthostatic blood pressure changes did not occur. In 1 affected individual, [18F]-6-fluoro-L-dopa PET showed decreased uptake in a pattern consistent with PD. Autopsy of another affected individual showed neuronal and pigmentary loss, gliosis, and Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra pars compacta. The authors concluded that the disorder closely resembled, if it was not identical to, idiopathic PD.

Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) reported autosomal dominant PD over 5 generations of a Japanese family living in Sagamihara City. The clinical features did not differ from those of common PD. Autopsied cases, however, showed only mild to moderate loss of melanin-containing cells, a preserved number of locus ceruleus neurons, and no Lewy bodies in any region where Lewy bodies frequently appear in PD.

Funayama et al. (2002) studied a Japanese family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism whose clinical features were compatible with common PD (see MAPPING).

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported that 4 further members of the parkinsonian kindred first described in 1995 had become affected since the original report. Mean age at onset was 65 years. All 4 had the cardinal signs of PD and responded well to levodopa. Four autopsies showed neuronal loss and gliosis in the substantia nigra, with variable Lewy body and tau pathology (see PATHOGENESIS).

Zimprich et al. (2004) reported that affected LRRK2 mutation carriers in families A and D had late-onset parkinsonism. Some had clinical diagnoses of parkinsonism with dementia, amyotrophy, or both. Six postmortem examinations showed brainstem dopaminergic degeneration with strikingly diverse pathologies. ⚠︎

Nichols et al. (2005) reported that patients from multiplex PD families who were heterozygous or homozygous for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation had typical clinical findings of idiopathic PD.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 4 families with autosomal dominant PD from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil. The associated phenotype was broad and included both early and late disease onset.

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) described 4 Basque families with autosomal dominant PD. Their clinical features were those of typical PD, including good response to levodopa, rigidity, and akinesia, and mean age at onset was 55 years.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) reported that the disease in 3 families with heterozygous LRRK2 mutations had a phenotype indistinguishable from typical PD. Age at onset was very variable in all 3 families and was not explained by APOE genotype.

Ishihara et al. (2006) described the clinical features of 29 patients with familial PD who were homozygous for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation: 23 Tunisians, 2 Algerians, 2 patients from the US, 1 Canadian, and 1 Moroccan. The patients had typical clinical features of PD (see GENOTYPE/PHENOTYPE CORRELATIONS).

Rajput et al. (2006) identified family SK, in which the LRRK2 G2019S mutation segregated with slowly progressive parkinsonism. The affected proband had tau-immunopositive neurofibrillary tangle pathology.

Chen-Plotkin et al. (2008) screened 434 individuals with a range of neurodegenerative diseases (254 clinic patients and 180 brain bank autopsy cases) for G2019S and other previously reported LRRK2 mutations. Eight cases carried a mutation. Two of them presented with cognitive deficits and received clinical diagnoses of corticobasal syndrome and primary progressive aphasia. The authors concluded that LRRK2-associated neurodegenerative disease may be more clinically heterogeneous than previously thought.

Silveira-Moriyama et al. (2008) used the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) to assess 19 parkinsonian and 2 asymptomatic carriers of the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. In the parkinsonian carriers, mean UPSIT scores were lower than in healthy controls (p less than 0.001) and similar to those of patients with PD (p greater than 0.999). The 2 asymptomatic carriers had smell test results in the normal range.

Nandhagopal et al. (2008) performed multitracer PET of presynaptic dopaminergic integrity in 13 members of family D, which carries the LRRK2 R1441C mutation, and rescanned 5 of them 2 to 3 years later. In 2 mutation carriers and 1 genealogically at-risk subject, PET markers declined significantly faster than the expected rate in healthy controls.

Alcalay et al. (2009) identified 34 carriers of the LRRK2 G2019S mutation among 925 probands with early-onset PD (onset at 50 years or younger). In carriers with a complete UPDRS assessment, mean age at onset was 42.9 years and mean age at examination was 52.7 years. Among the heterozygous carriers, 24 of 26 had the postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) motor phenotype and 1 of 26 had a tremor-dominant phenotype. Onset was asymmetric in 23 of 26, rest tremor was the presenting symptom in 11 of 25, hallucinations were reported in 1 of 26, and 5 of 24 had a first-degree relative with PD. ⚠︎

Saunders-Pullman et al. (2011) used the UPSIT to compare 31 PD patients carrying LRRK2 G2019S, 30 PD patients without the mutation, and 46 controls. Olfaction in the G2019S PD patients was impaired compared with controls but less so than in non-LRRK2 PD patients (difference = 6.13, p = 0.0012). The 28 nonmanifesting G2019S carriers also had lower mean olfactory scores than controls (difference = -3.518, p = 0.006). Because only a subset of nonmanifesting carriers had this impairment, the authors suggested that olfaction might be a marker for developing PD in this group.

Marras et al. (2011) compared 25 individuals with LRRK2 G2019S-associated PD with 84 individuals with idiopathic PD. Tremor was more often a presenting feature in the LRRK2 group. LRRK2-PD patients also had better olfactory identification, higher Beck Depression Inventory scores, and higher error scores on the Farnsworth-Munsell 100-Hue test of color discrimination, although overall the phenotype largely overlapped with idiopathic PD. Postural or action tremor was more common among 29 nonmanifesting G2019S carriers than among 53 noncarrier family members, suggesting that it may be an early sign. Nonparkinsonian family members had higher UPDRS motor scores, more constipation, and worse color discrimination than controls, whether or not they carried the mutation.

In a cross-sectional study of 490 Jewish patients with PD, Inzelberg et al. (2012) found that 79 (16.1%) carried the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. Non-skin cancer had occurred in 18 (23%) carriers compared with 49 (12%) noncarriers (odds ratio 2.18, 95% CI 1.19-3.99, p = 0.01). Among Ashkenazi Jewish patients, the age-adjusted odds ratio for a concomitant non-skin cancer in carriers was 3.38 (95% CI 1.64-6.97, p = 0.0009).

### Biochemical Features

Aasly et al. (2012) measured CSF amyloid beta-42, total tau, and phosphorylated tau in 26 LRRK2 mutation carriers, 18 of them asymptomatic and 8 with a phenotype resembling sporadic PD. They compared these levels with striatal dopaminergic function on PET using FD, DTBZ, and MP tracers. Lower CSF amyloid beta-42 and tau levels correlated with lower striatal dopaminergic function, and the association between amyloid beta-42 and FD uptake was significant. When the analysis was restricted to G2019S carriers, significant correlations were also seen for tau.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of parkinsonism was observed in family D by Wszolek et al. (1995) and in the Japanese Sagamihara family by Hasegawa and Kowa (1997).

In the Sagamihara family, Funayama et al. (2002) found that the disease-associated haplotype was shared by all patients and also by some unaffected members, suggesting incomplete penetrance. They proposed that environmental or other genetic factors modify expression of the disease. Ross et al. (2006) found the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 1 control subject and 1 patient with Alzheimer disease, which they took to reflect reduced penetrance. Alcalay et al. (2010) found that early-onset PD patients who were PRKN carriers were more likely than LRRK2 carriers to report a family history of PD, even though LRRK2 disease is autosomal dominant. They suggested that this might partly reflect incomplete penetrance of the G2019S mutation.

### Mapping

Funayama et al. (2002) performed a genomewide linkage analysis in the Japanese Sagamihara family with autosomal dominant parkinsonism. The highest 2-point lod score was 4.32 at D12S345 (12p11.21), and multipoint analysis gave a maximum lod score of 4.71 at D12S85 (12q12). Haplotype analysis placed the disease-associated haplotype in a 13.6-cM interval in 12p11.2-q13.1, bounded by recombinations at D12S1631 and D12S339. Nonparametric (penetrance-independent) analyses gave maximum lod scores of 14.2 (2-point) and 24.9 (multipoint) at D12S345. These results supported a locus at 12p11.23-q13.11, a region different from any known hereditary parkinsonism locus, and the new locus was designated PARK8. ⚠︎

Zimprich et al. (2004) studied 21 white families with PD compatible with autosomal dominant transmission (84 affected and 83 unaffected members) using 29 markers in the PARK8 region on chromosome 12. Under heterogeneity with an affecteds-only model, the maximum multipoint lod score was 2.01 (P = 0.0047), and the estimated proportion of linked families was 0.32. Two families reached significant linkage on their own: a German Canadian family (family A) and a family from western Nebraska (family D). With an affecteds-only model, their combined maximum multipoint lod score was 3.33, with a lod score of 1.67 (P = 0.0028) in each family. A penetrance-dependent model gave a combined lod score of 3.92 (family A, 1.68; family D, 2.24). The 1-lod support interval placed the most likely disease location between a CA repeat on genomic clone AC025253 (44.5 Mb) and marker D12S1701 (47.7 Mb).

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported that genetic studies of the parkinsonian kindred first described in 1995 suggested linkage to the PARK8 locus on chromosome 12.

Paisàn-Ruìz et al. (2005) found evidence for linkage of autosomal dominant PD to the PARK8 locus in 4 Basque families. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.21 (theta = 0.00) at marker D12S345.

### Heterogeneity

Zimprich et al. (2004) concluded that the PARK8 locus causes disease in a subset of white families with autosomal dominant parkinsonism, suggesting that it could be a more common locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2004) cloned a novel gene in the PARK8 region. In 5 families from England and Spain with PARK8-linked PD, several of Basque descent, they found missense mutations in this gene that segregated with the disease.

Zimprich et al. (2004) studied 46 families with autosomal dominant, late-onset parkinsonism linked to chromosome 12p11.2-q13.1 (PARK8). Using high-resolution recombination mapping and candidate gene sequencing, they identified 6 disease-segregating mutations in the LRRK2 gene: 5 missense mutations and 1 putative splice site mutation. Affected carriers included members of families A and D. The authors noted that LRRK2 belongs to the ROCO protein family and contains a protein kinase domain of the MAPKKK class along with several other major functional domains. ⚠︎

Nichols et al. (2005) screened 767 affected individuals from 358 multiplex PD families for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. The mutation was present in 35 individuals (5%), 34 of whom were heterozygous and 1 homozygous.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found the heterozygous LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 4 of 61 (6.6%) unrelated families with autosomal dominant PD.

Gilks et al. (2005) sequenced exon 41 of LRRK2 in 482 patients with idiopathic PD, 263 of whom had pathologically confirmed disease. They found the heterozygous G2019S mutation, which results from a 2877510G-A transition, in 8 patients (1.6%).

Funayama et al. (2005) detected a missense mutation in the kinase domain of LRRK2 in affected members of the Sagamihara family, the Japanese family on which the PARK8 locus was originally defined. Neither this mutation nor other known LRRK2 mutations were found in Japanese patients with sporadic PD.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in 23 probands from families with dominantly inherited PD. Three probands had heterozygous mutations: 2 had G2019S and 1 had a novel I1371V substitution. In a large family, G2019S segregated completely with PD. Mutation frequencies were evaluated in 180 neurologically normal controls.

Lesage et al. (2009) sequenced all 51 coding exons of LRRK2 in index cases from 226 PD families compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance, most of them from France (182) or North Africa (14). They found 8 potentially or proven pathogenic mutations in 22 probands (9.7%), most located in functional domains of the LRRK2 protein. G2019S was present in 13 probands, and the known heterozygous R1441H and I1371V mutations were each found in 2 probands. The E334K variant was found in 1 patient. They also found 4 novel amino acid substitutions that were potentially pathogenic: S52F, N363S, I810V, and R1325Q. In total they identified 79 LRRK2 sequence variants, 29 of them novel, and considered 2 novel variants, H1216R and T1410M, probably not causative.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with early-onset PD (onset at or before age 50 years) from 13 movement disorder centers. Thirty-five (3.6%) carried the LRRK2 G2019S mutation. Thirty-one carried G2019S alone (30 heterozygotes and 1 homozygote). Four carried G2019S together with a variant in a second gene: a heterozygous PRKN exon 5-6 deletion in a Jewish man with onset at 41 years, GBA L444P in a non-Jewish man with onset at 31 years, and GBA N370S in a Jewish man with onset at 47 years and a Jewish woman with onset at 49 years. One subject carried the LRRK2 G2385R variant, and no other pathogenic LRRK2 mutations were found.

In a Chinese sporadic PD case-control series of 932 individuals, Skipper et al. (2005) found significant association with LRRK2 tagging SNPs, both individually and as haplotypes. One haplotype markedly increased disease risk when present in 2 copies (OR = 5.5, 95% CI 2.1-14.0, P = 0.0001).

In a genomewide association study with 2 replication studies, totaling 2,011 Japanese patients with PD and 18,381 controls, Satake et al. (2009) found strong association at LRRK2 on 12q12 (P = 2.72 x 10(-8)). LRRK2 was a risk locus shared with populations of European ancestry, and the authors concluded that loci for autosomal dominant parkinsonism are involved in typical PD.

Tan et al. (2010) studied 13 SNPs at PD GWAS-linked loci in 2 case-control cohorts totaling 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects. PARK8 SNPs were associated with an increased risk of PD. A pooled analysis that added 2 Japanese replication cohorts (1,366 PD patients and 16,669 controls) confirmed robust association with the PARK8 locus, with similar strength of association in Chinese and Japanese subjects. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ishihara et al. (2006) found no observable differences between the phenotypes of PD patients homozygous for LRRK2 G2019S and previously published heterozygous patients. They concluded that LRRK2-related PD has typical clinical features, and that the similarity between homozygotes and heterozygotes does not support a gene dosage effect.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) found that women and patients with a positive family history of PD were significantly overrepresented among Jewish Israeli G2019S carriers with PD. Age at onset was similar in carriers and noncarriers. They suggested that gender affects the frequency of the mutation among patients.

Lesage et al. (2008) examined whether the LRRK2 G2019S mutation is related to dyskinesias in North African patients with PD.

Among 925 early-onset PD probands, Alcalay et al. (2009) found that LRRK2 G2019S carriers had lower tremor scores and were more likely than noncarriers to have the PIGD phenotype (92.3% vs 58.9%). The association persisted after adjustment for disease duration and Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry (odds ratio 17.7) and was present in Ashkenazi Jewish and non-Ashkenazi probands analyzed separately. It was seen mainly in the shortest disease-duration tertile: nearly all carriers had PIGD regardless of disease duration, whereas in noncarriers PIGD prevalence rose with disease duration. Carriers did not differ from noncarriers in age at onset, disease duration, UPDRS-III score, levodopa dose, or MMSE score.

Gan-Or et al. (2010) screened 600 Ashkenazi PD patients for LRRK2 G2019S and for 8 GBA mutations. More LRRK2 G2019S carriers reported muscle stiffness (rigidity, p = 0.007) and balance disturbances (p = 0.008) as initial symptoms, whereas more GBA mutation carriers reported slowness (bradykinesia, p = 0.021). The authors suggested that LRRK2 and GBA mutations have distinct effects on the initial symptoms of PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that early-onset PD patients carrying LRRK2 G2019S were more likely than noncarriers to have the PIGD motor phenotype (96% vs 58%).

### Pathogenesis

In the Sagamihara family, whose clinical features did not differ from common PD, Hasegawa and Kowa (1997) found that autopsied cases had only mild to moderate loss of melanin-containing cells, preserved locus ceruleus neurons, and no Lewy bodies. Funayama et al. (2005) later described the pathology of this family as pure nigral degeneration without Lewy bodies.

Wszolek et al. (2004) reported variable pathology in 4 autopsied members of the PARK8-linked kindred. All 4 had neuronal loss and gliosis in the substantia nigra. Lewy bodies were limited to brainstem nuclei in 1 case and diffuse in another, a third had neurofibrillary tangles without Lewy bodies, and the fourth had neither tangles nor Lewy bodies.

Zimprich et al. (2004) found that 6 postmortem examinations of affected LRRK2 mutation carriers in families A and D showed brainstem dopaminergic degeneration with strikingly diverse pathologies. These included Lewy body PD, diffuse Lewy body disease, nigral degeneration without distinctive histopathology, and progressive supranuclear palsy-like pathology. They suggested that LRRK2 may be central to the pathogenesis of several major neurodegenerative disorders associated with parkinsonism.

Ross et al. (2006) found the LRRK2 G2019S mutation in 8 of 405 autopsied PD/Lewy body disease cases and reported that Lewy body disease was the most common neuropathology of G2019S-associated PD. ⚠︎

Giasson et al. (2006) showed by biochemical analysis that Lrrk2 is mainly a soluble cytoplasmic protein of about 250 kD, expressed throughout the brain and in many other organs. Of 3 patients with the G2019S mutation, 2 had classic PD with Lewy bodies, and 1 of these also had concurrent pathologic changes consistent with Alzheimer disease. The third had parkinsonism without Lewy bodies but showed dystrophic neurites in the substantia nigra that stained intensely for Lrrk2. Lrrk2 accumulated only in this patient and was not detected in other types of pathologic inclusions. The authors suggested that LRRK2 mutations may make select neuronal populations more vulnerable to cellular insults that can lead to aberrant protein aggregation.

Zhu et al. (2006) reported that LRRK2 protein is a component of Lewy bodies.

Rajput et al. (2006) found no evidence that tau or alpha-synuclein interacts directly with LRRK2. Based on family SK, in which the G2019S mutation segregated with parkinsonism and the proband had neurofibrillary tangle pathology, they suggested that alpha-synucleinopathy and tauopathy, the main pathologies associated with parkinsonism, may be alternate outcomes of the same genetic cause.

Silveira-Moriyama et al. (2008) found alpha-synuclein accumulation with Lewy bodies in the rhinencephalon in all 4 parkinsonian G2019S carriers examined postmortem.

Aasly et al. (2012) concluded that the disposition of amyloid beta and tau is likely important in both LRRK2-related and sporadic PD, even in early phases of the disease, and that these proteins may be useful biomarkers. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Nichols et al. (2005) recommended that screening for the LRRK2 G2019S mutation be part of genetic testing for PD. Gilks et al. (2005) suggested that testing for G2019S would be important in the management and genetic counseling of patients with PD.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) concluded that testing symptomatic patients may help establish the diagnosis of PD. They considered screening of asymptomatic individuals to be of questionable value until the penetrance and age-dependent risk of G2019S are better known.

Nandhagopal et al. (2008) found that reduced dopamine transporter binding was the earliest sign of subclinical dopaminergic dysfunction in presymptomatic LRRK2 mutation carriers. Progression to clinical disease was generally associated with the appearance of abnormal fluorodopa uptake.

In a review, Dächsel and Farrer (2010) noted that mutations predisposing to PD, including those in LRRK2, are diagnostically useful in early or atypical presentations. They added that affected and asymptomatic LRRK2 carriers offer a chance to define the natural history of PD, including penetrance, motor and nonmotor features, and related biomarkers.

### Clinical Management

Ross et al. (2006) suggested that treatments aimed at modulating LRRK2 kinase activity may be important for patients with genetically defined familial PD or typical sporadic PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) concluded that the penetrance of these PD mutations remains unknown. They stated that genetic testing for PD currently has little or no role in family planning or in predictive or prenatal testing. However, they suggested that patients with early-onset PD may benefit from genetic counseling if they are of Jewish or Hispanic ancestry, had onset at or before age 30 years, or have an affected first-degree relative.

In the Pakistan Genome Resource, Koch et al. (2026) found kidney dysfunction in individuals homozygous for LRRK2 loss-of-function variants. They stated that this supports the renal findings in LRRK2-knockout rodents and in preclinical studies of LRRK2 inhibitors, and recommended monitoring kidney function during long-term LRRK2 inhibition for PD, while noting that the number of carriers was small. They also noted that heterozygous loss of function in LRRK2 had not been associated with any phenotype, including PD.

### Population Genetics

Nichols et al. (2005) concluded that the LRRK2 G2019S mutation causes PD in 5% of individuals with familial disease. Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found G2019S in families originating from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil, showing that it is present in different populations and is a common mutation in dominantly inherited PD. Gilks et al. (2005), who found G2019S in 1.6% of patients with idiopathic PD, concluded that a common single mendelian mutation is implicated in sporadic PD.

Lesage et al. (2005) performed LRRK2 haplotype analyses in European and North African families with PD and reported a common founder for the G2019S mutation dating from the 13th century.

In a case-control study of 121 PD patients (predominantly white Canadians, 84% with onset before age 50) and 250 controls, Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) found no association between PD and 4 common coding LRRK2 polymorphisms. Neither these genotypes nor APOE genotypes modified age at onset, although a modest risk effect could not be excluded.

Farrer et al. (2005) used allelic discrimination assays to screen for 9 LRRK2 mutations in 786 PD probands, 32 affected sibs, 1,044 unaffected sibs, and 278 unrelated controls. They found mutations in 6 probands, 1 affected sib, 1 unaffected sib, and 1 unrelated control, and concluded that LRRK2 mutations only rarely cause idiopathic PD.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) screened Jewish Israeli patients with PD for LRRK2 mutations and found only G2019S. It was present in 14.8% of Ashkenazi and 2.7% of non-Ashkenazi patients, and in 26% of Ashkenazi familial cases and 10.6% of apparently sporadic cases. Carrier frequencies were 2.4% in Ashkenazi controls and 0.4% in non-Ashkenazi controls. Haplotype analysis of 6 microsatellite markers spanning LRRK2 in 76 G2019S carriers showed a common shared haplotype in all non-Ashkenazi and half-Ashkenazi carriers, and in all but 2 of the full-Ashkenazi carriers tested.

Choi et al. (2008) studied 72 unrelated Korean patients with early-onset PD (onset at or before age 50 years). Nine patients (12.5%) were heterozygous for the LRRK2 G2385R variant, which the authors called a polymorphism, and none carried G2019S. Overall, 25% of the patients carried variants in PARKIN, PINK1, LRRK2, or SNCA that the authors considered causes or genetic risk factors, and PARKIN mutations were the most common genetic cause.

Lesage et al. (2009) found G2019S in 13 of 226 probands (5.8%) with autosomal dominant PD. Six of them were from North Africa, accounting for 43% of North African probands. They concluded that LRRK2 mutations are a common cause of autosomal dominant PD in Europe and North Africa.

Alcalay et al. (2009) found G2019S in 34 (3.7%) of 925 early-onset PD probands from 13 centers. No I2020T, R1441C, or Y1699C mutations were found. Carriers were more likely than noncarriers to be Ashkenazi Jewish (55.9% vs 11.9%) but were not more likely to have a first-degree relative with PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that G2019S carriers with early-onset PD were more often of Jewish ancestry: 17 of 31 carriers of G2019S alone were Jewish, compared with 12% of noncarriers. Grandparents' country of origin was known for 11 of the 15 G2019S carriers who did not report Jewish ancestry. Of these, 4 reported Southern European descent, 4 mixed European descent, 1 Eastern European ancestry, and 2 Puerto Rican ancestry.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2008) used the GAL4/UAS system to generate transgenic Drosophila expressing wildtype human LRRK2 or LRRK2 G2019S. Expression of either form in photoreceptor cells caused retinal degeneration. Neuronal expression produced adult-onset selective loss of dopaminergic neurons, locomotor dysfunction, and early mortality. The G2019S mutant caused a more severe parkinsonism-like phenotype than equivalent levels of wildtype LRRK2. L-DOPA treatment improved the locomotor impairment caused by mutant LRRK2 but did not prevent the loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons. The authors stated that this was, to their knowledge, the first in vivo gain-of-function model to recapitulate several key features of LRRK2-linked parkinsonism.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 8, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
LRRK2, GLY2019SER [c.2877510G>A; c.6055G>A; p.G2019; p.G2019S]

Nichols et al. (2005) screened 767 affected individuals from 358 multiplex PD families and found the gly2019-to-ser (G2019S) mutation in the LRRK2 gene in 35 of them: 34 heterozygotes and 1 homozygote. All had typical clinical findings of idiopathic PD.

Di Fonzo et al. (2005) found the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 4 of 61 unrelated families with autosomal dominant PD, originating from Italy, Portugal, and Brazil. Onset was early in some patients and late in others.

Gilks et al. (2005) found the heterozygous G2019S mutation, which results from a 2877510G-A transition, in 8 (1.6%) of 482 patients with idiopathic PD.

Paisán-Ruíz et al. (2005) identified the heterozygous G2019S mutation in 2 probands with dominantly inherited PD. In a large family, the mutation segregated completely with PD.

Ishihara et al. (2006) described 29 patients with familial PD who were homozygous for the 6055G-A (G2019S) mutation: 23 Tunisians, 2 Algerians, 2 patients from the US, 1 Canadian, and 1 Moroccan. Their phenotype did not differ from that of previously published heterozygous patients.

Rajput et al. (2006) found that G2019S segregated with slowly progressive parkinsonism in family SK. The affected proband had tau-immunopositive neurofibrillary tangle pathology.

Orr-Urtreger et al. (2007) found G2019S in 14.8% of Ashkenazi and 2.7% of non-Ashkenazi Jewish Israeli PD patients. A common shared haplotype was present in all non-Ashkenazi and half-Ashkenazi carriers and in all but 2 of the full-Ashkenazi carriers tested.

Lesage et al. (2009) found G2019S in 13 of 226 probands with autosomal dominant PD, 6 of whom were from North Africa.
