### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polycystic kidney disease-2 with or without polycystic liver disease (PKD2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PKD2 gene on chromosome 4q21-q23. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is an inherited nephropathy, usually of late onset, characterized mainly by fluid-filled cysts in the kidneys. It is genetically heterogeneous. Deltas (2001) noted that the disease caused by mutation in PKD1 and the disease caused by mutation in PKD2 cannot be told apart on clinical grounds. However, patients with PKD2 mutations have a milder course, with onset on average 10 to 20 years later and a lower probability of reaching end-stage renal failure. Mutations in PKD2 are thought to account for about 15% of ADPKD cases (Torra et al., 1999; Koptides et al., 1999).

### Nomenclature

When Kimberling et al. (1993) mapped the second ADPKD locus to chromosome 4q, they designated it PKD4. Other reports used the designations PKD2 or ADPKD2 for the second ADPKD locus (Kumar et al., 1990; San Millán et al., 1995; Mochizuki et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

Kimberling et al. (1988) described a 5-generation kindred descended from Sicilian immigrants with ADPKD that was not linked to the alpha-hemoglobin complex on chromosome 16. Diagnoses were confirmed by ultrasound examination. The clinical findings in this family were indistinguishable from those in other families with polycystic kidney disease.

Romeo et al. (1988) reported an Italian family with ADPKD in which the disease was not linked to alpha-globin. The clinical features in this family were indistinguishable from those in families linked to chromosome 16.

Bachner et al. (1990) described a large 3-generation family with ADPKD and reduced expression. Ultrasonographic screening of 60 family members found 20 individuals, aged 10 to 80 years, with one or several cysts in only one kidney, and 7 individuals with cysts in both kidneys. Transmission of unilateral cysts appeared to be autosomal dominant, although there were some generation gaps. The authors concluded that the family had a form of ADPKD with reduced penetrance. They also considered other explanations, including 2 distinct hereditary diseases in the family, or a new mutation in one branch of the family together with a high frequency of isolated renal cysts.

Ravine et al. (1992) compared 18 families (285 affected members) with mutations at the PKD1 locus and 5 families (49 affected individuals) in which involvement of PKD1 could be excluded. Compared with PKD1 patients, the non-PKD1 patients: - lived longer (median survival 71.5 vs 56.0 years) - had a lower risk of progressing to renal failure (odds ratio 0.35) - were less likely to have hypertension (adjusted odds ratio 0.29) - were diagnosed at an older age (median 69.1 vs 44.8 years) - had fewer renal cysts at diagnosis Most PKD1 families had been ascertained through clinics treating patients with renal impairment, but no non-PKD1 family was identified through this source. The authors concluded that non-PKD1 ADPKD has a much milder phenotype and that its reported prevalence is probably an underestimate.

Bear et al. (1992) studied 17 Newfoundland families with ADPKD. In 2 families in which ADPKD was not co-inherited with PKD1 markers, only 11% of members younger than 30 years had kidney cysts. The mean age of onset of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) was 68.7 years in affected members of these families, compared with 56.3 years in persons with the PKD1 form (P = 0.01).

Jeffery et al. (1993) reported a family of Sicilian origin with ADPKD that was unlinked to chromosome 16 markers. The clinical phenotype was consistent with that of the other unlinked families reported in the literature, which have a milder progression than families linked to 16p13.3.

Coto et al. (1995) studied 17 large Spanish families with ADPKD using ultrasonography of all family members. In 5 families the disease was linked to 4q rather than to 16p13.3. Renal cysts developed at an earlier age in PKD1 mutation carriers, and end-stage renal failure occurred at an older age in persons with PKD2.

The European PKD1-PKD2 Study Group (Hateboer et al., 1999) compared clinical data from 333 people with PKD1 (31 families), 291 people with PKD2 (31 families), and 398 geographically matched controls. Median age at death or onset of ESRD was: - 53.0 years in PKD1 - 69.1 years in PKD2 - 78.0 years in controls Women with PKD2 had a significantly longer median survival than men (71.0 vs 67.3 years), whereas no sex effect was seen in PKD1. Median age at presentation with kidney failure was 74.0 years in PKD2 versus 54.3 years in PKD1. Compared with PKD1 patients, PKD2 patients were less likely to have hypertension (odds ratio 0.25), a history of urinary tract infection (odds ratio 0.50), or hematuria (odds ratio 0.59). The authors concluded that although PKD2 is milder than PKD1, it shortens overall life expectancy and cannot be regarded as benign.

Bergmann et al. (2008) reported perinatal deaths in a family with ADPKD and a PKD2 mutation.

Bataille et al. (2011) reported 3 unrelated patients with ADPKD and left-right laterality defects. One had dextrocardia and 2 had situs inversus totalis. No left-right anomalies were found in other members of the 3 families.

Losekoot et al. (2012) reported a patient with neonatal-onset polycystic kidney disease.

### Other Features

Khonsari et al. (2013) used 3-dimensional photography and dense surface modeling to analyze the faces of patients with ADPKD. They found specific facial characteristics, some of which correlated with features of mice with conditional deletion of Pkd2. The abstract did not state which ADPKD genes were involved in these patients.

### Inheritance

Kimberling et al. (1988) and Romeo et al. (1988) established autosomal dominant inheritance of ADPKD not linked to chromosome 16. Mutations in PKD2 segregated with the disease in families (Mochizuki et al., 1996). ⚠︎

### Mapping

Kimberling et al. (1988) carried out linkage studies on 163 members of a 5-generation Sicilian-descended kindred, 71 of whom were affected or at risk. The recombination frequency between the alpha-hemoglobin complex and the region known to contain the ADPKD mutation was over 24%. The authors concluded that there is a second gene for ADPKD.

In an Italian family, Romeo et al. (1988) found no linkage between ADPKD and alpha-globin, indicating that the disease can be caused by mutations in a second gene.

Bachner et al. (1990) found no linkage between ADPKD and several PKD1 markers in a large 3-generation family. Lod scores with the most informative marker, 3-prime HVR, ranged from 1.31 to -21.47 (theta = 0), depending on the penetrance model and on how individuals with unilateral cysts were classified. Smith's test of heterogeneity gave a conditional probability of nonlinkage between 0.9 and 1.0.

Kumar et al. (1990) typed members of a large ADPKD family unlinked to chromosome 16 for 12 marker loci on chromosome 1. Multipoint analysis excluded ADPKD2 from about 61% of chromosome 1: the region between D1S81 and D1S67 on the long arm, and between Rh and PGM1 on the short arm. In the same kindred, which had more than 250 members, Kumar et al. (1991) analyzed 29 informative marker loci on 11 autosomes. The data significantly excluded linkage at 17 marker loci.

Nørby and Schwartz (1990) suggested a possible locus for polycystic kidney disease on chromosome 2.

Fossdal et al. (1993) studied 7 Icelandic ADPKD families using markers flanking the PKD1 region. In 4 families the disease locus was in the PKD1 region, and 3 families were unlinked to 16p13.3. In one of the unlinked families, the disease locus was excluded from part of the long arm of chromosome 2. The authors supported the idea that more than 2 loci cause ADPKD.

Peters et al. (1993) studied 8 families with clear evidence against linkage to chromosome 16 markers and assigned a second ADPKD gene to chromosome 4. Linkage analysis with markers D4S231 and D4S423 gave a multipoint lod score of 22.42.

Kimberling et al. (1993) typed more than 100 microsatellite markers in informative members of the large Sicilian kindred. They localized the second ADPKD gene to chromosome 4q13-q23, flanked by D4S231 and D4S414 in a segment of about 9 cM.

San Millán et al. (1995) confirmed linkage to chromosome 4q in 8 Spanish ADPKD families not linked to 16p13.3. Marker D4S423 gave a maximum lod score of 9.03 at a recombination fraction of 0.00. Multipoint analysis and recombinant haplotypes placed the PKD2 locus between D4S1542 and D4S1563, an interval of approximately 1 cM.

### Heterogeneity

Elles et al. (1990) typed 24 families with adult-onset polycystic kidney disease for markers flanking the PKD1 locus. The aggregated results gave a significant lod score in favor of linkage to PKD1. Two families showed unusual features, including recombinations (some double), and one family had an unexpectedly high proportion of affected people. The authors proposed comparing the numbers of meioses that were apparently nonrecombinant and doubly recombinant for flanking markers. Using this test, neither their 2 families nor the published data provided compelling evidence for a second locus for adult polycystic kidney disease. This conclusion contrasted with those of Kimberling et al. (1988) and Romeo et al. (1988).

Fossdal et al. (1993) supported the existence of more than 2 ADPKD loci. In contrast, from their study of Spanish families, Coto et al. (1995) concluded that mutations at only 2 loci cause the disease in most, if not all, families.

Pei et al. (2001) studied a large family (NFL10) that had been excluded from linkage to both the PKD1 and PKD2 loci, as part of a search for a third ADPKD gene. They found bilineal disease, with independently segregating PKD1 and PKD2 mutations. The authors noted that bilineal disease needs to be considered as a confounder in the search for the PKD3 locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochizuki et al. (1996) identified the PKD2 gene by positional cloning. Nonsense mutations in PKD2 segregated with the disease in 3 PKD2 families. The predicted 968-amino acid protein has 6 transmembrane spans and similarity to PKD1 and to voltage-activated calcium (and sodium) channels.

Veldhuisen et al. (1997) screened the PKD2 gene by SSCP analysis in 35 families with the second type of ADPKD and identified 20 mutations. Most mutations appeared to be unique and occurred throughout the gene, with no evidence of clustering. They included small deletions, insertions, and substitutions leading to premature translation stops, as well as 1 amino acid substitution and 5 possible splice site mutations. The authors suggested that the first step toward cyst formation is the loss of one functional copy of polycystin-2.

Viribay et al. (1997) used heteroduplex and SSCP analysis to screen all 15 exons of PKD2 in chromosome 4-linked ADPKD families. They identified 7 novel mutations, a detection rate of about 90%. All of the mutations caused premature termination of translation: 4 were nonsense changes and 3 were frameshifting deletions (of 4 T nucleotides in 1 case and of 1 G nucleotide in 2 cases). All mutations were unique and distributed throughout the gene.

Pei et al. (1998) screened 11 Canadian ADPKD families for PKD2 mutations by SSCP analysis. Linkage to PKD2 had been documented in 4 families. In the other 7, smaller families, one or more affected members had late-onset ESRD at age 70 or older. Mutations were found in 8 of 11 families (approximately 73%), with no difference in detection rate between the linked families and the families with late-onset ESRD. In 3 unrelated families, an adenosine was inserted or deleted in an (A)8 tract at nucleotides 2152-2159 in exon 11, suggesting that this tract is prone to slipped-strand mispairing. All mutations lay between exons 1 and 11 and were predicted to produce a truncated polycystin-2 lacking the calcium-binding EF-hand domain and the cytoplasmic domains required for interaction with polycystin-1 and with itself. The authors suggested that most PKD2 mutations are inactivating.

Pei et al. (2001) studied a large family (NFL10), screening 48 members by ultrasonography and genotyping them at the PKD1 and PKD2 loci; 28 were affected. In 12 affected members they found a PKD2 mutation (2152delA; L736X). Linkage analysis supported a PKD1 mutation in 15 other affected members who lacked the PKD2 mutation. Two affected individuals were trans-heterozygous for mutations in both genes and had more severe renal disease than individuals with either mutation alone. These findings showed that trans-heterozygous PKD1 and PKD2 mutations are not necessarily embryonic lethal in humans.

Deltas (2001) reviewed reported PKD2 mutations. Mutations of all kinds occur over the entire PKD2 sequence, with no apparent significant clustering. Most families have private mutations, but a few recurrent mutations have been reported in unrelated families.

Bataille et al. (2011) screened PKD1 and PKD2 in 3 unrelated patients with ADPKD and laterality defects. They found a large gene deletion, a single-exon duplication, and an in-frame duplication, respectively, which the authors considered pathogenic PKD2 mutations. In each family, the variant was present in all tested relatives with ADPKD and absent in unaffected relatives.

Losekoot et al. (2012) reported a patient with neonatal-onset polycystic kidney disease who was homozygous for a PKD2 missense variant with incomplete penetrance. The homozygosity resulted from uniparental disomy.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Viribay et al. (1997) compared specific mutations with the clinical profile in ADPKD2 families and found no clear correlation. Similarly, Pei et al. (1998) found no correlation between the location of mutations in the PKD2 coding sequence and disease severity. In contrast, Deltas (2001), reviewing reported PKD2 mutations, noted some evidence of a genotype/phenotype correlation.

Pei et al. (2001) found that 2 individuals trans-heterozygous for PKD1 and PKD2 mutations had more severe renal disease than family members with either mutation alone.

### Pathogenesis

Torra et al. (1999) studied 30 renal cysts from a patient with PKD2 whose germline mutation was a deletion encompassing most of the gene. Loss of heterozygosity (LOH) studies showed loss of the wild-type allele in 10% of cysts. SSCP screening of 6 exons detected 8 different somatic mutations, all expected to produce truncated proteins. Overall, at least 37% of cysts had somatic mutations. There was no LOH at the PKD1 gene or at locus D3S1478. The authors suggested that second-hit mutations could be a crucial event in cystogenesis in ADPKD type 2.

Koptides et al. (1999) examined 21 cysts from both kidneys of an ADPKD2 patient. Seven cysts (33%) had the same C insertion in the inherited wild-type allele. Two other cysts had a nonsense mutation or a splice site AG deletion in a PKD2 allele whose parental origin could not be determined. The authors concluded that ADPKD2 occurs by a cellular recessive mechanism, supporting a 2-hit model of cyst formation.

In cystic DNA from the kidney of an ADPKD1 patient, Koptides et al. (2000) found somatic mutations in PKD1 in some cysts and somatic PKD2 mutations in others. Together with the germline PKD1 mutation, the somatic PKD2 mutations created a trans-heterozygous state. The authors considered this genetic evidence that polycystins 1 and 2 interact.

Watnick et al. (2000) found somatic PKD2 mutations in 71% of ADPKD2 cysts analyzed. Clonal somatic PKD1 mutations were found in a subset of cysts that lacked PKD2 mutations, suggesting a pathogenic effect of trans-heterozygous mutations.

### Diagnosis

Kimberling et al. (1988) and Romeo et al. (1988) cautioned that, because of locus heterogeneity, linkage must be shown in each family before DNA probes can be used reliably for prenatal and presymptomatic diagnosis. Bear et al. (1992) found that only 11% of members younger than 30 years in families not co-inheriting ADPKD with PKD1 markers had kidney cysts. They noted that the risk of false-negative ultrasonographic diagnosis appeared to be largely restricted to families in which ESRD occurs relatively late. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

ADPKD affects approximately 1 in 1,000 live births (Pei et al., 1998). Deltas (2001) gave a frequency of approximately 1 in 1,000 Caucasians. Peters et al. (1993) estimated that the chromosome 16 locus causes the disease in roughly 86% of affected European families. Mutations in PKD2 are thought to account for approximately 15% of cases (Torra et al., 1999; Koptides et al., 1999).

### Animal Model

Wu et al. (1998) introduced a mutant exon 1 in tandem with the wild-type exon 1 at the mouse Pkd2 locus, creating an unstable allele that becomes a null allele through somatic intragenic homologous recombination. Mice heterozygous or homozygous for this allele, as well as Pkd2 +/- mice, developed polycystic kidney and liver lesions indistinguishable from the human phenotype. Renal cysts arose from tubular cells that had lost Pkd2 protein. The authors suggested that PKD2 occurs by a cellular recessive mechanism.

Wu et al. (2000) found that Pkd2 -/- mice died in utero between embryonic day 13.5 and birth. They had cardiac septation defects and cysts in maturing nephrons and pancreatic ducts. Adult mice carrying the unstable allele over a null allele developed pancreatic ductal cysts, and kidney cyst formation was associated with renal failure and early death (median survival 65 weeks vs 94 weeks in controls). Adult Pkd2 +/- mice had intermediate survival without cystic disease or renal failure.

Pennekamp et al. (2002) found that mouse embryos homozygous for a Pkd2 knockout allele had right pulmonary isomerism and randomization of embryonic turning, heart looping, and abdominal situs. Leftb and nodal were not expressed in the left lateral plate mesoderm, Ebaf was absent from the floorplate, and Pitx2 was expressed bilaterally in posterior lateral plate mesoderm but absent anteriorly. The authors suggested that polycystin-2 acts downstream of or in parallel to shh and upstream of the nodal cascade.

Khonsari et al. (2013) deleted Pkd2 in neural crest-derived cells of mice using Wnt1-Cre. The mutant mice had fractured molar roots, distorted incisors, alveolar bone loss, compressed temporomandibular joints, and abnormal skull shapes. None of these features was present at embryonic stages. The authors suggested that Pkd2 acts as a mechanoreceptor in craniofacial growth.

### History

Kimberling et al. (1993) noted that the genetic heterogeneity of ADPKD was first discovered in the large Sicilian kindred described by Kimberling et al. (1988). ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 POLYCYSTIC KIDNEY DISEASE 2**  
PKD2, 1-BP DEL, 2152A [2152delA; L736X]

In 3 unrelated Canadian families with ADPKD, Pei et al. (1998) found insertion or deletion of an adenosine in an (A)8 tract at nucleotides 2152-2159 in exon 11 of the PKD2 gene. The mutation was predicted to produce a truncated polycystin-2.

In 12 affected members of a large ADPKD family (NFL10) with bilineal disease, Pei et al. (2001) found a 2152delA mutation in the PKD2 gene, predicted to result in a leu736-to-ter (L736X) substitution. They identified the mutation by single-stranded conformational analysis. Two affected family members were trans-heterozygous for this PKD2 mutation and a PKD1 mutation and had more severe renal disease.
