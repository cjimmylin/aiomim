### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polycystic kidney disease-2 with or without polycystic liver disease (PKD2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PKD2 gene on chromosome 4q21-q22. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is an inherited kidney disease. It usually begins between the third and seventh decades and is mainly characterized by fluid-filled cysts forming in the kidneys. Disease caused by PKD2 mutations follows a course very similar to that of PKD1 disease. However, patients with PKD2 mutations have a milder course: on average, onset is 10 to 20 years later and they are less likely to reach end-stage renal failure (Deltas, 2001). Mutations in PKD2 are expected to account for approximately 15% of ADPKD cases (Torra et al., 1999).

### Nomenclature

When Kimberling et al. (1993) mapped a second ADPKD locus to chromosome 4q, they designated the new locus PKD4.

### Clinical Features

Kimberling et al. (1988) described a 5-generation kindred descended from Sicilian immigrants in which ADPKD occurred without linkage to the alpha-hemoglobin complex on chromosome 16. Blood samples were obtained from 163 family members, 71 of whom were affected by or at risk for ADPKD. Diagnoses were confirmed by ultrasound. The clinical findings in this family were indistinguishable from those in other families with polycystic kidney disease.

Romeo et al. (1988) reported an Italian family with ADPKD that was not linked to alpha-globin. The clinical features of the disease in this family were indistinguishable from those in the 'linked' families.

Bachner et al. (1990) described a large 3-generation family with ADPKD. Ultrasound screening of 60 family members found 20 individuals, aged 10 to 80 years, with one or several cysts in only one kidney, and 7 individuals with cysts in both kidneys. The authors concluded that this family had a form of ADPKD with reduced penetrance and no linkage to the PKD1 locus. They also considered other explanations, including 2 distinct hereditary diseases in the family, or a new mutation in one branch of the family together with a high frequency of isolated renal cysts.

Ravine et al. (1992) compared 18 families (285 affected members) with mutations at the PKD1 locus with 5 families (49 affected individuals) in which involvement of PKD1 could be dismissed. Compared with PKD1 patients, non-PKD1 patients: - lived longer (median survival 71.5 vs 56.0 years); - had a lower risk of progressing to renal failure (odds ratio 0.35); - were less likely to have hypertension (adjusted odds ratio 0.29); - were diagnosed at an older age (median 69.1 vs 44.8 years); - had fewer renal cysts at diagnosis. No non-PKD1 family was identified through clinics treating patients with renal impairment. The authors concluded that non-PKD1 ADPKD has a much milder phenotype and that its prevalence is probably underestimated.

Bear et al. (1992) studied members of 17 Newfoundland families with ADPKD. In 2 families, ADPKD was not co-inherited with PKD1 markers. In these 2 families, only 11% of members younger than 30 years had kidney cysts. Mean (SE) age at onset of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) was 68.7 (1.7) years in affected members of these families, compared with 56.3 (1.8) years in persons with the PKD1 form (P = 0.01).

Jeffery et al. (1993) described a family of Sicilian origin with ADPKD that was unlinked to chromosome 16 markers. The clinical phenotype was consistent with that of other reported unlinked families, which progressed more mildly than families linked to 16p13.3.

Coto et al. (1995) studied 17 large Spanish families with ADPKD and performed ultrasound on all family members. In 5 families the disease was linked to 4q rather than 16p13.3. Renal cysts developed at an earlier age in PKD1 mutation carriers, and end-stage renal failure occurred at an older age in people with PKD2. The authors concluded that subjects with ADPKD2 had a better prognosis.

In a multicenter European survey, Hateboer et al. (1999) compared 333 people with PKD1 (31 families), 291 people with PKD2 (31 families) and 398 geographically matched controls. Median age at death or onset of ESRD was 53.0 years with PKD1, 69.1 years with PKD2, and 78.0 years in controls. Women with PKD2 had a significantly longer median survival than men (71.0 vs 67.3 years), whereas no sex effect was seen in PKD1. Patients with PKD2 presented with kidney failure later than those with PKD1 (median age 74.0 vs 54.3 years). They were also less likely to have hypertension, a history of urinary tract infection, or hematuria. The authors concluded that although PKD2 is clinically milder than PKD1, it shortens overall life expectancy and cannot be regarded as benign.

Bergmann et al. (2008) reported perinatal deaths in a family with ADPKD and a PKD2 mutation. Only the title of this report was available.

Bataille et al. (2011) studied 3 unrelated patients with ADPKD and laterality defects: 1 had dextrocardia and 2 had situs inversus totalis. No left-right anomalies were found in other members of the 3 families. The authors suggested a possible association between heterotaxia and PKD2 mutation.

Losekoot et al. (2012) reported a patient with neonatal-onset polycystic kidney disease who was homozygous for an incompletely penetrant PKD2 missense variant as a result of uniparental disomy.

Khonsari et al. (2013) noted that facial anomalies had never been identified in ADPKD patients. They analyzed 3-dimensional photographs of ADPKD patients' faces using dense surface modeling. The analysis revealed specific facial characteristics, some of which correlated with features of mice with conditional Pkd2 deletion in neural crest-derived cells.

### Inheritance

Kimberling et al. (1988) and Romeo et al. (1988) established that the disorder is autosomal dominant. They described families in which ADPKD was not linked to markers on chromosome 16. Mochizuki et al. (1996) found that nonsense mutations in the PKD2 gene segregated with the disease in 3 families. In the large family reported by Bachner et al. (1990), transmission of unilateral renal cysts appeared to be autosomal dominant, although some generations were skipped. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Kimberling et al. (1988) studied a 5-generation Sicilian-derived kindred. The recombination frequency between the alpha-hemoglobin complex and the region known to contain the polycystic kidney disease mutation exceeded 24%. The authors concluded that this indicated a mutation at a different locus. Romeo et al. (1988) found no linkage to alpha-globin in an Italian family, also pointing to a second gene.

Nørby and Schwartz (1990) suggested a possible locus for polycystic kidney disease on chromosome 2. Only the title of this report was available.

Kumar et al. (1990) typed members of a large ADPKD family unlinked to chromosome 16 for 12 marker loci on chromosome 1. Multipoint analysis excluded the disease locus from about 61% of chromosome 1. Kumar et al. (1991) studied a large Sicilian kindred with more than 250 members, unlinked to chromosome 16, using 29 informative marker loci on 11 autosomes. Linkage was significantly excluded for 17 of the marker loci, and there was no evidence of close linkage to the others.

Fossdal et al. (1993) studied 7 Icelandic ADPKD families. Four were linked to the PKD1 region and 3 were unlinked to 16p13.3. In 1 of the unlinked families, the disease locus was excluded from part of the long arm of chromosome 2.

Using highly polymorphic microsatellite markers, Peters et al. (1993) assigned a second ADPKD gene to chromosome 4. They studied 8 families with clear evidence against linkage to chromosome 16 markers and obtained a multipoint lod score of 22.42 with markers D4S231 and D4S423.

Kimberling et al. (1993) typed more than 100 microsatellite markers in the large Sicilian kindred in which the heterogeneity was first discovered. Every family member's affected or unaffected status was confirmed by renal ultrasound. The second ADPKD gene was localized to chromosome 4q13-q23, flanked by markers D4S231 and D4S414, a segment of about 9 cM.

San Millán et al. (1995) confirmed linkage to chromosome 4q in 8 Spanish ADPKD families that were not linked to 16p13.3, with a maximum lod score of 9.03 at a recombination fraction of 0.00 for marker D4S423. Multipoint linkage analysis and study of recombinant haplotypes placed the PKD2 locus between D4S1542 and D4S1563, a genetic interval of approximately 1 cM.

### Heterogeneity

Elles et al. (1990) typed 24 families with adult-onset polycystic kidney disease for markers flanking PKD1. The pooled results gave a significant lod score in favor of linkage to PKD1. Two families showed unusual features, including double recombinations. However, using a test that compares apparently nonrecombinant meioses with doubly recombinant meioses, the authors found that neither their 2 families nor the data then published gave compelling evidence for a second locus.

Fossdal et al. (1993) supported the idea that more than 2 loci are responsible for ADPKD. By contrast, Coto et al. (1995) concluded from their study of Spanish families that mutations at only 2 loci account for the disease in most, if not all, families.

Pei et al. (2001) studied a large family (NFL10) that had previously been excluded from linkage to both the PKD1 and PKD2 loci. They found that the disease in this family was bilineal, caused by independently segregating PKD1 and PKD2 mutations. The authors noted that bilineal disease should be considered as a confounder in the search for a PKD3 locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochizuki et al. (1996) identified the PKD2 gene by positional cloning. Nonsense mutations in PKD2 segregated with the disease in 3 PKD2 families. The predicted 968-amino acid protein has 6 transmembrane spans with intracellular N and C termini. It shows amino acid similarity with PKD1 and with voltage-activated calcium (and sodium) channels, and it contains a potential calcium-binding domain.

Veldhuisen et al. (1997) screened the PKD2 gene by SSCP analysis in 35 families with the second type of ADPKD and identified 20 mutations. Most of the mutations appeared to be unique and were spread throughout the gene without clustering. Besides small deletions, insertions and substitutions causing premature translation stops, they found 1 amino acid substitution and 5 possible splice site mutations. The authors suggested that loss of one functional copy of polycystin-2 is the first step toward cyst formation.

Viribay et al. (1997) screened all 15 exons of PKD2 in chromosome 4-linked ADPKD families using heteroduplex and SSCP analyses. They identified 7 novel mutations, with a detection rate of approximately 90%. All of the mutations caused premature termination of translation: 4 were nonsense changes and 3 were frameshift deletions, 1 of 4 T nucleotides and 2 of 1 G nucleotide. All mutations were unique and distributed throughout the gene.

Pei et al. (1998) used SSCP analysis to screen all 15 PKD2 exons in 1 affected member of each of 11 Canadian ADPKD families. Linkage to PKD2 had previously been documented in 4 of the families. In the other 7, at least 1 affected member had late-onset ESRD at age 70 or older. Mutations were found in 8 of the 11 families (approximately 73%), with no difference in detection rate between the 2 groups. In 3 unrelated families, an adenosine was inserted or deleted in a polyadenosine tract ((A)8 at nucleotides 2152-2159) in exon 11. All the mutations lay between exons 1 and 11 and were predicted to produce a truncated polycystin-2 lacking the EF-hand calcium-binding domain and the 2 cytoplasmic domains needed for interaction with polycystin-1 and with itself.

Deltas (2001) reviewed reported PKD2 mutations. Mutations of all kinds had been found across the entire PKD2 sequence without apparent significant clustering. Most families carried private mutations, but a few recurrent mutations had been reported in unrelated families.

In the large NFL10 family, Pei et al. (2001) screened 48 members by ultrasound and genotyped them at both the PKD1 and PKD2 loci; 28 were affected with ADPKD. Using SSCP analysis, they found a PKD2 mutation (2152delA; L736X) in 12 affected members. Linkage analysis at the PKD1 locus gave significant lod scores (greater than 3.0), supporting a PKD1 mutation in 15 other affected members who lacked the PKD2 mutation. Two further affected individuals were trans-heterozygous for mutations in both genes.

Bataille et al. (2011) screened PKD1 and PKD2 for mutations or large genomic rearrangements in 3 unrelated ADPKD patients with laterality defects. They found a large gene deletion in 1 patient, a single-exon duplication in another, and an in-frame duplication in the third. Each variant was present in all tested relatives with ADPKD and absent in unaffected relatives. A pathogenic PKD2 mutation was detected in all 3 probands.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Viribay et al. (1997) compared specific PKD2 mutations with the clinical profile in ADPKD2 families and found no clear correlation. Pei et al. (1998) likewise found no correlation between the location of PKD2 mutations and disease severity. In contrast, Deltas (2001) noted some evidence of genotype/phenotype correlation in his review of PKD2 mutations.

Pei et al. (2001) found that 2 individuals who were trans-heterozygous for PKD1 and PKD2 mutations had more severe renal disease than affected relatives with either mutation alone. They concluded that in humans, trans-heterozygous mutations in both PKD1 and PKD2 are not necessarily embryonically lethal.

Losekoot et al. (2012) described neonatal-onset polycystic kidney disease in a patient who was homozygous, through uniparental disomy, for an incompletely penetrant PKD2 missense variant.

### Pathogenesis

Torra et al. (1999) studied 30 renal cysts from a patient with PKD2 whose germline mutation was a deletion covering most of the gene. Loss of the wild-type allele was found in 10% of the cysts. SSCP screening of 6 exons detected 8 different somatic mutations, all expected to produce truncated proteins. Overall, at least 37% of the cysts had somatic mutations. No loss of heterozygosity was seen for PKD1 or for locus D3S1478. The authors suggested that second-hit mutations may be a crucial event in cyst formation in ADPKD type 2.

Koptides et al. (1999) found multiple somatic PKD2 mutations in cyst epithelial cells from both kidneys of an ADPKD2 patient. Of 21 cysts examined, 7 (33%) had the same C insertion in the inherited wild-type allele. Two other cysts had a nonsense mutation and a splice site AG deletion in a PKD2 allele that could not be identified as either the inherited wild-type or mutant allele. The authors suggested that ADPKD2 arises by a cellular recessive mechanism, supporting a 2-hit model of cyst formation.

In cystic DNA from the kidney of an ADPKD1 patient, Koptides et al. (2000) found somatic mutations in PKD1 in some cysts and in PKD2 in others. This created a trans-heterozygous state with a germinal PKD1 mutation and a somatic PKD2 mutation. The authors considered this the first direct genetic evidence that polycystins 1 and 2 interact.

Watnick et al. (2000) found somatic PKD2 mutations in 71% of the ADPKD2 cysts they analyzed. Clonal somatic PKD1 mutations were found in a subset of cysts that lacked PKD2 mutations, suggesting a pathogenic effect of trans-heterozygous mutations.

### Population Genetics

Peters et al. (1993) estimated that a gene defect on the short arm of chromosome 16 is responsible for ADPKD in roughly 86% of affected European families. Pei et al. (1998) stated that ADPKD affects approximately 1 in 1,000 live births. They also stated that most cases (approximately 85%) are due to PKD1 mutations and that PKD2 mutations account for most of the rest.

### Animal Model

Wu et al. (1998) introduced a mutant exon 1 in tandem with the wild-type exon 1 at the mouse Pkd2 locus. This created an unstable allele that undergoes somatic inactivation by intragenic homologous recombination to produce a true null allele. Mice heterozygous or homozygous for this allele, as well as Pkd2 +/- mice, developed polycystic kidney and liver lesions indistinguishable from the human phenotype. In all cases, renal cysts arose from tubular cells that had lost the ability to produce Pkd2 protein. The authors concluded that somatic loss of Pkd2 expression is both necessary and sufficient for renal cyst formation.

Wu et al. (2000) found that Pkd2 -/- mice died in utero between embryonic day 13.5 and birth. These embryos had structural defects in cardiac septation and cysts in maturing nephrons and pancreatic ducts. Adult Pkd2(WS25/-) mice developed pancreatic ductal cysts, as well as kidney cysts associated with renal failure and early death (median survival 65 weeks vs 94 weeks in controls). Adult Pkd2 +/- mice had intermediate survival without cystic disease or renal failure.

Pennekamp et al. (2002) generated a Pkd2 knockout allele in mice. Homozygous mutant embryos had right pulmonary isomerism and randomized embryonic turning, heart looping and abdominal situs. Leftb and nodal were not expressed in the left lateral plate mesoderm. The authors suggested that polycystin-2 acts downstream of or in parallel to shh and upstream of the nodal cascade.

Khonsari et al. (2013) analyzed mice with conditional deletion of Pkd2 in neural crest-derived cells, using Wnt1Cre mice. The mutants showed many signs of mechanical trauma, including fractured molar roots, distorted incisors, alveolar bone loss and compressed temporomandibular joints, as well as abnormal skull shapes. None of these features were present at embryonic stages.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 POLYCYSTIC KIDNEY DISEASE 2**  
PKD2, 1-BP DEL, 2152A [2152delA; L736X]

In the large ADPKD family NFL10, Pei et al. (2001) used SSCP analysis to identify a 1-bp deletion in the PKD2 gene (2152delA). The deletion is predicted to cause a premature stop codon (leu736-to-ter; L736X). It was found in 12 affected family members. The family had bilineal disease, with a PKD1 mutation inferred in 15 other affected members. Two affected individuals were trans-heterozygous for PKD1 and PKD2 mutations and had more severe renal disease than relatives with either mutation alone.
