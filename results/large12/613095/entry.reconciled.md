### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polycystic kidney disease-2 with or without polycystic liver disease (PKD2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PKD2 gene (173910) on chromosome 4q22.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Polycystic kidney disease-2 (PKD2) is a form of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) caused by mutation in the PKD2 gene on chromosome 4. Koptides et al. (1999) stated that PKD2 accounts for approximately 15% of ADPKD cases, compared with approximately 85% for PKD1 on chromosome 16. Deltas (2001) noted that PKD1 and PKD2 disease could not be told apart on clinical grounds, but that patients with PKD2 mutations had a milder course, with an average age of onset 10 to 20 years later and a lower likelihood of reaching end-stage renal failure. Hateboer et al. (1999) concluded that, although clinically milder than PKD1, PKD2 has a deleterious impact on life expectancy and cannot be regarded as benign.

### Nomenclature

Kimberling et al. (1993) designated the new ADPKD locus they mapped to chromosome 4q as PKD4.

### Clinical Features

Kimberling et al. (1988) described a 5-generation kindred, descended from Sicilian immigrants, with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease not linked to the alpha-hemoglobin complex on chromosome 16p. Diagnoses were confirmed by ultrasound examination, and the clinical findings in this family were indistinguishable from those in other families with polycystic kidney disease.

Romeo et al. (1988) reported an Italian family with adult-type polycystic kidney disease not linked to the alpha-globin genes on 16p. The clinical features in this family were indistinguishable from those found in families linked to chromosome 16.

Bachner et al. (1990) studied a large 3-generation family with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease with reduced expression. Ultrasonographic screening of 60 family members revealed 20 individuals, aged 10 to 80 years, with 1 or several cysts in only 1 kidney, and 7 individuals with cysts in both kidneys.

Ravine et al. (1992) compared 5 ADPKD families (49 affected individuals) in which involvement of the PKD1 locus could be dismissed with 18 PKD1 families (285 affected members). The non-PKD1 patients lived longer (median survival 71.5 vs 56.0 years), had a lower risk of renal failure (odds ratio 0.35) and of hypertension (adjusted odds ratio 0.29), were diagnosed at an older age (median 69.1 vs 44.8 years), and had fewer renal cysts at the time of diagnosis.

Bear et al. (1992) found that in 2 Newfoundland families in which ADPKD was not coinherited with PKD1 markers, only 11% of members younger than 30 years had kidney cysts. The mean age of onset of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in affected members of these families was 68.7 years, compared with 56.3 years in persons with the PKD1 form (P = 0.01).

Jeffery et al. (1993) reported a family of Sicilian origin with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease unlinked to chromosome 16 markers. The clinical phenotype was consistent with that of other reported non-linked families, all outside the United Kingdom, which had a milder progression than families linked to 16p13.3.

Coto et al. (1995) studied 5 Spanish ADPKD families showing linkage to 4q. End-stage renal failure occurred at an older age in persons affected with PKD2 than in those with PKD1.

Pei et al. (1998) studied Canadian families with PKD2 mutations; in 7 smaller families, 1 or more affected members had late-onset ESRD at age 70 or older. ⚠︎

Hateboer et al. (1999) compared clinical data from 291 individuals with PKD2 from 31 European families with data from 333 individuals with PKD1 (31 families) and 398 geographically matched controls. Median age at death or onset of ESRD was 69.1 years in PKD2, 53.0 years in PKD1, and 78.0 years in controls, and median age at presentation with kidney failure was 74.0 years in PKD2 versus 54.3 years in PKD1. PKD2 patients were less likely than PKD1 patients to have hypertension (odds ratio 0.25), a history of urinary tract infection (odds ratio 0.50), or hematuria (odds ratio 0.59). ⚠︎

Pei et al. (2001) found that 2 individuals in a large family (NFL10) who were trans-heterozygous for a PKD2 mutation and a linked PKD1 mutation had renal disease that was more severe than that in affected individuals with either mutation alone.

Bergmann et al. (2008) reported perinatal deaths in a family with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease and a PKD2 mutation.

Bataille et al. (2011) reported 3 unrelated patients with ADPKD and PKD2 mutations who had laterality defects: dextrocardia in 1 and situs inversus totalis in 2. No left-right anomalies were found in other members of the 3 families. Noting that Pkd2-null mouse and zebrafish models have laterality defects and that all 3 probands with ADPKD and laterality defects had a PKD2 mutation, although PKD2 mutations account for only 15% of ADPKD families, the authors suggested a possible association between heterotaxia and PKD2 mutations and recommended systematic screening for laterality defects in larger cohorts of ADPKD patients with PKD2 mutations.

Losekoot et al. (2012) reported a patient with neonatal-onset polycystic kidney disease who was homozygous for an incompletely penetrant PKD2 missense variant as a result of uniparental disomy.

Khonsari et al. (2013) analyzed the faces of ADPKD patients using 3-dimensional photography and dense surface modeling and found specific facial characteristics, some of which correlated with those of Pkd2 conditional mutant mice.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of PKD2 is autosomal dominant. Kimberling et al. (1988) described the disorder in a 5-generation kindred, and Bachner et al. (1990) noted that transmission of unilateral renal cysts in a large 3-generation family appeared to be autosomal dominant, although there were some generation gaps. Mochizuki et al. (1996) found that heterozygous nonsense mutations in the PKD2 gene segregated with the disease in 3 PKD2 families. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Kimberling et al. (1988) performed linkage studies with DNA probes on 163 members of a Sicilian-descended kindred, 71 of whom were affected by or at risk for ADPKD, and excluded linkage to the alpha-hemoglobin complex on chromosome 16p.

Norby and Schwartz (1990) proposed a possible locus for polycystic kidney disease on chromosome 2.

Kumar et al. (1990) typed members of a large ADPKD family unlinked to chromosome 16 for 12 chromosome 1 markers. Multipoint analysis excluded the ADPKD2 locus from the region between D1S81 and D1S67 on the long arm and between Rh and PGM1 on the short arm, excluding it from about 61% of chromosome 1.

Kumar et al. (1991) studied a large Sicilian kindred with over 250 members, unlinked to chromosome 16. Analysis of 29 informative marker loci on 11 autosomes significantly excluded linkage to 17 marker loci and showed no evidence of close linkage to the others.

Jeffery et al. (1993) excluded linkage to the PKD1 region in a Sicilian family: lod scores for the flanking markers 3-prime HVR and SM7 were -1.4 and -2.33, respectively, with theta max of 0.5 for each marker.

Peters et al. (1993) used highly polymorphic microsatellite markers to assign a second ADPKD gene to chromosome 4. In 8 families with clear evidence against linkage to chromosome 16 markers, linkage analysis with markers D4S231 and D4S423 gave a multipoint lod score of 22.42.

Kimberling et al. (1993) typed over 100 microsatellite markers in the large Sicilian kindred in which genetic heterogeneity of ADPKD was first discovered; affected and unaffected status of every family member was confirmed by renal ultrasonography. They mapped the second ADPKD gene to chromosome 4q13-q23, in a segment of about 9 cM flanked by D4S231 and D4S414.

In 1 of 3 Icelandic families unlinked to 16p13.3, Fossdal et al. (1993) excluded the disease locus from part of the long arm of chromosome 2, and suggested that more than 2 loci may be responsible for ADPKD.

San Millan et al. (1995) confirmed linkage to chromosome 4q markers in 8 Spanish ADPKD families not linked to 16p13.3, obtaining a maximum lod score of 9.03 at theta = 0.00 with marker D4S423. Multipoint analysis and recombinant haplotypes placed the PKD2 locus between D4S1542 and D4S1563, an interval of approximately 1 cM.

Mochizuki et al. (1996) identified the PKD2 gene by positional cloning.

### Heterogeneity

Kimberling et al. (1988) found that in a Sicilian-descended kindred with ADPKD the recombination frequency with the alpha-hemoglobin complex on 16p exceeded 24%, indicating a mutation at a different locus, and concluded that there is a second gene for ADPKD. Romeo et al. (1988) likewise found no linkage to the alpha-globin genes in an Italian family and concluded that the condition can be caused by mutations in a second gene.

Elles et al. (1990) typed 24 families with adult-onset polycystic kidney disease for markers flanking PKD1; aggregated results gave a significant lod score in favor of linkage to PKD1. Two families showed unusual features, including double recombinations with flanking markers and, in 1 family, an unexpectedly high proportion of affected persons. Comparing apparently nonrecombinant with doubly recombinant meioses, the authors concluded that neither their families nor previously published data gave compelling evidence for a second locus. ⚠︎

Bachner et al. (1990) found no linkage to PKD1 markers in a large 3-generation family with ADPKD with reduced penetrance; lod scores with the 3-prime HVR marker ranged from 1.31 to -21.47 at theta = 0 depending on the model, and Smith's heterogeneity test gave a conditional probability of nonlinkage between 0.9 and 1.0. They also considered the alternative explanations of 2 distinct hereditary diseases in the family, or a new mutation in one branch together with a high frequency of isolated renal cysts.

Ravine et al. (1992) identified 5 ADPKD families in which linkage to the PKD1 locus could be excluded, supporting locus heterogeneity. Bear et al. (1992) found that of 17 Newfoundland ADPKD families, 10 showed linkage to PKD1 markers, whereas in 2 families the disease was not coinherited with PKD1 markers.

Jeffery et al. (1993) suggested that clinic populations may represent the most severe forms of the disease while non-PKD1 disease is less aggressive, so that the genetic heterogeneity of ADPKD in the population may be much greater than suggested. Fossdal et al. (1993) found that in 7 Icelandic ADPKD families the disease locus mapped to the PKD1 region in 4, but 3 families were unlinked to 16p13.3.

Coto et al. (1995) studied 17 large Spanish ADPKD families, 5 of which showed negative linkage to 16p13.3 markers and significant linkage to 4q, and concluded that mutations at only 2 loci cause the disease in most, if not all, of the families.

Pei et al. (2001) found bilineal disease in a large family (NFL10) previously excluded from linkage to both PKD1 and PKD2, and cautioned that bilineal disease must be considered as a confounder in the search for a putative PKD3 locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochizuki et al. (1996) found that nonsense mutations in the PKD2 gene segregated with the disease in 3 PKD2 families. The predicted 968-amino acid PKD2 protein has 6 transmembrane spans with intracellular N and C termini, shows amino acid similarity to PKD1, its C. elegans homolog, and the family of voltage-activated calcium (and sodium) channels, and contains a potential calcium-binding domain. ⚠︎

Veldhuisen et al. (1997) screened the PKD2 gene by SSCP analysis in 35 families with type 2 ADPKD and identified 20 mutations, most of which appeared unique and were distributed throughout the gene without clustering. They included small deletions, insertions, and substitutions leading to premature translation stops, 1 amino acid substitution, and 5 possible splice site mutations.

Viribay et al. (1997) screened all 15 exons of the PKD2 gene in chromosome 4-linked ADPKD families by heteroduplex and SSCP analyses and identified 7 novel mutations, a detection rate of approximately 90%. All resulted in premature termination of translation: 4 nonsense changes and 3 frameshifting deletions (of 4 T nucleotides in 1 case and 1 G nucleotide in 2). The mutations were unique and distributed throughout the gene without clustering.

Pei et al. (1998) used SSCP to screen all 15 exons of PKD2 in 11 Canadian ADPKD families, 4 with previously documented linkage to PKD2 and 7 including members with ESRD at age 70 or older. They found mutations in 8 of the 11 families (about 73%), with no difference in detection rate between the 2 groups. All mutations lay between exons 1 and 11 and were predicted to produce a truncated polycystin-2 lacking the EF-hand domain and the cytoplasmic interaction domains, suggesting that most PKD2 mutations are inactivating. In 3 unrelated families, insertion or deletion of an adenosine in a polyadenosine (A)8 tract at nucleotides 2152-2159 in exon 11 was found, suggesting that this mononucleotide repeat is prone to mutation by slipped-strand mispairing.

In a large family (NFL10) previously excluded from linkage to both PKD1 and PKD2, Pei et al. (2001) used single-stranded conformational analysis and found a PKD2 mutation (2152delA; L736X) in 12 affected members; 15 other affected members lacking the PKD2 mutation showed linkage to PKD1 (lod score greater than 3.0), and 2 affected individuals were trans-heterozygous for mutations in both genes. ⚠︎

Deltas (2001) reviewed reported PKD2 mutations. Mutations of all kinds had been reported over the entire sequence of the gene, with no apparent significant clustering. Most families harbored private mutations, but a few recurrent mutations had been reported in unrelated families.

Bergmann et al. (2008) reported a PKD2 mutation in a family with ADPKD and perinatal deaths.

In 3 unrelated patients with ADPKD and laterality defects, Bataille et al. (2011) found heterozygous PKD2 mutations: a large gene deletion, a single-exon duplication, and an in-frame duplication, respectively. The mutations were found in all tested relatives with ADPKD and were absent in unaffected relatives. ⚠︎

Losekoot et al. (2012) found that a patient with neonatal-onset polycystic kidney disease was homozygous for an incompletely penetrant PKD2 missense variant as a result of uniparental disomy.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Coto et al. (1995) found that renal cysts developed at an earlier age in PKD1 mutation carriers and that end-stage renal failure occurred at an older age in persons with PKD2, so that patients with ADPKD2 had a better prognosis than those with PKD1.

Viribay et al. (1997) found no clear correlation between specific PKD2 mutations and the clinical profile in ADPKD2 families. Pei et al. (1998) found no correlation between the position of a mutation in the PKD2 coding sequence and disease severity.

Hateboer et al. (1999) found that women with PKD2 had a significantly longer median survival than men (71.0 vs 67.3 years), whereas no sex influence was apparent in PKD1.

Pei et al. (2001) found that 2 individuals trans-heterozygous for PKD1 and PKD2 mutations had more severe renal disease than those with either mutation alone, indicating that trans-heterozygosity is not necessarily embryonically lethal in humans.

Losekoot et al. (2012) noted that renal cystic disease is generally milder in patients with PKD2 mutations than in those with PKD1 mutations, and reported neonatal-onset disease in a patient homozygous for an incompletely penetrant PKD2 missense variant.

### Pathogenesis

Veldhuisen et al. (1997) concluded that their mutation findings suggested that the first step toward cyst formation in PKD2 patients is the loss of 1 functional copy of polycystin-2.

Torra et al. (1999) studied 30 renal cysts from a PKD2 patient whose germline mutation was a deletion encompassing most of the PKD2 gene. Loss of the wildtype allele was found in 10% of cysts, and SSCP screening of 6 exons found 8 different somatic mutations, all predicted to truncate the protein; overall, at least 37% of cysts had somatic mutations. There was no loss of heterozygosity for PKD1 or locus D3S1478, showing that the somatic changes were specific to PKD2. The authors suggested that a second-hit mechanism could be a crucial event in cystogenesis in ADPKD type 2.

Koptides et al. (1999) found somatic PKD2 mutations in renal cyst epithelial cells from both kidneys of an ADPKD2 patient: 7 of 21 cysts (33%) had the same C insertion in the inherited wildtype allele, and 2 others had a nonsense mutation or a splice site AG deletion in an allele that could not be identified as the inherited wildtype or mutant allele. They suggested that ADPKD2 occurs by a cellular recessive mechanism, supporting a 2-hit model for cyst formation.

Koptides et al. (2000) cited their finding that 9 of 23 cysts from a human ADPKD2 kidney had somatic mutations inactivating the inherited normal allele, supporting a 2-hit loss-of-function model for ADPKD2 cystogenesis. In cysts from an ADPKD1 patient with a germline PKD1 mutation, some cysts had somatic PKD2 mutations, creating a trans-heterozygous state, which the authors took as the first direct genetic evidence that polycystins 1 and 2 interact, perhaps as part of a larger complex.

Watnick et al. (2000) found somatic PKD2 mutations in 71% of renal cysts analyzed from patients with ADPKD2. They also identified clonal somatic PKD1 mutations in a subset of ADPKD2 cysts lacking somatic PKD2 mutations, suggesting a pathogenic effect of trans-heterozygous mutations in PKD1 and PKD2, whose products associate to form heteromeric complexes.

Deltas (2001) stated that renal cysts most probably arise after a second somatic event that inactivates the inherited healthy allele of the same gene, or perhaps an allele of the other polycystin gene, producing a trans-heterozygous state.

### Diagnosis

Kimberling et al. (1988) cautioned that, because of locus heterogeneity, prenatal and presymptomatic diagnosis of ADPKD by linkage must be approached with caution until the 2 forms can be distinguished. Romeo et al. (1988) noted that linkage has to be shown separately in each family before predictive tests with DNA probes can be used reliably.

Elles et al. (1990) proposed testing for a second locus by comparing the relative numbers of meioses that appear nonrecombinant and doubly recombinant for markers flanking the disease locus, and suggested that for genetic counseling in families too small to show evidence for or against linkage the extra uncertainty be handled by using a higher recombination rate.

Bear et al. (1992) reported that only 11% of members younger than 30 years in 2 non-PKD1 families had kidney cysts, whereas in PKD1-linked families the false-negative ultrasonographic diagnosis rate was estimated at 36% below age 10 years and 8% or less thereafter.

Kimberling et al. (1993) suggested that using flanking markers for both the second ADPKD gene and PKD1 would help identify the ADPKD subtype in a family and make linkage-based presymptomatic diagnosis more accurate.

### Population Genetics

Ravine et al. (1992) noted that no non-PKD1 family had been ascertained through renal impairment clinics and concluded that, partly because of its milder phenotype, the reported prevalence of non-PKD1 ADPKD is probably an underestimate. Peters et al. (1993) and San Millan et al. (1995) noted that a gene defect at 16p13.3 is responsible for ADPKD in roughly 86% of affected European families.

### Animal Model

Wu et al. (1998) placed a mutant exon 1 next to the wildtype exon 1 at the mouse Pkd2 locus, creating an unstable allele that becomes a true null through somatic intragenic homologous recombination. Mice heterozygous or homozygous for this allele, and Pkd2 +/- mice, developed polycystic kidney and liver lesions indistinguishable from the human phenotype. Renal cysts always arose from tubular cells that had lost the ability to make Pkd2 protein, and the authors concluded that somatic loss of Pkd2 expression is both necessary and sufficient for renal cyst formation, suggesting a cellular recessive mechanism.

Wu et al. (2000) generated an unstable Pkd2 allele (WS25) that can undergo somatic rearrangement to a null allele, and a true null allele (WS183). Pkd2 -/- mice died in utero between embryonic day 13.5 and birth, with defects in cardiac septation and cysts in maturing nephrons and pancreatic ducts. Adult Pkd2 WS25/- mice developed kidney cysts, renal failure, pancreatic ductal cysts, and early death (median survival 65 weeks vs 94 weeks in controls). Adult Pkd2 +/- mice had survival intermediate between controls and WS25/- mice despite having no cystic disease or renal failure, the first sign that Pkd2 haploinsufficiency harms long-term survival.

Pennekamp et al. (2002) found that Pkd2 was expressed throughout mouse embryos at the headfold and early somite stages, with higher levels in the floorplate and notochord. Homozygous Pkd2 knockout embryos had right pulmonary isomerism and randomized embryonic turning, heart looping, and abdominal situs. Leftb and nodal were not expressed in the left lateral plate mesoderm, Ebaf was absent from the floorplate, and Pitx2 was expressed bilaterally in posterior lateral plate mesoderm but absent anteriorly, whereas the midline was present and Foxa2 and shh were expressed normally, suggesting that polycystin-2 acts downstream of or in parallel to shh and upstream of the nodal cascade.

Khonsari et al. (2013) found that Pkd2 was expressed in craniofacial tissues and deleted Pkd2 in neural crest-derived cells of mice using Wnt1Cre. The mutants showed fractured molar roots, distorted incisors, alveolar bone loss, compressed temporomandibular joints, and abnormal skull shapes, none of which was seen at embryonic stages. The authors suggested that Pkd2 likely plays a critical role in craniofacial growth as a mechanoreceptor.
