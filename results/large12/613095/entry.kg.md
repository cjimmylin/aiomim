### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polycystic kidney disease-2 with or without polycystic liver disease (PKD2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PKD2 gene (173910) on chromosome 4q22.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) caused by heterozygous mutation in PKD1 or PKD2 usually begins in adulthood. Renal cystic disease is generally milder in PKD2 patients than in PKD1 patients (Losekoot et al., 2012). About 85% of ADPKD cases are caused by PKD1 mutations, and most of the rest are caused by PKD2 mutations. PKD1-linked families reach end-stage renal disease (ESRD) earlier than PKD2-linked families, at a mean age of 56 versus 70 years (Pei et al., 1998). Deltas (2001) noted that the clinical courses of PKD1- and PKD2-related disease cannot be told apart. However, PKD2 mutation carriers had a milder course, with average onset 10 to 20 years later and a lower probability of reaching end-stage renal failure.

### Nomenclature

Kimberling et al. (1993) designated the second ADPKD locus, which they mapped to chromosome 4q, as PKD4.

### Clinical Features

Kimberling et al. (1988) studied a kindred of Sicilian descent with ADPKD that was not linked to chromosome 16p. Diagnoses in the kindred were confirmed by ultrasound, and the clinical findings could not be distinguished from those in other families with polycystic kidney disease.

Romeo et al. (1988) reported an Italian family with polycystic kidney disease that was not linked to chromosome 16. The clinical features in this family could not be told apart from those in families linked to chromosome 16. ⚠︎

Bachner et al. (1990) described a large 3-generation family with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease. Ultrasound screening of 60 family members found 20 individuals, aged 10 to 80 years, with one or several cysts in only one kidney, and 7 individuals with cysts in both kidneys.

Ravine et al. (1992) compared 5 ADPKD families (49 affected individuals) in which the PKD1 locus had been excluded with 18 PKD1-linked families (285 affected members). The non-PKD1 patients lived longer than the PKD1 patients (median survival 71.5 vs 56.0 years) and had a lower risk of progressing to renal failure (odds ratio 0.35). They were also less likely to have hypertension (adjusted odds ratio 0.29), were diagnosed at an older age (median 69.1 vs 44.8 years), and had fewer renal cysts at diagnosis.

Bear et al. (1992) studied Newfoundland families in which ADPKD was not co-inherited with PKD1 markers. In these families, the mean age at onset of end-stage renal disease was 68.7 years (SE 1.7), compared with 56.3 years (SE 1.8; P = 0.01) in PKD1 families. In the 2 non-PKD1 families, only 11% of members younger than 30 years had kidney cysts.

Jeffery et al. (1993) reported a Sicilian family with ADPKD that was not linked to 16p13.3. They stated that the clinical phenotype in this family was consistent with that of other unlinked families, all of which had been reported from outside the United Kingdom. Those families showed milder progression than families linked to 16p13.3.

In a European multicenter survey, Hateboer et al. (1999) compared clinical data from 291 individuals with PKD2 in 31 families, 333 individuals with PKD1 in 31 families, and 398 geographically matched controls. The median age at death or onset of ESRD was 69.1 years in PKD2, 53.0 years in PKD1, and 78.0 years in controls. The median age at presentation with kidney failure was 74.0 years in PKD2 and 54.3 years in PKD1.

Pei et al. (2001) studied a large ADPKD family (NFL10) that had previously been excluded from linkage to both the PKD1 and PKD2 loci. Of 48 family members screened by ultrasonography and genotyped at both loci, 28 had ADPKD. Haplotype inspection suggested bilineal disease, with PKD1 and PKD2 mutations segregating independently.

Bergmann et al. (2008) reported perinatal deaths in a family with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease and a PKD2 mutation.

Bataille et al. (2011) reported 3 unrelated patients with ADPKD who also had left-right laterality defects. One had dextrocardia and 2 had situs inversus totalis. No left-right anomalies were found in other members of the 3 families.

### Other Features

Facial anomalies had not previously been identified in ADPKD patients. Khonsari et al. (2013) analyzed 3-dimensional photographs of ADPKD patients' faces using dense surface modeling. The analysis showed specific facial characteristics in the patients, some of which correlated with features of Pkd2 conditional mutant mice.

### Inheritance

In the family reported by Bachner et al. (1990), transmission of unilateral renal cysts appeared to be autosomal dominant, although some generations were skipped. The authors concluded that the family had a form of autosomal dominant PKD with reduced penetrance. Mochizuki et al. (1996) found PKD2 mutations that segregated with the disease in 3 PKD2-linked families. Bataille et al. (2011) found that in each of 3 families, the PKD2 mutation was present in all tested relatives with ADPKD and absent in unaffected relatives.

### Mapping

Kumar et al. (1990) genotyped members of a large ADPKD family not linked to chromosome 16 for 12 marker loci on both arms of chromosome 1. Multipoint analysis excluded the disease locus from the long-arm region between D1S81 (pTHH33) and D1S67 (pHHH106) and from the short-arm region between Rh and PGM1. Together these regions covered about 61% of chromosome 1, about 160 cM.

Nørby and Schwartz (1990) proposed a possible locus for polycystic kidney disease on chromosome 2.

In a large Sicilian ADPKD kindred unlinked to chromosome 16, Kumar et al. (1991) analyzed 29 informative marker loci on 11 autosomes. Linkage with the disease locus was significantly excluded for 17 marker loci, and there was no evidence of close linkage with the remaining loci.

Peters et al. (1993) assigned a second ADPKD gene to chromosome 4 using highly polymorphic microsatellite markers. In 8 families with clear evidence against linkage to chromosome 16 markers, linkage analysis with markers D4S231 and D4S423 gave a multipoint lod score of 22.42.

Kimberling et al. (1993) studied the large Sicilian kindred in which genetic heterogeneity of ADPKD was first discovered. They typed more than 100 microsatellite markers spread across all chromosomes in informative family members, whose affected or unaffected status was confirmed by renal ultrasonography. They localized a second ADPKD gene to chromosome 4q13-q23, in a segment of about 9 cM flanked by markers D4S231 and D4S414.

In one of the Icelandic ADPKD families that was unlinked to 16p13.3, Fossdal et al. (1993) excluded the disease locus from part of the long arm of chromosome 2. They supported the theory that more than 2 loci cause ADPKD. Their data also confirmed that the YNH24 locus (D2S44) maps to chromosome 2q13-q24.

Coto et al. (1995) studied 17 large Spanish ADPKD families using ultrasonography and microsatellite markers closely linked to PKD1 on 16p13.3. Five of the families showed negative linkage to 16p13.3, and in these 5 families significant linkage to the PKD2 locus on 4q was obtained.

San Millán et al. (1995) confirmed linkage of ADPKD to chromosome 4q microsatellite markers in 8 Spanish families whose disease was not linked to 16p13.3. Marker D4S423 gave a maximum lod score of 9.03 at a recombination fraction of 0.00. Multipoint linkage analysis and study of recombinant haplotypes placed the PKD2 locus between D4S1542 and D4S1563, a genetic interval of approximately 1 cM.

### Heterogeneity

Kimberling et al. (1988) described a 5-generation kindred, descended from Sicilian immigrants, with ADPKD that was not linked to the alpha-hemoglobin complex on chromosome 16p. They studied linkage in 163 family members, 71 of whom were affected or at risk. The recombination frequency between the alpha-globin complex and the PKD1 region exceeded 24%, and the authors concluded that a second gene for ADPKD exists.

Romeo et al. (1988) found no linkage between autosomal dominant adult-type polycystic kidney disease and the alpha-globin genes on chromosome 16p in an Italian family. They concluded that the disorder can be caused by mutations in a second gene.

Elles et al. (1990) typed 24 families with adult-onset polycystic kidney disease for markers flanking the PKD1 locus on chromosome 16. The combined results gave a significant lod score in favor of linkage to PKD1. Two families had unusual features: recombinations with the flanking markers, including double recombinations, and, in one family, an unexpectedly high proportion of affected people. The authors considered whether the disease in these families might result from mutation at a second locus, PKD2. They used a test comparing the numbers of apparently nonrecombinant and doubly recombinant meioses. They concluded that neither their 2 families nor the published data on other families provided compelling evidence for a second locus.

Bachner et al. (1990) found no linkage between the disease and several PKD1-locus markers on 16p in their large family. Depending on the penetrance model and on how individuals with unilateral cysts were classified, lod scores for the 3-prime HVR marker ranged from 1.31 to -21.47 at theta = 0. Smith's test of heterogeneity gave a conditional probability of nonlinkage between 0.9 and 1.0. The authors also considered other explanations: 2 distinct hereditary diseases in the family, or a new mutation in one branch combined with a high frequency of isolated renal cysts.

Kumar et al. (1990) studied a large ADPKD family that was not linked to chromosome 16. Kumar et al. (1991) studied a large Sicilian ADPKD kindred with more than 250 members that was unlinked to the PKD1 locus, which supported the existence of at least a second ADPKD locus.

Ravine et al. (1992) concluded that ADPKD not linked to PKD1 has a much milder phenotype than PKD1-linked disease. None of their non-PKD1 families had been ascertained through renal impairment clinics. Partly because of this milder severity, the authors suggested that the reported prevalence of the non-PKD1 genotype is probably an underestimate.

Bear et al. (1992) studied 17 Newfoundland ADPKD families. The disease showed linkage to PKD1-locus markers in 10 families and was not co-inherited with PKD1 markers in 2 families.

Jeffery et al. (1993) reported a family of Sicilian origin with ADPKD unlinked to chromosome 16 markers. The lod scores for 3-prime HVR and SM7, the markers flanking PKD1, were -1.4 and -2.33, respectively, with theta max of 0.5 for each. The authors suggested that ADPKD may be much more genetically heterogeneous in the population than currently thought. Their reasoning was that clinic populations represent the most severe forms of the disease and that the non-PKD1 phenotype is less aggressive.

Peters et al. (1993) noted that a gene on 16p accounts for roughly 86% of affected European families. They identified 8 families with clear evidence against linkage to chromosome 16 markers. Kimberling et al. (1993) noted that at least 2 ADPKD loci exist: one on 16p, and a second, less common locus that had not been mapped before their study.

Fossdal et al. (1993) used markers 3-prime HVR (D16S85), 16AC2.5 (D16S291), and SM7 (D16S283), which flank the PKD1 region, to study 7 Icelandic ADPKD families. The disease locus was in the PKD1 region in 4 families, and 3 families were unlinked to 16p13.3.

Coto et al. (1995) concluded that ADPKD in the Spanish population is genetically heterogeneous. However, mutations at only 2 loci, PKD1 and PKD2, appeared to cause the disease in most if not all families: of their 17 families, 12 were linked to 16p13.3 and 5 to 4q. San Millán et al. (1995) noted that a gene at 16p13.3 is responsible in approximately 86% of affected European families, with the 4q13-q23 locus a candidate in the remaining families. ⚠︎

In family NFL10, Pei et al. (2001) recoded the PKD2 mutation carriers as unknown and repeated linkage analysis with PKD1-locus markers. This gave significant lod scores (greater than 3.0), strongly supporting a PKD1 mutation in 15 other affected members who lacked the PKD2 mutation. This was the first documentation of bilineal disease in ADPKD. The authors cautioned that bilineal disease must be considered as a confounder in the search for a PKD3 locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochizuki et al. (1996) identified PKD2, a second gene for ADPKD, by positional cloning. They found nonsense mutations in PKD2 that segregated with the disease in 3 PKD2-linked families. The predicted PKD2 protein has 968 amino acids and 6 transmembrane spans, with intracellular N and C termini. It shares amino acid similarity with PKD1, with the C. elegans homolog of PKD1, and with the family of voltage-activated calcium (and sodium) channels. It also contains a potential calcium-binding domain.

Veldhuisen et al. (1997) screened the PKD2 gene by SSCP analysis in 35 families with the second type of ADPKD and identified 20 mutations. Most of the mutations appeared to be unique and occurred throughout the gene, with no evidence of clustering. Besides small deletions, insertions, and substitutions that led to premature translation stops, they found 1 amino acid substitution and 5 possible splice site mutations.

Viribay et al. (1997) screened all 15 exons of PKD2 in chromosome 4-linked ADPKD families by heteroduplex and SSCP analyses. They found 7 new mutations, a detection rate of about 90% in the population studied. Every mutation caused premature termination of translation: 4 were nonsense changes and 3 were frameshifting deletions, one removing 4 T nucleotides and two each removing a single G. All mutations were unique and were spread across the gene without clustering.

Pei et al. (1998) screened 11 Canadian ADPKD families for PKD2 mutations. Four of the families had documented linkage to PKD2. The other 7 were smaller families in which 1 or more affected members developed ESRD at age 70 years or older. SSCP analysis of all 15 PKD2 exons in 1 affected member per family found mutations in 8 of the 11 families (about 73%). The detection rate was the same in the PKD2-linked families and the late-onset ESRD families. In 3 unrelated families, an adenosine was inserted into or deleted from a polyadenosine tract ((A)8, nucleotides 2152-2159) in exon 11. The authors suggested that this mononucleotide repeat tract is prone to mutation through slipped-strand mispairing.

In the NFL10 ADPKD family, Pei et al. (2001) used SSCP analysis to identify a PKD2 mutation, 2152delA (L736X), in 12 affected pedigree members.

Deltas (2001) reviewed the reported PKD2 mutations. Mutations of all kinds were spread over the whole PKD2 sequence with no apparent significant clustering. Most families had private mutations, but a few recurrent mutations had been reported in unrelated families.

Bataille et al. (2011) screened PKD1 and PKD2 in 3 unrelated probands with ADPKD and laterality defects. Each proband had a pathogenic PKD2 mutation: a large gene deletion, a single-exon duplication, and an in-frame duplication, respectively. In each family, the mutation was present in all tested relatives with ADPKD and absent in unaffected relatives. The authors suggested a possible association between PKD2 mutations and heterotaxy. They based this on laterality defects in Pkd2-null mouse and zebrafish models, and on finding PKD2 mutations in all 3 probands even though PKD2 mutations account for only 15% of ADPKD families.

Losekoot et al. (2012) reported a patient with neonatal-onset polycystic kidney disease who was homozygous for an incompletely penetrant PKD2 missense variant. The homozygosity resulted from uniparental disomy.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Coto et al. (1995) found that renal cysts developed at an earlier age in PKD1 mutation carriers and that end-stage renal failure occurred at an older age in individuals with PKD2. They concluded that ADPKD2 has a better prognosis than PKD1-linked disease.

Viribay et al. (1997) found no clear correlation between the specific PKD2 mutations and the clinical profiles of the ADPKD2 families.

Pei et al. (1998) reported that all of the mutations in their families lay between exons 1 and 11. All were predicted to produce a truncated polycystin-2 lacking the calcium-binding EF-hand domain and both cytoplasmic domains needed for interaction with polycystin-1 and with itself. They found no correlation between mutation location and disease severity and suggested that most PKD2 mutations are inactivating.

Hateboer et al. (1999) found that PKD2 patients were less likely than PKD1 patients to have hypertension (odds ratio 0.25), a history of urinary tract infection (odds ratio 0.50), or hematuria (odds ratio 0.59). Women with PKD2 had a significantly longer median survival than men (71.0 vs 67.3 years), whereas no sex influence was apparent in PKD1. The authors concluded that, although PKD2 is clinically milder than PKD1, it harms overall life expectancy and cannot be regarded as a benign disorder.

In family NFL10, Pei et al. (2001) identified 2 affected individuals who were trans-heterozygous for the PKD1 mutation and the PKD2 mutation. They had more severe renal disease than individuals with either mutation alone. The authors concluded that trans-heterozygous PKD1/PKD2 mutations in humans are not necessarily embryonic lethal.

Deltas (2001) reported some evidence of genotype/phenotype correlation among PKD2 mutations.

### Pathogenesis

Veldhuisen et al. (1997) suggested that the first step toward cyst formation in PKD2 patients is the loss of one functional copy of polycystin-2.

Wu et al. (1998) found that, in all cases, renal cysts in Pkd2 mutant mice arose from renal tubular cells that had lost the ability to produce Pkd2 protein. They concluded that somatic loss of Pkd2 expression is both necessary and sufficient for renal cyst formation in ADPKD, which suggests that PKD2-related disease occurs by a cellular recessive mechanism.

Torra et al. (1999) studied 30 renal cysts from a patient with PKD2 whose germline mutation was a deletion covering most of the PKD2 gene. Loss of the wildtype allele (LOH) was found in 10% of the cysts. SSCP screening of 6 exons found 8 different somatic mutations, all expected to produce truncated proteins. Overall, at least 37% of the cysts had somatic mutations. No LOH was found at the PKD1 gene or at locus D3S1478, which showed that the somatic changes were specific to PKD2. The authors suggested that second-hit inactivation of the normal PKD2 allele could be a crucial event in cyst formation in ADPKD type 2.

Koptides et al. (1999) examined 21 renal cysts from both kidneys of a patient with ADPKD2 and found several novel somatic PKD2 mutations in the cyst epithelial cells. Seven of the 21 cysts (33%) carried the same C insertion in the inherited wildtype allele. In 2 other cysts, a nonsense mutation and a splice site AG deletion were found in a PKD2 allele that could not be identified as either the inherited wildtype allele or the mutant allele. The authors suggested that ADPKD2, although dominantly inherited, arises through a cellular recessive mechanism, which supports a 2-hit model of cyst formation.

Based on their mouse models, Wu et al. (2000) concluded that the kidney and liver cysts associated with Pkd2 deficiency form by a 2-hit mechanism, in which a second somatic event inactivates the remaining Pkd2 allele. ⚠︎

Koptides et al. (2000) referred to their own earlier study of a human ADPKD2 kidney, in which 9 of 23 cysts carried somatic mutations that inactivated the inherited normal PKD2 allele. They took this as support for a 2-hit loss-of-function model of cyst formation in ADPKD2. In a patient with ADPKD1 and a germline PKD1 mutation, they analyzed DNA from individual renal cysts. Some cysts had somatic PKD1 mutations and others had somatic PKD2 mutations, so the latter were trans-heterozygous for mutations in both genes. The authors considered this the first direct genetic evidence that polycystins 1 and 2 interact, perhaps as part of a larger complex.

Watnick et al. (2000) found somatic PKD2 mutations in 71% of the renal cysts they analyzed from patients with ADPKD2. In a subset of cysts without PKD2 mutations, they found clonal somatic PKD1 mutations. Because the PKD1 and PKD2 products form heteromeric complexes, the authors suggested that trans-heterozygous mutations (a germline PKD2 mutation plus a somatic PKD1 mutation) may cause cysts. ⚠︎

Deltas (2001) suggested that renal cysts most probably arise after a second somatic event. This event would inactivate the inherited normal allele of the same gene, or possibly one allele of the other gene (PKD1 or PKD2), producing a trans-heterozygous state. Polycystin-1 and polycystin-2 were hypothesized to interact with each other and to take part in a common signaling pathway.

### Diagnosis

Kimberling et al. (1988) cautioned that, because of locus heterogeneity, prenatal and presymptomatic diagnosis of ADPKD by linkage should be approached with caution until a way is found to tell the 2 forms apart. Romeo et al. (1988) pointed out that, because there are 2 polycystic kidney disease genes, linkage must be shown separately in each family before predictive testing with DNA probes can be relied on.

For genetic counseling in families too small to give their own evidence for or against linkage, Elles et al. (1990) suggested handling the extra uncertainty by using a higher recombination rate.

Bear et al. (1992) estimated that, in PKD1-linked families, ultrasonography gave false-negative diagnoses in 36% of members younger than 10 years and in 8% or less of older members. A false-negative ultrasonographic diagnosis of PKD1 disease was unlikely after age 30 years. The risk of a false-negative result appeared largely restricted to families in which ESRD occurred relatively late.

Kimberling et al. (1993) stated that using flanking markers for both PKD1 and the 4q locus would make subtype diagnosis and linkage-based presymptomatic diagnosis more accurate, by reducing errors caused by genetic heterogeneity.

### Animal Model

Wu et al. (1998) introduced a mutant exon 1 in tandem with the wildtype exon 1 at the mouse Pkd2 locus. This created an unstable allele that undergoes somatic inactivation by intragenic homologous recombination to produce a true null allele. Mice heterozygous or homozygous for this allele, as well as Pkd2 +/- mice, developed polycystic kidney and liver lesions that the authors described as indistinguishable from the human phenotype.

Wu et al. (2000) produced 2 mouse Pkd2 mutations: an unstable allele (WS25) that can undergo somatic rearrangement by homologous recombination to form a null allele, and a true null allele (WS183). Pkd2 -/- mice died in utero between embryonic day 13.5 and birth, with structural defects in cardiac septation and cysts in maturing nephrons and pancreatic ducts. Adult Pkd2 WS25/- mice developed kidney cysts, pancreatic ductal cysts, renal failure, and early death, with a median survival of 65 weeks compared with 94 weeks in controls. Adult Pkd2 +/- mice had no cystic disease or renal failure, but their survival fell between that of the other 2 groups. The authors took this as a first indication that Pkd2 haploinsufficiency harms long-term survival.

Pennekamp et al. (2002) found that mouse Pkd2 was ubiquitously expressed at headfold and early somite stages, with higher levels in the floorplate and notochord. They generated a Pkd2 knockout allele. In addition to previously described malformations, homozygous mutant embryos showed right pulmonary isomerism and randomization of embryonic turning, heart looping, and abdominal situs. In these embryos, Leftb and nodal were not expressed in the left lateral plate mesoderm (LPM), Ebaf was absent from the floorplate, and Pitx2 was expressed bilaterally in posterior LPM but was absent anteriorly. The embryonic midline was present, with normal Foxa2 and shh expression. The authors suggested that polycystin-2 acts downstream of or in parallel to shh and upstream of the nodal cascade.

Khonsari et al. (2013) found Pkd2 expression in craniofacial tissues. They used Wnt1Cre to delete Pkd2 conditionally in neural crest-derived cells in mice. The mutant mice had many signs of mechanical trauma, including fractured molar roots, distorted incisors, alveolar bone loss, and compressed temporomandibular joints, as well as abnormal skull shapes. None of these phenotypes was present at embryonic stages, when the head is under no significant mechanical stress in utero. The authors suggested that Pkd2 likely plays a critical role as a mechanoreceptor in craniofacial growth.

PKD2, 1-BP INS/DEL, (A)8 TRACT, NT2152-2159

Pei et al. (1998) studied Canadian ADPKD families. In 3 unrelated families, they found insertion or deletion of an adenosine in a polyadenosine tract ((A)8, nucleotides 2152-2159) in exon 11 of the PKD2 gene. They suggested that this mononucleotide repeat tract is prone to mutation through slipped-strand mispairing.

**.0002 POLYCYSTIC KIDNEY DISEASE 2 WITH OR WITHOUT POLYCYSTIC LIVER DISEASE**  
PKD2, 1-BP DEL, 2152A [c.2152dela; p.L736*]

In the large ADPKD family NFL10, which had bilineal disease, Pei et al. (2001) used SSCP analysis to identify a 1-bp deletion in the PKD2 gene (2152delA), resulting in a leu736-to-ter (L736X) substitution, in 12 affected pedigree members. Two affected individuals were trans-heterozygous for this PKD2 mutation and a PKD1 mutation that segregated in the same family. These 2 individuals had more severe renal disease than family members with either mutation alone.
