### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital prothrombin deficiency, including hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding prothrombin (F2) on chromosome 11.

### Description

Congenital prothrombin (factor II) deficiency is among the rarest inherited coagulation disorders (Lancellotti and De Cristofaro, 2009). Two main phenotypes are distinguished: hypoprothrombinemia (type I deficiency), with concomitantly low levels of prothrombin activity and antigen, and dysprothrombinemia (type II deficiency), with normal or near-normal synthesis of a dysfunctional protein; compound heterozygosity for hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia has also been described (Lancellotti and De Cristofaro, 2009). Low prothrombin activity typically prolongs both the activated partial thromboplastin time and the prothrombin time, and clinical manifestations are predominantly mucosal or surgical- or trauma-associated bleeding, although joint bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage have been reported (Meeks and Abshire, 2008).

### Nomenclature

Dysfunctional prothrombin variants have been designated by the place of origin of the affected family, for example prothrombin Barcelona, described in 4 sibs by Josso et al. (1971), prothrombin Padua (Girolami et al., 1974), prothrombin Quick (Owen et al., 1978), prothrombin Houston (Weinger et al., 1980), prothrombin Gainesville (Smith et al., 1981), prothrombin Metz (Josso et al., 1982), prothrombin Habana (Rubio et al., 1983), prothrombin Segovia (Rocha et al., 1986), prothrombin Poissy (Dumont et al., 1987), prothrombin Tokushima (Inomoto et al., 1987), prothrombin 'Mexico City' (Valls-de-Ruiz et al., 1987), prothrombin Magdeburg (Lutze et al., 1989), prothrombin Himi (Morishita et al., 1992), and prothrombin Denver (Lefkowitz et al., 2000). Owen et al. (1978) proposed that the dysprothrombin of one of the first patients identified by Quick be designated prothrombin Quick. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Shapiro et al. (1969) studied a large family in which 11 members had half-normal plasma concentrations of biologic prothrombin activity but normal quantities of immunoreactive prothrombin. They suggested that affected individuals were heterozygotes in whom half of the prothrombin molecules synthesized were structurally abnormal and incapable of releasing the active enzyme, thrombin.

Owen et al. (1978) restudied one of the first patients identified by Quick and colleagues as having a defect in plasma prothrombin and found that she had about half the normal amount of prothrombin antigen but virtually no prothrombin function (prothrombin Quick). An additional patient first described by Quick was found to be truly hypoprothrombinemic, lacking both functional and antigenic prothrombin. Owen et al. (1978) noted that only 10 families with hypoprothrombinemia and 6 families with dysprothrombinemia had been observed, and that an as yet undefined prothrombin abnormality had been recognized in 8 other families.

Weinger et al. (1980) described a 72-year-old man with a lifelong history of easy bruisability and posttraumatic bleeding who had a prolonged prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time. A dysprothrombin (prothrombin Houston) was confirmed by the discrepancy between plasma prothrombin coagulant activity and prothrombin antigen levels.

Smith et al. (1981) evaluated a pair of identical twins for menometrorrhagia and mild postoperative bleeding. Each had a slightly prolonged partial thromboplastin time and prothrombin time; plasma prothrombin activity was 23% and 25% of normal with a Russell viper venom assay, and each twin had 70% of normal plasma prothrombin antigen. They stated that prothrombin Gainesville was the twelfth dysprothrombin reported.

Josso et al. (1982) investigated a mild hemorrhagic tendency in a French family in which the father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia and the mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia. Three of the children were double heterozygotes, which allowed discovery of an abnormal prothrombin, prothrombin Metz, that generated an abnormal thrombin less sensitive than normal to inactivation by antithrombin III.

Rubio et al. (1983) described a Cuban family in which the propositus, a 5-year-old girl, presented with umbilical bleeding after birth followed by easy bruising and a bleeding tendency throughout her life. Prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time were prolonged, and prothrombin activity was less than 10% in several 1- and 2-stage systems, whereas staphylocoagulase-complexed prothrombin and immunologic methods yielded levels of about 50%. The father had approximately 50% prothrombin activity and antigen, whereas the mother had 45% prothrombin activity but about 100% prothrombin antigen. Rubio et al. (1983) suggested that the propositus was heterozygous for an abnormal prothrombin (prothrombin Habana) and heterozygous for true prothrombin deficiency.

Rocha et al. (1986) reported a 21-year-old man who presented simultaneously with hemarthrosis of the left knee and an extensive hematoma following minor trauma. Prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. Prothrombin activity was very low with physiologic activators (7% by a 1-stage method and 20% by a 2-stage method) and with several snake venoms, but was borderline to normal with Taipan viper venom (64%) and normal by staphylocoagulase and immunologic methods. The parents were consanguineous, and both had a 50% reduced prothrombin level with physiologic activators or Echis carinatus venom. The variant was named prothrombin Segovia. ⚠︎

Dumont et al. (1987) described a newborn girl who presented with severe bleeding from the second day of life. The prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were very prolonged, with about 2% prothrombin activity in a 1-stage assay; prothrombin antigen was 47%. Both normal and abnormal prothrombin were present in the plasma of the father, mother, and brother. The propositus was thought to be homozygous for a 'lazy' dysprothrombin, prothrombin Poissy, which was slowly and incompletely activated. ⚠︎

Inomoto et al. (1987) purified a mutant prothrombin, prothrombin Tokushima, from the plasma of a proband with 12% of normal plasma clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) found a functionally normal but structurally abnormal prothrombin variant, prothrombin 'Mexico City,' in a Mexican family; they described it as an asymptomatic autosomal dominant variant. The variant had a more acidic isoelectric point (4.01) than normal prothrombin (4.29) and showed abnormal cleavage by factor Xa and Echis carinatus venom, although both yielded functionally normal thrombin; the authors suggested a mutation at the fragment 2 level.

Lutze et al. (1989) found that 7 members of 1 family over 3 generations had a reduction to about 0.5 of the ratio between factor II clotting activity and factor II concentration (prothrombin Magdeburg). Five of the 7 had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, manifest especially as more marked or prolonged posttraumatic and postoperative bleeding. The authors concluded that this was an autosomal hereditary dysprothrombinemia in which the affected persons were heterozygotes.

Poort et al. (1994) studied a patient with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%). In the family, only the 5 homozygous sibs of the propositus were clinically affected, with severe hemorrhages including epistaxis and soft tissue, muscle, and joint bleeding in all, and severe hemorrhages in the 2 women.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) restudied the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement. Factor II activity was 5 units/dl and factor II antigen 21 units/dl.

### Biochemical Features

Shapiro et al. (1969) isolated prothrombin from the plasma of an affected family member and obtained normal yields of protein but half-normal amounts of prothrombin activity. Activation of this material gave half or less of the expected thrombin clotting and esterase activities. Immunoelectrophoresis of serum revealed a normal nonthrombin 'pro piece' and an additional activation product with electrophoretic mobility intermediate between that of prothrombin and of the 'pro piece.'

In the patient with prothrombin Houston, Weinger et al. (1980) found by crossed immunoelectrofocusing that the prothrombin antigen was more basic than normal. Barium sulfate eluate of patient plasma, when reacted with Echis carinatus venom, clotted purified fibrinogen at a rate slower than normal, although the prothrombin was cleaved, suggesting that the coagulopathy resulted from generation of a dysfunctional thrombin.

Board et al. (1982) described an electrophoretic procedure that revealed 3 allelic variants at the prothrombin structural locus (FII); in each case the structural variation was located on a profragment and not on the enzymatically active thrombin molecule.

Rabiet et al. (1984) found that thrombin Metz, derived from prothrombin Metz, was physicochemically identical to normal thrombin but had less than 4% of fibrinogen clotting activity and was less than 5% as effective as normal thrombin in inducing platelet aggregation. Its interaction with antithrombin III was slower than normal, an abnormality not observed in the presence of heparin. The authors predicted that the defect affected the catalytic site or its vicinity and the region of interaction with antithrombin III and heparin.

Inomoto et al. (1987) found that thrombin Tokushima had the same molecular weight as normal thrombin but less than 22% of normal clotting activity, a kcat/Km less than one-tenth of normal with a synthetic substrate, and was 2.5% as effective as normal thrombin in inducing platelet aggregation; its interaction with antithrombin III was much slower than normal. They concluded that the molecular defect resided in the thrombin portion of prothrombin Tokushima.

Morishita et al. (1992) found that prothrombin Himi showed reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but retained full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates.

### Inheritance

Poort et al. (1994) found that the bleeding tendency in a family with hypoprothrombinemia was inherited as an autosomal recessive trait: both parents of the homozygous propositus were heterozygous for the mutation, and only the homozygous sibs were clinically affected. Akhavan et al. (2000) and Meeks and Abshire (2008) described prothrombin deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder.

In families with dysprothrombinemia, Shapiro et al. (1969) and Lutze et al. (1989) described autosomal transmission with heterozygous family members showing reduced prothrombin activity; in the family studied by Lutze et al. (1989), 5 of 7 heterozygotes had a slightly increased bleeding tendency. Girolami et al. (1998) stated that heterozygotes are usually asymptomatic. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Girolami et al. (1998) and Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) stated that the prothrombin gene is located on chromosome 11.

### Molecular Genetics

In the proband with prothrombin Tokushima, Iwahana et al. (1992) used PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP analyses and identified a C-to-T substitution at position 9490 of the prothrombin gene, resulting in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution, which caused loss of an MspI site. The mutation was inherited from the mother of the proband. They considered this the molecular basis of prothrombin Tokushima because there were no distinct changes on Southern blot analysis, the mutation was consistent with the molecular weight of the abnormal gene product, and no other amino acid abnormalities were found in proteolytic peptide fragments. Iwahana et al. (1992) stated that this was the first report of gene analysis of dysprothrombinemia.

Iwahana et al. (1992) analyzed a compound heterozygote for dysprothrombinemia of prothrombin Tokushima and hypoprothrombinemia. In addition to the single-nucleotide substitution responsible for prothrombin Tokushima, they identified by PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and sequencing a single-base insertion of T at position 4177 in exon 6, which caused a frameshift altering the amino acid sequence from codon 114 and creating a premature termination codon at codon 174 in exon 7. Because exon 7 encodes the kringle-2 domain preceding the thrombin sequence, the frameshift was predicted to lead to a null prothrombin phenotype. The hypoprothrombinemia allele was shown to be inherited from the father.

In the proband with prothrombin Himi, Morishita et al. (1992) performed PCR amplification of exons 8 through 14, which encode the thrombin moiety, followed by SSCP analysis and sequencing, and identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in exon 10: a T-to-C transition at position 8751, resulting in a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I), inherited from the father, and a G-to-A transition at position 8904, resulting in an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II), inherited from the mother. Both variant alleles were also inherited by 1 of the proband's sisters.

In a patient with severe hypoprothrombinemia, Poort et al. (1994) identified by direct sequencing of PCR fragments a homozygous A-to-G substitution in exon 3 of the prothrombin gene, predicting a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C) substitution (TAC-to-TGC). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation, and family studies showed complete cosegregation of the mutation with prothrombin deficiency.

In the original patient with prothrombin Denver, Lefkowitz et al. (2000) sequenced the thrombin portion of the prothrombin gene (exons VIII-XIV) and exons I-II and IV-VII and found compound heterozygosity for 2 different glu-to-lys mutations, at amino acid positions 300 (E300K) and 309 (E309K). Genomic DNA from the mother and brother was also obtained. Assays of plasma from the proband suggested that the functional defect was in activation of the zymogen to the enzyme.

Akhavan et al. (2000) screened 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency for alterations in the prothrombin gene and identified 9 novel candidate mutations. Of 11 patients with hypoprothrombinemia, 10 were homozygous for 5 mutations and 1 was a compound heterozygote; the 2 patients with dysprothrombinemia were homozygous for 2 mutations. Eight of the mutations were missense, causing substitutions in the propeptide (Arg-1Gln, Arg-2Trp), the kringle-1 domain (given as Asp118Try), the kringle-2 domain (Arg220Cys), and the catalytic serine protease domain (Gly330Ser, Ser354Arg, Arg382His, and Arg538Cys); the ninth was an in-frame 3-bp deletion resulting in omission of 1 amino acid (del Lys301/302).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that in true prothrombin deficiency, activity levels less than 10% of normal are usually found in homozygotes and between 40 and 60% in heterozygotes. Factor II levels in dysprothrombinemias are more variable, usually between 1 and 50% of normal, since homozygotes, heterozygotes, and compound heterozygotes may be encountered; antigen levels are normal, near normal, or slightly decreased but always higher than the clotting activity. Bleeding manifestations may be severe in homozygous true deficiency and more variable in dysprothrombinemias, and prognosis is generally in agreement with the prothrombin activity level.

### Pathogenesis

Using crystal structures of alpha-thrombin and prothrombin fragments 1 and 2, Akhavan et al. (2000) inferred that hypoprothrombinemia mutations affect either cleavage of the propeptide from the Gla domain, stability of the kringle-1 and -2 domains, or close association of the A and B chains of the serine protease domain, whereas dysprothrombinemia mutations directly affect catalytic function through their location at the active site crevice or exosite 1 within the serine protease domain.

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) noted that about 40 different mutations had been identified in prothrombin deficiency, many surrounding the catalytic site and another 'hot spot' in the anion binding exosite I (fibrinogen recognition site), with mutations also in the Na(+)-binding loop and the light A chain of thrombin. Most hypoprothrombinemia-associated mutations are missense, but nonsense mutations and 1 single-nucleotide deletion have also been found. No living patient with undetectable plasma prothrombin had been reported.

### Diagnosis

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that the diagnosis of prothrombin deficiency or abnormality can be made using a combination of clotting, chromogenic, and immunologic assays. In true deficiency, a parallel decrease in all assays is observed regardless of the activating agent, whereas discrepancies among clotting assays, particularly with viper venoms, should raise suspicion of dysprothrombinemia. Iwahana et al. (1992) concluded that PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP were useful for detecting the abnormal gene and for directly diagnosing carrier status for dysprothrombinemia.

### Clinical Management

In the identical twins with prothrombin Gainesville studied before and during hysterectomy, Smith et al. (1981) found that infused prothrombin (Konyne) had a volume of distribution of approximately 1.5 times the plasma volume and a half-life of 34 hours.

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that substitution therapy is based on administration of prothrombin complex concentrates or plasma, and that the long half-life of injected prothrombin, about 70 hours, allows hemostatically effective levels (about 50% of normal) to be achieved without difficulty. Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that the hemostatic level of prothrombin is thought to be between 20 and 40%, with a half-life of approximately 3 days; no purified prothrombin products were available, and fresh frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrates may be used for treatment.

### Population Genetics

Meeks and Abshire (2008) estimated that prothrombin deficiency occurs in approximately 1 in 1 to 2 million people, and Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) gave a prevalence of approximately 1:2,000,000.

Board et al. (1982) found that one electrophoretic prothrombin variant allele (FII4) reached polymorphic frequencies in some Pacific Island populations.

### History

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that prothrombin deficiency was first described in 1947 by Quick et al., and that the first prothrombin abnormality was reported in 1969 by Shapiro et al.
