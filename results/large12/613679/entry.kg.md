### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital prothrombin deficiency, including hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding prothrombin (F2; 176930) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

Congenital prothrombin (factor II) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive coagulation disorder with a moderately severe bleeding tendency (Akhavan et al., 2000; Meeks and Abshire, 2008). Prothrombin is activated to thrombin, which cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin and contributes to a stable fibrin clot (Meeks and Abshire, 2008).

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) described 2 main phenotypes. Hypoprothrombinemia (type I deficiency) has low levels of both prothrombin activity and antigen. Dysprothrombinemia (type II deficiency) has normal or near-normal synthesis of a dysfunctional protein. Hypoprothrombinemia together with dysprothrombinemia had also been described in compound heterozygotes. Lefkowitz et al. (2000) noted that dysprothrombinemia is a rare congenital cause of bleeding, and that fewer than 25 families with a functional prothrombin defect had been reported.

### Clinical Features

Van Creveld (1954) reported on congenital idiopathic hypoprothrombinemia. Quick et al. (1955) reported on congenital hypoprothrombinemic states.

Pool et al. (1962) reported severe congenital hypoprothrombinemia in a Black boy. Josso et al. (1962) studied a case of congenital hypoprothrombinemia. Quick and Hussey (1962) reported on hereditary hypoprothrombinemias.

Shapiro et al. (1969) studied a large family in which 11 members had half-normal plasma levels of biologic prothrombin activity but normal quantities of immunoreactive prothrombin, whose immunologic properties appeared identical to those of the normal zymogen.

Kattlove et al. (1970) reported on hereditary prothrombin deficiency.

Josso et al. (1971) described a congenital abnormality of the prothrombin molecule, which they called prothrombin Barcelona, in 4 sibs.

Girolami et al. (1974) described prothrombin Padua, which they called a 'new' congenital dysprothrombinemia.

Owen et al. (1978) restudied one of the first patients identified by Quick and colleagues as having a plasma prothrombin defect. She had about half the normal amount of prothrombin antigen but almost no prothrombin function, and the authors proposed calling this dysprothrombin 'prothrombin Quick.' A second patient first described by Quick had true hypoprothrombinemia, lacking both functional and antigenic prothrombin.

Weinger et al. (1980) described a 72-year-old man with a lifelong history of easy bruisability and posttraumatic bleeding who had a prolonged prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time. His plasma Stypven, Taipan, and Echis carinatus venom clotting times were also prolonged. The variant was named prothrombin Houston.

Smith et al. (1981) described a pair of identical twins with dysprothrombinemia (prothrombin Gainesville) who were evaluated for menometrorrhagia and mild postoperative bleeding. Each twin had a slightly prolonged partial thromboplastin time and prothrombin time; apart from this, the full hemostatic evaluation was normal.

Josso et al. (1982) studied a French family with a mild hemorrhagic tendency. The father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia and the mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia. All possible genetic combinations were seen among the children, and 3 of them were double heterozygotes.

Rubio et al. (1983) described a Cuban family with congenital dysprothrombinemia (prothrombin Habana). The proband, a 5-year-old girl, had umbilical bleeding after birth, then easy bruising and a bleeding tendency throughout her life. Her prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time were prolonged.

Shirakami and Kawauchi (1984) reported on congenital dysprothrombinemia.

Rocha et al. (1986) described a 21-year-old man, born of consanguineous parents, with congenital dysprothrombinemia who presented with hemarthrosis of the left knee together with an extensive hematoma after minor trauma. His prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged.

Dumont et al. (1987) described a newborn girl with congenital dysprothrombinemia (prothrombin Poissy) who had severe bleeding from the second day of life. Prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were very prolonged.

Lutze et al. (1989) described dysprothrombinemia (prothrombin Magdeburg) in 7 members of 1 family spanning 3 generations. Five of the 7 had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, seen mainly as heavier or prolonged bleeding after trauma and surgery.

Poort et al. (1994) described a male patient with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%). Five homozygous brothers and sisters of the propositus were also clinically affected, with severe hemorrhages including epistaxis and soft tissue, muscle, and joint bleeding in all, and severe hemorrhages in the 2 women.

Girolami et al. (1998) reported that bleeding can be severe in homozygous true prothrombin deficiency, with hemarthroses and intracranial bleeding described. Bleeding is more variable in dysprothrombinemias, and heterozygotes are usually asymptomatic. Prognosis is variable and generally matches the prothrombin activity level.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) described the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder and was treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement. Factor II activity was 5 units/dl and factor II antigen was 21 units/dl, consistent with moderately severe dysprothrombinemia.

Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that bleeding in prothrombin deficiency is mostly mucosal or follows surgery or trauma, but joint bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage had also been reported.

### Biochemical Features

Shapiro et al. (1969) isolated prothrombin from the plasma of an affected individual and obtained normal protein yields but half-normal prothrombin activity; activation via intrinsic and extrinsic systems, concentrated sodium citrate, or trypsin yielded half or less of the expected thrombin clotting and esterase activities. During clotting all prothrombin-mobility material disappeared, and serum immunoelectrophoresis showed a normal 'pro piece' plus an additional activation product of intermediate mobility. ⚠︎

Rabiet et al. (1979) studied the activation of prothrombin Barcelona and reported evidence that active high molecular weight intermediates form during its activation.

Weinger et al. (1980) confirmed a dysprothrombin in their patient (prothrombin Houston) by a discrepancy between plasma prothrombin coagulant activity and prothrombin antigen levels. The prothrombin was fully adsorbed to and eluted from barium sulfate, and on crossed immunoelectrophoresis it resembled normal prothrombin, but crossed immunoelectrofocusing showed that the patient's prothrombin antigen was more basic than normal.

Smith et al. (1981) found that plasma prothrombin activity in the identical twins with prothrombin Gainesville was 23% and 25% of normal by a Russell's viper venom assay, whereas each twin had 70% of normal plasma prothrombin antigen, indicating a dysprothrombinemia. The authors stated that prothrombin Gainesville was the twelfth such dysprothrombin reported. ⚠︎

Josso et al. (1982) found that prothrombin Metz generated an abnormal thrombin that was less sensitive to inactivation by antithrombin III than normal thrombin. Rabiet et al. (1984) purified thrombin Metz, made by activating prothrombin Metz with factor Xa in the presence of calcium, phospholipid, and factor Va. By physicochemical criteria it was identical to normal thrombin, but it had less than 4% of normal fibrinogen clotting activity, abnormal Km and Kcat on S2238, and only 34% of normal DAPA fluorescence enhancement, and it was less than 5% as effective as normal thrombin at inducing platelet aggregation. Thrombin Metz released both fibrinopeptides A and B more slowly than normal, with fibrinopeptide B more affected, leading to slow fibrin polymerization. Its interaction with antithrombin III was slower than normal, but not in the presence of heparin; it bound less tightly to heparin-Sepharose, and heparin inhibited its activity on S2238 less strongly. Rabiet et al. (1984) predicted that the defect affects the catalytic site or its vicinity and, jointly or consequently, the region that interacts with antithrombin III and heparin.

In the proband with prothrombin Habana, Rubio et al. (1983) found prothrombin activity below 10% in several one- and two-stage assays, whereas staphylocoagulase-complexed prothrombin and immunologic methods gave levels of about 50%. The abnormal prothrombin migrated more anodically on single and two-dimensional immunoelectrophoresis, and adding calcium did not change its migration.

Rocha et al. (1986) found that their propositus had very low prothrombin activity with physiologic activators (7% by one-stage and 20% by two-stage assay), Russell viper venom-cephalin (23%), Notechis scutatus scutatus venom (15%), and Echis carinatus venom (17%), whereas levels were borderline to normal with Taipan venom (64%) and normal by staphylocoagulase and immunologic methods, pointing to a dysfunctional prothrombin molecule. A line of identity between normal and abnormal prothrombin was observed on immunodiffusion. The abnormal prothrombin migrated less anodically, and adding calcium did not change its migration. On bidimensional immunoelectrophoresis the patient's serum showed 3 bands, compared with 2 in normal serum. The authors proposed the name prothrombin Segovia. ⚠︎

In the proband with prothrombin Poissy, Dumont et al. (1987) found prothrombin activity of about 2% in a one-stage assay, 35% with staphylocoagulase, and 25% with Echis carinatus venom. Prothrombin antigen was 47%, and the protein migrated abnormally on crossed immunoelectrophoresis. In a thrombin generation system free of natural inhibitors, the abnormal prothrombin was activated slowly and incompletely; the authors called it a 'lazy' dysprothrombin.

Inomoto et al. (1987) purified prothrombin Tokushima from the plasma of a proband with 12% of normal plasma prothrombin clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen. The purified variant had the same SDS-PAGE mobility as normal prothrombin, and its factor Xa-catalyzed proteolysis was identical to that of normal prothrombin. Thrombin Tokushima had normal molecular weight but less than 22% of normal clotting activity and a kcat/Km toward a fluorogenic substrate less than one-tenth of normal, although active-site titration showed no difference from normal thrombin. It was 2.5% as effective as normal thrombin in inducing platelet aggregation and 33% as effective in forming a complex with antithrombin III. The authors concluded that the molecular defect resides in the thrombin portion of prothrombin and that binding sites for various substrates appear greatly impaired.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) found a functionally normal but structurally abnormal prothrombin variant in a Mexican family and named it prothrombin 'Mexico City.' On immunoisoelectric focusing, the variant had a more acidic isoelectric point (4.01) than normal prothrombin (4.29); its molecular weight on SDS-PAGE was normal. Two-dimensional immunoelectrophoresis showed abnormal cleavage by factor Xa and by Echis carinatus venom, although both produced functionally normal thrombin. Calcium binding and overall antigenic structure were normal.

Lutze et al. (1989) found that in affected members of the prothrombin Magdeburg family, the ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration was reduced to about 0.5; this reduction was not seen when prothrombin was measured after activation with staphylocoagulase. SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis showed 'normal' prothrombin plus 2 abnormal prothrombins with higher molecular weights and lower isoelectric points.

Morishita et al. (1992) described prothrombin Himi, an inherited dysfunctional prothrombin with reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates.

Girolami et al. (1998) reported that factor II activity is usually less than 10% of normal in homozygotes for true deficiency and 40 to 60% in heterozygotes. In congenital dysprothrombinemia, factor II levels are more variable, usually 1 to 50% of normal, because patients may be homozygotes, heterozygotes, or compound heterozygotes; compound heterozygotes may carry an abnormality plus a true deficiency, or 2 distinct abnormalities. Antigen levels in dysprothrombinemia are normal, near normal, or slightly decreased, but always higher than clotting activity.

### Inheritance

Shapiro et al. (1969) found the pattern of inheritance of dysprothrombinemia in a large family to be autosomal, and suggested that the affected individuals, who had half-normal prothrombin activity, were heterozygotes. Lutze et al. (1989) likewise concluded that prothrombin Magdeburg is an autosomal hereditary dysprothrombinemia in which the affected persons, who had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, are heterozygotes. Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) described prothrombin Mexico City as an asymptomatic prothrombin variant with autosomal dominant inheritance. ⚠︎

Girolami (1971) studied the hereditary transmission of congenital 'true' hypoprothrombinemia.

In the family of the proband with prothrombin Habana, Rubio et al. (1983) found that the father had about 50% prothrombin activity and antigen (a true deficiency), whereas the mother had 45% prothrombin activity but about 100% prothrombin antigen (a dysfunctional prothrombin). They suggested that the proband was heterozygous for an abnormal prothrombin and heterozygous for true prothrombin deficiency. ⚠︎

Rocha et al. (1986) found that the consanguineous parents of their propositus both had prothrombin levels reduced by 50% when physiologic activators or Echis carinatus venom were used, consistent with both parents being heterozygous carriers. Dumont et al. (1987) found both normal and abnormal prothrombin in the plasma of the father, mother, and brother of the proband with prothrombin Poissy, and suggested that the proband was homozygous for the dysprothrombin. ⚠︎

Poort et al. (1994) concluded that the bleeding tendency in their family appeared to be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, since only homozygous family members were clinically affected and the parents were heterozygous.

### Heterogeneity

Owen et al. (1978) reviewed the reported inherited prothrombin defects: there were 10 families with hypoprothrombinemia (true reductions of prothrombin), 6 families with dysprothrombinemia (functionally abnormal prothrombin), and 8 more families with a prothrombin abnormality that had not yet been classified.

Board et al. (1982) described an electrophoretic procedure for localizing prothrombin that revealed 3 allelic variants at the prothrombin structural locus. In each case, investigation of the activated products showed that the structural variation was located on a profragment and not on the enzymatically active thrombin molecule.

### Molecular Genetics

Josso et al. (1982) identified an abnormal prothrombin, which they named prothrombin Metz, in 3 children of a French family who were double heterozygotes, each carrying hypoprothrombinemia inherited from the father and dysprothrombinemia inherited from the mother.

Iwahana et al. (1992) analyzed the F2 gene in a proband who was a compound heterozygote for dysprothrombinemia (prothrombin Tokushima) and hypoprothrombinemia. Using PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and mutated primer-mediated PCR-RFLP, they detected a single nucleotide substitution responsible for prothrombin Tokushima, along with 3 polymorphisms. The hypoprothrombinemia allele, inherited from the father, was a 1-bp insertion of thymine at position 4177 in exon 6, identified by PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and genomic sequencing; the frameshift was predicted to lead to a null prothrombin phenotype.

Using PCR-SSCP, PCR-RFLP, and sequencing of PCR-amplified genomic DNA, Iwahana et al. (1992) found that the prothrombin Tokushima allele carried a C-to-T substitution at nucleotide 9490, resulting in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution and abolishing an MspI site. The proband had inherited the mutant gene from the mother. The authors stated that this was the first report of gene analysis of dysprothrombinemia.

In a female proband with prothrombin Himi, Morishita et al. (1992) amplified exons 8 to 14 of the F2 gene by PCR, screened them by SSCP analysis, and by sequencing identified compound heterozygosity for 2 point mutations in exon 10: met337-to-thr (M337T), inherited from the father, and arg388-to-his (R388H), inherited from the mother. One of the proband's sisters also inherited both variant alleles.

In a patient with severe hypoprothrombinemia, Poort et al. (1994) identified by direct sequencing a homozygous tyr44-to-cys (Y44C) mutation in exon 3 of the F2 gene. Both parents were heterozygous, and family studies showed complete cosegregation of the mutation with prothrombin deficiency; only the homozygous sibs were clinically affected.

In the original patient with prothrombin Denver, Lefkowitz et al. (2000) sequenced PCR-amplified F2 exons (the entire thrombin portion, exons VIII-XIV, and exons I-II and IV-VII) and identified compound heterozygosity for 2 different glu-to-lys mutations, glu300-to-lys (E300K) and glu309-to-lys (E309K). DNA was also obtained from the patient's mother and brother.

Akhavan et al. (2000) screened the F2 gene in 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency and found 9 novel candidate mutations. Of the 11 patients with hypoprothrombinemia, 10 were homozygous for 5 mutations and 1 was a compound heterozygote; the 2 patients with dysprothrombinemia were homozygous for 2 mutations. Eight mutations were missense: arg-1-to-gln and arg-2-to-trp in the propeptide, a substitution at asp118 in kringle-1 (reported as Asp118Try), arg220-to-cys in kringle-2, and gly330-to-ser, ser354-to-arg, arg382-to-his, and arg538-to-cys in the serine protease domain. The ninth was an in-frame 3-bp deletion that removed 1 amino acid (del Lys301/302).

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) reported that 40 different mutations had been identified in prothrombin deficiency. Many clustered around the catalytic site, and another hot spot was in anion-binding exosite I (the fibrinogen recognition site); mutations had also been found in the Na(+)-binding loop and in the thrombin light A chain. Most hypoprothrombinemia-associated mutations were missense, but nonsense mutations and 1 single-nucleotide deletion had also been described.

### Pathogenesis

Shapiro et al. (1969) concluded that in affected heterozygotes half of the synthesized prothrombin molecules are structurally abnormal, undergoing some alterations during activation but being incapable of releasing active thrombin.

Weinger et al. (1980) found that the barium sulfate eluate from their patient, activated by Echis carinatus venom, clotted purified fibrinogen more slowly than normal eluate, although SDS gel electrophoresis showed that the prothrombin was cleaved by the venom. They suggested that the coagulopathy of prothrombin Houston results from the generation of a dysfunctional thrombin.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) suggested that prothrombin Mexico City results from a simple substitution or translocation mutation at the level of fragment 2, and considered the variant different from previously described prothrombin variants.

Plasma assays in the prothrombin Denver proband suggested to Lefkowitz et al. (2000) that the functional defect was in the activation of the zymogen to the enzyme.

Akhavan et al. (2000) mapped the missense mutations onto crystal structures of alpha-thrombin and prothrombin fragments 1 and 2. They inferred that the hypoprothrombinemia mutations affected cleavage of the propeptide from the Gla domain, the stability of kringle-1 and kringle-2, or the close association of the A and B chains of the serine protease domain, whereas the dysprothrombinemia mutations directly impaired catalytic function because they lie at the active site crevice or exosite 1 of the serine protease domain.

### Diagnosis

Iwahana et al. (1992) concluded that PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP (loss of an MspI site) were useful for detecting the abnormal prothrombin gene and for direct diagnosis of carrier status in dysprothrombinemia.

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that prothrombin deficiency or abnormality can be diagnosed with a combination of clotting, chromogenic, and immunologic assays. In true deficiency, all assays decrease in parallel whatever the activating agent, whereas discrepancies among clotting assays, particularly with viper venoms, suggest dysprothrombinemia.

Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that low prothrombin activity usually prolongs both the activated partial thromboplastin time and the prothrombin time. The hemostatic level of prothrombin is thought to be 20 to 40%, and its half-life is about 3 days.

### Clinical Management

Smith et al. (1981) obtained prothrombin kinetic data in twins with prothrombin Gainesville before and during hysterectomy. Infused prothrombin (Konyne) had a volume of distribution of about 1.5 times the plasma volume and a half-life of 34 hours.

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that substitution therapy uses prothrombin complex concentrates or plasma; because infused prothrombin has a long half-life of about 70 hours, hemostatically effective levels of about 50% of normal are easily achieved. Meeks and Abshire (2008) stated that no purified prothrombin products were available for replacement therapy, and that fresh frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrates both contain prothrombin and may be used for treatment.

### Population Genetics

Meeks and Abshire (2008) stated that prothrombin deficiency occurs in about 1 in 1 to 2 million people. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) stated that it is among the rarest inherited coagulation disorders, with a prevalence of about 1 in 2,000,000, and that no living patient with undetectable plasma prothrombin had been reported.

Board et al. (1982) found that one prothrombin electrophoretic variant allele, FII4, reached polymorphic frequencies in some Pacific Island populations.

### History

Girolami et al. (1998) noted that Quick et al. first described prothrombin deficiency in 1947, and that Shapiro et al. reported the first prothrombin abnormality in 1969.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PROTHROMBIN DEFICIENCY, CONGENITAL (HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA)**  
F2, 1-BP INS, 4177T [c.4177inst]

In a proband who was compound heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia (prothrombin Tokushima), Iwahana et al. (1992) identified, by PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and genomic sequencing, a 1-bp insertion of thymine at position 4177 in exon 6 of the F2 gene. The frameshift altered the amino acid sequence from codon 114 and created a premature TGA stop at codon 174 in exon 7; because exon 7 encodes the kringle 2 domain preceding the thrombin sequence, the authors concluded that this leads to a null prothrombin phenotype. The allele was inherited from the father. The other allele carried the prothrombin Tokushima mutation, a single nucleotide substitution.

**.0002 PROTHROMBIN DEFICIENCY, CONGENITAL (DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA; PROTHROMBIN TOKUSHIMA)**  
F2, ARG418TRP [c.9490C>T; p.R418W]

Inomoto et al. (1987) characterized prothrombin Tokushima, purified from a proband with 12% of normal prothrombin clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen; the derived thrombin had markedly impaired clotting, substrate, platelet-aggregating, and antithrombin III-binding activities.

Using PCR-SSCP, PCR-RFLP, and sequencing of PCR-amplified genomic DNA, Iwahana et al. (1992) found that prothrombin Tokushima resulted from a C-to-T substitution at nucleotide 9490 of the F2 gene, resulting in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution and abolishing an MspI site. The proband, who was compound heterozygous with a 1-bp insertion (4177insT) causing hypoprothrombinemia, had inherited the R418W mutation from the mother. The authors considered this point mutation the likely molecular basis because Southern blot analysis showed no distinct changes, the variant gene product had the same molecular weight as the abnormal protein, and the proteolytic peptide fragments showed no other amino acid abnormalities. ⚠︎

**.0003 PROTHROMBIN DEFICIENCY, CONGENITAL (DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA; PROTHROMBIN HIMI)**  
F2, MET337THR [c.8751T>C; p.M337T]

In a female proband with prothrombin Himi, a dysfunctional prothrombin with reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates, Morishita et al. (1992) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in exon 10 of the F2 gene, found by PCR-SSCP screening and sequencing. One was a T-to-C transition at nucleotide 8751, resulting in a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I), inherited from the father; the other was R388H, inherited from the mother. One of the proband's sisters also inherited both variant alleles.

**.0004 PROTHROMBIN DEFICIENCY, CONGENITAL (DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA; PROTHROMBIN HIMI)**  
F2, ARG388HIS [c.8904G>A; p.R388H]

In the proband with prothrombin Himi, Morishita et al. (1992) identified a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 8904 in exon 10 of the F2 gene, resulting in an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II), inherited from her mother. Together with M337T from her father, it made the proband compound heterozygous for 2 dysfunctional prothrombin molecules.

**.0005 PROTHROMBIN DEFICIENCY, CONGENITAL (HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA)**  
F2, TYR44CYS [p.Y44; p.Y44C]

In a male patient with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%), Poort et al. (1994) identified by direct sequencing a homozygous A-to-G substitution in exon 3 of the F2 gene, predicting a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C; TAC-to-TGC) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous, and the mutation cosegregated completely with prothrombin deficiency in the family; only the homozygous sibs, 5 of whom had severe hemorrhages, were clinically affected.

**.0006 PROTHROMBIN DEFICIENCY, CONGENITAL (DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA; PROTHROMBIN DENVER)**  
F2, GLU300LYS [p.E300K]

In the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder with factor II activity of 5 units/dl and antigen of 21 units/dl, Lefkowitz et al. (2000) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the F2 gene, glu300-to-lys (E300K) and glu309-to-lys (E309K), by sequencing PCR-amplified exons. Plasma assays suggested that the functional defect was in the activation of the zymogen to the enzyme.
