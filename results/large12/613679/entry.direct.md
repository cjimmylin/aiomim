### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital prothrombin deficiency, which includes hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding prothrombin (F2) on chromosome 11.

### Description

Congenital prothrombin (factor II) deficiency is among the rarest inherited coagulation disorders. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) distinguished 2 main phenotypes: hypoprothrombinemia (type I deficiency), with low levels of both activity and antigen, and dysprothrombinemia (type II deficiency), with normal or near-normal synthesis of a dysfunctional protein. In some compound heterozygotes, hypoprothrombinemia occurs together with dysprothrombinemia. Meeks and Abshire (2008) reported that low prothrombin activity typically prolongs both the activated partial thromboplastin time and the prothrombin time, and that bleeding is mainly mucosal or associated with surgery or trauma, although joint bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage have been reported.

### Nomenclature

Owen et al. (1978) separated inherited prothrombin defects into hypoprothrombinemia, a significant reduction of the plasma protein, and dysprothrombinemia, a functionally abnormal prothrombin. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) called these type I and type II deficiency, respectively. Individual dysfunctional variants were named after the place where they were found, for example prothrombin Barcelona (Josso et al., 1971), Padua (Girolami et al., 1974), Quick (Owen et al., 1978), Houston (Weinger et al., 1980), and Gainesville (Smith et al., 1981). ⚠︎

Girolami et al. (1998) noted that cases with a parallel decrease in prothrombin activity and antigen should not be considered examples of hypoprothrombinemia.

### Clinical Features

Van Creveld (1954) reported congenital idiopathic hypoprothrombinemia. Quick et al. (1955) described congenital hypoprothrombinemic states.

Pool et al. (1962) described severe congenital hypoprothrombinemia in a black boy. Josso et al. (1962) studied a case of congenital hypoprothrombinemia. Quick and Hussey (1962) reported hereditary hypoprothrombinemias.

Shapiro et al. (1969) studied a large family in which 11 members had half-normal plasma levels of biologic prothrombin activity but normal amounts of immunoreactive prothrombin. Prothrombin purified from 1 affected person gave normal protein yields but half-normal activity. When activated, it produced half or less of the thrombin clotting and esterase activity expected from normal prothrombin. Immunoelectrophoresis of serum showed a normal nonthrombin 'pro piece' and an extra activation product. The authors concluded that affected people were heterozygotes in whom half of the prothrombin molecules were structurally abnormal and could not release active thrombin. They called the disorder congenital dysprothrombinemia.

Kattlove et al. (1970) reported hereditary prothrombin deficiency.

Josso et al. (1971) described a congenital abnormality of the prothrombin molecule in 4 sibs and named it prothrombin Barcelona.

Girolami et al. (1974) described a congenital dysprothrombinemia that they named prothrombin Padua.

Owen et al. (1978) restudied patients first described by Quick and colleagues. One patient had about half the normal amount of prothrombin antigen but almost no prothrombin function, and the authors named this dysprothrombin prothrombin Quick. Another patient was truly hypoprothrombinemic, lacking both functional and antigenic prothrombin. Owen et al. (1978) reported that only 10 families with significant reductions of prothrombin, and 6 families with a functionally abnormal prothrombin, had been observed. An undefined prothrombin abnormality had been found in 8 other families.

Weinger et al. (1980) described prothrombin Houston in a 72-year-old man who had bruised easily and bled after trauma all his life. His prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged, as were his Stypven, Taipan, and Echis carinatus venom clotting times. Plasma prothrombin coagulant activity was low relative to prothrombin antigen. On crossed immunoelectrofocusing, the patient's prothrombin antigen was more basic than normal. Prothrombin eluted from his plasma was cleaved by Echis carinatus venom but clotted purified fibrinogen more slowly than normal eluate. The authors concluded that the disorder resulted from the generation of a dysfunctional thrombin.

Smith et al. (1981) described prothrombin Gainesville in a pair of identical twins evaluated for menometrorrhagia and mild postoperative bleeding. Each twin had a slightly prolonged partial thromboplastin time and prothrombin time. Plasma prothrombin activity was 23% and 25% of normal by Russell's viper venom assay, whereas prothrombin antigen was 70% of normal in each. The authors stated that prothrombin Gainesville was the twelfth dysprothrombin reported.

Josso et al. (1982) investigated a mild hemorrhagic tendency in a French family. The father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia and the mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia. Three of the children were double heterozygotes, which allowed the authors to identify an abnormal prothrombin, prothrombin Metz. This variant generated an abnormal thrombin that was less sensitive than normal thrombin to inactivation by antithrombin III.

Rubio et al. (1983) described prothrombin Habana in a Cuban family. The proposita was a 5-year-old girl who had umbilical bleeding after birth, followed by easy bruising and a lifelong bleeding tendency. Her prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. Prothrombin activity was less than 10% in several 1- and 2-stage systems, whereas staphylocoagulase-complexed and immunologic assays gave levels of about 50%. Her father had about 50% prothrombin activity and antigen. Her mother had 45% prothrombin activity but about 100% antigen. The authors suggested that the proposita was heterozygous for an abnormal prothrombin and heterozygous for true prothrombin deficiency.

Shirakami and Kawauchi (1984) reported congenital dysprothrombinemia.

Rocha et al. (1986) described prothrombin Segovia in a 21-year-old man who presented with hemarthrosis of the left knee and an extensive hematoma after minor trauma. His prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. Prothrombin activity was very low with physiologic activators (7% by the 1-stage method and 20% by the 2-stage method) and with several snake venoms. It was borderline to normal with Taipan viper venom (64%) and normal by staphylocoagulase and immunologic methods. His parents were consanguineous, and both had a 50% reduction in prothrombin level with physiologic activators or Echis carinatus venom.

Dumont et al. (1987) described prothrombin Poissy in a newborn girl who had severe bleeding from the second day of life. Her prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were very prolonged, and prothrombin activity was about 2% in a 1-stage assay. Staphylocoagulase and Echis carinatus venom assays gave 35% and 25%, respectively. Prothrombin antigen was 47%, with abnormal migration on crossed immunoelectrophoresis. Her father, mother, and brother had both normal and abnormal prothrombin. The abnormal prothrombin was activated slowly and incompletely, and the authors thought the proposita was homozygous for a 'lazy' dysprothrombin.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) found prothrombin Mexico City, a functionally normal but structurally abnormal variant, in a Mexican family. The variant had a more acidic isoelectric point (4.01) than normal prothrombin (4.29) and was cleaved abnormally by factor Xa and Echis carinatus venom, but it yielded functionally normal thrombin. The authors described the variant as asymptomatic and autosomal dominant.

Lutze et al. (1989) described prothrombin Magdeburg in 7 members of 3 generations of a family. In each, the ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration was reduced to about 0.5. Two abnormal prothrombins with higher molecular weights and lower isoelectric points were present in addition to normal prothrombin. Five of the 7 had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, seen especially as more marked or prolonged bleeding after trauma or surgery.

Poort et al. (1994) studied a patient with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%). Only the 5 homozygous brothers and sisters of the propositus were clinically affected. All had severe hemorrhages, including epistaxis and soft tissue, muscle, and joint bleeding, and the 2 women had severe hemorrhages.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) studied the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement. Factor II activity was 5 units/dl and factor II antigen was 21 units/dl. Plasma assays suggested that the functional defect lay in the activation of zymogen to enzyme.

In a review, Girolami et al. (1998) noted that bleeding may be severe in homozygous true deficiency and is more variable in dysprothrombinemias, whereas heterozygotes are usually asymptomatic. Hemarthroses and intracranial bleeding had been described in homozygous true deficiency, and prognosis generally matched the prothrombin activity level. Meeks and Abshire (2008) reported that bleeding was mainly mucosal or associated with surgery or trauma, although joint bleeding and intracranial hemorrhages had been reported.

### Biochemical Features

Rabiet et al. (1979) studied the activation of prothrombin Barcelona and found evidence of active high molecular weight intermediates.

Rabiet et al. (1984) purified thrombin Metz. It was physicochemically identical to normal thrombin but had less than 4% of normal fibrinogen clotting activity and abnormal Km and Kcat toward S2238. It released fibrinopeptides A and B more slowly than normal, the effect being greater for fibrinopeptide B, so that fibrin polymerized slowly. Thrombin Metz was less than 5% as effective as normal thrombin in inducing platelet aggregation. Its interaction with antithrombin III was slower than normal, but not in the presence of heparin. It bound less tightly to heparin-Sepharose. The authors predicted that the defect affected the catalytic site or its vicinity, and the region that interacts with antithrombin III and heparin.

Inomoto et al. (1987) purified prothrombin Tokushima from a proband with 12% of normal plasma clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen. Factor Xa cleaved it in the same way as normal prothrombin. The resulting thrombin Tokushima had the normal molecular weight but less than 22% of normal clotting activity. Its kcat/Km for a synthetic substrate was less than one-tenth of normal, although active site titration showed no difference from normal. It was 2.5% as effective as normal thrombin in inducing platelet aggregation and 33% as effective in forming a complex with antithrombin III. The authors concluded that the molecular defect lay in the thrombin portion of the molecule.

Morishita et al. (1992) found that prothrombin Himi had reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates.

### Inheritance

Shapiro et al. (1969) found autosomal inheritance of half-normal prothrombin activity in a large family with dysprothrombinemia, and they regarded affected members as heterozygotes. Girolami (1971) studied the hereditary transmission of congenital 'true' hypoprothrombinemia. Lutze et al. (1989) described an autosomal dysprothrombinemia in which affected heterozygotes had a slightly increased bleeding tendency. Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) described an asymptomatic prothrombin variant with autosomal dominant inheritance.

Poort et al. (1994) found that a severe bleeding tendency cosegregated with homozygosity for a prothrombin mutation, with clinical disease only in homozygotes, and concluded that the bleeding tendency is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Akhavan et al. (2000) and Meeks and Abshire (2008) described prothrombin deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder.

### Mapping

Girolami et al. (1998) and Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) stated that the prothrombin gene is on chromosome 11. The 2 reviews differed on the gene's structure. Girolami et al. (1998) described it as having 10 exons and 8 introns, whereas Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) described a gene of about 21 kb with 14 exons.

### Molecular Genetics

In a proband with prothrombin Tokushima, Iwahana et al. (1992) used PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP analysis to identify a C-to-T substitution at position 9490 of the prothrombin gene. The change resulted in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution and abolished an MspI site. The mutation was inherited from the proband's mother. This was the first gene analysis of a dysprothrombinemia.

Iwahana et al. (1992) showed that the proband with prothrombin Tokushima was a compound heterozygote for dysprothrombinemia and hypoprothrombinemia. Screening by PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion, followed by sequencing, identified a 1-bp insertion of T at position 4177 in exon 6. This insertion caused a frameshift, altering the amino acid sequence from codon 114 and creating a premature termination codon at codon 174 in exon 7, which was predicted to lead to a null prothrombin phenotype. The insertion was inherited from the father.

In a proband with prothrombin Himi, Morishita et al. (1992) used PCR-SSCP analysis of exons 8 through 14 followed by sequencing. They identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in exon 10: a T-to-C transition at position 8751 causing a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I), and a G-to-A transition at position 8904 causing an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II). The M337T mutation was inherited from the father and the R388H mutation from the mother. A sister of the proband had inherited both alleles.

Poort et al. (1994) sequenced the prothrombin gene in a patient with severe hypoprothrombinemia. They found a homozygous A-to-G substitution in exon 3 that predicted a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The mutation cosegregated completely with prothrombin deficiency in the family.

In the patient with prothrombin Denver, Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found compound heterozygosity for 2 glu-to-lys point mutations in the prothrombin gene, at amino acid positions 300 (E300K) and 309 (E309K). The mother and brother of the patient were also studied.

Akhavan et al. (2000) screened the prothrombin gene in 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency and identified 9 novel candidate mutations. Of 11 patients with hypoprothrombinemia, 10 were homozygous for 5 mutations and 1 was a compound heterozygote. The 2 patients with dysprothrombinemia were homozygous for 2 mutations. Eight mutations were missense substitutions: arg-1-to-gln and arg-2-to-trp in the propeptide, asp118-to-tyr in kringle-1, arg220-to-cys in kringle-2, and gly330-to-ser, ser354-to-arg, arg382-to-his, and arg538-to-cys in the serine protease domain. The ninth mutation was an in-frame 3-bp deletion removing lys301/302. Using crystal structures, the authors inferred that the hypoprothrombinemia mutations affected propeptide cleavage from the Gla domain, the stability of the kringle domains, or the association of the A and B chains. They inferred that the dysprothrombinemia mutations directly affected catalytic function through their location at the active site crevice or exosite 1. ⚠︎

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) reported that 40 different mutations had been identified in prothrombin deficiency. Many lay around the catalytic site, and another hotspot was the anion-binding exosite I (fibrinogen recognition site). Mutations had also been found in the Na(+)-binding loop and in the thrombin light A chain. Most hypoprothrombinemia-associated mutations were missense, but nonsense mutations and a single-nucleotide deletion had also been found. Meeks and Abshire (2008) stated that more than 40 mutations in prothrombin were known. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Girolami et al. (1998) reported that in true deficiency, activity levels are usually less than 10% of normal in homozygotes and between 40 and 60% in heterozygotes. In dysprothrombinemias, factor II levels are more variable, usually 1 to 50% of normal, because patients may be homozygotes, heterozygotes, or compound heterozygotes. Compound heterozygotes may combine a heterozygous abnormality with a heterozygous true deficiency, or carry 2 distinct abnormalities. Antigen levels in dysprothrombinemias are normal, near normal, or slightly decreased, but always higher than clotting activity.

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) noted that no living patient with undetectable plasma prothrombin had been reported.

### Diagnosis

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that prothrombin deficiency or abnormality can be diagnosed with a combination of clotting, chromogenic, and immunologic assays. In true deficiency, all assays decrease in parallel regardless of the activating agent. Discrepancies among clotting assays, particularly with viper venoms, should raise suspicion of dysprothrombinemia. Iwahana et al. (1992) found PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP useful for detecting the abnormal gene and for direct carrier diagnosis of dysprothrombinemia.

### Clinical Management

Smith et al. (1981) measured the kinetics of infused prothrombin (Konyne) in twins with prothrombin Gainesville before and during hysterectomy. Its volume of distribution was about 1.5 times the plasma volume and its half-life was 34 hours.

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that substitution therapy uses prothrombin complex concentrates or plasma. They gave the half-life of infused prothrombin as about 70 hours, which allows hemostatically effective levels (about 50% of normal) to be reached without difficulty. Meeks and Abshire (2008) gave the hemostatic level of prothrombin as 20 to 40% and its half-life as about 3 days. They noted that no purified prothrombin products were available, but that fresh frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrates could be used. Lefkowitz et al. (2000) reported a patient with prothrombin Denver who was treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement.

### Population Genetics

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) gave the prevalence of prothrombin deficiency as about 1 in 2,000,000. Meeks and Abshire (2008) estimated that it occurs in about 1 in 1 to 2 million people.

Using a new electrophoretic method, Board et al. (1982) identified 3 allelic variants at the prothrombin structural locus, in each case located on a profragment rather than on thrombin. One allele, FII4, reached polymorphic frequencies in some Pacific Island populations.

### History

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that Quick et al. first described prothrombin deficiency in 1947, and that Shapiro et al. (1969) reported the first prothrombin abnormality.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA (PROTHROMBIN TOKUSHIMA)**  
F2, ARG418TRP [9490C-T; Arg418Trp]

In a proband with prothrombin Tokushima, a dysfunctional variant previously characterized by Inomoto et al. (1987), Iwahana et al. (1992) used PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP to identify a C-to-T substitution at position 9490 of the prothrombin gene. The change resulted in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution and abolished an MspI site. The mutation was inherited from the mother. Southern blot analysis showed no other distinct changes, and the mutant protein had the same molecular weight as normal prothrombin.

Iwahana et al. (1992) showed that the proband was compound heterozygous for R418W and a 1-bp insertion that caused hypoprothrombinemia. ⚠︎

**.0002 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, 1-BP INS, 4177T [4177insT]

In the proband with prothrombin Tokushima, Iwahana et al. (1992) found a 1-bp insertion of T at position 4177 in exon 6 of the prothrombin gene in compound heterozygosity with the R418W mutation. The frameshift altered the amino acid sequence from codon 114 and created a premature termination codon at codon 174 in exon 7, which was predicted to give a null prothrombin phenotype. The insertion was inherited from the father. ⚠︎

**.0003 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA (PROTHROMBIN HIMI)**  
F2, MET337THR [8751T-C; Met337Thr]

In a proband with prothrombin Himi and in 1 of her sisters, Morishita et al. (1992) identified compound heterozygosity for a T-to-C transition at position 8751 in exon 10 of the prothrombin gene. The transition resulted in a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I). The second mutation was R388H. M337T was inherited from the father.

**.0004 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA (PROTHROMBIN HIMI)**  
F2, ARG388HIS [8904G-A; Arg388His]

In the proband with prothrombin Himi and 1 of her sisters, Morishita et al. (1992) identified a G-to-A transition at position 8904 in exon 10 of the prothrombin gene, resulting in an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II). R388H was inherited from the mother and was present in compound heterozygosity with M337T.

**.0005 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, TYR44CYS [TAC-TGC; Tyr44Cys]

In a patient with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, antigen 5%), Poort et al. (1994) identified a homozygous A-to-G substitution in exon 3 of the prothrombin gene. The substitution predicted a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C; TAC-to-TGC) change. Both parents were heterozygous, and the mutation cosegregated completely with prothrombin deficiency in the family. The 5 homozygous sibs of the propositus were clinically affected.

**.0006 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA (PROTHROMBIN DENVER)**  
F2, GLU300LYS [Glu300Lys]

In the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder, Lefkowitz et al. (2000) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the prothrombin gene: a glu300-to-lys (E300K) substitution and E309K.

**.0007 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA (PROTHROMBIN DENVER)**  
F2, GLU309LYS [Glu309Lys]

In the patient with prothrombin Denver, Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found a glu309-to-lys (E309K) substitution in compound heterozygosity with E300K. Plasma assays suggested a defect in the activation of zymogen to enzyme.
