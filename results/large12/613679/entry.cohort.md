### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital prothrombin deficiency, which includes hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the F2 gene (176930) on chromosome 11p11.2. Heterozygosity for some dysfunctional prothrombin variants has also been reported in families with autosomal dominant transmission of dysprothrombinemia. ⚠︎

### Description

Meeks and Abshire (2008) described prothrombin (factor II) deficiency as a rare autosomal recessive coagulation disorder that occurs in about 1 in 1 to 2 million people. Both hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia have been described. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) distinguished 2 main phenotypes. Hypoprothrombinemia (type I deficiency) has low activity and low antigen levels together. Dysprothrombinemia (type II deficiency) has normal or near-normal synthesis of a dysfunctional protein. In some compound heterozygous cases, hypoprothrombinemia occurs together with dysprothrombinemia. Akhavan et al. (2000) described prothrombin deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder with a moderately severe bleeding tendency. Lefkowitz et al. (2000) described dysprothrombinemia as a rare congenital cause of bleeding and noted that fewer than 25 families with a functional prothrombin defect had been reported.

### Nomenclature

Individual dysfunctional prothrombins have been named after the place where they were found or after a patient. Josso et al. (1971) used the name prothrombin Barcelona for a congenital abnormality of the prothrombin molecule that they found in 4 sibs. Owen et al. (1978) proposed the name prothrombin Quick for the dysfunctional prothrombin of a patient first described by Quick. Rocha et al. (1986) proposed the term prothrombin Segovia, and Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) proposed the name prothrombin 'Mexico City' for a variant that differed from those described before. ⚠︎

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) called hypoprothrombinemia type I deficiency and dysprothrombinemia type II deficiency.

### Clinical Features

Van Creveld (1954) reported on congenital idiopathic hypoprothrombinemia.

Pool et al. (1962) reported severe congenital hypoprothrombinemia in a Negro boy.

Josso et al. (1962) studied a case of congenital hypoprothrombinemia.

Shapiro et al. (1969) studied a large family in which 11 members had half the normal plasma concentration of biologic prothrombin activity. Affected family members had normal amounts of immunoreactive prothrombin, which appeared immunologically identical to the normal zymogen. Inheritance was autosomal.

Josso et al. (1971) described a congenital abnormality of the prothrombin molecule, prothrombin Barcelona, in 4 sibs.

Girolami et al. (1974) described prothrombin Padua, a 'new' congenital dysprothrombinemia.

Owen et al. (1978) restudied 2 patients first described by Quick. One, a woman, had about half the normal amount of prothrombin antigen but virtually no prothrombin function (prothrombin Quick). The other was truly hypoprothrombinemic, lacking both functional and antigenic prothrombin.

Weinger et al. (1980) reported a 72-year-old man with lifelong easy bruising and posttraumatic bleeding (prothrombin Houston). His prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. The presence of a dysprothrombin was confirmed by the discrepancy between plasma prothrombin coagulant activity and prothrombin antigen levels.

Smith et al. (1981) evaluated a pair of identical twin women for menometrorrhagia and mild postoperative bleeding (prothrombin Gainesville). Each twin had a slightly prolonged partial thromboplastin time and prothrombin time. Full hemostatic evaluation was otherwise normal, except that plasma prothrombin activity in a Russell's viper venom assay was 23% and 25% of normal. Each twin had 70% of normal plasma prothrombin antigen.

Josso et al. (1982) investigated a mild hemorrhagic tendency in a French family. The father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia and the mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia. Three of the children were double heterozygotes, which allowed the identification of an abnormal prothrombin, prothrombin Metz.

Rubio et al. (1983) reported a 5-year-old Cuban girl with prothrombin Habana. She had umbilical bleeding after birth, followed by easy bruising and a bleeding tendency throughout her life. Her prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. Prothrombin activity was less than 10% in several one- and two-stage systems, but staphylocoagulase-complexed prothrombin and immunologic methods gave levels of about 50%. Her father had about 50% prothrombin activity and antigen. Her mother had 45% prothrombin activity but about 100% prothrombin antigen.

Rocha et al. (1986) reported a 21-year-old man with prothrombin Segovia. He presented at the same time with hemarthrosis of the left knee and an extensive hematoma after minor trauma. His prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. Functional prothrombin activity was very low (7% in a one-stage and 20% in a two-stage assay), whereas the immunologic level was normal. His parents were consanguineous. Both had prothrombin levels reduced by 50% when physiologic activators or Echis carinatus venom were used.

Dumont et al. (1987) described prothrombin Poissy in a newborn girl with severe bleeding from the second day of life. Her prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were very prolonged. Prothrombin activity was about 2% in a one-stage assay, and prothrombin antigen was 47% with abnormal migration on crossed immunoelectrophoresis. Her father, mother and brother had both normal and abnormal prothrombin in their plasma.

Inomoto et al. (1987) purified a mutant prothrombin, prothrombin Tokushima, from the plasma of a proband with 12% of normal plasma clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) found a functionally normal but structurally abnormal prothrombin variant, prothrombin Mexico City, in a Mexican family. It was asymptomatic and inherited as an autosomal dominant trait.

Lutze et al. (1989) reported prothrombin Magdeburg in 7 members of a family over 3 generations. Each had the ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration reduced to about 0.5. Five of the 7 had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, seen mainly as more marked or prolonged bleeding after trauma and surgery.

Poort et al. (1994) studied a man with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%). Only his 5 homozygous brothers and sisters were clinically affected. All 5 had severe hemorrhages, including epistaxis and bleeding into soft tissue, muscle and joints, and the 2 women had severe hemorrhages.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) restudied the original patient with prothrombin Denver. The patient had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement. Factor II activity was 5 units/dl and factor II antigen 21 units/dl.

Akhavan et al. (2000) studied 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency, 11 with hypoprothrombinemia and 2 with dysprothrombinemia.

In a review, Girolami et al. (1998) reported that bleeding may be severe in homozygous true deficiency, in which hemarthroses and intracranial bleeding have been described. Bleeding is more variable in the dysprothrombinemias, and heterozygotes are usually asymptomatic. Prognosis generally follows the prothrombin activity level. Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that bleeding is mostly mucosal or follows surgery or trauma, and that joint bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage have also been reported. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) noted that no living patient with undetectable plasma prothrombin had been reported.

### Biochemical Features

Shapiro et al. (1969) isolated prothrombin from the plasma of an affected person and obtained normal amounts of protein but only half the normal prothrombin activity. When activated by the intrinsic or extrinsic systems, concentrated sodium citrate, or trypsin, it gave half or less of the expected thrombin clotting and esterase activity.

Rabiet et al. (1979) studied the activation of prothrombin Barcelona and found evidence for active high molecular weight intermediates.

Weinger et al. (1980) found by crossed immunoelectrofocusing that the prothrombin antigen of the patient with prothrombin Houston was more basic than normal. However, crossed immunoelectrophoresis was similar to normal, and the prothrombin was completely adsorbed onto and eluted from barium sulfate.

Josso et al. (1982) found that prothrombin Metz generated an abnormal thrombin that was less sensitive than normal to inactivation by antithrombin III. Rabiet et al. (1984) purified thrombin Metz after activating the variant prothrombin with factor Xa. By physicochemical criteria it was identical to normal thrombin. However, it had less than 4% of normal fibrinogen clotting activity, an abnormal Km and Kcat on S2238, and less than 5% of normal effectiveness in inducing platelet aggregation. Thrombin Metz released fibrinopeptides A and B more slowly than normal, B more so, which slowed fibrin polymerization. It also interacted with antithrombin III more slowly than normal, except in the presence of heparin, and bound less tightly to heparin-Sepharose.

Rubio et al. (1983) found that prothrombin Habana migrated more anodically than normal in single and bidimensional immunoelectrophoresis, and adding calcium did not change its migration.

Rocha et al. (1986) found that prothrombin Segovia had very low activity with physiologic activators (7% in a one-stage and 20% in a two-stage assay), Russell's viper venom-cephalin (23%), Notechis scutatus scutatus venom (15%) and Echis carinatus venom (17%). In contrast, the level was borderline to normal with Taipan venom (64%) and normal by staphylocoagulase and immunologic methods. On immunodiffusion, normal prothrombin and prothrombin Segovia formed a line of identity. The abnormal prothrombin migrated less anodically than normal, and adding calcium did not change its migration. On bidimensional immunoelectrophoresis, the patient's serum showed 3 bands, compared with 2 in normal serum.

In the propositus with prothrombin Poissy, Dumont et al. (1987) found prothrombin levels of 35% with staphylocoagulase and 25% with Echis carinatus venom, while prothrombin antigen was 47%. In a thrombin-generation system free of natural inhibitors, the abnormal prothrombin was activated slowly and incompletely. The authors thought the propositus was homozygous for a 'lazy' dysprothrombin.

Inomoto et al. (1987) found that thrombin Tokushima, produced by factor Xa activation, had a normal molecular weight but less than 22% of normal clotting activity. Its kcat/Km toward a synthetic substrate was less than one-tenth of normal, although active-site titration showed no difference from normal thrombin. Thrombin Tokushima was 2.5% as effective as normal thrombin at inducing platelet aggregation and 33% as effective at forming a complex with antithrombin III.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) found that prothrombin Mexico City had a more acidic isoelectric point than normal prothrombin (4.01 vs 4.29) but a normal molecular weight. Factor Xa and Echis carinatus venom cleaved it abnormally, yet both produced functionally normal thrombin. Its calcium binding and overall antigenic structure were normal.

In prothrombin Magdeburg, Lutze et al. (1989) found that the reduced ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration disappeared when prothrombin was measured after activation with staphylocoagulase. SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis showed, besides normal prothrombin, 2 abnormal prothrombins with higher molecular weights and lower isoelectric points.

Morishita et al. (1992) found that prothrombin Himi had reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but kept full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates.

Girolami et al. (1998) reported that in true deficiency, activity is usually less than 10% of normal in homozygotes and 40 to 60% in heterozygotes. In the congenital dysprothrombinemias, factor II levels are more variable, usually 1 to 50% of normal. In these cases, antigen levels are normal, near normal or slightly decreased, but always higher than clotting activity.

### Inheritance

In the large family with dysprothrombinemia reported by Shapiro et al. (1969), inheritance was autosomal, and affected members were thought to be heterozygotes. Lutze et al. (1989) described prothrombin Magdeburg as an autosomal hereditary dysprothrombinemia in which affected persons were heterozygotes, over 3 generations. Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) described the asymptomatic, functionally normal prothrombin Mexico City variant as autosomal dominant.

In a family with a homozygous tyr44-to-cys mutation in the F2 gene, Poort et al. (1994) found that only homozygous individuals were clinically affected. They concluded that the bleeding tendency appeared to be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Akhavan et al. (2000) and Meeks and Abshire (2008) also described prothrombin deficiency as autosomal recessive. Compound heterozygosity for a dysprothrombinemia allele and a hypoprothrombinemia allele was reported by Josso et al. (1982) and Iwahana et al. (1992).

### Heterogeneity

Owen et al. (1978) noted reports of 10 families with true hypoprothrombinemia, 6 families with dysprothrombinemia (a functionally abnormal prothrombin), and 8 further families with an unclassified prothrombin abnormality. Smith et al. (1981) reviewed the dysprothrombins reported before their study and stated that prothrombin Gainesville was the twelfth such disorder. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In the French family with prothrombin Metz, Josso et al. (1982) found that 3 children with a mild hemorrhagic tendency were double heterozygotes. They had inherited a hypoprothrombinemia allele from their heterozygous father and a dysprothrombinemia allele from their heterozygous mother. ⚠︎

From family studies, Rubio et al. (1983) suggested that the propositus with prothrombin Habana was compound heterozygous. They proposed that she had an abnormal prothrombin inherited from her mother, who had 45% activity but normal antigen, and true prothrombin deficiency inherited from her father, who had about 50% activity and antigen. ⚠︎

In the proband with prothrombin Tokushima, Iwahana et al. (1992) found a C-to-T change at nucleotide 9490 of the F2 gene. The change replaced arginine with tryptophan at position 418 (R418W), and the proband had inherited the mutant gene from the mother. The authors considered this mutation the likely molecular basis of prothrombin Tokushima for 3 reasons. Southern blot analysis showed no distinct changes. The variant protein had the same molecular weight as the abnormal gene product. No other amino acid abnormality was found in the proteolytic peptide fragments. The mutation was detected by altered migration on PCR-SSCP and by loss of an MspI site on PCR-RFLP.

In a proband with prothrombin Tokushima dysprothrombinemia and hypoprothrombinemia, Iwahana et al. (1992) found compound heterozygosity in the F2 gene. One allele carried the single-nucleotide substitution responsible for prothrombin Tokushima, and 3 polymorphisms were also detected. The other carried a 1-bp insertion of T at position 4177 in exon 6. This insertion caused a frameshift from codon 114 and a premature stop codon (TGA) at codon 174 in exon 7. Because exon 7 encodes the kringle 2 domain that precedes the thrombin sequence, the authors concluded that the frameshift leads to a null prothrombin phenotype. PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and mutated primer-mediated PCR-RFLP showed that the hypoprothrombinemia allele was inherited from the father. ⚠︎

Morishita et al. (1992) used PCR-SSCP to analyze exons 8 through 14 of the F2 gene, which encode the thrombin moiety, in a family with prothrombin Himi. They found 2 variant conformers in exon 10. The female proband was compound heterozygous for a T-to-C transition at position 8751, resulting in a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I), and a G-to-A transition at position 8904, resulting in an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II). Sequencing of the parents showed that thrombin Himi I came from the father and thrombin Himi II from the mother. One of the proband's sisters had also inherited both alleles.

Poort et al. (1994) screened the F2 gene of a patient with severe bleeding and hypoprothrombinemia and found that he was homozygous for an A-to-G substitution in exon 3. The substitution predicts a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C) replacement. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Family studies showed complete cosegregation of the mutation with prothrombin deficiency, and only the 5 homozygous sibs of the propositus were clinically affected.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found that the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had moderately severe dysprothrombinemia, was compound heterozygous for 2 glu-to-lys point mutations in the F2 gene. The mutations were at amino acid positions 300 (E300K) and 309 (E309K).

Akhavan et al. (2000) identified 9 novel mutations in the F2 gene in 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency. Of the 11 patients with hypoprothrombinemia, 10 were homozygous for 5 mutations and 1 was a compound heterozygote. The 2 patients with dysprothrombinemia were homozygous for 2 mutations. Eight of the mutations were missense mutations. Two affected the propeptide (arg-1-to-gln and arg-2-to-trp), 1 the kringle-1 domain (asp118 to a residue the authors designated 'Try'), 1 the kringle-2 domain (arg220-to-cys), and 4 the catalytic serine protease domain (gly330-to-ser, ser354-to-arg, arg382-to-his and arg538-to-cys). The ninth was an in-frame 3-bp deletion removing 1 amino acid (del lys301/302).

In reviews, Girolami et al. (1998) noted that the prothrombin gene lies on chromosome 11 and has 10 exons, and that several point mutations had been found in some of the dysprothrombinemias. Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that more than 40 prothrombin mutations were known. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) reviewed 40 mutations identified in prothrombin deficiency. Many surround the catalytic site, and another hotspot lies in anion-binding exosite I (the fibrinogen recognition site). Mutations were also found in the Na(+)-binding loop and the thrombin light A chain. Most hypoprothrombinemia-associated mutations are missense, but nonsense mutations and a single-nucleotide deletion had also been reported.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Girolami et al. (1998) noted that patients with dysprothrombinemia may be homozygotes, heterozygotes or compound heterozygotes. Compound heterozygotes may carry a heterozygous abnormality together with a heterozygous true deficiency, or 2 distinct abnormalities. The authors added that cases with a parallel decrease in prothrombin activity and antigen should not be considered examples of hypoprothrombinemia.

Akhavan et al. (2000) used crystal structures of alpha-thrombin and prothrombin fragments 1 and 2 to interpret their mutations. They inferred that hypoprothrombinemia mutations affect cleavage of the propeptide from the Gla domain, the stability of the kringle-1 and -2 domains, or the close association of the A and B chains of the serine protease domain. Dysprothrombinemia mutations, located at the active-site crevice or exosite 1, directly affect catalytic function. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Shapiro et al. (1969) found that serum from an affected person contained a normal 'pro piece' plus an additional activation product of intermediate electrophoretic mobility. They suggested that affected individuals are heterozygotes in whom half the prothrombin molecules are structurally abnormal. These molecules change during activation but cannot release active thrombin.

Echis carinatus venom cleaved prothrombin Houston, but Weinger et al. (1980) found that the activated eluate clotted purified fibrinogen more slowly than normal. They suggested that the coagulopathy results from the generation of a dysfunctional thrombin.

Rabiet et al. (1984) predicted that the defect in thrombin Metz lies at or near the catalytic site. They proposed that, jointly or as a consequence, it also affects the region where thrombin interacts with antithrombin III and heparin. Consistent with this, fluorescence enhancement of the DAPA probe on binding to thrombin Metz was only 34% of that seen with normal thrombin.

Inomoto et al. (1987) concluded that the molecular defect of prothrombin Tokushima lies in the thrombin portion of the molecule, and that the binding sites for various substrates appear to be greatly impaired.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) suggested that the abnormality in prothrombin Mexico City was a simple (substitution or translocation) mutation in the fragment 2 region.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found that plasma assays in the prothrombin Denver proband suggested that the functional defect lay in the activation of the zymogen to the enzyme.

### Diagnosis

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that prothrombin deficiency or abnormality can be diagnosed with a combination of clotting, chromogenic and immunologic assays. In true deficiency, all of these assays decrease in parallel, whatever the activating agent. Discrepancies among clotting assays, particularly those using viper venoms, should raise suspicion of dysprothrombinemia. Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that low prothrombin activity typically prolongs both the activated partial thromboplastin time and the prothrombin time. They also noted that the hemostatic level of prothrombin is thought to be 20 to 40% and that its half-life is about 3 days.

Iwahana et al. (1992) concluded that PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP are useful for detecting an abnormal prothrombin gene and for directly diagnosing carrier status in dysprothrombinemia.

### Clinical Management

Smith et al. (1981) collected prothrombin kinetic data in the twins with prothrombin Gainesville before and during hysterectomy. Infused prothrombin (Konyne) had a volume of distribution of about 1.5 times the plasma volume and a half-life of 34 hours.

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that substitution therapy uses prothrombin complex concentrates or plasma. Because injected prothrombin has a long half-life of about 70 hours, hemostatically effective levels of about 50% of normal are easily reached. Meeks and Abshire (2008) stated that no purified prothrombin product is available for replacement therapy, but that fresh frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrates, which both contain prothrombin, may be used.

The prothrombin Denver patient studied by Lefkowitz et al. (2000) was treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement.

### Population Genetics

Meeks and Abshire (2008) estimated that prothrombin deficiency occurs in about 1 in 1 to 2 million people. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) stated that it is among the rarest inherited coagulation disorders, with a prevalence of about 1 in 2,000,000.

Using a new electrophoretic technique, Board et al. (1982) found 3 allelic variants at the prothrombin structural locus (FII). One allele, FII4, reached polymorphic frequencies in some Pacific Island populations.

### History

Girolami et al. (1998) noted that prothrombin deficiency was first described by Quick et al. in 1947, and that the first prothrombin abnormality was reported by Shapiro et al. in 1969.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, PROTHROMBIN METZ

Josso et al. (1982) identified the abnormal prothrombin Metz in a French family with a mild hemorrhagic tendency. The mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia and the father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia. Three children were double heterozygotes, which allowed the abnormal prothrombin to be found. Prothrombin Metz generated an abnormal thrombin that was less sensitive than normal to inactivation by antithrombin III.

**.0002 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA ALLELE, METZ FAMILY

In the French family with prothrombin Metz, Josso et al. (1982) found that the father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia. Three of his children, who had a mild hemorrhagic tendency, carried this allele together with the maternal prothrombin Metz dysprothrombinemia allele. ⚠︎

**.0003 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, PROTHROMBIN TOKUSHIMA

Inomoto et al. (1987) purified prothrombin Tokushima from the plasma of a proband with 12% of normal plasma clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen. Thrombin Tokushima, produced by factor Xa activation, had a normal molecular weight but less than 22% of normal clotting activity. Its kcat/Km toward a synthetic substrate was less than one-tenth of normal. It was 2.5% as effective as normal thrombin at inducing platelet aggregation and 33% as effective at forming a complex with antithrombin III. The authors concluded that the defect lies in the thrombin portion of the molecule. ⚠︎

**.0004 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, PROTHROMBIN MAGDEBURG

Lutze et al. (1989) described prothrombin Magdeburg in 7 heterozygous members of a family over 3 generations. Each had the ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration reduced to about 0.5. Five of the 7 had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, mainly prolonged bleeding after trauma and surgery. SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis showed, besides normal prothrombin, 2 abnormal prothrombins with higher molecular weights and lower isoelectric points.

**.0005 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, PROTHROMBIN TOKUSHIMA SUBSTITUTION

In a proband with prothrombin Tokushima dysprothrombinemia and hypoprothrombinemia, Iwahana et al. (1992) detected the single-nucleotide substitution responsible for prothrombin Tokushima in the F2 gene. It was present in compound heterozygosity with a 1-bp insertion that causes hypoprothrombinemia.

**.0006 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, 1-BP INS, 4177T [c.4177inst]

In a proband with prothrombin Tokushima dysprothrombinemia and hypoprothrombinemia, Iwahana et al. (1992) found a 1-bp insertion of T at position 4177 in exon 6 of the F2 gene. It was in compound heterozygosity with the prothrombin Tokushima substitution. The insertion causes a frameshift from codon 114 and a premature stop codon (TGA) at codon 174 in exon 7, which the authors concluded leads to a null prothrombin phenotype. PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and mutated primer-mediated PCR-RFLP showed that the proband had inherited the allele from the father.

**.0007 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, ARG418TRP [c.9490C>T; p.R418W]

In a proband with prothrombin Tokushima, Iwahana et al. (1992) found a 9490C-T transition in the F2 gene, resulting in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution. The proband had inherited the mutation from the mother. The mutation was detected by altered migration on PCR-SSCP and by loss of an MspI site on PCR-RFLP. The authors considered it the likely molecular basis of prothrombin Tokushima because Southern blot analysis showed no distinct changes, the variant protein had the same molecular weight as the abnormal gene product, and no other amino acid abnormality was found in the proteolytic peptide fragments.

**.0008 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, MET337THR [c.8751T>C; p.M337T]

In a female proband with prothrombin Himi, Morishita et al. (1992) found compound heterozygosity in the F2 gene for an 8751T-C transition, resulting in a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I), and an arg388-to-his substitution. The M337T mutation was inherited from the father. One of the proband's sisters had also inherited both alleles. Prothrombin Himi had reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but kept full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates.

**.0009 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, ARG388HIS [c.8904G>A; p.R388H]

In a female proband with prothrombin Himi, Morishita et al. (1992) found compound heterozygosity in the F2 gene for an 8904G-A transition, resulting in an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II), and the M337T mutation. The R388H mutation was inherited from the mother. One of the proband's sisters had also inherited both alleles.

**.0010 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, TYR44CYS [p.Y44; p.Y44C]

Poort et al. (1994) found that a man with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%) was homozygous for an A-to-G substitution in exon 3 of the F2 gene. The substitution predicts a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C) replacement. Both parents were heterozygous. The mutation cosegregated completely with prothrombin deficiency in the family. Only the 5 homozygous sibs of the propositus were clinically affected, with epistaxis and bleeding into soft tissue, muscle and joints.

**.0011 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, GLU300LYS [p.E300K]

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found that the original prothrombin Denver patient was compound heterozygous in the F2 gene for a glu300-to-lys (E300K) and a glu309-to-lys substitution. The patient had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder, with factor II activity of 5 units/dl and factor II antigen of 21 units/dl. Plasma assays suggested a defect in the activation of the zymogen to the enzyme.

**.0012 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**
