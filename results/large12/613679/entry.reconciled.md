### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital prothrombin deficiency, including hypoprothrombinemia and dysprothrombinemia, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the F2 gene (176930) on chromosome 11p11.2. Heterozygous carriers of some dysprothrombinemia alleles have also been reported to have reduced prothrombin activity and a mild bleeding tendency. ⚠︎

### Description

Prothrombin (factor II) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive coagulation disorder. Meeks and Abshire (2008) estimated that it occurs in about 1 in 1 to 2 million people, and Akhavan et al. (2000) described it as associated with a moderately severe bleeding tendency. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) distinguished 2 main phenotypes. Hypoprothrombinemia (type I deficiency) shows low prothrombin activity and low antigen levels together. Dysprothrombinemia (type II deficiency) shows normal or near-normal synthesis of a dysfunctional protein. Some compound heterozygotes have hypoprothrombinemia together with dysprothrombinemia. Lefkowitz et al. (2000) described dysprothrombinemia as a rare congenital cause of bleeding and noted that fewer than 25 families with a functional prothrombin defect had been reported.

### Nomenclature

Variant dysfunctional prothrombins have been named after the place of origin of the family or after the investigator. Josso et al. (1971) used the name prothrombin Barcelona for a congenital abnormality of the prothrombin molecule that they found in 4 sibs. Owen et al. (1978) proposed the name prothrombin Quick for the dysfunctional prothrombin of a patient first described by Quick, who had about half-normal prothrombin antigen but virtually no prothrombin function. Rocha et al. (1986) proposed the term prothrombin Segovia for a new congenital prothrombin abnormality. Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) proposed the name prothrombin 'Mexico City' because the variant they studied differed from those previously described. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Van Creveld (1954) reported on congenital idiopathic hypoprothrombinemia.

Pool et al. (1962) reported severe congenital hypoprothrombinemia in a black boy.

Josso et al. (1962) studied a case of congenital hypoprothrombinemia.

Shapiro et al. (1969) studied a large family in which 11 members had about half the normal plasma concentration of biologic prothrombin activity. Affected family members had normal amounts of immunoreactive prothrombin, and its immunologic properties appeared identical to those of the normal zymogen. The authors described the pattern of inheritance as autosomal.

Josso et al. (1971) described a congenital abnormality of the prothrombin molecule, prothrombin Barcelona, in 4 sibs.

Girolami et al. (1974) described prothrombin Padua, a 'new' congenital dysprothrombinemia.

Owen et al. (1978) restudied patients first described by Quick and colleagues. A woman with a defect in her plasma prothrombin had about half the normal amount of prothrombin antigen but virtually no prothrombin function (prothrombin Quick). A second patient was truly hypoprothrombinemic, lacking both functional and antigenic prothrombin.

Weinger et al. (1980) reported a 72-year-old man with prothrombin Houston who had a lifelong history of easy bruising and posttraumatic bleeding. His prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. A dysprothrombin was confirmed by the discrepancy between his plasma prothrombin coagulant activity and his prothrombin antigen level.

Smith et al. (1981) evaluated a pair of identical twins with prothrombin Gainesville for menometrorrhagia and mild postoperative bleeding. Each twin had a slightly prolonged partial thromboplastin time and prothrombin time. Full hemostatic evaluation was otherwise normal. Plasma prothrombin activity was 23% and 25% of normal by a Russell's viper venom assay, and each twin had 70% of normal plasma prothrombin antigen.

Josso et al. (1982) investigated a French family with a mild hemorrhagic tendency. The father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia and the mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia. Three of the children were double heterozygotes, and study of these children identified an abnormal prothrombin, prothrombin Metz.

Rubio et al. (1983) reported a 5-year-old Cuban girl with prothrombin Habana. She had umbilical bleeding after birth, followed by easy bruising and a lifelong bleeding tendency. Her prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. Prothrombin activity was less than 10% in several 1- and 2-stage systems, whereas staphylocoagulase-complexed prothrombin and immunologic methods gave levels of about 50%. Her father had about 50% prothrombin activity and antigen. Her mother had 45% prothrombin activity but about 100% prothrombin antigen.

Rocha et al. (1986) reported a 21-year-old man with prothrombin Segovia. He presented with hemarthrosis of the left knee and, at the same time, an extensive hematoma following minor trauma. His prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were prolonged. Functional prothrombin activity was very low (7% in a 1-stage assay and 20% in a 2-stage assay), but the immunologic level was normal. His parents were consanguineous, and both had a 50% reduction in prothrombin level when measured with physiologic activators or Echis carinatus venom.

Dumont et al. (1987) described prothrombin Poissy in a newborn girl who had severe bleeding from the second day of life. Her prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time were very prolonged, prothrombin activity was about 2% in a 1-stage assay, and prothrombin antigen was 47% with abnormal migration on crossed immunoelectrophoresis. Her father, mother, and brother had both normal and abnormal prothrombin in their plasma.

Inomoto et al. (1987) purified a mutant prothrombin, prothrombin Tokushima, from the plasma of a proband with 12% of normal plasma clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) found a functionally normal but structurally abnormal prothrombin variant, prothrombin Mexico City, in a Mexican family. They described it as an asymptomatic autosomal dominant variant.

Lutze et al. (1989) reported prothrombin Magdeburg in 7 members of a family spanning 3 generations. In affected members, the ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration was reduced to about 0.5. Five of the 7 had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, seen especially as more marked or prolonged bleeding after trauma and surgery.

Morishita et al. (1992) studied a woman with prothrombin Himi, a congenitally dysfunctional prothrombin. It had reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but retained full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates.

Poort et al. (1994) reported a man with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%). Of his family members, only his 5 brothers and sisters who were also homozygous for the mutation were clinically affected. All 5 had severe hemorrhages including epistaxis and soft tissue, muscle, and joint bleeding, and the 2 women had severe hemorrhages.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) restudied the original patient with prothrombin Denver. He had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement. His factor II activity was 5 units/dl and his factor II antigen was 21 units/dl.

Akhavan et al. (2000) studied 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency, 11 with hypoprothrombinemia and 2 with dysprothrombinemia.

In a review, Girolami et al. (1998) stated that bleeding may be severe in homozygous true deficiency, in which hemarthroses and intracranial bleeding have been described. Bleeding is more variable in the dysprothrombinemias, and heterozygotes are usually asymptomatic. Prognosis generally follows the level of prothrombin activity. Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that bleeding is mostly mucosal or follows surgery or trauma, and that joint bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage have also been reported. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) noted that no living patient with undetectable plasma prothrombin had been reported.

### Biochemical Features

Shapiro et al. (1969) isolated prothrombin from the plasma of an affected person and obtained normal amounts of protein but only half the normal prothrombin activity. When it was activated by the intrinsic or extrinsic systems, concentrated sodium citrate, or trypsin, it yielded half or less of the expected thrombin clotting and esterase activity.

Rabiet et al. (1979) studied the activation of prothrombin Barcelona and found evidence for active high molecular weight intermediates.

Weinger et al. (1980) found by crossed immunoelectrofocusing that the prothrombin antigen of the patient with prothrombin Houston was more basic than normal. Crossed immunoelectrophoresis, however, was similar to normal, and the prothrombin was completely adsorbed onto and eluted from barium sulfate. ⚠︎

Josso et al. (1982) found that prothrombin Metz generated an abnormal thrombin that was less sensitive than normal thrombin to inactivation by antithrombin III. Rabiet et al. (1984) purified thrombin Metz after activating the variant prothrombin with factor Xa. By physicochemical criteria it was identical to normal thrombin. However, it had less than 4% of normal fibrinogen clotting activity, an abnormal Km and Kcat on S2238, and less than 5% of normal effectiveness in inducing platelet aggregation. Thrombin Metz released fibrinopeptides A and B more slowly than normal, with B more affected, which slowed fibrin polymerization. Its interaction with antithrombin III was slower than normal except in the presence of heparin, and it bound less tightly to heparin-Sepharose.

Rubio et al. (1983) found that prothrombin Habana migrated more anodically than normal on single and bidimensional immunoelectrophoresis, and its migration did not change when calcium was added.

Rocha et al. (1986) found that prothrombin Segovia had very low activity with physiologic activators (7% in a 1-stage assay, 20% in a 2-stage assay). Activity was also low with Russell's viper venom-cephalin (23%), Notechis scutatus scutatus venom (15%), and Echis carinatus venom (17%). In contrast, it was borderline to normal with Taipan venom (64%) and normal by staphylocoagulase and immunologic methods. On immunodiffusion, prothrombin Segovia showed a line of identity with normal prothrombin. It migrated less anodically than normal, and adding calcium did not change its migration. On bidimensional immunoelectrophoresis the patient's serum showed 3 bands, compared with 2 in normal serum.

In the propositus with prothrombin Poissy, Dumont et al. (1987) found prothrombin levels of 35% by staphylocoagulase assay and 25% by Echis carinatus venom assay, with prothrombin antigen at 47%. Thrombin generation in a system free of natural inhibitors showed that the abnormal prothrombin was activated slowly and incompletely. The propositus was thought to be homozygous for a 'lazy' dysprothrombin.

Inomoto et al. (1987) found that thrombin Tokushima, generated by factor Xa activation, had a normal molecular weight but less than 22% of normal clotting activity. Its kcat/Km for a synthetic substrate was less than one-tenth of normal, although active site titration showed no difference from normal thrombin. Thrombin Tokushima was 2.5% as effective as normal thrombin in inducing platelet aggregation and 33% as effective in forming a complex with antithrombin III. The authors concluded that the molecular defect lies in the thrombin portion of the molecule and that the binding sites for various substrates appear to be greatly impaired.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) found that prothrombin Mexico City had a more acidic isoelectric point than normal prothrombin (4.01 vs 4.29) but a normal molecular weight. Factor Xa and Echis carinatus venom both cleaved it abnormally, yet both produced functionally normal thrombin. Its calcium binding and overall antigenic structure were normal.

Lutze et al. (1989) found that the reduced ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration in prothrombin Magdeburg was not seen when prothrombin was measured after activation with staphylocoagulase. SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis showed 2 abnormal prothrombins with higher molecular weights and lower isoelectric points, in addition to normal prothrombin.

In a review, Girolami et al. (1998) stated that in true deficiency, activity levels are usually less than 10% of normal in homozygotes and 40 to 60% in heterozygotes. Factor II levels are more variable in the congenital dysprothrombinemias, usually 1 to 50% of normal. In these cases antigen levels are normal, near normal, or slightly decreased, but always higher than the clotting activity.

### Inheritance

Poort et al. (1994) studied a family with a homozygous tyr44-to-cys mutation in the F2 gene. Only homozygous individuals were clinically affected, and both parents were heterozygous. The authors concluded that the bleeding tendency was inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Akhavan et al. (2000) also described prothrombin deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder.

Some heterozygous dysprothrombinemias show autosomal transmission of a laboratory and sometimes clinical phenotype. Shapiro et al. (1969) described the pattern of inheritance in their large family as autosomal. Lutze et al. (1989) described prothrombin Magdeburg as an autosomal hereditary dysprothrombinemia in which the affected persons were heterozygotes. Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) described prothrombin Mexico City as an asymptomatic autosomal dominant variant. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Owen et al. (1978) noted that 10 families with true hypoprothrombinemia and 6 families with dysprothrombinemia had been reported, along with 8 further families with an unclassified prothrombin abnormality. Smith et al. (1981) stated that prothrombin Gainesville was the twelfth dysprothrombin disorder reported.

### Molecular Genetics

Josso et al. (1982) studied a French family with prothrombin Metz. The father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia and the mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia, and 3 of the children were double heterozygotes.

On the basis of family studies, Rubio et al. (1983) suggested that the propositus with prothrombin Habana was a compound heterozygote. They proposed that she carried an abnormal prothrombin allele from her mother, who had 45% activity but normal antigen, and a true prothrombin deficiency allele from her father, who had about 50% activity and antigen. ⚠︎

In the proband with prothrombin Tokushima, first studied by Inomoto et al. (1987), Iwahana et al. (1992) found a C-to-T change at nucleotide 9490 of the F2 gene, resulting in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution. The mutant gene was inherited from the proband's mother. Southern blot analysis showed no distinct changes, the variant protein had the same molecular weight as the abnormal gene product, and no other amino acid abnormalities were found in proteolytic peptide fragments. On this basis the authors considered the mutation the likely molecular basis of prothrombin Tokushima. They detected it by altered migration on PCR-SSCP and by the loss of an MspI site on PCR-RFLP. ⚠︎

In a second report on the same proband, Iwahana et al. (1992) found that the proband was a compound heterozygote for the prothrombin Tokushima dysprothrombinemia allele and a hypoprothrombinemia allele. The hypoprothrombinemia allele was a 1-bp insertion of T at position 4177 in exon 6 of the F2 gene. It caused a frameshift altering the amino acid sequence from codon 114 and created a premature stop codon (TGA) at codon 174 in exon 7. Exon 7 encodes the kringle 2 domain preceding the thrombin sequence, so the authors concluded that the frameshift leads to a null prothrombin phenotype. They showed by PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and by mutated primer-mediated PCR-RFLP that this allele was inherited from the father.

Morishita et al. (1992) performed PCR-SSCP analysis of exons 8 through 14 of the F2 gene, which encode the thrombin moiety, in a family with prothrombin Himi. They identified 2 variant conformers in exon 10. Sequencing showed that the proband was a compound heterozygote for a T-to-C transition at position 8751, resulting in a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I), and a G-to-A transition at position 8904, resulting in an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II). Sequencing of the parents showed that M337T was inherited from the father and R388H from the mother. One of the proband's sisters had also inherited both alleles.

In a man with severe bleeding and hypoprothrombinemia, Poort et al. (1994) screened the F2 gene and found a homozygous A-to-G substitution in exon 3, predicting a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Family studies showed complete cosegregation of the mutation with prothrombin deficiency, and the 5 clinically affected sibs were also homozygous.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found that the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had moderately severe dysprothrombinemia, was compound heterozygous for 2 different glu-to-lys point mutations in the F2 gene, at amino acid positions 300 (E300K) and 309 (E309K).

Akhavan et al. (2000) identified 9 novel mutations in the F2 gene in 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency. Of 11 patients with hypoprothrombinemia, 10 were homozygous for 5 mutations and 1 was a compound heterozygote. The 2 patients with dysprothrombinemia were homozygous for 2 mutations. Eight of the mutations were missense substitutions. Two were in the propeptide (arg-1-to-gln and arg-2-to-trp), one in the kringle-1 domain (designated asp118-to-try), and one in the kringle-2 domain (arg220-to-cys). The other 4 were in the catalytic serine protease domain (gly330-to-ser, ser354-to-arg, arg382-to-his, and arg538-to-cys). The ninth mutation was a 3-bp in-frame deletion that removed 1 amino acid (del lys301/302).

In a review, Girolami et al. (1998) noted that the prothrombin gene is located on chromosome 11, has 10 exons, and that several point mutations had been found in some of the dysprothrombinemias. Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that more than 40 prothrombin mutations were known. Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) reviewed 40 different mutations identified in prothrombin deficiency. Many surround the catalytic site, and another hotspot is in anion-binding exosite I, the fibrinogen recognition site. Mutations were also found in the Na(+)-binding loop and in the thrombin light A chain. Most mutations associated with hypoprothrombinemia are missense, but nonsense mutations and a single-nucleotide deletion have also been reported.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Girolami et al. (1998) noted that patients with dysprothrombinemia may be homozygotes, heterozygotes, or compound heterozygotes. Compound heterozygotes may carry a heterozygous abnormality together with a heterozygous true deficiency, or 2 distinct abnormalities. The authors added that cases with a parallel decrease in prothrombin activity and antigen should not be considered examples of hypoprothrombinemia.

Akhavan et al. (2000) used crystal structures of alpha-thrombin and prothrombin fragments 1 and 2 to infer how their mutations act. Hypoprothrombinemia mutations affect cleavage of the propeptide from the Gla domain, stability of the kringle-1 and kringle-2 domains, or the close association of the A and B chains of the serine protease domain. Dysprothrombinemia mutations directly affect catalytic function because of their location at the active site crevice or exosite 1. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Shapiro et al. (1969) found that serum from an affected person contained a normal 'pro piece' plus an additional activation product of intermediate electrophoretic mobility. They suggested that affected individuals are heterozygotes in whom half the prothrombin molecules are structurally abnormal. These molecules change during activation but cannot release active thrombin.

Weinger et al. (1980) showed that Echis carinatus venom cleaved prothrombin Houston, but the activated eluate clotted purified fibrinogen more slowly than normal. They suggested that the coagulopathy results from generation of a dysfunctional thrombin.

Rabiet et al. (1984) predicted that the defect in thrombin Metz affects the catalytic site or its vicinity and, jointly or as a consequence, the region where thrombin interacts with antithrombin III and heparin. In support, fluorescence enhancement of the DAPA probe on binding to thrombin Metz was only 34% of that seen with normal thrombin.

Valls-de-Ruiz et al. (1987) suggested that prothrombin Mexico City results from a simple mutation (substitution or translocation) in the fragment 2 region.

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found that assays of plasma from the prothrombin Denver proband suggested that the functional defect lies in the activation of the zymogen to the enzyme.

### Diagnosis

Iwahana et al. (1992) concluded that PCR-SSCP and PCR-RFLP were both useful for detecting the abnormal prothrombin Tokushima gene and for directly diagnosing carrier status in dysprothrombinemia.

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that prothrombin deficiency or abnormality can be diagnosed with a combination of clotting, chromogenic, and immunologic assays. In true deficiency, all these assays decrease in parallel whatever the activating agent. Discrepancies among clotting assays, particularly those using viper venoms, should raise suspicion of dysprothrombinemia. Meeks and Abshire (2008) noted that low prothrombin activity typically prolongs both the activated partial thromboplastin time and the prothrombin time. They also noted that the hemostatic level of prothrombin is thought to be 20 to 40%, and its half-life is about 3 days.

### Clinical Management

Smith et al. (1981) measured prothrombin kinetics in the twins with prothrombin Gainesville before and during hysterectomy. Infused prothrombin (Konyne) had a volume of distribution of about 1.5 times the plasma volume and a half-life of 34 hours.

Girolami et al. (1998) stated that substitution therapy uses prothrombin complex concentrates or plasma. Injected prothrombin has a long half-life of about 70 hours, so hemostatically effective levels of about 50% of normal are reached without difficulty. Meeks and Abshire (2008) stated that no purified prothrombin products were available for replacement therapy. Fresh frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrates both contain prothrombin and may be used for treatment.

### Population Genetics

Lancellotti and De Cristofaro (2009) stated that prothrombin deficiency is among the rarest inherited coagulation disorders, with a prevalence of about 1 in 2,000,000. Meeks and Abshire (2008) estimated a frequency of about 1 in 1 to 2 million people.

Board et al. (1982) used a new electrophoretic technique to find 3 allelic variants at the prothrombin structural locus. One allele, FII4, reached polymorphic frequencies in some Pacific Island populations. Board et al. (1982) studied the activated products of 3 variant prothrombins and found that in each case the structural variation was on a profragment, not on the enzymatically active thrombin molecule.

### History

Girolami et al. (1998) noted that prothrombin deficiency was first described in 1947 by Quick et al. They also noted that the first prothrombin abnormality was reported in 1969 by Shapiro et al.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, PROTHROMBIN METZ

Josso et al. (1982) investigated a mild hemorrhagic tendency in a French family. The mother was heterozygous for dysprothrombinemia and the father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia. Three of the children were double heterozygotes, and study of these children identified the abnormal prothrombin, prothrombin Metz. The abnormal prothrombin generated a thrombin that was less sensitive than normal to inactivation by antithrombin III.

**.0002 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA ALLELE, METZ FAMILY

In the French family with prothrombin Metz reported by Josso et al. (1982), the father was heterozygous for hypoprothrombinemia. Three children were double heterozygotes for this allele and the prothrombin Metz dysprothrombinemia allele from the mother, and the family had a mild hemorrhagic tendency.

**.0003 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, ARG418TRP [c.9490C>T; p.R418W]

Inomoto et al. (1987) purified a mutant prothrombin, prothrombin Tokushima, from the plasma of a proband with 12% of normal clotting activity and 42% of normal prothrombin antigen. Thrombin Tokushima had a normal molecular weight but less than 22% of normal clotting activity, a kcat/Km for a synthetic substrate less than one-tenth of normal, and reduced effectiveness in inducing platelet aggregation and in forming a complex with antithrombin III.

In the proband with prothrombin Tokushima, Iwahana et al. (1992) found a C-to-T change at nucleotide 9490 of the F2 gene, resulting in an arg418-to-trp (R418W) substitution. The mutant gene was inherited from the proband's mother and could be detected by PCR-SSCP and by the loss of an MspI site on PCR-RFLP. In a second report, Iwahana et al. (1992) found that the proband was a compound heterozygote for this dysprothrombinemia mutation and a 1-bp insertion causing hypoprothrombinemia (4177insT), which was inherited from the father. ⚠︎

**.0004 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, PROTHROMBIN MAGDEBURG

Lutze et al. (1989) described prothrombin Magdeburg in 7 heterozygous members of a family spanning 3 generations. Affected members had a ratio of factor II clotting activity to factor II concentration of about 0.5, and 5 of the 7 had a slightly increased bleeding tendency, particularly after trauma and surgery. SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis showed 2 abnormal prothrombins with higher molecular weights and lower isoelectric points, in addition to normal prothrombin.

**.0005 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, 1-BP INS, 4177T [c.4177inst]

In the proband with prothrombin Tokushima, Iwahana et al. (1992) identified a 1-bp insertion of T at position 4177 in exon 6 of the F2 gene, in compound heterozygosity with the R418W dysprothrombinemia mutation. The insertion caused a frameshift altering the amino acid sequence from codon 114 and created a premature stop codon (TGA) at codon 174 in exon 7. The authors concluded that this leads to a null prothrombin phenotype. Inheritance of the allele from the father was shown by PCR-SSCP with endonuclease digestion and by mutated primer-mediated PCR-RFLP. ⚠︎

**.0006 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, MET337THR [c.8751T>C; p.M337T]

Morishita et al. (1992) found that a woman with prothrombin Himi was compound heterozygous for 2 mutations in exon 10 of the F2 gene. One was a T-to-C transition at position 8751, resulting in a met337-to-thr (M337T) substitution (thrombin Himi I). The other was the R388H mutation. M337T was inherited from her father, and a sister also carried both mutations. Prothrombin Himi showed reduced fibrinogen clotting activity but retained full hydrolytic activity toward synthetic substrates.

**.0007 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, ARG388HIS [c.8904G>A; p.R388H]

In the proband with prothrombin Himi, Morishita et al. (1992) identified a G-to-A transition at position 8904 of the F2 gene, resulting in an arg388-to-his (R388H) substitution (thrombin Himi II), in compound heterozygosity with the M337T mutation. R388H was inherited from the proband's mother, and a sister of the proband also carried both mutations.

**.0008 HYPOPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, TYR44CYS [p.Y44; p.Y44C]

In a man with a severe bleeding tendency and hypoprothrombinemia (factor II activity 2%, factor II antigen 5%), Poort et al. (1994) identified a homozygous A-to-G substitution in exon 3 of the F2 gene, predicting a tyr44-to-cys (Y44C) substitution (TAC to TGC). Both parents were heterozygous. The mutation cosegregated completely with prothrombin deficiency in the family. Only the 5 sibs who were also homozygous were clinically affected, with severe hemorrhages including epistaxis and soft tissue, muscle, and joint bleeding.

**.0009 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, GLU300LYS [p.E300K]

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found that the original patient with prothrombin Denver was compound heterozygous for 2 glu-to-lys point mutations in the F2 gene, glu300-to-lys (E300K) and glu309-to-lys (E309K). The patient had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder treated with weekly prophylactic factor replacement, with factor II activity of 5 units/dl and factor II antigen of 21 units/dl. Plasma assays suggested that the functional defect lies in the activation of the zymogen to the enzyme.

**.0010 DYSPROTHROMBINEMIA**  
F2, GLU309LYS [p.E309K]

Lefkowitz et al. (2000) found that the original patient with prothrombin Denver, who had a severe hemophilia-like bleeding disorder, was compound heterozygous for glu309-to-lys (E309K) and glu300-to-lys (E300K) mutations in the F2 gene.

**.0011 PROTHROMBIN DEFICIENCY, CONGENITAL**  
F2, ARG-1GLN

Akhavan et al. (2000) identified an arg-1-to-gln substitution in the propeptide of prothrombin as 1 of 9 novel F2 mutations in a study of 13 patients with prothrombin deficiency.
