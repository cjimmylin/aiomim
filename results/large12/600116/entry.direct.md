### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive juvenile Parkinson disease-2 (PARK2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding parkin (PRKN) on chromosome 6q25.2-q27.

### Description

Autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (AR-JP) is a parkinsonism that responds to levodopa. Pathologically it shows highly selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra (Matsumine et al., 1997). In Japanese families, onset was early, with a mean age at onset of 27.8 years, progression was slow, and parkinsonian symptoms improved after sleep. Foot dystonia and hyperreflexia were common, and levodopa-induced dyskinesias and wearing-off occurred frequently (Ishikawa and Tsuji, 1996). Kitada et al. (1998) showed that the disorder is caused by mutations in the gene encoding parkin, and mutations in this gene were later found in patients whose phenotype resembled typical idiopathic Parkinson disease (Klein et al., 2000; Poorkaj et al., 2004).

### Nomenclature

Matsumine et al. (1998) concluded that early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF) and autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (ARJP) are allelic disorders. They proposed that both belong to the spectrum of a single clinicogenetic entity, chromosome 6q-linked parkinsonism. Kitada et al. (1998) named the protein product of the causative gene 'Parkin.'

### Clinical Features

Yamamura et al. (1973) described paralysis agitans of early onset with marked diurnal fluctuation of symptoms.

Takahashi et al. (1994) described a family in which the parents were first cousins and 4 of 5 sibs developed parkinsonism at an early age. The parkinsonism improved after sleep. One sib, a 67-year-old woman, came to autopsy. Pathologic changes were confined to the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNPC) and the locus ceruleus. The medial and ventrolateral regions of the SNPC showed obvious neuronal loss and gliosis. In the rest of the SNPC and in the locus ceruleus, neurons were reduced in number and most had low melanin content, but there was no detectable gliosis or extraneuronal free melanin pigment. There were no Lewy bodies, and the authors considered the overall pathologic picture different from that of Lewy body Parkinson disease.

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) described 17 patients from 12 Japanese families with familial juvenile parkinsonism suggesting autosomal recessive inheritance. Affected individuals were predominantly female, the mean age at onset was 27.8 years, and progression was slow. The parkinsonian triad of tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia was mild. Gait freezing, hyperreflexia, foot dystonia, and retropulsion were relatively prominent. In all patients, parkinsonian symptoms improved after sleep. The response to levodopa was satisfactory, but dopa-induced choreic limb dyskinesia and the wearing-off phenomenon were frequent. The authors concluded that AR-JP is a distinct clinical entity.

Matsumine et al. (1998) described early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF) as a dopa-responsive parkinsonism characterized by early onset and improvement of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep. In 1 case studied pathologically, there was selective neuronal degeneration in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra without Lewy body formation. They suggested that the disease could be characterized by a benign clinical course. ⚠︎

Tassin et al. (1998) studied 1 Algerian family and 10 European multiplex families with early-onset Parkinson disease. The clinical spectrum in the linked families was broader than previously reported: age at onset ranged up to 58 years, and some patients had painful dystonia.

Mori et al. (1998) carried out pathologic and biochemical studies in a patient with 6q-linked AR-JP. Neuronal loss and gliosis were restricted to the substantia nigra and the locus ceruleus. There were no Lewy bodies, but neurofibrillary tangles and argyrophilic astrocytes were present in the cerebral cortex and brainstem nuclei. Because these findings had not been reported in AR-JP before, the authors suggested that 6q-linked AR-JP is pathologically heterogeneous.

Klein et al. (2000) studied a large pedigree from South Tyrol in northern Italy with adult-onset, clinically typical, tremor-dominant parkinsonism of apparently autosomal dominant inheritance. The 4 affected brothers could not be distinguished clinically from most patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease. None of them showed the clinical hallmarks described in earlier patients with parkin mutations: diurnal fluctuations, benefit from sleep, foot dystonia, hyperreflexia, or early susceptibility to levodopa-induced dyskinesias.

Hayashi et al. (2000) reported a 70-year-old man with AR-JP who developed a dystonic gait at age 32 years. Hand tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and impaired postural reflexes followed, and levodopa improved these symptoms. At autopsy there was loss of pigmented neurons and gliosis in the SNPC, most pronounced in the medial and ventrolateral regions, and in the locus ceruleus. The remaining SNPC neurons had low melanin content, a feature that was less marked in the locus ceruleus. There were no Lewy bodies, as confirmed by alpha-synuclein immunostaining. The authors also found neuronal loss and fibrillary gliosis in the substantia nigra pars reticulata, which had not been reported in AR-JP before.

van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) reported a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism and parkin mutations. Parkinsonism was the main clinical syndrome, but the proband also had mild gait ataxia. Pathologically, the proband had neuronal loss in parts of the spinocerebellar system in addition to selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SNPC. Lewy bodies and neurofibrillary tangles were absent, but tau pathology was present.

Portman et al. (2001) used PET to measure nigrostriatal dopaminergic function and cerebral energy metabolism in 2 brothers with early-onset parkinsonism caused by parkin mutation. Energy metabolism did not differ, but the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pattern was clearly different from that of sporadic Parkinson disease. The authors concluded that the parkinsonism in these patients was pathophysiologically different from Parkinson disease. ⚠︎

Farrer et al. (2001) reported 2 families with parkin mutations. At autopsy, the proband of the smaller kindred, who was compound heterozygous for parkin mutations, had Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease. The authors suggested that compound heterozygous parkin mutations and loss of parkin protein may lead to early-onset parkinsonism with Lewy body pathology.

Sasaki et al. (2004) reported an autopsy of a patient with AR-JP who was homozygous for an exon 3 deletion in the parkin gene. Brain lesions were limited to the dopaminergic neuron system, including the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Melanin-containing neurons in the pars compacta of the substantia nigra were moderately to severely depleted, with gliosis. Neurons in the locus ceruleus were mildly decreased and extraneuronal melanin pigment was present. There were no Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra pars compacta or the locus ceruleus. However, occasional basophilic inclusion bodies were found in the neuropils of the pedunculopontine nucleus, and these were positive for alpha-synuclein and ubiquitin and had a doughnut or round shape. ⚠︎

Ohsawa et al. (2005) performed nerve conduction studies in 9 PARK2 patients and 8 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease. Sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude was significantly reduced in 8 PARK2 patients, most of whom had no symptoms of neuropathy. The authors suggested that sensory axonal neuropathy may be a common clinical feature of PARK2 and that a reduced sural SNAP amplitude could be a diagnostic indicator.

Pramstaller et al. (2005) reported a pedigree of 196 individuals spanning 7 generations, 25 of whom had levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. A 73-year-old patient with 2 mutant parkin alleles came to autopsy. There was Parkinson disease-type cell loss, reactive gliosis, and alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Surviving neurons reacted with antibodies to the N terminus of parkin but not with antibodies to the In-Between-RING (IBR) domain, which both mutations had deleted.

Chien et al. (2006) studied a genetic isolate from northeastern Brazil. Genealogic studies connected 255 individuals, 15 of whom had Parkinson disease, and there were multiple consanguineous marriages. In the 10 early-onset patients examined, the average age at onset of symptoms was 30.8 years (range, 12 to 46 years).

Alcalay et al. (2011) administered the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) to 44 probands with Parkinson disease onset at or before age 50 years and to 80 of their family members. Among probands, those with 2 parkin mutations had higher UPSIT scores (31.9) than heterozygotes (20.1) or noncarriers (19.9) (p less than 0.001). Among relatives without Parkinson disease, UPSIT performance was similar in heterozygotes (32.5) and noncarriers (32.4). The authors concluded that patients with 2 parkin mutations have olfaction within the normal range.

### Inheritance

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) strongly suspected autosomal recessive inheritance for juvenile parkinsonism in 12 Japanese families. The parents were consanguineous in all but 1 family, and all patients belonged to a single generation. Matsumine et al. (1998) found that 8 of 17 families with early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation were nonconsanguineous.

Klein et al. (2000) described a pedigree with apparently autosomal dominant parkinsonism. The 4 affected brothers were compound heterozygous for parkin deletions, and 2 affected females carried only 1 of the deletions.

Lücking et al. (2001) reported an Italian family with pseudodominant inheritance of parkinsonism due to parkin mutations. They noted that pseudodominant inheritance should be considered in early-onset parkinsonism.

### Mapping

Matsumine et al. (1997) used a diallelic polymorphism of the manganese superoxide dismutase gene (SOD2) for linkage analysis. They found a family with AR-JP in which the disease segregated perfectly with the SOD2 locus. Extending the analysis to 13 families, they obtained strong evidence for localization of the AR-JP gene to chromosome 6q25.2-q27. Maximum cumulative pairwise lod scores were 7.26 at D6S305 (theta = 0.03) and 7.71 at D6S253 (theta = 0.02). Obligate recombination events and multipoint analysis placed the gene in a 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S264.

Matsumine et al. (1998) studied 53 members of 17 families with early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation and analyzed 151 meioses. Multipoint linkage analysis gave a peak lod score of 14.2 at 1.0 cM telomeric to D6S305 and placed the disease locus in the 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S253, the same position mapped for ARJP. The highest 2-point lod score was 10.13 at D6S305 (theta = 0). Because many families were nonconsanguineous, no haplotypes were commonly shared, and the families came from widespread geographic areas, the authors suggested that there are multiple founder mutations.

Saito et al. (1998) refined the AR-JP locus using densely placed markers. The highest cumulative maximum pairwise lod score was 9.13 at D6S1579 (theta = 0.05), and the highest multipoint lod score was 12.4 at 3 cM telomeric to D6S1599. Obligate recombinations narrowed the candidate region to 13 cM between D6S1579 and D6S264. Markers D6S1579 and D6S1599 showed strong linkage disequilibrium with the AR-JP locus, suggesting that the gene most likely lies near the 4-cM region encompassing these 2 markers.

Tassin et al. (1998) tested 1 Algerian and 10 European multiplex families with early-onset Parkinson disease for linkage to D6S305. Eight families had a conditional probability of linkage greater than 0.9. Haplotype reconstruction and the smallest region of homozygosity in 2 consanguineous families reduced the candidate interval to 11.3 cM. In the Algerian family, 2 microsatellite markers (D6S411 and D6S1550) appeared deleted and segregated with the disease; taking this into account, the candidate region was less than 1 cM. The authors concluded that PARK2 is an important locus for AR-JP in European patients.

Jones et al. (1998) found linkage to 6q25.2-q27 in 15 families from 4 distinct ethnic backgrounds, and a full genomic screen excluded other candidate regions. Recombination events restricted the juvenile parkinsonism locus to a 6.9-cM region and excluded SOD2. In 1 family, apparent homozygosity for null alleles at D6S955 suggested a deletion.

### Heterogeneity

Mitsui et al. (1994) reported 2 sibs with hereditary parkinsonism, pyramidal signs, and cerebellar ataxia. Anticholinergics had a dramatic beneficial effect, but levodopa did not. The authors proposed that this was a new hereditary variant of early-onset Parkinson disease, distinct from the levodopa-sensitive forms of juvenile Parkinson disease.

Ishikawa and Miyatake (1995) reported a family with autosomal dominant hereditary juvenile dystonia-parkinsonism. Eight members in 3 generations had parkinsonism, sleep benefit, marked efficacy of levodopa, wearing-off phenomenon, and dopa-induced choreic dyskinesia. One case had mainly dystonic movements that worsened with levodopa. Patients also had neck dystonia, such as torticollis and retrocollis, foot dystonia, and other dystonic movements.

### Molecular Genetics

Kitada et al. (1998) used positional cloning within a microdeletion involving D6S305 in a Japanese patient with AR-JP to isolate the parkin gene. The gene spans more than 500 kb and has 12 exons. In this patient, exons 3 through 7 were deleted. Four other patients with AR-JP from 3 unrelated families had a deletion of exon 4 alone. A 4.5-kb parkin transcript was shorter in brain tissue from one group of patients with the exon 4 deletion.

In the South Tyrolean pedigree with adult-onset, tremor-dominant parkinsonism, Klein et al. (2000) identified compound heterozygous deletions in the parkin gene, 1 large and 1 truncating, in 4 affected brothers. Two affected females carried only the truncating deletion together with an apparently normal allele.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found that a patient with AR-JP studied at autopsy and his sibs were homozygous for a deletion of exon 4 of the parkin gene.

van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) found a heterozygous missense mutation together with a heterozygous exon deletion in the parkin gene in a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism.

Hedrich et al. (2001) studied 21 patients with early-onset parkinsonism. Conventional screening by SSCP and sequencing detected 3 mutations: a single base pair substitution in exon 3 and small deletions in exon 9. Quantitative duplex PCR detected gene dosage alterations in 7 patients. These included heterozygous and compound heterozygous deletions of exons 2, 3, 5, and 7, the exon 7 deletion also in the homozygous state, as well as 2 heterozygous duplications of exon 4. Two patients carried more than 2 parkin mutations. The authors concluded that mutation analysis of the parkin gene should include gene dosage studies.

In an Italian family with pseudodominant inheritance, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father, whose disease began at age 57 years, was homozygous for a triplication of exon 2 of the parkin gene. The unaffected mother was heterozygous for a deletion of exons 3 and 4. Their son, whose disease began at age 31 years, was compound heterozygous for both mutations.

Farrer et al. (2001) sequenced the parkin gene in 38 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before age 41 years) and followed up 2 probands with mutations. Haplotype analysis showed a common chromosome 6 carrying a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3, which cosegregated with disease. The proband of the smaller kindred also carried an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7, and RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on different transcripts. The authors suggested that a single heterozygous mutation may confer increased susceptibility to typical Parkinson disease. ⚠︎

Hoenicka et al. (2002) studied 19 Spanish kindreds with familial Parkinson disease. They found 5 different parkin mutations in 5 of the 13 families with recessive inheritance: 2 novel mutations, V56E and C212Y, and deletion of exons 3 and 5 and 225delA, which had been described in other ethnic groups. Heterozygous carriers of a single parkin mutation were either asymptomatic or developed symptoms in late adulthood or after brief exposure to haloperidol. The authors estimated that parkin mutations account for 38% of families with recessive parkinsonism in Spain.

Foroud et al. (2003) screened the parkin gene by quantitative PCR and exon sequencing in families with at least 2 sibs with Parkinson disease. They identified 25 different mutations in 103 individuals from 47 families. Forty-one individuals had mutations on both alleles and 62 had a single mutation. Thirty-five subjects (34%) with a parkin mutation had onset at age 60 years or older, and 30 of these 35 (86%) had a mutation detected on only 1 allele. Individuals with 2, 1, or no mutated copies differed little clinically. ⚠︎

Oliveira et al. (2003) analyzed the parkin gene in 174 multiplex families from a genomic screen and 133 additional families with Parkinson disease. Mutations were found in 18% of early-onset families and 2% of late-onset families, and age at onset in patients with parkin mutations ranged from 12 to 71 years. Excluding exon 7 mutations, the mean age at onset was 31.5 years. The exon 7 mutations, in the first RING finger (cys253-to-trp, arg256-to-cys, arg275-to-trp, and asp280-to-asn), were found mainly in heterozygous patients with a later mean age at onset of 49.2 years and were absent from controls. The authors suggested that heterozygous mutations, especially in exon 7, act as susceptibility alleles for late-onset Parkinson disease.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) analyzed all 12 exons of parkin by sequencing and dosage analysis in 39 clinic patients with Parkinson disease onset at or before age 40 years. Mutations were found in 7 patients: 2 were compound heterozygous and 5 were heterozygous. The mutations included deletions of exons 2, 3, and 8, duplications of exons 2 through 4 and of exon 9, and a P437L substitution, and 78% were deletions or multiplications. Phenotypes in mutation carriers were consistent with idiopathic Parkinson disease. The authors considered it unclear whether heterozygous mutations are pathogenic.

Sasaki et al. (2004) found a homozygous deletion of exon 3 of the parkin gene in a patient with AR-JP who had alpha-synuclein-positive inclusions at autopsy.

In a 7-generation pedigree, Pramstaller et al. (2005) identified parkin mutations in 77 individuals. Of the 25 patients with parkinsonism, 5 were compound heterozygous and met criteria for definite Parkinson disease, 8 carried only a heterozygous mutation, 7 had no parkin mutation, and the mutation status of 5 deceased patients was unknown. Age at onset in compound heterozygotes differed significantly from that in heterozygous carriers and in subjects without detectable mutations. A patient studied at autopsy carried a deletion of exon 7 and del1072T.

In a Brazilian genetic isolate, Chien et al. (2006) found that patients with Parkinson disease were homozygous for markers across the PARK2 locus. Sequencing identified a novel homozygous splice site mutation in the parkin gene (IVS1+1G-T) that cosegregated completely with early-onset Parkinson disease. cDNA analysis showed total loss of parkin transcript in homozygous carriers. One relative with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism and 13 of 15 healthy relatives were heterozygous carriers. The authors concluded that the mutation is truly recessive and suggested that parkin haploinsufficiency is not a relevant risk factor for early- or late-onset Parkinson disease, although it could facilitate neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism.

Kay et al. (2007) sequenced the parkin gene in 302 patients and 301 controls and replicated the findings in 1,260 additional patients and 1,657 controls. They detected 34 variants, of which 12 were polymorphisms and 22 were rare variants. Patients and controls did not differ in the frequency, type, or functional location of the variants. Even P437L, a common mutation thought to be pathogenic, was found in unaffected controls. The authors concluded that a single point mutation in a patient without a second mutation should not be considered the cause of disease unless supported by family data and functional studies.

Wang et al. (2008) used the kin-cohort method to estimate penetrance. Parkin mutations were identified in 25 of 247 probands with Parkinson disease onset at or before age 50 years (10.1%), and 18 (72.0%) of these were heterozygotes. The estimated cumulative incidence of Parkinson disease to age 65 years was 7.0% (95% CI, 0.4% to 71.9%) in carrier relatives, compared with 1.7% in noncarrier relatives of cases (p = 0.59).

Kay et al. (2010) tested 1,686 controls and 2,091 patients with Parkinson disease for parkin deletions, multiplications, and copy number variations (CNVs). Heterozygous CNV mutations were found in 0.95% of controls and 0.86% of patients. All CNVs in the 16 controls involved exons 1 to 4, sparing exons that encode functionally critical domains, whereas mutations in patients spanned exons 2 to 9. Thirteen patients had 2 CNV mutations and 5 had 1 CNV plus 1 point mutation. In whites, a single CNV was not associated with increased risk (odds ratio 1.05, p = 0.89) or with earlier onset of Parkinson disease.

Zhu et al. (2022) evaluated PRKN mutations in the NIH-PD + AMP-PD cohort (4,094 participants with whole-genome sequencing) and in the UK Biobank. In the UK Biobank, they identified 2,692 PRKN copy number variants; deletions or duplications involving exon 2 accounted for roughly half, and 88% involved exons 2, 3, or 4. A pathogenic PRKN mutation was present in 1.8% of UK Biobank participants and 2 mutations in about 1 in 7,800. Participants with 1 pathogenic variant were as likely as noncarriers to have Parkinson disease (odds ratio 0.91, p = 0.76) or a parent with the disease (odds ratio 1.12, p = 0.19), with similar findings in the NIH-PD + AMP-PD cohort (odds ratio 1.29, p = 0.43). In the NIH-PD cohort, functional assays of patient fibroblasts resolved variants of unclear significance in biallelic carriers. The authors concluded that heterozygous pathogenic PRKN mutations are common but do not increase the risk of Parkinson disease.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Lohmann et al. (2003) compared 146 patients with early-onset parkinsonism who had parkin mutations with 250 patients without mutations. Patients with mutations had significantly earlier and more symmetric onset, more frequent dystonia at onset and hyperreflexia, slower progression, and a tendency toward a greater response to lower doses of levodopa. After multivariate analysis, dystonia at onset and brisk reflexes correlated with age at onset rather than with mutation status. The younger the age at onset, the slower the disease progressed. Carriers of at least 1 missense mutation had higher UPDRS motor scores than carriers of 2 truncating mutations, and missense mutations in functional domains of parkin were associated with earlier onset. Compared with patients with 2 mutations, patients with a single heterozygous mutation had significantly later and more asymmetric onset and more frequent levodopa-induced fluctuations and dystonia.

Khan et al. (2005) used 18F-dopa PET to study 13 unrelated asymptomatic parkin heterozygotes. Compared with controls, they had significantly reduced uptake in the caudate, putamen, and ventral and dorsal midbrain, and 4 had subtle extrapyramidal signs. The authors concluded that parkin heterozygosity is a risk factor for nigrostriatal dysfunction.

### Pathogenesis

Shimura et al. (2001) identified a protein complex in normal human brain made up of parkin as the E3 ubiquitin ligase, UbcH7 as the associated E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme, and a 22-kD glycosylated form of alpha-synuclein (alphaSp22) as the substrate. Normal parkin bound alphaSp22, but mutant parkin associated with autosomal recessive Parkinson disease did not. In an in vitro assay, normal parkin modified alphaSp22 into polyubiquitinated high molecular weight species, but mutant parkin did not. AlphaSp22 accumulated in a nonubiquitinated form in parkin-deficient brains with Parkinson disease.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from 5 patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous parkin mutations. Compared with cells from 6 controls, mitochondrial membrane potential was lower by an average of 30%, falling by an average of 70% in galactose medium. Complex I activity was lower by an average of 42%, complex I-linked ATP production was reduced, and cellular ATP levels were lower by 58%. Mutant cells also showed increased mitochondrial branching, which correlated with lower complex I-linked ATP production. Parkin knockdown by siRNA in control fibroblasts reproduced these effects, but a 50% knockdown, mimicking haploinsufficiency, did not impair mitochondrial function or morphology. Parkin-mutant cells had reduced functional connectivity of the mitochondrial matrix, which worsened after exposure to rotenone. Treatment with the glutathione precursor oxothiazolidine 4-carboxylate restored mitochondrial membrane potential.

### Population Genetics

In the CORE-PD study of 956 patients with Parkinson disease onset before age 51 years, Marder et al. (2010) found parkin mutations in 64 (6.7%): 3.9% were heterozygous, 0.6% homozygous, and 2.2% compound heterozygous. Copy number variation was present in 52.3% of mutation carriers. Mutation prevalence declined with age at onset, from 57% (8 of 14) in patients with onset before age 20 years to 3% (20 of 644) in those with onset between 40 and 50 years. Younger age at onset, Hispanic ethnicity, and a family history of Parkinson disease in a first-degree relative were associated with carrying any parkin mutation. Deletions of exons 3 and 4 and 255delA were common among Hispanics, particularly Puerto Ricans, and both exon 3-4 deletion homozygotes were of Puerto Rican descent.

In 953 patients from the same study with early-onset Parkinson disease, Alcalay et al. (2010) found that 33 were PRKN heterozygotes and 27 were homozygotes or compound heterozygotes. Mean age at onset was 29 years in carriers of 2 PRKN mutations, 37 years in heterozygotes, and 42 years in noncarriers. Hispanic individuals were more likely than non-Hispanic individuals to be PRKN carriers (15.6% vs 5.9%, p = 0.003).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-7DEL

Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion of exons 3 through 7 of the parkin gene in a Japanese patient with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism. The deletion included marker D6S305 and was the microdeletion used for positional cloning of the gene.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX4DEL

Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion of exon 4 alone of the parkin gene in 4 patients with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism from 3 unrelated families. The parkin transcript was shorter in brain tissue from one group of these patients.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found a homozygous exon 4 deletion in a 70-year-old man with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism and in his sibs. The man's symptoms began at age 32 years. At autopsy there was loss of pigmented neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus ceruleus without Lewy bodies.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX2 TRIPLICATION

In an Italian family with pseudodominant inheritance, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father, whose disease began at age 57 years, was homozygous for a triplication of exon 2 of the parkin gene. His son, whose disease began at age 31 years, was compound heterozygous for the triplication and a deletion of exons 3 and 4 inherited from his unaffected mother.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 40-BP DEL, EX3

Farrer et al. (2001) identified a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3 of the parkin gene on a common chromosome 6 haplotype in 2 families. The deletion cosegregated with disease. In the proband of the smaller kindred, it was present in compound heterozygosity with R275W, and the 2 mutations were on different transcripts. At autopsy this proband had Lewy body pathology.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, ARG275TRP [R275W]

Farrer et al. (2001) found an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7 of the parkin gene, together with a 40-bp deletion in exon 3, in a proband with early-onset parkinsonism. RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on different transcripts, and at autopsy the proband had Lewy body pathology.

Oliveira et al. (2003) observed the arg275-to-trp mutation, along with other exon 7 RING finger mutations, mainly in heterozygous patients with later-onset Parkinson disease (mean age at onset, 49.2 years). These mutations were not found in controls.

**.0006 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, VAL56GLU [V56E]

Hoenicka et al. (2002) reported a novel V56E mutation in the parkin gene in a Spanish family with recessive familial Parkinson disease. The abstract does not state the zygosity.

**.0007 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, CYS212TYR [C212Y]

Hoenicka et al. (2002) reported a novel C212Y mutation in the parkin gene in a Spanish family with recessive familial Parkinson disease. The abstract does not state the zygosity.

**.0008 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3DEL

Sasaki et al. (2004) reported a patient with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism who was homozygous for a deletion of exon 3 of the parkin gene. At autopsy, degeneration was limited to the dopaminergic system without Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra or locus ceruleus. However, alpha-synuclein- and ubiquitin-positive inclusions were present in the pedunculopontine nucleus.

**.0009 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 1-BP DEL, 1072T [del1072T]

In a large 7-generation pedigree with parkin mutations, Pramstaller et al. (2005) studied a 73-year-old patient at autopsy. The patient carried a deletion of exon 7 and a 1-bp deletion (del1072T) in the parkin gene, both of which deleted the IBR domain. The autopsy showed alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus.

**.0010 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, IVS1DS, G-T, +1 [IVS1+1G/T]

Chien et al. (2006) studied a genetic isolate from northeastern Brazil with early-onset Parkinson disease (average age at onset 30.8 years) and multiple consanguineous marriages. Affected individuals were homozygous for a splice site mutation in the parkin gene (IVS1+1G-T), which cosegregated completely with the phenotype. cDNA analysis showed total loss of parkin transcript in homozygous carriers. Heterozygous carriers included 13 of 15 healthy relatives and 1 relative with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism.
