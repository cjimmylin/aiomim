### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive juvenile Parkinson disease-2 (PARK2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PRKN gene (602544) on chromosome 6q26. ⚠︎

### Description

Matsumine et al. (1997) described autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (AR-JP) as a levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. Its pathology is a highly selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra. Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) noted early onset, slow progression, a satisfactory response to levodopa, and improvement of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep, and concluded that AR-JP is a distinct clinical entity.

### Nomenclature

Matsumine et al. (1998) concluded that early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF) and ARJP are allelic disorders. They proposed that both belong to the spectrum of a single clinicogenetic entity, chromosome 6q-linked parkinsonism.

### Clinical Features

Yamamura et al. (1973) described paralysis agitans (parkinsonism) of early onset with marked diurnal fluctuation of symptoms.

Takahashi et al. (1994) reported a family in which 4 of 5 sibs, whose parents were first cousins, developed parkinsonism with a similar onset at an early age. Parkinsonism improved after sleep. One affected sib, a woman, was autopsied at age 67 years (see PATHOGENESIS).

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) described 17 patients from 12 Japanese families with familial juvenile parkinsonism suggesting autosomal recessive inheritance. Females predominated, and the mean age at onset was 27.8 years. The parkinsonian triad of tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia was mild, but gait freezing, hyperreflexia, foot dystonia, and retropulsion were relatively prominent. All patients showed improvement of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep. Progression was slow and response to levodopa was satisfactory, but dopa-induced choreic limb dyskinesia and wearing-off phenomenon occurred frequently. The authors concluded that the similarity of clinical findings among these patients, and their differences from other types of parkinsonism, indicated that AR-JP is a distinct clinical entity.

Matsumine et al. (1998) examined 53 members of 17 families with early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF), a dopa-responsive parkinsonism characterized by early onset and improvement of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep. They noted that a pathologic study of one EPDF case had shown selective neuronal degeneration in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra without Lewy body formation, and suggested that the disease could be characterized by a benign clinical course.

Saito et al. (1998) described autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism as a hereditary neurodegenerative disorder with levodopa-responsive parkinsonism; the mean age at onset was 23.2 years.

In European families and an Algerian family with early-onset parkinsonism linked to the PARK2 locus, Tassin et al. (1998) found a clinical spectrum broader than previously reported: age at onset ranged up to 58 years, and some patients had painful dystonia.

Klein et al. (2000) reported a large pedigree from South Tyrol in northern Italy with adult-onset, clinically typical tremor-dominant parkinsonism of apparently autosomal dominant inheritance. Four affected male sibs with compound heterozygous parkin deletions displayed none of the clinical hallmarks previously described in parkin patients, including diurnal fluctuations, benefit from sleep, foot dystonia, hyperreflexia, and early susceptibility to levodopa-induced dyskinesias. Two affected females in the pedigree carried only 1 of the deletions.

Hayashi et al. (2000) reported a man with AR-JP who developed a dystonic gait at age 32 years, followed by hand tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and impaired postural reflex. His symptoms responded to levodopa. He was autopsied at age 70 years (see PATHOGENESIS).

Van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) reported a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism. Besides parkinsonism, the proband had mild gait ataxia. Pathologically, the proband showed neuronal loss in parts of the spinocerebellar system in addition to selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta. Lewy bodies and neurofibrillary tangles were absent, but tau pathology was present.

Lohmann et al. (2003) compared 146 patients with early-onset parkinsonism who had parkin mutations with 250 patients without them. Patients with mutations had significantly earlier and more symmetrical onset, more frequent dystonia at onset and hyperreflexia, slower progression, and a tendency toward a greater response to levodopa despite lower doses. However, no single sign distinguished the groups. On multivariate analysis, dystonia at onset and brisk reflexes correlated with age at onset rather than with the presence of a parkin mutation, and the younger the age at onset, the slower the evolution of disease. ⚠︎

Ohsawa et al. (2005) performed nerve conduction studies in 9 PARK2 patients and 8 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease. Sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude was significantly reduced in 8 of the PARK2 patients, most of whom were asymptomatic. The authors suggested that sensory axonal neuropathy may be a common clinical feature of PARK2.

Pramstaller et al. (2005) described a 7-generation pedigree of 196 individuals, 25 of whom had levodopa-responsive parkinsonism; Parkin mutations were found in 77 subjects. Of the 25 patients, 5 were compound heterozygotes who met UK PD Society Brain Bank criteria for definite Parkinson disease, 8 carried a single heterozygous mutation, 7 had no Parkin mutation, and the status of 5 deceased patients was unknown.

Chien et al. (2006) studied a genetic isolate from north-eastern Brazil with geographic isolation and multiple consanguineous marriages. DNA was obtained from 26 members: 10 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease, 1 with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism, and 15 unaffected relatives. The average age at onset of PD symptoms was 30.8 years (range, 12 to 46 years).

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) found mean ages at onset of 28.5 years in 6 homozygous parkin mutation carriers, 28.9 years in 21 compound heterozygotes, and 37.2 years in 37 heterozygotes. Dystonia as a presenting sign did not differ between parkin carriers (0%) and noncarriers (1.7%), and neither did response to levodopa (93.4% of carriers vs 90.5% of noncarriers).

Alcalay et al. (2010) reported a mean age at onset of 29 years in 27 PRKN homozygotes or compound heterozygotes, compared with 37 years in 33 PRKN heterozygotes, among patients with Parkinson disease onset at or before age 50 years.

Alcalay et al. (2011) gave the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) to 44 CORE-PD probands with PD onset at or before age 50 years: 9 Parkin compound heterozygotes, 10 Parkin heterozygotes, and 25 noncarriers. Compound heterozygotes scored higher (31.9) than heterozygotes (20.1) or noncarriers (19.9) (p less than 0.001), and the difference persisted after adjustment for age, gender, disease duration, and smoking. The authors concluded that compound heterozygotes with PD have olfaction within the normal range.

### Inheritance

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) strongly suspected autosomal recessive inheritance of juvenile parkinsonism in 12 Japanese families because the parents were consanguineous in all but one family and all patients belonged to a single generation. Takahashi et al. (1994) had reported affected sibs born to first-cousin parents.

Lücking et al. (2001) reported pseudo-dominant inheritance in an Italian family. An affected father homozygous for an exon 2 triplication and an unaffected mother heterozygous for exon 3-4 deletions had a compound heterozygous affected son. The authors concluded that pseudo-dominant inheritance of parkin mutations has to be considered in early-onset parkinsonism. Klein et al. (2000) also described a South Tyrolean pedigree with apparently autosomal dominant inheritance caused by parkin deletions.

Reports have disagreed about the role of heterozygous mutations. Poorkaj et al. (2004) noted that 70% of their parkin cases were heterozygous and that it was unclear whether heterozygous mutations are pathogenic; if parkin is recessive, only 5% of early-onset cases, those with compound mutations, could be attributed to this locus. Khan et al. (2005) concluded that although parkin disease is usually autosomal recessive, parkin heterozygosity is a risk factor for nigrostriatal dysfunction and may contribute to late-onset Parkinson disease in some carriers. In contrast, Chien et al. (2006) found no PD in heterozygous carriers in a Brazilian genetic isolate, indicating a genuinely recessive nature, although haploinsufficiency could facilitate neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism. Marder et al. (2010) found that heterozygous carriers had a younger age at onset than noncarriers after adjustment for ethnicity and family history, and suggested that parkin heterozygosity may increase susceptibility to PD rather than directly cause it. Alcalay et al. (2010) noted that PRKN carriers were more likely than LRRK2 carriers to report a family history of PD, and suggested that even a single PRKN mutation may be a risk factor for early-onset PD. Zhu et al. (2022) found that UK Biobank participants with 1 pathogenic PRKN variant were as likely as noncarriers to have Parkinson disease (OR = 0.91) or to have a parent with Parkinson disease (OR = 1.12), and that the same held in the NIH-PD + AMP-PD cohort (OR = 1.29). They concluded that heterozygous pathogenic PRKN mutations are common but do not increase the risk of Parkinson disease.

### Mapping

Matsumine et al. (1997) first found a family with AR-JP in which the disease segregated perfectly with a diallelic polymorphism of the Mn-superoxide dismutase gene (SOD2), and therefore extended linkage analysis to 13 families. They mapped the AR-JP gene to chromosome 6q25.2-q27. Maximal cumulative pairwise lod scores were 7.26 at D6S305 (theta = 0.03) and 7.71 at D6S253 (theta = 0.02). Obligate recombination events and multipoint analysis placed the gene in a 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S264.

Matsumine et al. (1998) studied 17 families with EPDF and analyzed 151 meioses. Multipoint linkage analysis gave a peak lod score of 14.2 at 1.0 cM telomeric to D6S305 and placed the locus in the 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S253, the same position as the ARJP locus. The highest 2-point lod score was 10.13 at theta = 0 for D6S305.

Saito et al. (1998) carried out detailed linkage analysis of AR-JP with densely spaced markers on 6q25.2-q27. The highest cumulative pairwise lod score was 9.13 at D6S1579 (theta = 0.05), and the highest multipoint lod score was 12.4 at a locus 3 cM telomeric to D6S1599. Obligate recombinations narrowed the candidate region to 13 cM between D6S1579 and D6S264. Strong linkage disequilibrium with D6S1579 and D6S1599 (P less than 0.0001 for both) suggested that the gene most likely lies near the 4-cM region spanning these markers.

Tassin et al. (1998) tested 1 Algerian and 10 European multiplex families with early-onset parkinsonism for linkage to 6q25.2-q27 using marker D6S305. Homogeneity analysis gave a conditional probability of linkage greater than 0.9 in 8 families. Haplotype reconstruction and the smallest region of homozygosity in 2 consanguineous families narrowed the candidate interval to 11.3 cM. In the Algerian family, apparent homozygous deletion of 2 colocalizing microsatellite markers, D6S411 and D6S1550, segregated with the disease; if taken into account, this deletion would reduce the candidate region to less than 1 cM.

Jones et al. (1998) found linkage of AR-JP to 6q25.2-q27 in 15 families from 4 distinct ethnic backgrounds, and a full genomic screen excluded other candidate regions. Recombination events restricted the locus to a 6.9-cM region. They excluded SOD2 as the juvenile parkinsonism gene by recombination. Apparent homozygosity for null alleles at D6S955 in one family suggested a deletion.

In the South Tyrolean pedigree with apparently dominant adult-onset parkinsonism, Klein et al. (2000) used haplotype analysis to exclude the autosomal dominant parkinsonism regions on chromosomes 2p, 4p, and 4q, and implicated the parkin locus on 6q instead.

Oliveira et al. (2003) had previously found linkage of idiopathic PD to the chromosome 6 region containing the Parkin gene (D6S305, lod score 5.47), in families with at least one member whose age at onset was younger than 40 years.

In a genetic isolate from north-eastern Brazil in which genealogical studies connected 255 individuals, 15 of them with PD, Chien et al. (2006) found that PD patients were homozygous for markers across the PARK2 locus.

### Heterogeneity

Mitsui et al. (1994) reported 2 sibs with hereditary parkinsonism with pyramidal signs and cerebellar ataxia in whom anticholinergics, but not levodopa, had a dramatic beneficial effect. They proposed this as a hereditary variant of early-onset Parkinson disease distinct from the levodopa-sensitive forms of juvenile Parkinson disease.

Ishikawa and Miyatake (1995) reported a family in which 8 members in 3 generations had autosomal dominant juvenile dystonia-parkinsonism with sleep benefit, marked levodopa response, wearing-off phenomenon, and dopa-induced choreic dyskinesia. The patients also had neck dystonia, such as torticollis and retrocollis, and foot dystonia; one patient had mainly dystonic movement that worsened with levodopa. The authors considered this a specific form of hereditary dystonia-parkinsonism.

Tassin et al. (1998) concluded that PARK2 appears to be an important locus for AR-JP in European patients.

### Molecular Genetics

Kitada et al. (1998) noted that marker D6S305, strongly linked to AR-JP, was deleted in a Japanese AR-JP patient. Using positional cloning within the microdeletion, they isolated a 2,960-bp cDNA whose 1,395-bp open reading frame encodes a 465-amino acid protein, which they named parkin (PRKN). The gene spans more than 500 kb and has 12 exons, 5 of which (exons 3-7) were deleted in the patient. Four other AR-JP patients from 3 unrelated families had a deletion affecting exon 4 alone. The 4.5-kb parkin transcript was shorter in brain tissue from one group of exon 4-deleted patients.

In 4 affected male sibs from the South Tyrolean pedigree with adult-onset tremor-dominant parkinsonism, Klein et al. (2000) identified compound heterozygous deletions in the PRKN gene, one large and one truncating. Two affected females carried only the truncating deletion, with an apparently normal second allele.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found that a patient with AR-JP who was autopsied at age 70 years and his sibs had a homozygous deletion of exon 4 of the PRKN gene.

In a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism, van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) found a heterozygous missense mutation combined with a heterozygous exon deletion in the PRKN gene.

Hedrich et al. (2001) screened 21 patients with early-onset parkinsonism. Conventional mutation screening (SSCP and sequencing) found 3 mutations: a single-basepair substitution in exon 3 and small deletions in exon 9. Quantitative duplex PCR found gene dosage changes in 7 patients: heterozygous and compound heterozygous deletions of exons 2, 3, 5, and 7, a homozygous exon 7 deletion, and 2 heterozygous duplications of exon 4. Two patients carried more than 2 parkin mutations. ⚠︎

In an Italian family, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father (onset at age 57 years) was homozygous for a triplication of PRKN exon 2, the unaffected mother was heterozygous for deletions of exons 3 and 4, and their affected son (onset at age 31 years) was a compound heterozygote carrying both mutations.

Farrer et al. (2001) sequenced the PRKN gene in 38 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before age 41 years) and studied the families of 2 probands with mutations. Haplotype analysis showed a common chromosome 6 carrying a novel 40-bp exon 3 deletion that cosegregated with disease. In the proband of the smaller kindred, an exon 7 R275W substitution was also found, and RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on alternate transcripts.

Hoenicka et al. (2002) studied 19 Spanish kindreds with familial Parkinson disease (13 recessive, 4 dominant, 2 uncertain) and found 5 different PRKN mutations in 5 of the 13 recessive families: the novel missense mutations V56E and C212Y, and deletions of exons 3 and 5 and 225delA, which had been described in other ethnic groups. They concluded that PRKN mutations accounted for 38% of families with recessive parkinsonism in Spain. Two carriers of a single heterozygous mutation developed symptoms in late adulthood or after brief exposure to haloperidol, and the authors suggested that heterozygous mutations could be a risk factor for parkinsonism.

Foroud et al. (2003) screened the PRKN gene, by quantitative PCR and exon sequencing, in families with at least 2 sibs with PD who had early-onset disease or a positive lod score at an intron 7 marker. They identified 25 different mutations in 103 individuals from 47 families: 41 had mutations in both alleles and 62 had a single mutation.

Oliveira et al. (2003) screened 174 multiplex PD families and 133 additional PD families and found PRKN mutations in 18% of early-onset and 2% of late-onset families (5% overall). Age at onset of mutation carriers ranged from 12 to 71 years. Mutations in exon 7, which encodes the first RING finger (C253W, R256C, R275W, and D280N), were seen mainly in heterozygous patients.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) found PRKN mutations in 7 of 39 movement-disorder clinic patients with PD onset at or before age 40 years; 2 were compound heterozygous and 5 were heterozygous. The mutations included deletions of exons 2, 3, and 8, duplications of exons 2-4 and exon 9, and the P437L substitution; 78% of the mutations were deletions or multiplications. A novel R402W substitution was found in 1 patient and 1 control, but none of the other point mutations was present in 96 controls. ⚠︎

Sasaki et al. (2004) found a homozygous exon 3 deletion in the PRKN gene in an autopsied patient with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism.

In the 7-generation pedigree reported by Pramstaller et al. (2005), a 73-year-old autopsied patient carried 2 mutant PRKN alleles, an exon 7 deletion and del1072T. Four other affected relatives were also compound heterozygotes, and 8 carried a single heterozygous mutation. ⚠︎

In the north-eastern Brazilian genetic isolate, Chien et al. (2006) identified by genomic sequencing a novel homozygous splice site mutation in the PRKN gene (IVS1+1G-T) that completely cosegregated with early-onset PD. Thirteen of the 15 healthy relatives and the patient with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism were heterozygous carriers. cDNA analysis showed total loss of parkin transcript in homozygous carriers, defining the mutation as loss of function.

Kay et al. (2007) sequenced the PRKN gene in 302 patients with Parkinson disease and 301 controls and replicated the findings in 1,260 more patients and 1,657 more controls. They found 34 variants, 21 of them novel (12 polymorphisms and 22 rare variants), and patients and controls did not differ in the frequency, type, or functional location of the variants. P437L, a common parkin mutation thought to be pathogenic, was present in unaffected controls. The authors concluded that a single heterozygous point mutation in a patient, without a second mutation, should not be taken as the cause of disease unless family data and functional studies support it.

Wang et al. (2008) screened 247 probands with PD onset at 50 years or younger for PRKN point mutations and exon deletions and duplications. Mutations were found in 25 probands (10.1%), 18 of whom (72.0%) were heterozygotes; 1 homozygote was reported in 2 sibs with PD. Using the kin-cohort method, they estimated the cumulative incidence of PD to age 65 years at 7.0% (95% CI, 0.4%-71.9%) in carrier relatives, compared with 1.7% in noncarrier relatives of cases (P = 0.59) and 1.1% in relatives of controls.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from 5 patients with early-onset parkinsonism and homozygous or compound heterozygous PRKN mutations: 255delA/exon 5 deletion (patient 1), homozygous 202_203delAG (patient 2), 202_203delAG/exon 2 deletion (patient 3), 202_203delAG/exon 4 deletion (patient 4), and c.101_102delAG/G430D (patient 5) (see PATHOGENESIS).

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) screened 956 patients with early-onset PD (onset before 51 years) from 13 centers and found PRKN mutations in 64 (6.7%): 3.9% heterozygous, 0.6% homozygous, and 2.2% compound heterozygous. The 6 homozygotes (5 male, 1 female; 4 white non-Hispanic and 2 Puerto Rican Hispanic) carried 255delA, the exon 3 40-bp deletion, an exon 3-4 deletion, or an exon 6 deletion; both exon 3-4 homozygotes were of Puerto Rican descent, and none of the homozygotes was from a consanguineous family. Mutations found in the 21 compound heterozygotes included 255delA with an exon 3-4 deletion, R275W with G430D, and R42P, R366Q, C212Y, R256C, and 202delAG. Copy number variation was found in 52.3% of carriers: 31.6% of heterozygotes, 83.3% of homozygotes, and 81.0% of compound heterozygotes.

Among 953 CORE-PD patients with onset at or before age 50 years, Alcalay et al. (2010) found 33 PRKN heterozygotes and 27 PRKN homozygotes or compound heterozygotes. Some heterozygotes also carried a mutation in another gene: an exon 5-6 deletion heterozygote also carried LRRK2 (609007) G2019S; an exon 2 deletion heterozygote and an R275W heterozygote each also carried GBA1 (606463) L444P; and a D53E heterozygote also carried GBA1 N370S.

Kay et al. (2010) screened 2,091 patients with Parkinson disease and 1,686 controls for PRKN deletions, multiplications, and copy number variants (CNVs). Thirteen patients had 2 CNV mutations, 5 had 1 CNV and 1 point mutation, and 18 had 1 CNV mutation. In whites, carrying 1 CNV was not associated with increased risk of PD (odds ratio 1.05, p = 0.89) or earlier onset. The authors found no compelling evidence that heterozygous PRKN mutations, alone or combined with known risk factors, are associated with PD.

Zhu et al. (2022) identified 2,692 PRKN copy number variants from genotyping array data in the UK Biobank. Deletions or duplications involving exon 2 accounted for roughly half, and 88% involved exons 2, 3, or 4.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Klein et al. (2000) concluded that the phenotypic spectrum of parkin mutations is broader than previously reported. Their patients with compound heterozygous parkin deletions were clinically indistinguishable from most patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease, and the authors suggested that the gene may be important in the more frequent late-onset typical Parkinson disease.

Foroud et al. (2003) found that 35 (34%) of the subjects with parkin mutations had onset at age 60 years or older, and 30 of these 35 (86%) had a detectable mutation on only 1 allele. Few significant clinical differences were seen among individuals with 2, 1, or no mutated parkin copies.

Oliveira et al. (2003) found that exon 7 (first RING finger) mutations occurred mainly in heterozygous PD patients with a later mean age at onset (49.2 years) and were absent from 920 control chromosomes. Excluding exon 7 mutations, the mean age at onset of mutation carriers was 31.5 years. The authors suggested that heterozygous Parkin mutations, especially those in exon 7, act as susceptibility alleles for late-onset PD.

Lohmann et al. (2003) found that carriers of at least 1 missense parkin mutation had a higher UPDRS motor score than carriers of 2 truncating mutations, and that missense mutations in functional domains resulted in earlier onset. Patients with a single heterozygous mutation had significantly later and more asymmetrical onset and more frequent levodopa-induced fluctuations and dystonia than patients with 2 mutations.

Pramstaller et al. (2005) found by survival analysis that the age-at-onset distribution of patients with compound heterozygous Parkin mutations differed significantly from that of both heterozygous carriers and patients without detectable mutations.

Marder et al. (2010) found that compound heterozygotes and homozygotes each had a significantly younger age at onset than heterozygotes, and that every carrier group had a younger onset than noncarriers. Onset before 40 years and a family history of PD in a first-degree relative were associated with carrying any parkin mutation. Alcalay et al. (2010) found that biallelic PRKN mutations were present in 35% of patients with onset at or before age 20 years, compared with 0.5% of those with onset at 41 to 50 years.

Among relatives without PD, Alcalay et al. (2011) found similar UPSIT scores in Parkin heterozygotes (32.5) and noncarriers (32.4), both better than heterozygotes with PD. Olfaction was significantly reduced among Parkin heterozygotes with PD, whereas compound heterozygotes with PD had normal olfaction.

### Pathogenesis

At autopsy of the 67-year-old affected woman from their family, Takahashi et al. (1994) found changes confined to the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNPC) and locus ceruleus. There was neuronal loss and gliosis in the medial and ventrolateral SNPC. Elsewhere in the SNPC and in the locus ceruleus, neuron numbers were reduced and melanin content was low, without the gliosis or extraneuronal free melanin that would suggest a neurodegenerative process. There were no Lewy bodies, and the picture differed from that of Lewy body Parkinson disease.

In a patient with 6q-linked AR-JP, Mori et al. (1998) found neuronal loss and gliosis restricted to the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus, and no Lewy bodies. Neurofibrillary tangles and argyrophilic astrocytes were present in the cerebral cortex and brainstem nuclei. These findings had not previously been reported in AR-JP, and the authors suggested pathologic heterogeneity of the disorder.

In their 70-year-old patient with a homozygous exon 4 deletion, Hayashi et al. (2000) found loss of pigmented neurons and gliosis in the SNPC (most pronounced medially and ventrolaterally) and locus ceruleus, low melanin content in remaining nigral neurons, and no Lewy bodies on alpha-synuclein immunostaining. They also found neuronal loss and fibrillary gliosis in the substantia nigra pars reticulata, which had not been reported previously in AR-JP.

Using PET, Portman et al. (2001) studied 2 brothers with early-onset parkinsonism caused by PRKN mutation. Cerebral energy metabolism did not differ from that in sporadic Parkinson disease, but the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pattern was clearly different. They concluded that parkinsonism in these patients was pathophysiologically different from PD.

Shimura et al. (2001) identified a protein complex in normal human brain made up of parkin as the E3 ubiquitin ligase, UbcH7 as its associated E2, and a 22-kD glycosylated form of alpha-synuclein (alphaSp22) as its substrate. Mutant parkin associated with autosomal recessive PD failed to bind alphaSp22. In vitro, normal parkin but not mutant parkin converted alphaSp22 into polyubiquitinated high molecular weight species, and alphaSp22 accumulated in a nonubiquitinated form in parkin-deficient PD brains. The authors concluded that loss of parkin function causes pathologic accumulation of alphaSp22.

Farrer et al. (2001) found Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease at autopsy of a compound heterozygous PRKN mutation carrier. They suggested that compound heterozygous mutations and loss of parkin protein may lead to early-onset parkinsonism with Lewy body pathology, whereas a hemizygous mutation may increase susceptibility to typical Parkinson disease.

In their autopsied patient with a homozygous exon 3 deletion, Sasaki et al. (2004) found moderate to severe depletion of melanin-containing neurons in the SNPC with gliosis, and mild neuronal loss in the locus ceruleus with extraneuronal melanin. Alpha-synuclein- and ubiquitin-positive inclusions with a doughnut or round shape were present in the neuropils of the pedunculopontine nucleus. The authors concluded that different parkin gene abnormalities may produce differences in the degree and distribution of neuronal degeneration, including the absence or presence of Lewy bodies.

At autopsy of a 73-year-old compound heterozygous patient (exon 7 deletion and del1072T), Pramstaller et al. (2005) found PD-type cell loss, reactive gliosis, and alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Surviving neurons were immunoreactive with antibodies to the Parkin N terminus but not to the IBR domain, which had been deleted by both mutations.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) found that fibroblasts from 5 patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous PRKN mutations had lower mitochondrial membrane potential (by 30% in glucose and 70% in galactose medium), complex I activity lower by about 42%, reduced complex I-linked ATP production, and cellular ATP levels reduced by 58%; complex II, III, and IV activities were not reduced. Mitochondrial branching was increased and correlated with the complex I defects, and FRAP assays showed reduced functional connectivity of the mitochondrial matrix that worsened after rotenone exposure. Knockdown of parkin by 80% with siRNA in control fibroblasts reproduced these changes, whereas 50% knockdown, mimicking haploinsufficiency, had no effect; the authors concluded that this was consistent with truly recessive inheritance.

### Diagnosis

Hedrich et al. (2001) developed a quantitative duplex PCR method using fluorescence resonance energy transfer on the LightCycler to screen all 12 parkin exons for deletions and duplications. They concluded that mutation analysis of the parkin gene should include gene dosage studies. Lücking et al. (2001) similarly recommended sensitive techniques, such as semiquantitative multiplex PCR, to detect rearrangements such as exon multiplications and deletions. ⚠︎

Poorkaj et al. (2004) concluded that parkin cases can present as typical idiopathic PD that can be distinguished only by molecular testing.

Ohsawa et al. (2005) proposed that reduced sural SNAP amplitude could serve as a diagnostic indicator of PARK2.

Alcalay et al. (2010) concluded that patients with early-onset PD who are of Jewish or Hispanic ancestry, who had onset at or before age 30 years, or who have a first-degree relative with PD may benefit from genetic counseling. Because penetrance is unknown, they saw little or no role for predictive or prenatal testing.

Zhu et al. (2022) validated genotyping arrays for detecting PRKN copy number variants in NIH-PD participants who were also genotyped by whole genome sequencing and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification. Functional assays in patient fibroblasts resolved variants of unclear significance in biallelic carriers.

### Clinical Management

Mortiboys et al. (2008) found that treating parkin-mutant fibroblasts with the glutathione precursor OTCA restored mitochondrial membrane potential to 95% of control levels and fully restored cellular ATP. This appeared to occur through a compensatory increase in complex II activity rather than correction of the complex I defect. Glutathione methyl ester gave only partial rescue.

### Population Genetics

Matsumine et al. (1998) suggested that EPDF has multiple founder mutations. They based this on the high rate of nonconsanguinity (8 of 17 families), the absence of commonly shared haplotypes, and the wide geographic spread of the families' origins.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) found that 10% of the PD patients in their movement-disorder clinic had early onset, and that the parkin mutation frequency among them was 0.12 (95% CI 0.04-0.19).

Kay et al. (2007) concluded that their study did not support heterozygous parkin sequence variants as risk factors for Parkinson disease, and that whether heterozygous dosage anomalies are associated with PD remained to be determined.

Marder et al. (2010) found that parkin mutation prevalence among early-onset PD patients declined with age at onset, from 57% with onset before 20 years to 3% with onset at 40 to 50 years. Hispanic ethnicity was associated with carrying a parkin mutation (OR 2.7 vs white non-Hispanics). The exon 3-4 deletion and 255delA were common among Hispanics, especially Puerto Ricans, and 255delA was not found in 139 Caribbean Hispanic controls. Alcalay et al. (2010) likewise found that Hispanic patients were more likely than non-Hispanic patients to carry PRKN mutations (15.6% vs 5.9%).

Kay et al. (2010) found heterozygous PRKN CNV mutations in 0.95% of controls and 0.86% of patients with PD. All CNVs in the 16 affected controls were in exons 1-4, which spare the exons encoding functionally critical protein domains, whereas mutations in patients spanned exons 2-9.

In the UK Biobank, Zhu et al. (2022) found a pathogenic PRKN mutation in 1.8% of participants and 2 mutations in about 1 in 7,800 participants.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-7DEL [p.D6S]

In a Japanese patient with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism, Kitada et al. (1998) found a microdeletion that included marker D6S305 and removed exons 3 through 7 of the PRKN gene. The gene was isolated by positional cloning within this deletion.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX4DEL

Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion affecting exon 4 alone of the PRKN gene in 4 patients with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism from 3 unrelated families. The parkin transcript was shorter in brain tissue from one group of exon 4-deleted patients.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found a homozygous exon 4 deletion in a man with AR-JP who developed a dystonic gait at age 32 years and was autopsied at age 70 years, and in his sibs. Autopsy showed nigral and locus ceruleus degeneration without Lewy bodies.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX2 TRIPLICATION

In an Italian family with pseudo-dominant inheritance of parkinsonism, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father (onset at age 57 years) was homozygous for a triplication of PRKN exon 2. His affected son (onset at age 31 years) was compound heterozygous for the triplication and a deletion of exons 3 and 4 inherited from his unaffected heterozygous mother.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-4DEL

In an Italian family, Lücking et al. (2001) found that an affected son (onset at age 31 years) was compound heterozygous for a deletion of PRKN exons 3 and 4, inherited from his unaffected mother, and a triplication of exon 2, inherited from his affected father.

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) found the exon 3-4 deletion in the homozygous state in 2 patients of Puerto Rican descent with early-onset PD. They also found it in compound heterozygous state with 255delA in Hispanic patients. The exon 3-4 deletion was common among Hispanics, especially Puerto Ricans.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 40-BP DEL, EX3

Farrer et al. (2001) found a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3 of the PRKN gene on a common chromosome 6 haplotype that cosegregated with disease in 2 kindreds. In the proband of the smaller kindred, the deletion was compound heterozygous with R275W, and RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on alternate transcripts; this proband had Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease at autopsy.

Marder et al. (2010) found the exon 3 40-bp deletion in the homozygous state among homozygous carriers, and in the heterozygous state among heterozygous carriers, in the CORE-PD study of early-onset PD.

**.0006 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**

In the proband of the smaller kindred studied by Farrer et al. (2001), an R275W substitution in exon 7 of the PRKN gene was found in compound heterozygosity with a 40-bp exon 3 deletion; RNA analysis showed that the mutations were on alternate transcripts. Autopsy of this proband showed Lewy body pathology.

Oliveira et al. (2003) found arg275-to-trp (R275W), among other exon 7 mutations, mainly in heterozygous PD patients with a later mean age at onset (49.2 years). These mutations were absent from 920 control chromosomes. ⚠︎

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) found R275W in compound heterozygosity with G430D, and in the heterozygous state in patients of white, Hispanic, and other ethnicity. Alcalay et al.
