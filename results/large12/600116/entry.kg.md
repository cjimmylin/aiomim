### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive juvenile Parkinson disease-2 (PARK2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the parkin gene (PRKN; 602544) on chromosome 6q26.

### Description

Autosomal recessive juvenile Parkinson disease (AR-JP) is a levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. Its pathologic hallmark is a highly selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra (Matsumine et al., 1997). Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) considered AR-JP a distinct clinical entity within juvenile parkinsonism, because their patients had similar clinical findings that differed from those of other types of parkinsonism. Matsumine et al. (1998) described the 6q-linked disorder as having a benign clinical course and relatively selective degeneration of nigral dopaminergic neurons without Lewy body formation.

### Nomenclature

Matsumine et al. (1998) concluded that early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF) and autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (ARJP) are allelic disorders. They proposed that both belong within the spectrum of a single clinicogenetic entity, chromosome 6q-linked parkinsonism.

### Clinical Features

Yamamura et al. (1973) described paralysis agitans (parkinsonism) with early onset and marked diurnal fluctuation of symptoms.

Takahashi et al. (1994) described a family in which 4 of 5 sibs, born to first-cousin parents, developed parkinsonism at similarly early ages. The parkinsonism improved after sleep. One affected sib, a 67-year-old woman, came to autopsy. Pathologic changes were confined to the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNPC) and the locus ceruleus. The medial and ventrolateral SNPC showed obvious neuronal loss and gliosis. Elsewhere in the SNPC and in the locus ceruleus, neurons were fewer and most had low melanin content, without detectable gliosis or extraneuronal free melanin. There were no Lewy bodies, and the authors concluded that the overall pathology differed from that of Lewy body Parkinson disease.

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) described 17 patients with AR-JP from 12 Japanese families. Females predominated, mean age at onset was 27.8 years, and the disease progressed slowly. The parkinsonian triad of tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia was mild. Gait freezing, hyperreflexia, foot dystonia, and retropulsion were relatively prominent. All 17 patients had amelioration of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep, which the authors considered characteristic.

Matsumine et al. (1997) described AR-JP as a levodopa-responsive parkinsonism whose pathologic finding is a highly selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra.

Matsumine et al. (1998) described early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF) as a dopa-responsive parkinsonism of early onset in which parkinsonian symptoms improved after sleep. In a pathologic study of one EPDF case they found selective neuronal degeneration in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra, without Lewy body formation.

Saito et al. (1998) described AR-JP as a hereditary neurodegenerative disorder with levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. Mean age at onset in their series was 23.2 years. ⚠︎

In European and Algerian families with chromosome 6-linked autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism, Tassin et al. (1998) found a broader clinical spectrum than previously reported. Age at onset was up to 58 years, and some patients had painful dystonia.

Mori et al. (1998) reported pathologic and biochemical studies in a patient with 6q-linked AR-JP. Neuronal loss and gliosis were confined to the substantia nigra and the locus ceruleus, and there were no Lewy bodies. Neurofibrillary tangles and argyrophilic astrocytes were seen in the cerebral cortex and brainstem nuclei. The authors stated that these changes had not been reported before in AR-JP.

Klein et al. (2000) described a large pedigree from South Tyrol, northern Italy, with adult-onset, clinically typical, tremor-dominant parkinsonism that appeared to be autosomal dominant. The 4 affected male sibs could not be clinically distinguished from most patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease. None had the hallmarks reported with earlier parkin mutations: diurnal fluctuations, benefit from sleep, foot dystonia, hyperreflexia, or early susceptibility to levodopa-induced dyskinesias.

Hayashi et al. (2000) reported a 70-year-old man with AR-JP who developed a dystonic gait at age 32 years. Hand tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and impaired postural reflexes followed, and levodopa improved these symptoms. At autopsy there was loss of pigmented neurons with gliosis in the SNPC and the locus ceruleus. In the SNPC the loss was most pronounced in the medial and ventrolateral regions. The remaining SNPC neurons had low melanin content, which was less marked in the locus ceruleus. Immunostaining for alpha-synuclein confirmed the absence of Lewy bodies. The authors also found neuronal loss and fibrillary gliosis in the substantia nigra pars reticulata, which they noted had not been reported before in AR-JP.

van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) described a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism. Parkinsonism was the main clinical syndrome, but the proband also had mild gait ataxia. Neuropathologic examination of the proband showed selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SNPC and neuronal loss in parts of the spinocerebellar system. There were no Lewy bodies or neurofibrillary tangles, but tau pathology was present.

Portman et al. (2001) used PET to study nigrostriatal dopaminergic function and cerebral energy metabolism in 2 brothers with early-onset parkinsonism caused by parkin mutation. Cerebral energy metabolism did not differ from that in sporadic PD, but the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pattern was clearly different. The authors concluded that the parkinsonism in these 2 patients was pathophysiologically different from PD. ⚠︎

In the Italian family reported by Lücking et al. (2001), the father developed parkinsonism at age 57 years and his son at age 31 years.

Farrer et al. (2001) found Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease at autopsy in a compound heterozygous proband. They noted that parkin-related disease had classically been associated with loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus without Lewy bodies.

Lohmann et al. (2003) compared 146 patients with early-onset parkinsonism and parkin mutations with 250 patients without parkin mutations. No single sign distinguished the 2 groups. However, patients with mutations had significantly earlier and more symmetrical onset, more frequent dystonia at onset, hyperreflexia, slower disease progression, and a tendency toward a greater response to levodopa despite lower doses.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) found that the phenotypes of their 7 parkin mutation carriers were consistent with idiopathic PD. They concluded that parkin cases could present as typical idiopathic PD and could be distinguished only by molecular testing.

Sasaki et al. (2004) reported autopsy findings in a patient with AR-JP. Brain lesions were limited to dopaminergic neuron systems such as the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Melanin-containing neurons in the SNPC were moderately to severely depleted, with gliosis. Neurons in the locus ceruleus were mildly decreased, and melanin pigment was seen outside neurons. There were no Lewy bodies in the neuropil of the SNPC or locus ceruleus. Occasional doughnut-shaped or round basophilic inclusion bodies in the neuropil of the pedunculopontine nucleus stained for alpha-synuclein and ubiquitin. The authors concluded that different parkin gene abnormalities may cause differences in the degree and distribution of neuronal degeneration, including whether Lewy bodies are present. They also suggested that parkin-induced parkinsonism may be related to idiopathic Parkinson disease. ⚠︎

Ohsawa et al. (2005) performed nerve conduction studies in 9 PARK2 patients and 8 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease. Sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude was significantly reduced in 8 of the PARK2 patients, most of whom were asymptomatic. The authors suggested that sensory axonal neuropathy may be a common clinical feature of PARK2.

Pramstaller et al. (2005) reported a 7-generation pedigree of 196 individuals, 25 of whom had levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. Parkin mutations were found in 77 subjects. Five of the 25 patients carried compound heterozygous mutations and met UK PD Society Brain Bank criteria for definite Parkinson disease. Eight carried only a heterozygous parkin mutation and 7 had no parkin mutation. The status of 5 deceased patients was unknown.

Chien et al. (2006) described a genetic isolate from north-eastern Brazil in which genealogical studies connected 255 individuals, 15 of whom had Parkinson disease. DNA was obtained from 10 patients with early-onset PD, 1 case with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism, and 15 unaffected relatives. Average age at onset of PD symptoms was 30.8 years (range, 12 to 46 years).

In the early-onset PD cohort studied by Marder et al. (2010), dystonia as a presenting sign did not differ between parkin mutation carriers (0%) and noncarriers (1.7%). Response to levodopa was also similar: 93.4% of carriers and 90.5% of noncarriers reported a response to antiparkinson medication.

Alcalay et al. (2011) gave the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) to 44 CORE-PD probands whose Parkinson disease began at or before age 50 years. Nine were parkin compound heterozygotes, 10 were heterozygotes, and 25 were noncarriers. Compound heterozygotes had higher UPSIT scores (31.9) than heterozygotes (20.1) or noncarriers (19.9) (p less than 0.001), and the difference persisted after adjustment for age, gender, disease duration, and smoking. The authors concluded that compound heterozygotes with PD have olfaction within the normal range.

### Inheritance

Takahashi et al. (1994) reported affected sibs born to first-cousin parents in a family with juvenile parkinsonism. Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) strongly suspected autosomal recessive inheritance in their 12 Japanese families, because the parents were consanguineous in all but 1 family and all patients were in a single generation.

Lücking et al. (2001) reported pseudo-dominant inheritance in an Italian family with parkinsonism due to parkin mutations. The affected father was homozygous for a parkin mutation, the unaffected mother was a heterozygous carrier, and their affected son was a compound heterozygote. The authors concluded that pseudo-dominant inheritance of parkin mutations has to be considered in early-onset parkinsonism.

Khan et al. (2005) concluded that heterozygosity for a parkin mutation is a risk factor for nigrostriatal dysfunction. They suggested that in some carriers it may contribute to late-onset Parkinson disease, even though parkin disease is usually autosomal recessive.

In a Brazilian kindred, Chien et al. (2006) noted that geographic isolation and multiple consanguineous marriages initially suggested autosomal recessive inheritance. Haplotype analysis showed that the PD patients were homozygous for markers across the PARK2 locus.

Wang et al. (2008) used the kin-cohort method to estimate the cumulative incidence of PD to age 65 years (age-specific penetrance) in first-degree relatives who carried parkin mutations. The estimate was 7.0% (95% CI, 0.4-71.9%), compared with 1.7% in noncarrier relatives of cases (P = 0.59) and 1.1% in relatives of controls. The risk in noncarrier relatives of early-onset cases was not significantly greater than the general population risk among controls. The authors concluded that age-specific penetrance among parkin carriers, particularly heterozygotes, deserved further study.

Marder et al. (2010) found that patients heterozygous for a parkin mutation had a younger age at onset than noncarriers after adjustment for ethnicity and family history, and concluded that this supports parkin heterozygosity as a susceptibility factor for PD. A family history of PD in a first-degree relative was associated with carrying any parkin mutation (OR 2.8): 11.8% of patients with a family history carried mutations, compared with 5.7% of those without one. In the same cohort, Alcalay et al. (2010) found that PRKN carriers were more likely than LRRK2 carriers to report a family history of PD, even though PRKN-related disease is considered recessive. They suggested that the high frequency of PRKN heterozygotes may support the idea that even a single PRKN mutation is a risk factor for early-onset PD. ⚠︎

In contrast, Zhu et al. (2022) found that UK Biobank participants carrying 1 pathogenic PRKN variant were no more likely than noncarriers to have Parkinson disease (OR 0.91, 0.58-1.38; P = 0.76) or a parent with Parkinson disease (OR 1.12; P = 0.19). In the NIH-PD + AMP-PD case-control cohort (whole genome sequencing of 4,094 participants), heterozygous carriers likewise had no increased risk (OR 1.29, 0.74-2.38; P = 0.43). The authors concluded that heterozygous pathogenic PRKN mutations are common but do not increase the risk of Parkinson disease.

### Cytogenetics

In an Algerian family, Tassin et al. (1998) found a small homozygous deletion of 2 microsatellite markers, D6S411 and D6S1550, which colocalize on the genetic map and segregated with the disease. Taking this deletion into account would reduce the candidate region to less than 1 cM.

Jones et al. (1998) observed apparent homozygosity for null alleles at marker D6S955 in one family. This suggested a deletion and a finer localization of the juvenile parkinsonism locus.

### Mapping

Matsumine et al. (1997) identified a family with AR-JP in which the disease segregated perfectly with a diallelic polymorphism of the Mn-superoxide dismutase gene (SOD2). Extending linkage analysis to 13 families with AR-JP, they mapped the AR-JP gene to chromosome 6q25.2-q27, a region that includes the SOD2 locus. Maximum cumulative pairwise lod scores were 7.26 at D6S305 (theta = 0.03) and 7.71 at D6S253 (theta = 0.02). Obligate recombination events and multipoint linkage analysis placed the gene in a 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S264.

Matsumine et al. (1998) studied 53 members of 17 families with EPDF (151 meioses). Multipoint linkage analysis gave a peak lod score of 14.2 at 1.0 cM telomeric to D6S305 and placed the disease locus in a 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S253, the same position mapped for ARJP. The highest 2-point lod score was 10.13 at theta = 0 for D6S305, and haplotype analysis agreed with the multipoint result.

Saito et al. (1998) narrowed the AR-JP locus on 6q25.2-q27 by linkage analysis with densely spaced markers. The highest cumulative pairwise lod score was 9.13 at D6S1579 (theta = 0.05), and the highest cumulative multipoint lod score was 12.4 at a locus 3 cM telomeric to D6S1599. Obligate recombination events reduced the candidate region to 13 cM between D6S1579 and D6S264. There was strong linkage disequilibrium between the AR-JP locus and D6S1579 (chi-square 84.22, P less than 0.0001; lambda = 0.40) and D6S1599 (chi-square 63.37, P less than 0.0001; lambda = 0.30). The authors suggested that the gene most likely lies near the 4-cM region containing these 2 markers.

Tassin et al. (1998) tested 1 Algerian and 10 European multiplex families with early-onset Parkinson disease for linkage to the AR-JP locus using marker D6S305. Homogeneity analysis gave a conditional probability in favor of linkage of greater than 0.9 in 8 families, which were then analyzed with 8 microsatellite markers spanning the 17-cM region. Haplotype reconstruction and the smallest region of homozygosity in 2 consanguineous families reduced the candidate interval to 11.3 cM.

Jones et al. (1998) found linkage of AR-JP to 6q25.2-q27 in 15 families from 4 distinct ethnic backgrounds, and a full genomic screen excluded other candidate regions. With a detailed genetic map of the linked region, recombination events restricted the locus to a 6.9-cM region. The candidate gene SOD2 mapped within the linked region, but recombination events excluded it as the disease gene. ⚠︎

Kitada et al. (1998) noted that AR-JP was strongly linked to markers D6S305 and D6S253 and that D6S305 was deleted in 1 Japanese AR-JP patient. They used positional cloning within this microdeletion to isolate the causative gene.

In a South Tyrolean family with apparently autosomal dominant parkinsonism, Klein et al. (2000) used haplotype analysis to exclude linkage to the regions on chromosomes 2p, 4p, and 4q that contain genes for autosomal dominant parkinsonism. The analysis instead implicated the parkin locus on chromosome 6q.

Oliveira et al. (2003) noted that they had previously found linkage of idiopathic Parkinson disease to the chromosome 6 region containing the parkin gene (D6S305, lod score 5.47) in families with at least 1 subject whose onset was before age 40 years.

### Heterogeneity

Mitsui et al. (1994) reported 2 sibs with hereditary parkinsonism accompanied by pyramidal signs and cerebellar ataxia. Anticholinergic drugs had a dramatic beneficial effect in both sibs, but levodopa did not. The authors proposed this as a new hereditary variant of early-onset Parkinson disease, distinct from the levodopa-sensitive forms of juvenile Parkinson disease.

Ishikawa and Miyatake (1995) reported a family with hereditary juvenile dystonia-parkinsonism in which 8 members in 3 generations were affected, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Patients had parkinsonism, sleep benefit, a marked response to levodopa, wearing-off phenomenon, and levodopa-induced choreic dyskinesia. They also had neck dystonia (torticollis and retrocollis), foot dystonia, and other dystonic movements, such as frequent lifting of the thigh. In 1 patient the main feature was dystonic movement, which worsened after levodopa was given. The authors considered this a specific form of hereditary dystonia-parkinsonism.

Matsumine et al. (1998) found that 8 of their 17 EPDF families were nonconsanguineous, that the families shared no common haplotypes, and that they came from widely separated geographic origins. They suggested that EPDF has multiple founder mutations.

Tassin et al. (1998) confirmed linkage of AR-JP to the PARK2 locus in European families and in an Algerian family; linkage was favored in 8 of the 11 families tested. They concluded that PARK2 appears to be an important locus for AR-JP in European patients.

Mori et al. (1998) suggested that the neurofibrillary tangles and argyrophilic astrocytes found in their patient pointed to pathologic heterogeneity in 6q-linked AR-JP.

Hoenicka et al. (2002) found no previously described mutations in alpha-synuclein (Park1) or UCHL1 (Park5) in any of 19 Spanish families with familial Parkinson disease.

### Molecular Genetics

Kitada et al. (1998) used positional cloning within a microdeletion at D6S305 in a Japanese AR-JP patient to isolate a 2,960-bp cDNA with a 1,395-bp open reading frame. It encoded a 465-amino acid protein, which they named parkin, with moderate similarity to ubiquitin at the N terminus and a RING-finger motif at the C terminus. The gene spans more than 500 kb and contains 12 exons. A 4.5-kb transcript was expressed in many human tissues and was abundant in brain, including the substantia nigra. In the patient with the D6S305 microdeletion, parkin exons 3 through 7 were deleted. In 4 AR-JP patients from 3 unrelated families, a deletion affected only exon 4, and brain tissue from 1 group of exon 4-deleted patients showed a shorter 4.5-kb parkin transcript. The authors concluded that mutations in parkin appear to be responsible for AR-JP.

In the South Tyrolean family with adult-onset, tremor-dominant parkinsonism, Klein et al. (2000) found that the 4 affected male sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 deletions in the parkin gene, one large and one truncating. Two affected women in the family carried only the truncating deletion, in the heterozygous state, with an apparently normal second allele.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found a homozygous deletion of parkin exon 4 in their autopsied patient with AR-JP and in his sibs.

In the Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism, van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) found compound heterozygous parkin mutations: a missense mutation and an exon deletion.

Hedrich et al. (2001) screened the parkin gene in 21 patients with early-onset parkinsonism. Conventional SSCP and sequence analysis found 3 mutations: a single base pair substitution in exon 3 and small deletions in exon 9. Quantitative duplex real-time PCR found gene dosage changes in 7 of the 21 patients. These included heterozygous and compound heterozygous deletions of exons 2, 3, 5, and 7, a homozygous exon 7 deletion, and 2 heterozygous duplications of exon 4. Two patients carried more than 2 parkin mutations.

In an Italian family with pseudo-dominant inheritance, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father was homozygous for a triplication of parkin exon 2, a mutation the authors described as so far unique. The unaffected mother was heterozygous for a deletion of exons 3 and 4. Their affected son was compound heterozygous for the paternal triplication and the maternal deletion. The authors recommended sensitive screening methods such as semiquantitative multiplex PCR.

Farrer et al. (2001) sequenced the parkin gene in 38 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before age 41 years) and followed up 2 probands with mutations. Haplotype analysis of the 2 families showed a shared chromosome 6 carrying a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3, which cosegregated with disease. The proband of the smaller kindred also carried an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7. RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on different transcripts, consistent with compound heterozygosity.

Hoenicka et al. (2002) studied 19 Spanish kindreds with familial Parkinson disease: 13 with recessive inheritance, 4 with dominant inheritance, and 2 with uncertain inheritance. Using SSCP, direct sequencing, and restriction digestion of PCR-amplified genomic DNA, they found 5 different parkin mutations in 5 of the 13 recessive families: deletions of exons 3 and 5, 225delA, and the novel missense mutations val56-to-glu (V56E) and cys212-to-tyr (C212Y). The authors concluded that parkin mutations account for 38% of families with recessive parkinsonism in Spain.

Foroud et al. (2003) screened the parkin gene by quantitative PCR and exon sequencing in families with at least 2 sibs with PD. The families had either early-onset PD (onset at or before age 50 years) or a positive lod score with a marker in parkin intron 7. They identified 25 different parkin mutations in 103 individuals from 47 families. Forty-one individuals had mutations in both parkin alleles and 62 had a mutation in only 1 allele.

Oliveira et al. (2003) screened the parkin gene in 174 multiplex families from a genomic screen and in 133 additional Parkinson disease families. They found mutations in 18% of early-onset families and 2% of late-onset families (5% of all families screened). Age at onset in patients with parkin mutations ranged from 12 to 71 years. Excluding exon 7 mutations, mean age at onset was 31.5 years.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) screened all 12 parkin exons by sequencing and dosage analysis in 39 movement disorder clinic patients with PD onset at or before 40 years of age. They found mutations in 7 patients, 2 compound heterozygous and 5 heterozygous. The mutations included deletions of exons 2, 3, and 8, duplications of exons 2-4 and 9, and a pro437-to-leu (P437L) substitution. Deletions and multiplications made up 78% of the mutations. None of the point mutations found in patients was detected in 96 controls. A novel substitution, arg402-to-trp (R402W), was found in 1 patient with early-onset PD and also in 1 control.

Sasaki et al. (2004) found a homozygous deletion of parkin exon 3 in their autopsied patient with AR-JP.

In a 73-year-old patient from their large pedigree, Pramstaller et al. (2005) identified compound heterozygous parkin mutations, a deletion of exon 7 and del1072T. Both mutations deleted the In-Between-RING (IBR) domain.

In 10 patients with early-onset PD from a Brazilian genetic isolate, Chien et al. (2006) identified a novel homozygous splice site mutation in the parkin gene, IVS1+1G-T, that cosegregated completely with the phenotype. cDNA analysis showed total loss of parkin transcript in homozygous carriers, indicating a loss-of-function mutation.

Kay et al. (2007) sequenced the parkin gene in 302 patients with Parkinson disease and 301 control subjects, with replication in 1,260 additional patients and 1,657 controls. They detected 34 variants, 21 of them novel: 12 were polymorphisms and 22 were rare variants. Patients and controls did not differ in the frequency, type, or functional location of the variants. The P437L variant, a common mutation thought to be pathogenic, was found in unaffected controls.

Wang et al. (2008) screened the parkin gene for point mutations and exon deletions and duplications in 247 probands with Parkinson disease (onset at or before age 50 years) and 104 control probands enrolled in the Genetic Epidemiology of Parkinson's Disease (GEPD) study. Parkin mutations were found in 25 probands with PD (10.1%). Eighteen of the 25 (72.0%) were heterozygotes, and 1 parkin homozygote was reported in 2 sibs with PD.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied skin fibroblasts from 5 patients with early-onset parkinsonism who had homozygous or compound heterozygous parkin mutations, identified by direct sequencing and MLPA gene dosage analysis. The genotypes were 255delA with an exon 5 deletion; homozygous 202_203delAG (exon 2); 202_203delAG with an exon 2 deletion; 202_203delAG with an exon 4 deletion; and c.101-102delAG (p.Gln34ArgfsX38) with c.1289G-A (gly430-to-asp; G430D).

Marder et al. (2010) screened the parkin gene for point mutations and exon copy number variation in 956 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before age 51 years) recruited at 13 movement disorders centers in the CORE-PD study. Sixty-four patients (6.7%) had parkin mutations: 3.9% were heterozygous, 0.6% homozygous, and 2.2% compound heterozygous. Copy number variation was present in 52.3% of mutation carriers (31.6% of heterozygotes, 83.3% of homozygotes, and 81.0% of compound heterozygotes). No consanguinity was reported. Recurrent compound heterozygous combinations included 255delA with an exon 3-4 deletion (3 Hispanic patients) and R275W with a variety of second alleles. R275W was also the most frequent heterozygous mutation, found in 8 patients. Other mutations included arg42-to-pro (R42P), G430D, and an exon 3 40-bp deletion.

In the CORE-PD cohort, Alcalay et al. (2010) described 3 patients with early-onset PD who were heterozygous for a PRKN mutation and also carried a GBA mutation: an exon 2 deletion with GBA L444P, R275W with GBA L444P, and D53E with GBA N370S. A fourth patient, of Jewish ancestry, carried a heterozygous PRKN exon 5-6 deletion together with LRRK2 G2019S. None of these patients reported Parkinson disease in a first-degree relative.

Kay et al. (2010) screened 2,091 patients with Parkinson disease (mean onset age 58.3 years) and 1,686 controls for PARK2 deletions, multiplications, and copy number variations (CNVs). They used semiquantitative PCR and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, with validation by real-time quantitative PCR, in subjects already tested for point mutations. Thirteen patients had 2 CNV mutations, 5 had 1 CNV plus 1 point mutation, and 18 had 1 CNV mutation. The mutations in patients spanned exons 2-9.

Zhu et al. (2022) identified 2,692 PRKN copy number variants from genotyping array data in the UK Biobank. Deletions or duplications involving exon 2 accounted for about half of all CNVs, and 88% involved exons 2, 3, or 4. They validated genotyping arrays for CNV detection in NIH-PD participants genotyped by whole genome sequencing, array, and MLPA. In the NIH-PD cohort, functional assays of patient fibroblasts were used to resolve variants of unclear significance in biallelic PRKN carriers. The results suggested that cryptic loss-of-function variants in monoallelic carriers are not a substantial confounder for association studies.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Klein et al. (2000) concluded that parkin mutations cause a wider range of phenotypes than previously reported. They suggested that the gene may be important in the etiology of the more common late-onset typical Parkinson disease.

Farrer et al. (2001) suggested that compound heterozygous parkin mutations and loss of parkin protein may lead to early-onset parkinsonism with Lewy body pathology. They also suggested that a single heterozygous ('hemizygous') mutation may increase susceptibility to typical Parkinson disease.

Hoenicka et al. (2002) found that Spanish heterozygous carriers of a single parkin mutation were either asymptomatic or developed symptoms in late adulthood or after brief exposure to haloperidol. Two simple heterozygous carriers were symptomatic. The authors suggested that heterozygous mutations could be a risk factor for parkinsonism.

Of the 103 individuals with parkin mutations reported by Foroud et al. (2003), 35 (34%) had PD onset at age 60 years or older, and 30 of these 35 (86%) had a detectable mutation on only 1 parkin allele. The authors concluded that parkin mutations occur in familial PD with older onset and that heterozygosity for a parkin mutation leads to later-onset PD. There were few significant clinical differences among individuals with 2, 1, or no mutated parkin copies.

Oliveira et al. (2003) found that mutations in parkin exon 7, the first RING finger (cys253-to-trp, arg256-to-cys, arg275-to-trp, and asp280-to-asn), occurred mainly in heterozygous Parkinson disease patients with much later onset (mean 49.2 years). These mutations were not found in controls in their study or in several previous reports (920 chromosomes). The authors suggested that heterozygous parkin mutations, especially those in exon 7, act as susceptibility alleles for late-onset Parkinson disease.

In multiple logistic regression analysis, Lohmann et al. (2003) found that dystonia at onset and brisk reflexes no longer differed significantly between parkin carriers and noncarriers. Instead, these features correlated with age at onset rather than mutation status. Among carriers, age at onset and rate of progression varied: the younger the age at onset, the slower the evolution. Carriers of at least 1 missense mutation had a higher UPDRS motor score than carriers of 2 truncating mutations, and missense mutations in functional domains of parkin led to earlier onset. Patients with a single heterozygous mutation had significantly later and more asymmetrical onset, and more frequent levodopa-induced fluctuations and dystonia, than patients with 2 mutations.

Khan et al. (2005) used 18F-dopa PET to study 13 unrelated asymptomatic heterozygous carriers of a single parkin mutation. Compared with controls, the carriers had significantly reduced 18F-dopa uptake in the caudate, putamen, and ventral and dorsal midbrain. Four of the carriers had subtle extrapyramidal signs.

In their large pedigree, Pramstaller et al. (2005) found that age at onset in patients with compound heterozygous parkin mutations differed significantly from that in both heterozygous carriers and patients without detectable parkin mutations. The authors suggested that the pedigree offered a chance to study heterozygous parkin mutations as a risk factor for PD.

In the Brazilian genetic isolate, Chien et al. (2006) found that the case with neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism and 13 of 15 healthy relatives were heterozygous carriers of the IVS1+1G-T mutation. Because heterozygotes did not have PD, the authors considered the mutation genuinely recessive. They suggested that parkin haploinsufficiency is not a relevant risk factor for early- or late-onset PD, although it could facilitate neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism.

Kay et al. (2007) concluded that parkin point mutations are not exclusive to Parkinson disease. A single point mutation in a patient, without a second mutation, should not be taken as the cause of disease unless corroborated by family data and functional studies. Their study did not support heterozygous parkin sequence variants as risk factors for PD, and whether heterozygous dosage anomalies are associated with PD remained to be determined.

Marder et al. (2010) found that the prevalence of parkin mutations fell as age at onset rose: 57% (8/14) with onset before age 20, 30% (13/43) at ages 20 to 29, 9% (23/254) at ages 30 to 39, and 3% (20/644) at ages 40 to 50. Every carrier group had a younger mean age at onset than noncarriers (heterozygotes 37.2 years, homozygotes 28.5, compound heterozygotes 28.9, noncarriers 42.0). Compound heterozygotes and homozygotes each had significantly younger onset than heterozygotes. Onset before age 40 was associated with carrying any parkin mutation (OR 5.0), and, compared with heterozygosity, carrying 2 mutations was associated with younger onset (OR 6.6).

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that age at onset was significantly younger in PRKN mutation carriers than in carriers of all other mutations (P less than 0.001). Mean age at onset was 29 years in carriers of 2 PRKN mutations and 37 years in PRKN heterozygotes, compared with 42 years in noncarriers. Patients with 2 PRKN mutations made up 35% of cases with onset at age 20 or younger, 17% of those with onset at 21 to 30 years, and 0.5% of those with onset at 41 to 50 years.

In white subjects, Kay et al. (2010) found that carrying 1 PARK2 CNV did not increase the risk of Parkinson disease (odds ratio 1.05, p = 0.89). Nor was it associated with earlier onset (64.7 years in heterozygotes vs 58.5 years in noncarriers). They concluded that there was no compelling evidence that heterozygous PARK2 mutations, alone or combined with known risk factors (SNCA, MAPT, APOE, smoking, coffee intake), are associated with PD.

Alcalay et al. (2011) found that olfaction was significantly reduced in PD patients heterozygous for a single parkin mutation but not in their heterozygous relatives without PD. Among relatives without PD, UPSIT scores were similar in heterozygotes (32.5) and noncarriers (32.4), and better than in heterozygotes with PD (p = 0.001). The authors stated that further research is needed to assess whether these findings reflect different neuropathology in parkin heterozygotes and compound heterozygotes.

### Pathogenesis

Mitsui et al. (1994) noted that the response to anticholinergics in their sibs resembled that reported in Joseph disease. They suggested that it pointed to dysfunction of an 'indirect pathway' involving the globus pallidus and the subthalamic nucleus, in addition to dysfunction of the nigrostriatal system.

Shimura et al. (2001) identified a protein complex in normal human brain made up of parkin as the E3 ubiquitin ligase, UbcH7 as its associated E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme, and a new 22-kD glycosylated form of alpha-synuclein (alphaSp22) as its substrate. In an in vitro ubiquitination assay, normal parkin converted alphaSp22 into polyubiquitinated, high molecular weight species. Mutant parkin associated with autosomal recessive PD did not bind or ubiquitinate alphaSp22. In parkin-deficient PD brains, alphaSp22 accumulated in a nonubiquitinated form. The authors concluded that loss of parkin function causes pathologic accumulation of alphaSp22. They suggested that this reaction underlies the accumulation of ubiquitinated alpha-synuclein in conventional PD.

At autopsy of a 73-year-old compound heterozygous parkin mutation carrier, Pramstaller et al. (2005) found PD-type cell loss, reactive gliosis, and alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Surviving neurons reacted with antibodies to the parkin N terminus but not to the IBR domain.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from 5 parkin-mutant patients. Mitochondrial membrane potential was reduced by about 30% in glucose medium and 70% in galactose medium. Complex I activity was reduced by about 42%, with normal complex III and IV activity, and complex I-linked (but not complex II-linked) ATP production was reduced. Cellular ATP levels were reduced by 58%, but cell viability and growth were unaffected. Mitochondrial branching was increased, and this correlated with lower complex I-linked ATP production and complex I activity, suggesting that the functional and morphologic effects of parkin deficiency on mitochondria are related. FRAP assays showed reduced functional connectivity of the mitochondrial matrix, which worsened with rotenone. Rotenone shortened mitochondria in parkin-mutant but not control cells. Knockdown of parkin by 80% with siRNA in control fibroblasts reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and cellular ATP and increased mitochondrial branching by 70%, indicating that parkin deficiency is sufficient to explain the defects in patient cells. In contrast, 50% knockdown, mimicking haploinsufficiency, had no effect. The authors considered this consistent with true recessive inheritance and not supportive of heterozygosity as a PD risk factor. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Hedrich et al. (2001) concluded that mutation analysis of the parkin gene should include gene dosage studies. They developed a real-time kinetic quantitative duplex PCR method, using fluorescence resonance energy transfer on the LightCycler, to screen all 12 parkin exons for deletions and duplications.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) concluded that parkin-based diagnosis and counseling require a better understanding of the mode of inheritance, penetrance, and carrier frequencies.

Ohsawa et al. (2005) suggested that a reduced sural SNAP amplitude could be a diagnostic indicator of PARK2.

### Clinical Management

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) reported that the response to levodopa in their 17 AR-JP patients was satisfactory, but levodopa-induced choreic limb dyskinesia and the wearing-off phenomenon were frequent.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) found that treating parkin-mutant fibroblasts with the glutathione precursor OTCA (10 microM) restored mitochondrial membrane potential to 95% of control levels and completely restored cellular ATP. The effect appeared to come from a compensatory increase in complex II activity rather than correction of the complex I defect. Glutathione methyl ester gave only partial rescue (60%). The authors suggested that patient fibroblasts could serve as a screening system for disease-modifying compounds.

### Population Genetics

Poorkaj et al. (2004) found that in the clinic setting 10% of PD patients had early-onset disease and 18% of these had parkin mutations. If parkin is recessive, however, only the 5% of early-onset cases with compound mutations could be attributed to this locus. The mutation frequency was 0.12 (95% CI 0.04-0.19), and 70% of parkin cases were heterozygous. The authors stated that it was unclear whether heterozygous mutations are pathogenic.

In the CORE-PD cohort, Marder et al. (2010) found that Hispanic ethnicity was associated with carrying a parkin mutation (15.6% of Hispanic patients vs 5.7% of white non-Hispanic patients; OR 2.7). None of the 12 Black non-Hispanic patients carried a mutation. Seven of the 12 Hispanic carriers were Puerto Rican. Six Hispanic patients (5 Puerto Rican and 1 Mexican) carried a deletion of exons 3-4, and both exon 3-4 homozygotes were of Puerto Rican descent. The second most common mutation in Hispanics was 255delA. It was found in 3 Puerto Ricans together with the exon 3-4 deletion and in 1 Mexican heterozygote, and was not detected in 139 Caribbean Hispanic controls.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset at age 50 years or younger) from 13 CORE-PD centers, recruited regardless of family history, for mutations in SNCA, PRKN, PINK1, DJ1, LRRK2, and GBA. The results identified 33 PRKN heterozygotes and 27 PRKN homozygotes or compound heterozygotes; the abstract gives 64 (6.7%) PRKN carriers. Hispanic patients were more likely than non-Hispanic patients to carry PRKN mutations (15.6% vs 5.9%, P = 0.003).

Kay et al. (2010) found heterozygous PARK2 CNV mutations in 0.95% of 1,686 controls and 0.86% of 2,091 patients with Parkinson disease. All CNV mutations in the 16 carrier controls were in exons 1-4, which spare the exons encoding functionally critical protein domains.

Zhu et al. (2022) evaluated PRKN mutations, including SNVs and copy number variants, using whole exome sequencing and genotyping array data from 200,606 UK Biobank participants. A pathogenic PRKN mutation was found in 1.8% of participants, and 2 mutations in approximately 1 in 7,800.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX4DEL

Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion affecting only exon 4 of the parkin gene in 4 patients with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (AR-JP) from 3 unrelated families. In brain tissue from 1 group of exon 4-deleted patients, the 4.5-kb parkin transcript was shorter.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found a homozygous deletion of parkin exon 4 in a man with AR-JP who developed a dystonic gait at age 32 years and came to autopsy at age 70, and in his sibs. Autopsy showed loss of pigmented neurons with gliosis in the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus ceruleus, with no Lewy bodies. ⚠︎

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-7DEL

In a Japanese AR-JP patient with a microdeletion at marker D6S305, Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion of exons 3 through 7 of the parkin gene.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 40-BP DEL, EX3

In 2 families with early-onset Parkinson disease, Farrer et al. (2001) found a shared chromosome 6 haplotype carrying a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3 of the parkin gene, which cosegregated with disease. The proband of the smaller kindred was compound heterozygous for this deletion and R275W. At autopsy this proband had Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, ARG275TRP [p.R275W]

Farrer et al. (2001) found an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7 of the parkin gene in the proband of a kindred with early-onset Parkinson disease. The proband also carried a 40-bp exon 3 deletion, and RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on different transcripts, consistent with compound heterozygosity.

Oliveira et al. (2003) found R275W among the exon 7 (first RING finger) mutations that occurred mainly in heterozygous Parkinson disease patients with later onset (mean 49.2 years). These mutations were not found in controls. The authors suggested that such heterozygous mutations act as susceptibility alleles for late-onset Parkinson disease.

In the CORE-PD early-onset cohort, Marder et al. (2010) found R275W in compound heterozygotes with a variety of second alleles. It was also the most frequent heterozygous mutation, found in 8 patients. Alcalay et al. (2010) described a patient with early-onset PD who was heterozygous for R275W and also carried the GBA L444P mutation.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX2 TRIPLICATION

In an Italian family with pseudo-dominant inheritance of parkinsonism, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father, with onset at age 57 years, was homozygous for a triplication of parkin exon 2. His affected son, with onset at age 31 years, was compound heterozygous for the triplication and a maternally inherited deletion of exons 3 and 4.

**.0006 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-4DEL

In an Italian family, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the unaffected mother was heterozygous for a deletion of parkin exons 3 and 4. Her affected son was compound heterozygous for this deletion and the paternal exon 2 triplication.

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) found a deletion of exons 3-4 in 6 Hispanic patients with early-onset PD (5 Puerto Rican and 1 Mexican). Both exon 3-4 homozygotes were of Puerto Rican descent, and 3 Puerto Rican patients were compound heterozygous for this deletion and 255delA.

**.0007 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, VAL56GLU [p.V56E]

Hoenicka et al. (2002) identified a previously unreported val56-to-glu (V56E) missense mutation in the parkin gene in Spanish families with autosomal recessive Parkinson disease. Mutations were detected by SSCP, direct sequencing, and restriction digestion of PCR-amplified genomic DNA. ⚠︎

**.0008 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, CYS212TYR [p.C212Y]

Hoenicka et al. (2002) identified a previously unreported cys212-to-tyr (C212Y) missense mutation in the parkin gene in Spanish families with autosomal recessive Parkinson disease. ⚠︎

**.0009 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 1-BP DEL, 225A [c.225dela]

Hoenicka et al. (2002) found a 1-bp deletion in the parkin gene, 225delA, among 5 different parkin mutations identified in 5 of 13 Spanish families with recessive Parkinson disease.

**.0010 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, PRO437LEU [p.P437L]

Poorkaj et al. (2004) found a pro437-to-leu (P437L) substitution in the parkin gene among the mutations detected in 7 of 39 clinic patients with PD onset at or before age 40 years. None of the point mutations found in patients was detected in 96 controls.

Kay et al. (2007) found the P437L variant, a common mutation thought to be pathogenic, in unaffected control subjects.

**.0011 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3DEL

Sasaki et al. (2004) found a homozygous deletion of parkin exon 3 in a patient with AR-JP. At autopsy, lesions were limited to dopaminergic systems such as the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus, and there were no Lewy bodies. Occasional alpha-synuclein- and ubiquitin-positive basophilic inclusions were present in the pedunculopontine nucleus. ⚠︎

**.0012 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 1-BP DEL, 1072T

In a 73-year-old patient from a 7-generation pedigree with 77 parkin mutation carriers, Pramstaller et al. (2005) identified compound heterozygous parkin mutations: del1072T and a deletion of exon 7. Both mutations deleted the In-Between-RING (IBR) domain. At autopsy there was PD-type cell loss, reactive gliosis, and alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Surviving neurons reacted with antibodies to the parkin N terminus but not to the IBR domain.

**.0013 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX7DEL

Pramstaller et al. (2005) found a deletion of parkin exon 7 in compound heterozygosity with del1072T in a 73-year-old patient with Lewy body Parkinson disease from a large pedigree. Both mutations deleted the IBR domain.

**.0014 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, IVS1DS, G-T, +1

In 10 patients with early-onset PD from a genetic isolate in north-eastern Brazil (average onset 30.8 years), Chien et al. (2006) identified a homozygous G-to-T transversion at the splice donor site of intron 1 of the parkin gene (IVS1+1G-T). The mutation cosegregated completely with the phenotype. cDNA analysis showed total loss of parkin transcript in homozygotes, indicating a loss-of-function mutation. A case with neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism and 13 of 15 healthy relatives were heterozygous carriers. Because heterozygotes did not have PD, the authors considered the mutation genuinely recessive.

**.0015 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 1-BP DEL, 255A [c.255dela]

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from a patient with early-onset parkinsonism who was compound heterozygous for a 1-bp deletion in the parkin gene (255delA) and an exon 5 deletion. Fibroblasts from their 5 parkin-mutant patients showed reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, complex I activity, and cellular ATP.

Marder et al. (2010) found 255delA in compound heterozygosity with an exon 3-4 deletion in 3 Puerto Rican patients with early-onset PD, and in the heterozygous state in 1 Mexican patient. The mutation was not detected in 139 Caribbean Hispanic controls.

**.0016 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 2-BP DEL, 202AG [c.202_203delag]

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from patients with early-onset parkinsonism carrying a 2-bp deletion in exon 2 of the parkin gene (202_203delAG). One patient was homozygous for the deletion, and others were compound heterozygous with an exon 2 deletion or an exon 4 deletion. Patient fibroblasts showed reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, complex I activity, and cellular ATP, and increased mitochondrial branching. siRNA knockdown of parkin in control fibroblasts reproduced these defects.

**.0017 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 2-BP DEL, 101AG [c.101-102delag; c.101_102delag; p.Q34R]

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from a patient with early-onset parkinsonism who was compound heterozygous for a 2-bp deletion in the parkin gene, c.101-102delAG (p.Gln34ArgfsX38), and c.1289G-A (G430D). Fibroblasts from their parkin-mutant patients had mitochondrial functional and morphologic defects.

**.0018 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, GLY430ASP [c.1289G>A; p.G430D]

Mortiboys et al. (2008) found a c.1289G-A transition in the parkin gene, resulting in a gly430-to-asp (G430D) substitution, in compound heterozygosity with c.101-102delAG in a patient with early-onset parkinsonism. The patient's fibroblasts were studied for mitochondrial function.

Marder et al. (2010) listed G430D among the parkin mutations found in the CORE-PD early-onset Parkinson disease cohort.
