### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive juvenile Parkinson disease-2 (PARK2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PRKN gene (602544) on chromosome 6q26. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (AR-JP) is a hereditary neurodegenerative disorder in which parkinsonism responds to levodopa (Matsumine et al., 1997; Saito et al., 1998). Its pathology is a highly selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra (Matsumine et al., 1997). A related presentation, early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF), is characterized by early onset and improvement of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep, and may follow a benign clinical course (Matsumine et al., 1998).

### Nomenclature

Matsumine et al. (1998) concluded that early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF) and AR-JP are allelic disorders, and proposed that both belong to the spectrum of a single clinicogenetic entity, chromosome 6q-linked parkinsonism.

### Clinical Features

Yamamura et al. (1973) described paralysis agitans (parkinsonism) of early onset with marked diurnal fluctuation of symptoms.

Takahashi et al. (1994) reported a family in which 4 of 5 sibs developed a similar onset of parkinsonism at an early age; the parents were first cousins. In one sib, a 67-year-old woman, pathologic changes at autopsy were confined to the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus ceruleus, and there were no Lewy bodies.

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) presented 17 patients from 12 Japanese families with familial juvenile parkinsonism suggesting autosomal recessive inheritance. There was a female predominance, and the mean age at onset was 27.8 years. The symptoms of the parkinsonian triad (tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia) were mild, but gait freezing, hyperreflexia, foot dystonia, and retropulsion were relatively prominent. A characteristic finding was amelioration of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep in all patients. Progression was slow. Response to levodopa was satisfactory, but dopa-induced choreic limb dyskinesia and wearing-off phenomenon occurred frequently. The authors concluded that the similarity of clinical findings among these patients, and their differences from other types of parkinsonism, indicated that AR-JP is a distinct clinical entity.

Matsumine et al. (1998) examined 53 members of 17 families with early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF), a dopa-responsive parkinsonism characterized by early onset and improvement of parkinsonian symptoms after sleep. They noted that a pathologic study of one EPDF case showed selective neuronal degeneration in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra without Lewy body formation, and suggested that the disease could be characterized by a benign clinical course.

Saito et al. (1998) reported a mean age at onset of 23.2 years in autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism.

Tassin et al. (1998) studied European families and an Algerian family with early-onset parkinsonism linked to the PARK2 locus. The clinical spectrum was broader than previously reported, with age at onset up to 58 years and painful dystonia in some patients.

Klein et al. (2000) reported a large pedigree from South Tyrol, northern Italy, with adult-onset, clinically typical tremor-dominant parkinsonism of apparently autosomal dominant inheritance. Four affected male sibs with compound heterozygous parkin deletions displayed none of the clinical hallmarks described in patients with previously reported parkin mutations, including diurnal fluctuations, benefit from sleep, foot dystonia, hyperreflexia, and early susceptibility to levodopa-induced dyskinesias. Two affected females in the pedigree carried only 1 of the deletions.

Hayashi et al. (2000) reported a man with AR-JP who developed a dystonic gait at age 32 years, followed by hand tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and impaired postural reflex. He came to autopsy at age 70 years.

van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) reported a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism. The proband had additional mild gait ataxia.

Lücking et al. (2001) reported an Italian family in which the father had onset of parkinsonism at age 57 years and his son at age 31 years.

Lohmann et al. (2003) compared 146 patients with early-onset parkinsonism and parkin mutations with 250 patients without mutations. Patients with mutations had significantly earlier and more symmetrical onset, dystonia more often at onset, hyperreflexia, slower progression, and a tendency toward a greater response to levodopa despite lower doses. However, no single sign distinguished the groups, and on multivariate analysis dystonia at onset and brisk reflexes correlated with age at onset rather than with the presence of a parkin mutation; the younger the age at onset, the slower the evolution of disease. ⚠︎

Ohsawa et al. (2005) performed nerve conduction studies in 9 PARK2 patients and 8 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease and found significantly reduced sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude in 8 of the PARK2 patients, most of whom were asymptomatic. They suggested that sensory axonal neuropathy may be a common clinical feature of PARK2.

Pramstaller et al. (2005) described a 7-generation pedigree of 196 individuals, 25 of whom had levodopa-responsive parkinsonism; Parkin mutations were found in 77 subjects. Of the 25 patients, 5 carried compound heterozygous mutations and met criteria for definite Parkinson disease according to UK PD Society Brain Bank guidelines, 8 carried a single heterozygous mutation, 7 had no Parkin mutation, and the status of 5 deceased patients was unknown.

Chien et al. (2006) studied a genetic isolate from north-eastern Brazil with geographic isolation and multiple consanguineous marriages. DNA was obtained from 26 members: 10 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease, 1 with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism, and 15 unaffected relatives. The average age at onset of PD symptoms was 30.8 years (range, 12 to 46).

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) identified 6 homozygous parkin mutation carriers (5 male, 1 female; 4 white non-Hispanic and 2 Hispanic of Puerto Rican descent) with a mean age at onset of 28.5 years, 21 compound heterozygotes with a mean age at onset of 28.9 years, and 37 heterozygotes with a mean age at onset of 37.2 years. Dystonia as a presenting sign did not differ between parkin carriers (0%) and noncarriers (1.7%), nor did response to levodopa (93.4% of carriers vs 90.5% of noncarriers).

Alcalay et al. (2010) reported 27 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease who were PRKN homozygotes or compound heterozygotes (11 female, 16 male), with a mean age at onset of 29 years, and 33 PRKN heterozygotes with a mean age at onset of 37 years.

Alcalay et al. (2011) gave the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) to probands with PD onset at or before age 50 years, including 9 Parkin compound heterozygotes and 10 heterozygotes. Compound heterozygotes scored higher (31.9) than heterozygotes (20.1) or noncarriers (19.9) (p less than 0.001), a difference that persisted after adjustment for age, gender, disease duration, and smoking. The authors concluded that compound heterozygotes with PD have olfaction within the normal range, whereas olfaction is significantly reduced among Parkin heterozygotes with PD.

### Inheritance

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) strongly suspected autosomal recessive inheritance of juvenile parkinsonism in 12 Japanese families because the parents were consanguineous in all but 1 family and all patients belonged to a single generation.

Klein et al. (2000) and Lücking et al. (2001) reported families with parkin mutations in which inheritance appeared to be dominant. In the Italian family of Lücking et al. (2001), an affected father homozygous for an exon 2 triplication and an unaffected mother heterozygous for exon 3-4 deletions had a compound heterozygous affected son; the authors concluded that pseudo-dominant inheritance of parkin mutations has to be considered in early-onset parkinsonism.

Chien et al. (2006) noted the absence of PD in heterozygous carriers of the parkin mutation in a Brazilian isolate, indicating a genuinely recessive nature and suggesting that parkin haploinsufficiency is not a relevant risk factor for early- or late-onset PD, although it could facilitate the emergence of neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism; one heterozygous carrier had likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism.

The role of heterozygous parkin mutations has been debated. Poorkaj et al. (2004) noted that 70% of their parkin cases were heterozygous and that it was unclear whether heterozygous mutations are pathogenic. Khan et al. (2005) concluded that although parkin disease is usually autosomal recessive, parkin heterozygosity is a risk factor for nigrostriatal dysfunction and may contribute to late-onset Parkinson disease in some carriers. Marder et al. (2010) found that heterozygous carriers had a younger age at onset than noncarriers after adjustment for ethnicity and family history and suggested that heterozygosity may increase susceptibility to PD rather than directly cause it. Alcalay et al. (2010) noted that PRKN carriers were more likely than LRRK2 carriers to report a family history of PD and suggested that even a single PRKN mutation may be a risk factor for early-onset PD. In contrast, Zhu et al. (2022) found that UK Biobank participants with 1 pathogenic PRKN variant were as likely as noncarriers to have Parkinson disease (OR = 0.91) or a parent with Parkinson disease (OR = 1.12), and that carriers of 1 pathogenic variant in the NIH-PD + AMP-PD cohort were as likely as noncarriers to have Parkinson disease (OR = 1.29); they concluded that heterozygous pathogenic PRKN mutations are common but do not increase the risk of Parkinson disease.

### Mapping

Matsumine et al. (1997) first found a family with AR-JP in which the disease segregated perfectly with a diallelic polymorphism of the manganese superoxide dismutase gene (SOD2). Extending linkage analysis to 13 families with AR-JP, they mapped the gene to chromosome 6q25.2-q27, with maximal cumulative pairwise lod scores of 7.26 at D6S305 (theta = 0.03) and 7.71 at D6S253 (theta = 0.02). Obligate recombination events and multipoint analysis placed the gene in a 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S264.

Matsumine et al. (1998) analyzed 151 meioses in 17 families with EPDF. Multipoint linkage analysis gave a peak lod score of 14.2 at 1.0 cM telomeric to D6S305 and placed the locus in the 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S253, the same position as the AR-JP locus. The highest 2-point lod score was 10.13 at theta = 0 for D6S305.

Saito et al. (1998) carried out detailed linkage analysis of AR-JP using densely spaced markers on 6q25.2-q27. The highest cumulative pairwise lod score was 9.13 at D6S1579 (theta = 0.05), and the highest multipoint lod score was 12.4 at a locus 3 cM telomeric to D6S1599. Obligate recombinations narrowed the candidate region to 13 cM between D6S1579 and D6S264. Strong linkage disequilibrium with D6S1579 and D6S1599 (P less than 0.0001 for both) suggested that the gene lies near the 4-cM region spanning these markers.

Tassin et al. (1998) tested 1 Algerian and 10 European multiplex families with early-onset parkinsonism for linkage to 6q25.2-q27 using marker D6S305. Homogeneity analysis gave a conditional probability of linkage greater than 0.9 in 8 families. Haplotype reconstruction and the smallest region of homozygosity in 2 consanguineous families narrowed the interval to 11.3 cM. In the Algerian family, 2 colocalizing microsatellite markers, D6S411 and D6S1550, were deleted in the homozygous state and segregated with the disease; taking this deletion into account would reduce the candidate region to less than 1 cM.

Jones et al. (1998) found linkage of AR-JP to 6q25.2-q27 in 15 families from 4 distinct ethnic backgrounds; a full genomic screen excluded other regions. Recombination events restricted the locus to a 6.9-cM region, and SOD2 was excluded by recombination. Apparent homozygosity for null alleles at D6S955 in 1 family suggested a deletion.

Kitada et al. (1998) noted that marker D6S305, to which AR-JP is strongly linked, was deleted in 1 Japanese AR-JP patient.

In the South Tyrolean pedigree with apparently dominant adult-onset parkinsonism, Klein et al. (2000) excluded linkage to the autosomal dominant parkinsonism regions on chromosomes 2p, 4p, and 4q; haplotype analysis implicated the parkin locus on 6q.

Oliveira et al. (2003) had found linkage of idiopathic PD to the chromosome 6 region containing the parkin gene (D6S305, lod score 5.47) in families with at least 1 member whose age at onset was younger than 40 years.

In a north-eastern Brazilian genetic isolate in which genealogic studies connected 255 individuals, 15 with PD, Chien et al. (2006) found homozygosity in PD patients for markers across the PARK2 locus.

### Heterogeneity

Tassin et al. (1998) concluded that PARK2 appears to be an important locus for AR-JP in European patients.

Mitsui et al. (1994) reported 2 sibs with hereditary parkinsonism with pyramidal signs and cerebellar ataxia in whom anticholinergics, but not levodopa, had a dramatic beneficial effect; they proposed this as a hereditary variant of early-onset Parkinson disease distinct from the levodopa-sensitive forms of juvenile Parkinson disease. Ishikawa and Miyatake (1995) reported a family in which 8 members in 3 generations had autosomal dominant juvenile dystonia-parkinsonism with sleep benefit, marked levodopa response, wearing-off, and dopa-induced choreic dyskinesia, together with neck and foot dystonia; they considered these patients to have a specific form of hereditary dystonia-parkinsonism.

### Molecular Genetics

Using positional cloning within a microdeletion, Kitada et al. (1998) isolated a 2,960-bp cDNA whose 1,395-bp open reading frame encodes a 465-amino acid protein with moderate similarity to ubiquitin at the N terminus and a RING-finger motif at the C terminus; they named the protein parkin. The gene spans more than 500 kb and contains 12 exons. In 1 Japanese AR-JP patient, exons 3 through 7 were deleted, and 4 other AR-JP patients from 3 unrelated families had a deletion affecting exon 4 alone. A 4.5-kb parkin transcript, abundant in brain including the substantia nigra, was shorter in brain tissue from 1 group of exon 4-deleted patients.

In 4 affected male sibs from the South Tyrolean pedigree, Klein et al. (2000) identified compound heterozygous deletions in the parkin gene, 1 large and 1 truncating. Two affected females carried only the truncating deletion in the heterozygous state with an apparently normal second allele.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found that an autopsied man with AR-JP and his sibs had a homozygous deletion of exon 4 of the parkin gene.

In a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism, van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) found a heterozygous missense mutation in combination with a heterozygous exon deletion in the parkin gene.

In 21 patients with early-onset parkinsonism, Hedrich et al. (2001) detected 3 parkin mutations (a single-base substitution in exon 3 and small deletions in exon 9) by SSCP and sequence analysis, and alterations of gene dosage in 7 patients by quantitative duplex PCR. These included heterozygous and compound heterozygous deletions of exons 2, 3, 5, and 7, a homozygous exon 7 deletion, and 2 heterozygous duplications of exon 4. Two patients carried more than 2 parkin mutations.

In an Italian family, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father (onset at age 57 years) was homozygous for a triplication of exon 2 of the parkin gene, the unaffected mother was heterozygous for deletions of exons 3 and 4, and the affected son (onset at age 31 years) was a compound heterozygote carrying both mutations.

Farrer et al. (2001) sequenced the parkin gene in 38 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before age 41 years) and followed up the families of 2 probands with mutations. Haplotype analysis revealed a common chromosome 6 carrying a novel 40-bp exon 3 deletion that cosegregated with disease. In the proband of the smaller kindred, an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7 was found in addition to the exon 3 deletion; RNA analysis showed that the mutations were on alternate transcripts.

Hoenicka et al. (2002) studied 19 Spanish kindreds with familial Parkinson disease (13 recessive, 4 dominant, 2 uncertain) and found 5 different Park2 mutations in 5 of the 13 recessive families: 2 novel mutations, V56E and C212Y, and deletion of exons 3 and 5 and 225delA, which had been described in other ethnic groups. They concluded that Park2 mutations accounted for 38% of families with recessive parkinsonism in Spain. Two heterozygous carriers of a single mutation developed symptoms either in late adulthood or after brief exposure to haloperidol, and the authors suggested that heterozygous mutations could be a risk factor for parkinsonism.

Foroud et al. (2003) screened the parkin gene by quantitative PCR and exon sequencing in families with at least 2 sibs with PD who had early-onset disease or a positive lod score at an intron 7 marker. They identified 25 different mutations in 103 individuals from 47 families; 41 had mutations in both alleles and 62 had a single mutation.

Oliveira et al. (2003) screened 174 multiplex PD families and 133 additional PD families and found parkin mutations in 18% of early-onset and 2% of late-onset families (5% overall). Age at onset of patients with mutations ranged from 12 to 71 years; excluding exon 7 mutations, the mean age at onset of carriers was 31.5 years. Mutations in exon 7, which encodes the first RING finger (C253W, R256C, R275W, and D280N), were observed primarily in heterozygous patients with a later mean age at onset (49.2 years).

Poorkaj et al. (2004) found parkin mutations in 7 of 39 clinic patients with PD onset at or before age 40 years; 2 were compound heterozygous and 5 heterozygous. Mutations included deletions in exons 2, 3, and 8, duplications in exons 2 to 4 and 9, and a P437L substitution; 78% of mutations were deletions or multiplications. None of the point mutations in patients was found in 96 controls, but a novel R402W substitution was found in 1 patient and 1 control. The phenotypes were consistent with idiopathic PD.

Sasaki et al. (2004) reported an autopsied patient with AR-JP and a homozygous exon 3 deletion in the parkin gene.

Khan et al. (2005) used 18F-dopa PET to study 13 unrelated asymptomatic carriers of a single parkin mutation and found significantly reduced 18F-dopa uptake in the caudate, putamen, and ventral and dorsal midbrain compared with controls; 4 of the 13 had subtle extrapyramidal signs.

In a 73-year-old patient from a 7-generation pedigree, Pramstaller et al. (2005) found 2 mutant parkin alleles, delExon7 and del1072T. Four other patients in the pedigree were compound heterozygous, and 8 patients carried only a heterozygous mutation. ⚠︎

In the Brazilian genetic isolate, Chien et al. (2006) identified by genomic sequencing a novel homozygous splice site mutation in the parkin gene (IVS1+1G-T) that completely cosegregated with early-onset PD. Thirteen of 15 healthy relatives and the patient with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism were heterozygous carriers. cDNA analysis showed total loss of parkin transcript in homozygous carriers, delineating the mutation as loss of function.

Kay et al. (2007) sequenced the parkin gene in 302 patients with Parkinson disease and 301 controls and replicated the findings in 1,260 more patients and 1,657 more controls. They found 34 variants, 21 of them novel (12 polymorphisms and 22 rare variants), and patients and controls did not differ in the frequency, type, or functional location of the variants. P437L, a common mutation thought to be pathogenic, was present in unaffected controls. They concluded that a single heterozygous point mutation in a patient, without a second mutation, should not be taken as the cause of disease unless family data and functional studies support it.

Wang et al. (2008) screened 247 probands with PD onset at age 50 years or younger for parkin point mutations and exon deletions or duplications and found mutations in 25 (10.1%), 18 (72.0%) of whom were heterozygotes; 1 parkin homozygote was reported in 2 sibs with PD. Using the kin-cohort method, they estimated the cumulative incidence of PD to age 65 years in carrier relatives at 7.0% (95% CI, 0.4%-71.9%), compared with 1.7% in noncarrier relatives of cases (P = 0.59) and 1.1% in relatives of controls. ⚠︎

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from 5 patients with early-onset parkinsonism and homozygous or compound heterozygous parkin mutations: 255delA in exon 2 and deletion of exon 5 (patient 1); homozygous 202_203delAG in exon 2 (patient 2); 202_203delAG and deletion of exon 2 (patient 3); 202_203delAG and deletion of exon 4 (patient 4); and c.101_102delAG (p.Gln34ArgfsX38) and c.1289G-A (gly430-to-asp, G430D) (patient 5). Mitochondrial membrane potential was lower in all 5 cultures, by an average of 30%.

Marder et al. (2010) found parkin mutations in 64 of 956 patients with early-onset PD (6.7%): 6 homozygotes, 21 compound heterozygotes, and 37 heterozygotes. Mutations found in homozygous state included 255delA, the exon 3 40-bp deletion, an exon 3-4 deletion, and an exon 6 deletion; compound heterozygous genotypes included 255delA with the exon 3-4 deletion and R275W with G430D, and other mutations in compound heterozygotes included R42P, R366Q, C212Y, R256C, and 202delAG. Copy number variation was found in 52.3% of carriers: 31.6% of heterozygotes, 83.3% of homozygotes, and 81.0% of compound heterozygotes.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with PD onset at or before age 50 years and found 33 PRKN heterozygotes and 27 homozygotes or compound heterozygotes. Several patients carried a heterozygous PRKN mutation together with a mutation in another gene: a Jewish man with onset at 41 years carried an exon 5-6 deletion and LRRK2 (609007) G2019S; a man with onset at 34 years carried an exon 2 deletion and GBA1 (606463) L444P; a woman with onset at 34 years carried R275W and GBA1 L444P; and a woman with onset at 42 years carried D53E and GBA1 N370S.

Kay et al. (2010) screened 2,091 patients with Parkinson disease and 1,686 controls for PARK2 deletions, multiplications, and copy number variations. Thirteen patients had 2 CNV mutations, 5 had 1 CNV and 1 point mutation, and 18 had 1 CNV mutation; mutations in patients spanned exons 2 to 9. In whites, carrying 1 CNV was not associated with increased risk (odds ratio 1.05, p = 0.89) or earlier onset of PD, and the authors found no compelling evidence that heterozygous PARK2 mutations, alone or combined with known risk factors (SNCA, MAPT, APOE, smoking, coffee intake), are associated with PD.

Zhu et al. (2022) identified 2,692 PRKN copy number variants from genotyping array data in the UK Biobank. Deletions or duplications involving exon 2 accounted for roughly half, and 88% involved exons 2, 3, or 4.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Klein et al. (2000) concluded that the phenotypic spectrum of parkin mutations is broader than previously reported: patients with compound heterozygous parkin deletions were clinically indistinguishable from most patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease, and the gene may be important in the more frequent late-onset typical form.

Foroud et al. (2003) found that 35 (34%) of subjects with parkin mutations had onset at age 60 years or older, and 30 of these 35 (86%) had a detectable mutation on only 1 allele. Few significant clinical differences were seen among individuals with 2, 1, or no mutated parkin copies.

Oliveira et al. (2003) found that exon 7 mutations occurred mainly in heterozygous patients with a later mean age at onset (49.2 years) and were absent from 920 control chromosomes; they suggested that heterozygous parkin mutations, especially those in exon 7, act as susceptibility alleles for late-onset PD.

Lohmann et al. (2003) found that carriers of at least 1 missense parkin mutation had a higher UPDRS motor score than those with 2 truncating mutations, and that missense mutations in functional domains resulted in earlier onset. Patients with a single heterozygous mutation had significantly later and more asymmetric onset and more frequent levodopa-induced fluctuations and dystonia than patients with 2 mutations.

By survival analysis, Pramstaller et al. (2005) found that the age-at-onset distribution of patients with compound heterozygous parkin mutations differed significantly from that of both heterozygous carriers and patients without detectable mutations.

Marder et al. (2010) found that compound heterozygotes and homozygotes each had a significantly younger age at onset than heterozygotes, and that every carrier group had a younger onset than noncarriers. Onset before 40 years and a family history of PD in a first-degree relative were associated with carrying any parkin mutation.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that age at onset was significantly younger in PRKN mutation carriers than in carriers of other mutations; biallelic PRKN mutations were found in 35% of patients with onset at or before age 20 years, compared with 0.5% of those with onset at 41 to 50 years.

Among relatives without PD, Alcalay et al. (2011) found similar UPSIT performance in Parkin heterozygotes (32.5) and noncarriers (32.4), both better than heterozygotes with PD, and suggested that further research is needed to assess whether these findings reflect different neuropathology in heterozygotes and compound heterozygotes.

### Pathogenesis

At autopsy of an affected woman, Takahashi et al. (1994) found neuronal loss and gliosis in the medial and ventrolateral substantia nigra pars compacta, with reduced neuron numbers and low melanin content elsewhere in the pars compacta and locus ceruleus but no gliosis or extraneuronal free melanin suggestive of a neurodegenerative process. There were no Lewy bodies, and the picture differed from that of Lewy body Parkinson disease.

In a patient with 6q-linked AR-JP, Mori et al. (1998) found neuronal loss and gliosis restricted to the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus and no Lewy bodies, but neurofibrillary tangles and argyrophilic astrocytes in the cerebral cortex and brainstem nuclei, findings not previously reported in AR-JP; they suggested pathologic heterogeneity of 6q-linked AR-JP.

At autopsy of a 70-year-old man with a homozygous parkin exon 4 deletion, Hayashi et al. (2000) found loss of pigmented neurons and gliosis in the substantia nigra pars compacta (most pronounced medially and ventrolaterally) and locus ceruleus, low melanin content in remaining nigral neurons, and no Lewy bodies on alpha-synuclein immunostaining. They also found neuronal loss and fibrillary gliosis in the substantia nigra pars reticulata, not previously reported in AR-JP.

In the proband of the Dutch family of van de Warrenburg et al. (2001), pathologic study showed neuronal loss in parts of the spinocerebellar system in addition to selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta. Lewy bodies and neurofibrillary tangles were absent, but tau pathology was found.

Using PET, Portman et al. (2001) found that 2 brothers with early-onset parkinsonism caused by parkin mutation had cerebral energy metabolism that did not differ, but a nigrostriatal dopaminergic pattern clearly different from that of sporadic PD; they concluded that the parkinsonism was pathophysiologically different from PD. ⚠︎

Shimura et al. (2001) identified in normal human brain a protein complex of parkin as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, UbcH7 as its associated E2 enzyme, and a 22-kD glycosylated form of alpha-synuclein (alphaSp22) as its substrate. Mutant parkin associated with autosomal recessive PD failed to bind alphaSp22, and in vitro normal but not mutant parkin converted alphaSp22 into polyubiquitinated high molecular weight species. AlphaSp22 accumulated in a non-ubiquitinated form in parkin-deficient PD brains. The authors concluded that loss of parkin function causes pathologic accumulation of alphaSp22.

Farrer et al. (2001) found Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease at autopsy in a compound heterozygous parkin mutation carrier and suggested that compound heterozygous mutations and loss of parkin protein may lead to early-onset parkinsonism with Lewy body pathology, while a hemizygous mutation may confer increased susceptibility to typical PD.

In an autopsied AR-JP patient with a homozygous exon 3 deletion, Sasaki et al. (2004) found moderate to severe depletion of melanin-containing neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta with gliosis, and mild neuronal loss with extraneuronal melanin in the locus ceruleus. Alpha-synuclein- and ubiquitin-positive inclusions with a doughnut or round shape were present in the neuropils of the pedunculopontine nucleus. They concluded that different parkin gene abnormalities may produce differences in the degree and distribution of neuronal degeneration, including the absence or presence of Lewy bodies.

Autopsy of the 73-year-old compound heterozygous patient of Pramstaller et al. (2005) showed PD-type cell loss, reactive gliosis, and alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Surviving neurons were immunoreactive with antibodies to the parkin N terminus but not to the IBR domain, which had been deleted by both mutations.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) found that fibroblasts from 5 patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous parkin mutations had lower mitochondrial membrane potential (by 30% in glucose and 70% in galactose medium), complex I activity lower by about 42%, reduced complex I-linked ATP production, and cellular ATP levels reduced by 58%; complex II, III, and IV activities were not reduced. Mitochondrial branching was increased and correlated with lower complex I-linked ATP production and complex I activity, and FRAP assays showed reduced functional connectivity of the mitochondrial matrix that worsened after rotenone exposure. siRNA knockdown of parkin by 80% in control fibroblasts reproduced these defects, whereas 50% knockdown, mimicking haploinsufficiency, had no effect; the authors concluded that this was consistent with true recessive inheritance.

### Diagnosis

Hedrich et al. (2001) developed a quantitative duplex PCR method using fluorescence resonance energy transfer on the LightCycler to screen all 12 parkin exons for deletions and duplications, and concluded that mutational analysis of the parkin gene should include gene dosage studies. Lücking et al. (2001) similarly recommended sensitive techniques, such as semiquantitative multiplex PCR, to detect exon multiplications and deletions.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) concluded that parkin cases can present as typical idiopathic PD distinguishable only by molecular testing, and that diagnosis and counseling require a better understanding of the mode of inheritance, penetrance, and carrier frequencies.

Ohsawa et al. (2005) proposed that reduced sural SNAP amplitude could serve as a diagnostic indicator of PARK2.

Alcalay et al. (2010) concluded that patients with early-onset PD who are of Jewish or Hispanic ancestry, who had onset at or before age 30 years, or who have a first-degree relative with PD may benefit from genetic counseling; because penetrance is unknown, they saw little or no role for predictive or prenatal testing.

Zhu et al. (2022) validated genotyping arrays for detecting PRKN copy number variants against whole genome sequencing and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification in NIH-PD participants. Functional assays in patient fibroblasts resolved variants of unclear significance in biallelic carriers.

### Clinical Management

Mortiboys et al. (2008) found that treatment of parkin-mutant fibroblasts with the glutathione precursor OTCA restored mitochondrial membrane potential to 95% of control levels and fully restored cellular ATP, apparently through a compensatory increase in complex II activity rather than correction of the complex I defect; glutathione methyl ester gave only partial rescue.

### Population Genetics

Matsumine et al. (1998) suggested that EPDF has multiple founder mutations, based on the high rate of nonconsanguinity (8 of 17 families), the absence of commonly shared haplotypes, and the wide geographic spread of the families' origins.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) found parkin mutations in 7 of 39 (18%) movement disorder clinic patients with PD onset at or before age 40 years; 10% of the clinic's PD patients had early onset, and the mutation frequency was 0.12 (95% CI 0.04-0.19).

Kay et al. (2007) concluded that their study did not support heterozygous parkin sequence variants as risk factors for PD; whether heterozygous dosage anomalies are associated with PD remained to be determined.

Wang et al. (2008) found parkin mutations in 25 of 247 (10.1%) probands with PD onset at 50 years or younger, 18 (72.0%) of whom were heterozygotes.

Marder et al. (2010) screened 956 patients with early-onset PD (onset before 51 years) from 13 centers and found parkin mutations in 6.7% (3.9% heterozygous, 0.6% homozygous, and 2.2% compound heterozygous). Prevalence declined with age at onset, from 57% with onset before 20 years to 3% with onset at 40 to 50 years. Hispanic ethnicity was associated with carrying a parkin mutation (OR 2.7 vs white non-Hispanics); the exon 3-4 deletion and 255delA were common among Hispanics, especially Puerto Ricans, and 255delA was not found in 139 Caribbean Hispanic controls.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with PD onset at or before age 50 years from 13 movement disorder centers and found PRKN mutations in 6.7%. Hispanic patients were more likely to carry PRKN mutations than non-Hispanic patients (15.6% vs 5.9%).

Kay et al. (2010) found heterozygous PARK2 CNV mutations in 0.95% of 1,686 controls and 0.86% of 2,091 patients with PD. All CNVs in the 16 control subjects were in exons 1 to 4, sparing exons encoding functionally critical domains, whereas mutations in patients spanned exons 2 to 9. ⚠︎

In the UK Biobank, Zhu et al. (2022) found a pathogenic PRKN mutation in 1.8% of participants and 2 mutations in about 1 in 7,800 participants.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-7 DEL [p.D6S]

In a Japanese patient with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism, Kitada et al. (1998) found a microdeletion including marker D6S305 that removed exons 3 through 7 of the parkin gene; this deletion was used to isolate the gene by positional cloning.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX4 DEL

Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion affecting exon 4 alone of the parkin gene in 4 patients with AR-JP from 3 unrelated families. The 4.5-kb parkin transcript was shorter in brain tissue from 1 group of exon 4-deleted patients.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found a homozygous exon 4 deletion of the parkin gene in a man with AR-JP who had onset with a dystonic gait at age 32 years and his sibs. At autopsy at age 70 years there was loss of pigmented neurons and gliosis in the substantia nigra pars compacta and locus ceruleus, neuronal loss and fibrillary gliosis in the pars reticulata, and no Lewy bodies.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX2 TRIPLICATION

In an Italian family with pseudo-dominant inheritance of parkinsonism, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father (onset at age 57 years) was homozygous for a triplication of exon 2 of the parkin gene, which was so far unique. His affected son (onset at age 31 years) was compound heterozygous for the triplication and deletions of exons 3 and 4 inherited from his unaffected heterozygous mother.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-4 DEL

In an Italian family, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected son (onset at age 31 years) was compound heterozygous for deletion of exons 3 and 4 of the parkin gene, inherited from his unaffected heterozygous mother, and a triplication of exon 2 inherited from his affected homozygous father.

Marder et al. (2010) found the exon 3-4 deletion in homozygous state in 2 patients with early-onset PD, both of Puerto Rican descent, and in compound heterozygous state with 255delA in Hispanic patients. They noted that the exon 3-4 deletion was common among Hispanics, especially Puerto Ricans.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 40-BP DEL, EX3

Farrer et al. (2001) found a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3 of the parkin gene on a common chromosome 6 haplotype that cosegregated with disease in 2 kindreds. In the proband of the smaller kindred, the deletion was compound heterozygous with R275W, the mutations lying on alternate transcripts by RNA analysis; this proband had Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease at autopsy.

Marder et al. (2010) found the exon 3 40-bp deletion in homozygous state among homozygous parkin mutation carriers with early-onset PD, and also in heterozygous carriers.

**.0006 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, ARG275TRP [p.R275W]

In the proband of a kindred with early-onset parkinsonism, Farrer et al. (2001) found an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7 of the parkin gene in compound heterozygosity with a 40-bp exon 3 deletion; RNA analysis demonstrated that the mutations were on alternate transcripts. The proband had Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease at autopsy.

Oliveira et al. (2003) found R275W among exon 7 (first RING finger) mutations that were observed primarily in heterozygous PD patients with a later mean age at onset (49.2 years) and were absent from 920 control chromosomes.

Marder et al. (2010) found R275W in compound heterozygosity with G430D in a white patient with early-onset PD, and in heterozygous state in other patients.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found heterozygous R275W together with the GBA1 L444P variant in a woman with onset of PD at age 34 years.
