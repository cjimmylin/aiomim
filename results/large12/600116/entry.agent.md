### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive juvenile Parkinson disease-2 (PARK2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the parkin gene (PRKN) on chromosome 6q25.2-q27.

### Description

Autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism (AR-JP) is a levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. Pathologically it shows highly selective degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra (Matsumine et al., 1997). Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) described it as a distinct clinical entity with early onset and slow progression. In their patients the parkinsonian triad was mild, while gait freezing, hyperreflexia, foot dystonia, and retropulsion were relatively prominent, and symptoms improved after sleep. Levodopa-induced dyskinesia and wearing-off phenomenon were frequent.

### Nomenclature

Matsumine et al. (1998) found that early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF) mapped to the same position as autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism. They concluded that EPDF and ARJP are allelic disorders and should be included in the spectrum of a single clinicogenetic entity of chromosome 6q-linked parkinsonism. Kitada et al. (1998) named the protein product of the causative gene 'Parkin.'

### Clinical Features

Yamamura et al. (1973) described paralysis agitans of early onset with marked diurnal fluctuation of symptoms.

Takahashi et al. (1994) described a family in which 4 of 5 sibs developed a similar juvenile-onset parkinsonism that improved after sleep. The parents were first cousins. One sib, a 67-year-old woman, came to autopsy. Pathologic changes were confined to the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNPC) and the locus ceruleus. The SNPC showed obvious neuronal loss and gliosis in the medial and ventrolateral regions. In the rest of the SNPC and in the locus ceruleus, neurons were reduced in number and most had low melanin content, but there was no detectable gliosis. There were no Lewy bodies, and the overall picture differed from Lewy body Parkinson disease.

Mitsui et al. (1994) reported 2 sibs with hereditary parkinsonism accompanied by pyramidal signs and cerebellar ataxia. Anticholinergics had a dramatic beneficial effect in both, but levodopa did not. The authors proposed that this was a new hereditary variant of early-onset Parkinson disease, distinct from the levodopa-sensitive forms of juvenile Parkinson disease.

Ishikawa and Miyatake (1995) reported a family with hereditary juvenile dystonia-parkinsonism, which they considered autosomal dominant. Eight members in 3 generations had parkinsonism, sleep benefit, marked efficacy of levodopa, wearing-off phenomenon, and dopa-induced choreic dyskinesia. One patient had mainly dystonic movement that worsened on levodopa. Patients also had neck dystonia (torticollis and retrocollis) and foot dystonia.

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) described 17 patients from 12 Japanese families with familial juvenile parkinsonism. The parents were consanguineous in all but 1 family. Patients were female-predominant, had a mean age at onset of 27.8 years, and showed slow progression. Tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia were mild, but gait freezing, hyperreflexia, foot dystonia, and retropulsion were relatively prominent. All patients improved after sleep. Response to levodopa was satisfactory, but dopa-induced choreic limb dyskinesia and wearing-off phenomenon occurred frequently.

Matsumine et al. (1998) described early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation (EPDF), a dopa-responsive parkinsonism with early onset and improvement after sleep. A pathologic study of 1 case showed selective neuronal degeneration in the zona compacta of the substantia nigra without Lewy bodies. The authors suggested that the disease could be characterized by a benign clinical course.

Tassin et al. (1998) found that the clinical spectrum in their European and Algerian families linked to the PARK2 locus was broader than previously reported. Age at onset was up to 58 years, and some patients had painful dystonia.

Mori et al. (1998) reported pathologic and biochemical studies in a patient with 6q-linked AR-JP. Neuronal loss and gliosis were restricted to the substantia nigra and the locus ceruleus. No Lewy bodies were found. However, there were neurofibrillary tangles and argyrophilic astrocytes in the cerebral cortex and brainstem nuclei, which the authors took to suggest pathologic heterogeneity.

Klein et al. (2000) reported a large pedigree from South Tyrol, northern Italy, with adult-onset, clinically typical, tremor-dominant parkinsonism and apparently autosomal dominant inheritance. The affected individuals were clinically indistinguishable from most patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease. None had diurnal fluctuations, benefit from sleep, foot dystonia, hyperreflexia, or early susceptibility to levodopa-induced dyskinesias. ⚠︎

Hayashi et al. (2000) reported the neuropathology of a 70-year-old man with AR-JP. At age 32 years he developed a dystonic gait, followed by hand tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and impaired postural reflexes, which responded to levodopa. At autopsy there was loss of pigmented neurons and gliosis in the SNPC, most pronounced in the medial and ventrolateral regions, and in the locus ceruleus. Remaining SNPC neurons had low melanin content. Alpha-synuclein immunostaining confirmed that there were no Lewy bodies. The authors also found neuronal loss and fibrillary gliosis in the substantia nigra pars reticulata, which had not been reported previously in AR-JP.

Van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) described a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism. Besides parkinsonism, the proband had mild gait ataxia. Pathologically there was neuronal loss in parts of the spinocerebellar system in addition to selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SNPC. Lewy bodies and neurofibrillary tangles were absent, but tau pathology was present.

Portman et al. (2001) used PET to study 2 brothers with early-onset parkinsonism caused by parkin mutation. Cerebral energy metabolism did not differ from sporadic Parkinson disease, but the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pattern clearly did. The authors concluded that the parkinsonism in these patients was pathophysiologically different from Parkinson disease.

Farrer et al. (2001) found Lewy body pathology typical of idiopathic Parkinson disease at autopsy in the proband of a kindred with compound heterozygous parkin mutations (see Molecular Genetics).

Sasaki et al. (2004) reported an autopsy of an AR-JP patient with a homozygous exon 3 deletion in the parkin gene. Lesions were limited to dopaminergic neuron systems such as the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Pigmented neurons in the pars compacta were moderately to severely depleted, with gliosis. No Lewy bodies were seen in the pars compacta or the locus ceruleus. However, alpha-synuclein- and ubiquitin-positive inclusions of doughnut or round shape were found in the neuropils of the pedunculopontine nucleus.

Pramstaller et al. (2005) reported a 7-generation pedigree of 196 individuals, 25 of whom had levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. A 73-year-old patient with 2 mutant parkin alleles came to autopsy. He had Parkinson disease-type cell loss, reactive gliosis, and alpha-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus. Surviving neurons reacted with antibodies to the N terminus of parkin but not to the In-Between-RING (IBR) domain, which both mutations had deleted.

Khan et al. (2005) performed 18F-dopa PET in 13 asymptomatic parkin heterozygotes. Compared with controls, uptake was significantly reduced in the caudate, putamen, and ventral and dorsal midbrain. Four of the heterozygotes had subtle extrapyramidal signs.

Ohsawa et al. (2005) performed nerve conduction studies in 9 PARK2 patients and 8 patients with idiopathic Parkinson disease. Sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude was significantly reduced in 8 of the PARK2 patients, most of whom had no symptoms of it. The authors suggested that sensory axonal neuropathy may be a common feature of PARK2.

In the CORE-PD study, Alcalay et al. (2011) gave the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) to 44 probands with Parkinson disease onset at 50 years or younger and to 80 of their family members. The probands comprised 10 parkin heterozygotes, 9 compound heterozygotes, and 25 noncarriers. Among probands, compound heterozygotes had higher UPSIT scores (31.9) than heterozygotes (20.1) or noncarriers (19.9). Among relatives without PD, heterozygotes (32.5) and noncarriers (32.4) scored similarly. Compound heterozygotes with Parkinson disease had olfaction within the normal range.

### Inheritance

Ishikawa and Tsuji (1996) strongly suspected autosomal recessive inheritance because the parents were consanguineous in all but 1 of 12 families and all patients belonged to a single generation. Matsumine et al. (1997) mapped the autosomal recessive form by linkage analysis.

Some families show apparently dominant transmission. In the South Tyrolean pedigree of Klein et al. (2000), inheritance appeared autosomal dominant, but the affected brothers were compound heterozygous for parkin deletions. Lücking et al. (2001) reported pseudodominant inheritance in an Italian family: an affected father was homozygous for a parkin mutation and his affected son was a compound heterozygote. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Matsumine et al. (1997) used a diallelic polymorphism of the SOD2 gene and found perfect cosegregation with the disease in a family with AR-JP. Extending the analysis to 13 families with AR-JP, they localized the gene to chromosome 6q25.2-q27. Maximal cumulative pairwise lod scores were 7.26 at D6S305 (theta = 0.03) and 7.71 at D6S253 (theta = 0.02). Obligate recombinations and multipoint analysis placed the gene in a 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S264.

Matsumine et al. (1998) studied 53 members of 17 families with early-onset parkinsonism with diurnal fluctuation, covering 151 meioses. Multipoint linkage analysis gave a peak lod score of 14.2 at 1.0 cM telomeric to D6S305. This placed the locus in the 17-cM interval between D6S437 and D6S253, the same position as AR-JP. The highest 2-point lod score was 10.13 at D6S305 (theta = 0).

Saito et al. (1998) refined the AR-JP locus with densely placed markers. The highest cumulative pairwise lod score was 9.13 at D6S1579 (theta = 0.05), and the highest multipoint lod score was 12.4 at 3 cM telomeric to D6S1599. Obligate recombinations narrowed the candidate region to 13 cM between D6S1579 and D6S264. D6S1579 and D6S1599 showed strong linkage disequilibrium with the AR-JP locus.

Tassin et al. (1998) tested 1 Algerian and 10 European multiplex families with early-onset Parkinson disease for linkage to the locus. Eight families were analyzed further. Haplotype reconstruction and the smallest region of homozygosity in 2 consanguineous families reduced the interval to 11.3 cM. In the Algerian family, 2 microsatellite markers (D6S411 and D6S1550) appeared to be deleted and segregated with the disease. Taking this into account would reduce the candidate region to less than 1 cM.

Jones et al. (1998) found linkage to 6q25.2-q27 in 15 families from 4 ethnic backgrounds, and a full genomic screen excluded other candidate regions. Recombination events restricted the locus to a 6.9-cM region and excluded SOD2. Apparent homozygosity for null alleles at D6S955 in 1 family suggested a deletion.

### Molecular Genetics

Kitada et al. (1998) cloned the gene by positional cloning within a microdeletion involving marker D6S305 in a Japanese AR-JP patient. The cDNA encoded a 465-amino acid protein with moderate similarity to ubiquitin at the N terminus and a RING-finger motif at the C terminus. The gene spans more than 500 kb and has 12 exons. Exons 3 to 7 were deleted in this patient. Four other AR-JP patients from 3 unrelated families had a deletion of exon 4 alone. The 4.5-kb transcript, abundant in brain including the substantia nigra, was shorter in brain tissue from 1 group of exon 4-deleted patients.

In the South Tyrolean pedigree, Klein et al. (2000) excluded linkage to the 2p, 4p, and 4q regions by haplotype analysis and implicated the parkin locus. Four affected male sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 deletions in the parkin gene, 1 large and 1 truncating. Two affected females carried the truncating deletion in heterozygous state with an apparently normal second allele.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found a homozygous deletion of exon 4 of the parkin gene in their autopsied AR-JP patient and in his sibs.

Hedrich et al. (2001) studied 21 patients with early-onset parkinsonism. Conventional screening (SSCP and sequencing) found 3 mutations: a single-base substitution in exon 3 and small deletions in exon 9. Quantitative duplex PCR found gene dosage alterations in 7 patients. These included heterozygous and compound heterozygous deletions of exons 2, 3, 5, and 7, a homozygous exon 7 deletion, and 2 heterozygous duplications of exon 4. Two patients carried more than 2 parkin mutations. ⚠︎

In a Dutch family with autosomal recessive early-onset parkinsonism, van de Warrenburg et al. (2001) found a heterozygous missense mutation in combination with a heterozygous exon deletion in the parkin gene.

Lücking et al. (2001) reported an Italian family with pseudodominant inheritance. The father, with onset at age 57 years, was homozygous for a triplication of exon 2. The unaffected mother was heterozygous for a deletion of exons 3 and 4. Their son, with onset at age 31 years, was a compound heterozygote carrying both mutations.

Farrer et al. (2001) sequenced the parkin gene in 38 patients with early-onset Parkinson disease (onset before age 41 years) and followed up 2 probands with mutations. Haplotype analysis showed that both families shared a common chromosome 6 carrying a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3, which cosegregated with disease. The proband of the smaller kindred also carried an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7. RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on different transcripts. The authors suggested that a hemizygous mutation may confer increased susceptibility to typical Parkinson disease.

Hoenicka et al. (2002) studied 19 Spanish kindreds with familial Parkinson disease: 13 with recessive, 4 with dominant, and 2 with uncertain inheritance. They found 5 different PARK2 mutations in 5 of the families with recessive inheritance. Two mutations, V56E and C212Y, were novel. The others were deletions of exons 3 and 5 and 225delA. PARK2 mutations accounted for 38% of the Spanish families with recessive parkinsonism. Heterozygous carriers of a single mutation either were asymptomatic or developed symptoms in late adulthood or after brief exposure to haloperidol.

Foroud et al. (2003) screened the parkin gene by quantitative PCR and exon sequencing in families with at least 2 sibs with Parkinson disease. Families were included if they had early-onset disease (onset at or before 50 years) or a positive lod score with an intron 7 marker. They identified 25 different mutations in 103 individuals from 47 families. Forty-one individuals had mutations on both alleles and 62 had a single mutation. Thirty-five mutation carriers (34%) had onset at 60 years or older, and 30 of these 35 (86%) had a detectable mutation on only 1 allele.

Oliveira et al. (2003) analyzed the parkin gene in 174 multiplex families from a genomic screen and 133 additional Parkinson disease families. Mutations were found in 18% of early-onset and 2% of late-onset families. Age at onset of mutation carriers ranged from 12 to 71 years. Excluding exon 7 mutations, mean age at onset was 31.5 years. Mutations in exon 7 (the first RING finger) were seen mainly in heterozygous patients with a much later mean onset of 49.2 years. These were cys253-to-trp, arg256-to-cys, arg275-to-trp, and asp280-to-asn, and none was found in controls. The authors suggested that heterozygous mutations, especially in exon 7, act as susceptibility alleles for late-onset Parkinson disease.

Poorkaj et al. (2004) used sequence and dosage analysis of all 12 exons to genotype 39 movement disorder clinic patients with Parkinson disease onset at age 40 years or younger. Mutations were found in 7 patients: 2 were compound heterozygous and 5 were heterozygous. Mutations included deletions of exons 2, 3, and 8, duplications of exons 2-4 and 9, and the substitution P437L. A novel R402W substitution was found in 1 patient and 1 control. The authors noted that it was unclear whether heterozygous mutations are pathogenic.

In the large pedigree of Pramstaller et al. (2005), 77 individuals carried parkin mutations. Of the 25 affected individuals, 5 were compound heterozygous and met criteria for definite Parkinson disease. Eight carried only a heterozygous mutation, 7 had no parkin mutation, and the status of 5 deceased patients was unknown. The autopsied patient carried an exon 7 deletion and del1072T.

Chien et al. (2006) studied a genetic isolate in northeastern Brazil, where genealogy connected 255 individuals, 15 of whom had Parkinson disease, with multiple consanguineous marriages. DNA was obtained from 10 patients with early-onset disease, 1 with likely neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism, and 15 unaffected relatives. Average age at onset was 30.8 years (range 12-46). Patients were homozygous for markers across the PARK2 locus. Sequencing identified a novel homozygous splice site mutation (IVS1+1G-T) that completely cosegregated with early-onset disease. cDNA analysis showed total loss of parkin transcript in homozygotes. The patient with neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism and 13 of 15 healthy relatives were heterozygous carriers.

Kay et al. (2007) sequenced the parkin gene in 302 patients and 301 controls and replicated the findings in 1,260 more patients and 1,657 more controls. Of 34 variants, 21 were novel, 12 were polymorphisms, and 22 were rare variants. Patients and controls did not differ in the frequency, type, or functional location of variants. Even P437L, a common mutation thought to be pathogenic, was found in unaffected controls. The authors concluded that a single point mutation without a second mutation should not be taken as a cause of disease unless supported by family data and functional studies.

Wang et al. (2008) assessed point mutations and exon deletions and duplications in the parkin gene in 247 probands with Parkinson disease onset at 50 years or younger and 104 control probands. Mutations were found in 25 probands (10.1%), 18 (72.0%) of whom were heterozygotes. Using the kin-cohort method, they estimated the cumulative incidence of Parkinson disease to age 65 years in carrier relatives at 7.0%. This compared with 1.7% in noncarrier relatives of cases (P = 0.59) and 1.1% in relatives of controls.

Marder et al. (2010) studied 956 patients with Parkinson disease onset before age 51 years from 13 movement disorder centers (the CORE-PD study). Sixty-four patients (6.7%) had parkin mutations: 3.9% heterozygous, 0.6% homozygous, and 2.2% compound heterozygous. Copy number variation was present in 52.3% of carriers. Mutation prevalence fell with age at onset, from 57% (8/14) with onset before 20 years to 3% (20/644) with onset at 40 to 50 years. Younger onset (before 40 years), Hispanic ethnicity, and a family history of Parkinson disease in a first-degree relative were associated with carrying any parkin mutation. No consanguinity was reported.

Alcalay et al. (2010) assessed known mutations in 6 genes in 953 patients with Parkinson disease onset before age 51 years. Of these, 33 were PRKN heterozygotes and 27 were PRKN homozygotes or compound heterozygotes. Age at onset was significantly younger in PRKN mutation carriers than in carriers of all other mutations.

Kay et al. (2010) tested 1,686 controls and 2,091 patients with Parkinson disease for PARK2 deletions, multiplications, and copy number variants (CNVs). Heterozygous CNVs were found in 0.95% of controls and 0.86% of patients. CNVs in the 16 control carriers were all in exons 1-4, sparing exons that encode functionally critical domains, whereas mutations in patients spanned exons 2-9. Thirteen patients had 2 CNVs, 5 had 1 CNV and 1 point mutation, and 18 had 1 CNV. In whites, a single CNV was not associated with increased risk of Parkinson disease (odds ratio 1.05) or with earlier onset.

Zhu et al. (2022) evaluated PRKN mutations in the NIH-PD + AMP-PD case-control cohort (whole genome sequencing in 4,094 participants) and in the UK Biobank. In UK Biobank genotyping array data from nearly half a million participants, they identified 2,692 PRKN CNVs. About half involved exon 2, and 88% involved exons 2, 3, or 4. In the UK Biobank, 1.8% of participants carried 1 pathogenic PRKN mutation. Carriers were as likely as noncarriers to have Parkinson disease (odds ratio 0.91) or a parent with Parkinson disease. Results in the NIH-PD + AMP-PD cohort were similar (odds ratio 1.29). Functional assays in patient fibroblasts resolved variants of unclear significance in biallelic carriers.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Lohmann et al. (2003) compared 146 early-onset parkinsonism patients with parkin mutations and 250 without. No single sign distinguished the groups. Patients with mutations had earlier and more symmetrical onset, dystonia at onset and hyperreflexia more often, slower progression, and a tendency toward greater levodopa response at lower doses. On multivariate analysis, dystonia at onset and brisk reflexes correlated with age at onset rather than with mutation status. The younger the onset, the slower the progression. Carriers of at least 1 missense mutation had higher UPDRS motor scores than carriers of 2 truncating mutations. Missense mutations in functional domains led to earlier onset. Patients with a single heterozygous mutation had later and more asymmetrical onset and more levodopa-induced fluctuations and dystonia than patients with 2 mutations.

Foroud et al. (2003) found few significant clinical differences between individuals with 2, 1, or no mutated parkin alleles. In the pedigree of Pramstaller et al. (2005), age-at-onset distribution differed significantly between compound heterozygotes and the groups of heterozygous carriers and noncarriers. In the CORE-PD cohort, Marder et al. (2010) found that compound heterozygotes and homozygotes each had a significantly younger age at onset than heterozygotes.

### Pathogenesis

Shimura et al. (2001) identified a protein complex in normal human brain made up of parkin as the E3 ubiquitin ligase, UbcH7 as its E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme, and a new 22-kD glycosylated form of alpha-synuclein (alpha-Sp22) as its substrate. Mutant parkin associated with autosomal recessive Parkinson disease failed to bind alpha-Sp22. In vitro, normal but not mutant parkin converted alpha-Sp22 into polyubiquitinated species. Non-ubiquitinated alpha-Sp22 accumulated in parkin-deficient Parkinson disease brains.

Mortiboys et al. (2008) studied fibroblasts from 5 patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous parkin mutations. Mitochondrial membrane potential was lower in all 5 by an average of 30%, and complex I activity was lower by an average of 42%. Complex I-linked ATP production was reduced, and cellular ATP levels were reduced by 58%. Mitochondrial branching was increased and correlated with reduced complex I-linked ATP production. FRAP assays showed reduced functional connectivity of the mitochondrial matrix, which worsened after rotenone exposure. siRNA knockdown of parkin in control fibroblasts reproduced these defects, whereas 50% knockdown, mimicking haploinsufficiency, did not. The glutathione precursor OTCA restored membrane potential to 95% of control levels, apparently through a compensatory increase in complex II activity. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Hedrich et al. (2001) showed that mutation analysis of the parkin gene should include gene dosage studies, because dosage alterations were missed by conventional screening. Lücking et al. (2001) likewise recommended sensitive techniques such as semiquantitative multiplex PCR and said pseudodominant inheritance must be considered in early-onset parkinsonism. Ohsawa et al. (2005) suggested that reduced sural SNAP amplitude could be a diagnostic indicator of PARK2. Kay et al. (2007) cautioned that a single heterozygous point mutation should not be taken as the cause of disease unless supported by family data and functional studies.

Alcalay et al. (2010) suggested that patients with early-onset Parkinson disease of Jewish or Hispanic ancestry, those with onset at age 30 or younger, and those with a first-degree relative with Parkinson disease may benefit from genetic counseling. They considered that there is little or no role for genetic testing in family planning, predictive, or prenatal testing at present.

### Population Genetics

In the 17 families studied by Matsumine et al. (1998), 8 were nonconsanguineous, there were no commonly shared haplotypes, and the families came from widely scattered places. The authors suggested that the disorder has multiple founder mutations.

In the CORE-PD cohort, Marder et al. (2010) found parkin mutations in 15.6% (12/77) of Hispanic patients and 5.7% (48/838) of white non-Hispanic patients. None of the 12 Black non-Hispanic patients carried a mutation. Deletions of exons 3 and 4 and 255delA were common among Hispanics, specifically Puerto Ricans. Both exon 3-4 deletion homozygotes were of Puerto Rican descent. Alcalay et al. (2010) likewise found that Hispanic patients were more likely than non-Hispanic patients to be PRKN carriers (15.6% vs 5.9%).

In the UK Biobank, Zhu et al. (2022) found a pathogenic PRKN mutation in 1.8% of participants and 2 mutations in about 1 in 7,800 participants.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-7DEL [deletion of exons 3-7]

Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion of exons 3 to 7 of the parkin gene in a Japanese patient with autosomal recessive juvenile parkinsonism. The microdeletion included marker D6S305 and was used to clone the gene.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX4DEL [deletion of exon 4]

Kitada et al. (1998) found a deletion of exon 4 alone in 4 AR-JP patients from 3 unrelated families. The parkin transcript was shorter in brain tissue from 1 group of these patients.

Hayashi et al. (2000) found a homozygous exon 4 deletion in a 70-year-old man with AR-JP, onset at age 32 years, and in his sibs. At autopsy he had nigral and locus ceruleus degeneration without Lewy bodies.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX7DEL [deletion of exon 7; delExon7]

Hedrich et al. (2001) used quantitative duplex PCR in patients with early-onset parkinsonism and found an exon 7 deletion in compound heterozygous and homozygous states. ⚠︎

In a 7-generation pedigree, Pramstaller et al. (2005) found an exon 7 deletion in compound heterozygosity with del1072T in a patient who died at age 73 years. Autopsy showed Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX2 TRIPLICATION [triplication of exon 2]

In an Italian family with pseudodominant inheritance, Lücking et al. (2001) found that the affected father (onset at age 57 years) was homozygous for a triplication of exon 2. His affected son (onset at age 31 years) was compound heterozygous for the triplication and a deletion of exons 3 and 4 inherited from the unaffected mother.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3-4DEL [deletion of exons 3 and 4]

Lücking et al. (2001) found a deletion of exons 3 and 4 in heterozygous state in an unaffected Italian mother. Her affected son was compound heterozygous for this deletion and an exon 2 triplication.

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) found that 6 patients carried exon 3-4 deletions: 5 Puerto Ricans and 1 Mexican. Both homozygotes were of Puerto Rican descent. Three Puerto Ricans carried the deletion together with 255delA.

**.0006 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, 40-BP DEL, EX3 [40-bp deletion in exon 3]

Farrer et al. (2001) identified a novel 40-bp deletion in exon 3 on a common chromosome 6 haplotype in 2 families with early-onset parkinsonism, where it cosegregated with disease. In the proband of the smaller kindred, it was on the other transcript from an R275W substitution. This proband had Lewy body pathology at autopsy.

**.0007 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, ARG275TRP [R275W; Arg275Trp]

Farrer et al. (2001) found an arg275-to-trp (R275W) substitution in exon 7 in compound heterozygosity with an exon 3 40-bp deletion in a proband with early-onset parkinsonism and Lewy body pathology. RNA analysis showed that the 2 mutations were on different transcripts.

Oliveira et al. (2003) observed R275W, among other exon 7 mutations, mainly in heterozygous patients with later-onset Parkinson disease. It was not found in controls.

**.0008 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, VAL56GLU [V56E]

Hoenicka et al. (2002) reported V56E as a novel PARK2 mutation. It was 1 of 5 different mutations found in 5 Spanish families with recessive parkinsonism. The abstract does not state the zygosity.

**.0009 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, CYS212TYR [C212Y; Cys212Tyr]

Hoenicka et al. (2002) reported C212Y as a novel PARK2 mutation. It was 1 of 5 different mutations found in 5 Spanish families with recessive parkinsonism.

In the CORE-PD study, Marder et al. (2010) found Cys212Tyr in compound heterozygosity with Arg275Trp in a white patient with early-onset Parkinson disease.

**.0010 PARKINSON DISEASE 2, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE JUVENILE**  
PRKN, EX3DEL [deletion of exon 3]

Sasaki et al. (2004) reported an AR-JP patient with a homozygous deletion of exon 3. At autopsy there was nigral and locus ceruleus degeneration without Lewy bodies, but alpha-synuclein-positive inclusions were present in the pedunculopontine nucleus. ⚠︎
