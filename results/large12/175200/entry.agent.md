### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the STK11 gene on chromosome 19p13.3. STK11 mutations have not been found in a proportion of PJS families, and a second locus on chromosome 19q13.4 has been proposed.

### Description

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by melanocytic macules of the lips, multiple gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, and an increased risk for various neoplasms, including gastrointestinal cancer (Jenne et al., 1998). Polyp-related complications include small bowel intussusception and chronic or acute bleeding, and patients are at increased risk of cancers within and outside the gastrointestinal tract, including rare tumors of the ovary (sex cord tumors with annular tubules), cervix (adenoma malignum), and testis (Sertoli cell tumors) (Westerman and Wilson, 1999).

### Clinical Features

Jeghers et al. (1949) described generalized intestinal polyposis with melanin spots of the oral mucosa, lips, and digits as a syndrome of diagnostic significance.

Christian et al. (1964) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with a functioning ovarian tumor.

Williams and Knudsen (1965) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome with a metastasizing duodenal carcinoma.

McAllister et al. (1967) reported 17 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in 4 generations.

Kieselstein et al. (1969) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in a family of Iraqi Jews with polycystic kidney disease.

Scully (1970) described the sex cord tumor with annular tubules as a distinctive ovarian tumor of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Sommerhaug and Mason (1970) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome with ureteral polyposis.

McAllister and Richards (1977) reported 20 patients in 5 generations, which they described as the largest personal series in the literature. They also presented a previously unreported instance of colon intussusception.

Lin et al. (1977) reported a patient with Peutz-Jeghers polyposis and duodenal carcinoma but without cutaneous pigmentation; the patient's vitiligo was considered coincidental. They noted that in only 2 of 14 reported cases of PJS with intestinal carcinoma was the tumor shown to originate in the polyp, and they believed that the carcinoma in their patient developed independently.

Cochet et al. (1979) described metastasizing gastrointestinal carcinoma in a 56-year-old woman with PJS and her 29-year-old son. The mother and son died from duodenal and gastric carcinomas, respectively, which developed in hamartomatous polyps; both also showed dysplastic areas within hamartomatous polyps.

Joishy et al. (1979) reported a patient from Malaysia with PJS complicated by successive intussusceptions requiring extensive small bowel resection.

Matuchansky et al. (1979) described a patient with PJS in whom an intestinal adenocarcinoma with metastases to the omentum and celiac lymph nodes was shown to originate in a jejunal hamartoma; adenocarcinoma was intermixed with smooth muscle bands, and a transitional zone of malignant cells was seen in glands of the polyp.

Cantu et al. (1980) described a 6-year-old boy with PJS and a unilateral feminizing Sertoli cell tumor. Plasma and urine estrogen levels were consistently high, with normal testosterone and dihydrotestosterone, and the estrogen levels did not change with exogenous gonadotropin administration. The authors concluded that gonadal tumors are a manifestation of the PJS gene in both males and females.

Riley and Swift (1980) reported a family in which the proband and her paternal grandmother had PJS and bilateral breast carcinoma. An ovarian sex cord tumor with annular tubules was found incidentally at oophorectomy performed as treatment for the proband's premenopausal breast cancer.

Griffith and Bisset (1980) reported 3 cases of PJS presenting in early childhood.

Peloquin et al. (1981) reported a 49-year-old woman with PJS who first presented with deep thrombophlebitis of the leg, which appeared to be paraneoplastic. She had an adenocarcinoma originating in a hamartoma of the terminal ileum, with ovarian and lymph node metastases, and later metastatic disease in the cervical lymph nodes.

Burdick and Prior (1982) followed a family group of 10 patients with PJS for 27 years. One member died of an adenocarcinoma of the jejunum arising in a Peutz-Jeghers polyp, with mesenteric lymph node metastases. Two members developed breast carcinomas and died, one of metastatic breast carcinoma and the other of a second primary jejunal adenocarcinoma arising in a Peutz-Jeghers polyp. Three family members had benign ovarian tumors, 1 had a benign breast tumor, and 1 had a benign colloid thyroid nodule. The authors considered the exact risk of intestinal cancer in PJS unknown but probably very small.

Solh et al. (1983) reported PJS associated with precocious puberty.

Parker and Knight (1983) reported PJS causing obstructive jaundice due to a polyp in the common bile duct.

Lehur et al. (1984) described a woman with PJS who successively developed bilateral breast carcinoma and malignant transformation of a duodenal hamartomatous polyp, from which she died.

Tweedie and McCann (1984) described a 39-year-old man with PJS and a cecal adenocarcinoma that had metastasized widely to regional lymph nodes; several other colonic Peutz-Jeghers polyps showed no dysplasia or malignancy, and it was not possible to determine whether the carcinoma had arisen from a polyp. The patient also had gastric and small intestinal polyps. Serum carcinoembryonic antigen was normal at presentation.

Wilson et al. (1986) described a 6-year-old boy with PJS, gynecomastia, and multifocal and bilateral testicular tumors, and suggested that males as well as females with PJS are at risk for gonadal tumors.

Banse-Kupin and Douglass (1986) reported a patient with PJS in whom the characteristic pigmented macules developed within preexisting psoriatic plaques, in sites extremely unusual for the syndrome.

Bowlby (1986) reported carcinoma of the pancreas in a 16-year-old male with PJS.

Giardiello et al. (1987) studied 31 patients with PJS followed from 1973 to 1985. Cancer developed in 15 of the 31 patients (48%): gastrointestinal carcinomas in 4, nongastrointestinal carcinomas in 10, and multiple myeloma in 1. Adenomatous polyps of the stomach and colon occurred in 3 other patients. The cancers were diagnosed when the patients were relatively young but after PJS had been diagnosed (interval between diagnoses, 25 +/- 20 years; range, 1 to 64). The observed development of cancer was 18 times greater than expected in the general population.

Foley et al. (1988) reported a 49-year follow-up of the 'Harrisburg family,' one of the original families reported by Jeghers, whose 12 affected members comprised the largest Peutz-Jeghers kindred reported. One member developed a duodenal carcinoma in a hamartoma with adenomatous changes. Ten patients underwent 75 polypectomies, 1 patient developed short bowel syndrome, and 3 died in young adulthood. Gastrointestinal malignancy developed in 2 of the 12 affected patients.

Spigelman et al. (1989) found that among 72 patients with PJS, malignant tumors had developed in 16 (22%), all but 1 of whom had died; 9 tumors were gastrointestinal and 7 nongastrointestinal. The relative risks of death from gastrointestinal cancer and from all cancers were 13 (95% CI 2.7-38.1) and 9 (95% CI 4.2-17.3), respectively, and the chance of dying of cancer by the age of 57 was 48%.

Young et al. (1995) reported multicentric Sertoli cell testicular tumors in 2 young boys with probable PJS. Each had prominent gynecomastia, rapid growth, and advanced bone age, and serum estradiol levels were markedly elevated. Bilateral orchiectomy was performed in each case. Microscopically, greatly enlarged seminiferous tubules were packed with ovoid Sertoli-like cells. Tumor tissue from both cases showed increased transcription of the aromatase cytochrome P450 gene using promoter II, at levels comparable to corpus luteum.

Fernandez Seara et al. (1995) reported a 15-day-old girl with generalized gastrointestinal polyposis resulting from PJS. Her manifestations included abdominal distention, hematemesis, bloody diarrhea, and edema, and she died at 1 year of age of multiple complications of the disease.

Westerman et al. (1999) retraced the original Dutch family first recognized by Peutz in 1921 and studied the natural history of PJS in 6 generations. Clinical features included gastrointestinal polyposis, mucocutaneous pigmentation, nasal polyposis, and rectal extrusion of polyps. Survival of affected members was reduced by intestinal obstruction and by the development of malignant disease.

Bergada et al. (2000) described a prepubertal boy with PJS, gynecomastia, and bilateral neoplastic Sertoli cell proliferation in whom the only abnormal hormonal finding was increased serum concentration of inhibin B and pro-alpha C.

Keller et al. (2002) presented molecular genetic evidence of an association between nasal polyposis and PJS.

### Inheritance

Giardiello et al. (1987) described PJS as an autosomal dominant hereditary disease, and multigenerational transmission was documented by McAllister et al. (1967) and McAllister and Richards (1977). The reports differed regarding penetrance: Amos et al. (1997) found that all carriers in 5 linked families expressed the PJS phenotype and no noncarriers displayed PJS sequelae, indicating complete penetrance, whereas Mehenni et al. (1997) described PJS as an autosomal dominant disease with variable expression and incomplete penetrance. Westerman et al. (1999) and Hernan et al. (2004) demonstrated de novo STK11 mutations.

### Cytogenetics

Markie et al. (1996) found a pericentric inversion of chromosome 6 in a patient with PJS. FISH with YAC clones placed one breakpoint within the centromeric alphoid array, in a 1-cM interval between D6S257 and D6S402, and the other in a 4-cM interval between D6S403 and D6S311; the estrogen receptor gene locus was excluded from the latter interval.

### Mapping

Hemminki et al. (1997) searched for a tumor suppressor locus using comparative genomic hybridization of Peutz-Jeghers polyps combined with loss of heterozygosity analysis. Linkage analysis in 12 families using markers from a deletion site demonstrated a high-penetrance locus in distal 19p, with a multipoint lod score of 7.00 at marker D19S886, without evidence of genetic heterogeneity.

Amos et al. (1997) confirmed the 19p location. In 5 families there were no recombinants with D19S886, the multipoint lod score at D19S886 was 7.52, and there was no evidence for genetic heterogeneity.

Mehenni et al. (1997) performed a genomewide linkage analysis in 6 families (2 from Spain, 2 from India, 1 from the United States, and 1 from Portugal) comprising 93 individuals, including 39 affected. Marker D19S886 yielded a maximum lod score of 4.74 at theta = 0.045, and multipoint analysis gave a lod score of 7.51 for the interval between D19S886 and 19pter. Markers on 19q13.4 also showed significant evidence for linkage (maximum lod score of 3.8 at theta = 0.13 for D19S880), most of it contributed by a single family, PJS07.

Olschwang et al. (1998) analyzed 20 further families and found the majority consistent with a PJS gene on 19p13.3; 3 families were unlinked to 19p13.3, and none of the available polyps from these families showed allele loss at D19S886. There were no obvious clinicopathologic or ethnic differences between linked and unlinked families.

Jenne et al. (1998) identified the STK11 gene 190 kb proximal to D19S886. Gruber et al. (1998) confirmed linkage to 19p13.3 in the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949 and 5 other families.

### Heterogeneity

Mehenni et al. (1997) found evidence for a potential second PJS locus on 19q13.4 in a minority of families, mainly from a single family, PJS07. Olschwang et al. (1998) found 3 of 20 families unlinked to 19p13.3 and suggested a minor locus or loci elsewhere.

Mehenni et al. (1998) failed to detect LKB1 mutations in 2 Indian families, one of which had previously shown linkage to markers on 19q13.3-q13.4. Buchet-Poyau et al. (2002) found no pathogenic mutations in the PSCD2, KLK10, PRKCG, and STK13 genes within the 19q13.3-q13.4 linked region in affected members of family PJS07, and also excluded STK11IP (LIP1) on 2q36 in this family.

Several mutation studies found no STK11 mutation in a substantial fraction of patients: Westerman et al. (1999) in 7 of 19 families, Jiang et al. (1999) in most of 10 unrelated families, and Boardman et al. (2000) in most sporadic cases. Olschwang et al. (2001) concluded that up to 30% of PJS cases are caused by mutation in an unidentified gene conferring high susceptibility to cancer.

Alhopuro et al. (2008) searched for MYH11 mutations because a recessive germline mutation in smooth-muscle myosin underlies the zebrafish meltdown phenotype, which has intestinal abnormalities reminiscent of PJS and juvenile polyposis. They found a protein-elongating frameshift mutation in the germline of 1 individual with PJS; functional evaluation showed that the frameshift mutations resulted in unregulated molecules with constitutive motor activity.

### Molecular Genetics

Jenne et al. (1998) identified a novel gene encoding the serine-threonine kinase STK11 within the PJS critical region on 19p13.3. In a 3-generation PJS family, they found an STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 segregating with the disease. Sequencing in 4 unrelated patients identified 3 nonsense mutations and 1 acceptor splice site mutation. All 5 germline mutations were predicted to disrupt the kinase domain. ⚠︎

Hemminki et al. (1998) identified truncating germline mutations in the LKB1 gene on chromosome 19p in multiple individuals with PJS. LKB1 showed strong homology to the cytoplasmic Xenopus serine/threonine kinase XEEK1.

Mehenni et al. (1998) screened 9 PJS families of American, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Turkish, and Indian origin and detected 7 novel LKB1 mutations: 2 frameshifts, a 4-amino acid deletion, 2 amino acid substitutions, and 2 splicing errors. Expression of mutant LKB1 proteins (K78I, D176N, W308C, and L67P) showed loss of autophosphorylation activity in all of them.

Gruber et al. (1998) identified germline STK11 mutations by genomic sequencing in all 6 families from the Johns Hopkins Polyposis Registry studied, including the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949.

In the original Dutch family described by Peutz, Westerman et al. (1999) found a germline LKB1 mutation, a T insertion at codon 66 in exon 1 resulting in a frameshift and stop at codon 162 in exon 4, that cosegregated with the disease.

Westerman et al. (1999) performed mutation analysis in 19 predominantly Dutch PJS families and identified 12 novel LKB1/STK11 mutations in 12 families: 1 nonsense mutation, 3 frameshift deletions, 3 frameshift insertions, 2 acceptor splice site mutations, and 3 missense mutations. No mutation occurred in more than 1 family, and some had arisen de novo.

Using the protein truncation test and genomic sequencing in 10 unrelated PJS families, Jiang et al. (1999) identified a truncating deletion spanning STK11 exons 2 to 7 in a single patient. Loss of heterozygosity studies in polyps identified an allelic deletion of D19S886 in another patient.

Boardman et al. (2000) screened 5 kindreds with more than 2 affected members, 5 probands with only 1 other affected relative, and 23 individuals with sporadic PJS, using conformation-sensitive gel electrophoresis, sequencing, and long-range PCR. LKB1 alterations were found in 2 probands with a family history of PJS and in 4 of the 23 sporadic cases.

Olschwang et al. (2001) analyzed the STK11/LKB1 locus in 34 PJS families by mutation and rearrangement screening, allele-specific expression tests, and linkage studies, and identified germline deleterious mutations in 70% of cases.

Le Meur et al. (2004) used quantitative multiplex PCR of short fluorescent fragments of the 19p13 region to identify a heterozygous deletion of approximately 250 kb removing the entire STK11 locus in a family with typical PJS features, including gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa.

Amos et al. (2004) detected STK11 mutations in 22 of 32 (69%) probands with PJS and in none of 10 probands referred to rule out PJS. Real-time PCR failed to detect large deletions in PJS participants without STK11 mutations.

In a simplex case of PJS, Hernan et al. (2004) identified a tyr246-to-ter (Y246X) mutation in exon 6 of the LKB1 gene, predicted to truncate the protein. The mutation was not found in either parent and was therefore de novo.

In 2 sibs with PJS and primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified a germline STK11 mutation, c.890delG, predicted to encode a truncated protein (p.Arg297fsX38). No germline CDH1 or TP53 mutations were found, and no inactivation of the wildtype STK11 allele by somatic mutation, deletion, or promoter hypermethylation was detected in the gastric cancer.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Olschwang et al. (2001) found that the cancer risk differed markedly between PJS families with and without an identified STK11/LKB1 alteration; patients without an identified mutation were at major risk for proximal biliary adenocarcinoma.

In a historical cohort of 51 individuals with PJS, Amos et al. (2004) found a median time to onset of gastrointestinal symptoms or polypectomy of 13 years, with wide variability; gastric polyps were frequent, with a median age at onset of 16 years. Individuals with missense mutations had a significantly later time to first polypectomy (p = 0.04) and to other symptoms than those with truncating mutations or no detectable mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Spigelman et al. (1989) found evidence for a hamartoma/carcinoma sequence in PJS. Hemminki et al. (1997) used comparative genomic hybridization and loss of heterozygosity analysis of PJS polyps to localize a putative tumor suppressor locus and considered this molecular evidence of malignant potential in hamartomas.

Gruber et al. (1998) found loss of heterozygosity of 19p markers near STK11 in 70% of hamartomas and adenocarcinomas from PJS patients, and haplotype analysis indicated that the retained allele carried the germline mutation, confirming STK11 as a tumor suppressor gene. LOH of 17p and 18q was found in an adenocarcinoma but not in hamartomas, and none of 18 PJS tumors showed microsatellite instability, LOH on 5q near APC, or K-ras codon 12 or 13 mutations.

Miyaki et al. (2000) studied 27 gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps from 10 patients in 9 PJS families and detected germline LKB1 mutations in 6 families. Somatic LKB1 mutations were found in 5 polyps and LOH at the LKB1 locus in 14 others. Adenomatous lesions within hamartomatous polyps had both beta-catenin mutation and 19p LOH, and a carcinomatous lesion in a gastric hamartomatous polyp also had a p53 mutation and LOH at the p53 locus. No APC mutation or 5q LOH was detected in hamartomatous polyps.

Entius et al. (2001) analyzed 39 polyps and 5 carcinomas from 17 patients in 13 PJS families. LOH at 19p was found in 15 of the 39 polyps and in all 5 carcinomas; 6 of 7 polyps from patients with cancer had LOH, compared with 9 of 31 polyps from other patients (p = 0.01). No LOH at 5q was observed, but an APC mutation was found in 4 of the 5 carcinomas.

In contrast, Hernan et al. (2004) studied microdissected hamartomatous polyps from a patient with a de novo LKB1 mutation and concluded that the results suggested a mechanism other than LOH in polyp formation. Shinmura et al. (2005) found no inactivation of the wildtype STK11 allele in a gastric cancer from a patient with a germline STK11 mutation.

### Diagnosis

Acquired mucocutaneous pigmentation resembling that of PJS has been reported in other settings. Babin et al. (1978) described oral hyperpigmentation in response to occult malignancy. Gass and Glatzer (1991) described a 74-year-old man with occult colon carcinoma who developed pigmentation of the mouth and penis typical of PJS as the first manifestation of bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation. Eng et al. (1991) described a 67-year-old man with esophageal adenocarcinoma and Peutz-Jeghers-like macules on the lips, face, nipples, and rectal mucosa that contained atypical melanocytes.

Veraldi et al. (1991) described 4 patients with Laugier-Hunziker syndrome, aged 39 to 57 years, who had pigmentation of the lower lip and hard palate, with involvement of the buccal mucosa in 2 and of the upper lip, gums, soft palate, and fingers in another.

DiLiberti et al. (1983) described a 7.5-year-old boy with macrocephaly, hamartomatous intestinal polyps, and cafe-au-lait spots on the penis, which they considered to represent the Ruvalcaba-Myhre-Smith syndrome.

Amos et al. (2004) recommended that STK11 mutation analysis be restricted to individuals who meet PJS criteria or their close relatives.

### Clinical Management

Foley et al. (1988) recommended that even asymptomatic gastric, duodenal, and colonic polyps be removed endoscopically, that intraoperative endoscopy with polypectomy be used if surgery is necessary to prevent short bowel syndrome, and that surveillance for extraintestinal malignancy appears to be warranted. Westerman and Wilson (1999) stated that surveillance and screening protocols need to be developed for PJS patients and their relatives.

Kato et al. (1998) treated labial lentigines in 2 children with PJS with ruby laser therapy, with an excellent response and no sequelae or recurrence.

### History

Westerman et al. (1999) noted that the association of heredity, gastrointestinal polyposis, and mucocutaneous pigmentation in PJS was first recognized in 1921 by Peutz in a Dutch family, which had been followed for more than 78 years. Keller et al. (2001) wrote about Jan Peutz and Harold Jeghers and the combination of polyposis and pigmentation that bears their names.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, DEL EX4-5 AND INV EX6-7

In a 3-generation family with PJS, Jenne et al. (1998) found an STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 segregating with the disease. The rearrangement was predicted to disrupt the kinase domain.

**.0002 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP INS, T, CODON 66

In the original Dutch PJS family first recognized by Peutz in 1921, Westerman et al. (1999) identified a germline insertion of a T at codon 66 in exon 1 of the LKB1 gene, resulting in a frameshift and a stop at codon 162 in exon 4. The mutation cosegregated with the disease phenotype.

**.0003 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, DEL EX2-7

Among 10 unrelated PJS families, Jiang et al. (1999) identified a truncating deletion spanning STK11 exons 2 to 7 in a single patient, using the protein truncation test and genomic sequencing.

**.0004 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 250-KB DEL

In a family with typical PJS features, including gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa, Le Meur et al. (2004) identified a heterozygous deletion of approximately 250 kb removing the entire STK11 locus, detected by quantitative multiplex PCR of short fluorescent fragments of the 19p13 region.

**.0005 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, TYR246TER [Tyr246X]

In a simplex case of PJS, Hernan et al. (2004) identified a substitution of cytosine for guanine at codon 246 in exon 6 of the LKB1 gene, resulting in a tyr246-to-ter (Y246X) mutation predicted to truncate the protein. The mutation was absent from both parents and was therefore de novo.

**.0006 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP DEL, 890G [c.890delG; p.Arg297fsX38]

In 2 sibs with PJS complicated by primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified a germline 1-bp deletion in the STK11 gene (c.890delG), encoding a truncated protein (p.Arg297fsX38). No germline CDH1 or TP53 mutations were found, and no inactivation of the wildtype STK11 allele was detected in the gastric cancer.
