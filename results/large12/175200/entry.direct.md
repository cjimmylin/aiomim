### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the STK11 gene (also known as LKB1) on chromosome 19p13.3.

### Description

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by melanocytic macules of the lips, multiple gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, and an increased risk for various neoplasms, including gastrointestinal cancer (Jenne et al., 1998). The melanin spots involve the lips, buccal mucosa, and digits, and gastrointestinal tumors as well as cancers of the breast, ovary, testis, and uterine cervix appear to be overrepresented in affected families (Hemminki et al., 1997). Complications of the polyps include small bowel intussusception and chronic or acute bleeding (Westerman and Wilson, 1999). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Jeghers et al. (1949) described generalized intestinal polyposis and melanin spots of the oral mucosa, lips, and digits as a syndrome of diagnostic significance. Gruber et al. (1998) later restudied the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949, and Foley et al. (1988) followed another of the original families reported by Jeghers (see below).

Bruwer et al. (1954) reported surface pigmentation and generalized intestinal polyposis under the designation Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Dormandy (1957) reported gastrointestinal polyposis with mucocutaneous pigmentation, and Bartholomew et al. (1962) reported intestinal polyposis associated with mucocutaneous pigmentation.

Sheward (1962) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in childhood with unusual radiologic features. Keen and Murray (1962) reported a further family with the syndrome. Michalany and Ferraz (1962) reported the disorder in a mulatto family, with special reference to the histologic structure of the intestinal polyps. Farmer et al. (1963) reported 3 cases illustrating the spectrum of the syndrome.

Christian et al. (1964) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with a functioning ovarian tumor. Williams and Knudsen (1965) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome with metastasizing duodenal carcinoma.

McAllister et al. (1967) reported 17 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in 4 generations. Kieselstein et al. (1969) reported mucocutaneous pigmentation and intestinal polyposis in a family of Iraqi Jews with polycystic kidney disease, with a chromosome study.

Scully (1970) described the sex cord tumor with annular tubules as a distinctive ovarian tumor of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Sommerhaug and Mason (1970) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome with ureteral polyposis. McKittrick et al. (1971) reported 2 cases, one with 30-year follow-up. Dodds et al. (1972) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome with gastrointestinal malignancy, and Brigg et al. (1976) reported unusual manifestations of the syndrome.

McAllister and Richards (1977) reported a personal series of 20 patients in 5 generations, including a previously unreported instance of colon intussusception.

Lin et al. (1977) reported a patient with Peutz-Jeghers polyposis associated with duodenal carcinoma but without cutaneous pigmentation; the patient's vitiligo was considered coincidental. They noted that of 14 reported cases of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with intestinal carcinoma, the tumor was shown to originate in the polyp in only 2, and they believed that the carcinoma in their patient had developed independently.

Cochet et al. (1979) described metastasizing gastrointestinal carcinoma in a 56-year-old woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and her 29-year-old son. Both died from duodenal and gastric carcinomas, respectively, which developed in hamartomatous polyps with extensive metastases; both also showed dysplastic areas within hamartomatous polyps, suggesting that hamartomatous polyps may in some cases be precursors of digestive tract carcinomas.

Joishy et al. (1979) reported a patient from Malaysia with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome complicated by successive intussusceptions requiring extensive small bowel resection.

Matuchansky et al. (1979) described a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in whom an intestinal adenocarcinoma with metastases to the omentum and celiac lymph nodes was shown histologically to originate in a jejunal hamartoma. The adenocarcinoma was intimately intricated with smooth muscle bands, a transitional zone of malignant cells was observed in several glands of the polyp, and glands close to malignant areas exhibited less differentiated epithelium still intermixed with smooth muscle bundles.

Cantu et al. (1980) described a 6-year-old boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and a unilateral feminizing Sertoli cell tumor. Plasma and urine estrogen levels were consistently high, with normal testosterone and dihydrotestosterone levels, and estrogen levels did not change with exogenous gonadotropin administration, indicating autonomy. Nontumorous testicular tissue showed seminiferous tubule changes suggesting both stimulating and atrophying effects of estrogens. The authors concluded that gonadal tumors are an additional manifestation of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome gene in both males and females.

Riley and Swift (1980) reported a proband and her paternal grandmother who both had bilateral breast carcinoma and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. An ovarian sex cord tumor with annular tubules was found incidentally at oophorectomy performed as treatment of the proband's premenopausal breast cancer. The authors suggested that the Peutz-Jeghers gene may be associated with an increased risk of breast tumors.

Griffith and Bisset (1980) reported 3 cases of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome presenting in early childhood.

Peloquin et al. (1981) reported a 49-year-old woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who first presented with deep thrombophlebitis of the leg, apparently as part of a paraneoplastic syndrome. She had an adenocarcinoma originating in a hamartoma of the terminal ileum, with ovarian and lymph node metastases, and later metastatic disease in the cervical lymph nodes that responded temporarily to chemotherapy.

Burdick and Prior (1982) followed a family group of 10 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome for 27 years; 8 family members had 1 or more manifestations of the syndrome. One member died after a bypass procedure for a nonresectable jejunal carcinoma, shown at autopsy to be an adenocarcinoma arising in a Peutz-Jeghers polyp with mesenteric lymph node metastases. Two members developed breast carcinomas, 1 arising in a fibroadenoma; 1 died of metastatic breast carcinoma and the other of a second primary jejunal adenocarcinoma arising in a Peutz-Jeghers polyp. Three family members had benign ovarian tumors, 1 had a benign breast tumor, and another had a benign colloid thyroid nodule.

Solh et al. (1983) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with precocious puberty. Parker and Knight (1983) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome causing obstructive jaundice due to a polyp in the common bile duct.

Lehur et al. (1984) reported a woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who successively developed bilateral breast carcinoma and malignant transformation of a duodenal hamartomatous polyp, from which she died.

Tweedie and McCann (1984) described a 39-year-old man with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and a cecal adenocarcinoma that had metastasized widely to regional lymph nodes; several other colonic Peutz-Jeghers polyps showed no dysplasia or malignancy, and it could not be determined whether the carcinoma had arisen from a polyp. The patient also had gastric and small intestinal polyps. Serum carcinoembryonic antigen was normal at presentation.

Wilson et al. (1986) described a 6-year-old boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, gynecomastia, and multifocal and bilateral testicular tumors, and suggested that males as well as females with the syndrome are at risk for gonadal tumors.

Banse-Kupin and Douglass (1986) reported a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in whom the characteristic pigmented macules developed within preexisting psoriatic plaques, at sites extremely unusual for the syndrome.

Bowlby (1986) reported carcinoma of the pancreas in a 16-year-old male with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Giardiello et al. (1987) studied 31 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome followed from 1973 to 1985. Cancer developed in 15 (48%): gastrointestinal carcinomas in 4, nongastrointestinal carcinomas in 10, and multiple myeloma in 1. Adenomatous polyps of the stomach and colon occurred in 3 other patients. Cancers were diagnosed at relatively young ages but after the diagnosis of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (interval 25 +/- 20 years; range, 1 to 64). The observed development of cancer was 18 times greater than expected in the general population (P less than 0.0001).

Foley et al. (1988) reported 49-year follow-up of the 'Harrisburg family,' one of the original families reported by Jeghers, with 12 affected members. One member developed a duodenal carcinoma in a hamartoma with adenomatous changes. Ten patients underwent 75 polypectomies, 1 developed short bowel syndrome, and 3 died in young adulthood. Gastrointestinal malignancy developed in 2 of the 12 affected patients.

Among 72 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Spigelman et al. (1989) found that malignant tumors had developed in 16 (22%), all but 1 of whom had died; there were 9 gastrointestinal and 7 nongastrointestinal tumors. The relative risks of death from gastrointestinal cancer and from all cancers were 13 (95% CI 2.7-38.1) and 9 (95% CI 4.2-17.3), respectively, and the chance of dying of cancer by age 57 was 48%.

Coen et al. (1991) reported an aromatase-producing sex cord tumor resulting in prepubertal gynecomastia.

Fernandez Seara et al. (1995) reported a 15-day-old girl with generalized gastrointestinal polyposis resulting from Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, who presented with abdominal distention, hematemesis, bloody diarrhea, and edema. She died at 1 year of age of multiple complications.

Young et al. (1995) reported 2 young boys with probable Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and multicentric Sertoli cell testicular tumors, noting 4 previously reported cases of such tumors in boys with the syndrome. Both boys had prominent gynecomastia, rapid growth, advanced bone age, and markedly elevated serum estradiol. Microscopically, greatly enlarged seminiferous tubules were packed with ovoid Sertoli-like cells, with prominent eosinophilic basement membrane forming hyalinized ovoid globules and microcalcifications. Tumor tissue from both cases showed increased transcription of the aromatase cytochrome P450 gene using promoter II, at levels comparable to corpus luteum.

Westerman et al. (1999) reported follow-up of more than 78 years of the original Dutch family in which Peutz recognized the syndrome in 1921, studying 6 generations. Clinical features included gastrointestinal polyposis, mucocutaneous pigmentation, nasal polyposis, and rectal extrusion of polyps. Survival of affected members was reduced by intestinal obstruction and by malignant disease.

Bergada et al. (2000) described a prepubertal boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, gynecomastia, and bilateral neoplastic Sertoli cell proliferation in whom the only abnormal hormonal finding was increased serum concentration of inhibin B and pro-alpha C.

Keller et al. (2002) presented molecular genetic evidence of an association between nasal polyposis and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

In a historical cohort of 51 individuals with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who completed questionnaires, Amos et al. (2004) found that the median time to onset of gastrointestinal symptoms or polypectomy was 13 years of age, with wide variability. Gastric polyps were frequent, with a median age at onset of 16 years.

### Other Features

Yamada et al. (1981) performed ultrastructural studies of the pigmented macules of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

### Inheritance

Multigenerational families with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome were reported by McAllister et al. (1967) (4 generations) and McAllister and Richards (1977) (5 generations). Giardiello et al. (1987) described the syndrome as an autosomal dominant hereditary disease.

Reports differed on penetrance. Amos et al. (1997) found that all carriers in 5 linked families expressed the phenotype and no noncarriers displayed PJS sequelae, indicating complete penetrance with no sporadic cases, whereas Mehenni et al. (1997) described the disorder as autosomal dominant with variable expression and incomplete penetrance.

De novo STK11 mutations were demonstrated by Westerman et al. (1999) and Hernan et al. (2004), and Boardman et al. (2000) studied 23 individuals with sporadic PJS.

### Cytogenetics

Markie et al. (1996) found a pericentric inversion of chromosome 6 in a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. FISH with YAC clones containing chromosome 6 markers and a centromeric alphoid repeat probe located one breakpoint within the alphoid array, in a 1-cM interval between D6S257 and D6S402, and the other in a 4-cM interval between D6S403 and D6S311. The estrogen receptor gene locus (ESR) was excluded from the latter interval.

### Mapping

Hemminki et al. (1997) searched for a tumor suppressor locus using comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) of Peutz-Jeghers polyps combined with loss of heterozygosity (LOH) studies. Linkage analysis in 12 families using markers from a deletion site demonstrated a high-penetrance locus in distal 19p, with a multipoint lod score of 7.00 at marker D19S886, without evidence of genetic heterogeneity.

Amos et al. (1997) confirmed the 19p location in 5 families, with no recombinants with marker D19S886 and a multipoint lod score of 7.52 at D19S886; they found no evidence for genetic heterogeneity.

Mehenni et al. (1997) performed a genomewide linkage analysis in 6 families (2 from Spain, 2 from India, 1 from the United States, and 1 from Portugal) comprising 93 individuals, including 39 affected. Marker D19S886 yielded a maximum lod score of 4.74 at theta = 0.045, and multipoint analysis gave a lod score of 7.51 for the interval between D19S886 and 19pter. Markers on 19q13.4 also showed significant evidence for linkage, e.g., a maximum lod score of 3.8 at theta = 0.13 for D19S880, mostly contributed by a single family, PJS07, suggesting a potential second locus on 19q13.4 in a minority of families.

Olschwang et al. (1998) analyzed 20 further families and found that most were consistent with a PJS gene on 19p13.3, but 3 families were unlinked to 19p13.3, and none of the available polyps from these families showed allele loss at D19S886. There were no obvious clinicopathologic or ethnic differences between linked and unlinked families.

### Heterogeneity

Mehenni et al. (1997) found evidence for a potential second PJS locus on 19q13.4, mostly from family PJS07, and Olschwang et al. (1998) found 3 of 20 families unlinked to 19p13.3 (see MAPPING). Mehenni et al. (1998) failed to detect any LKB1 mutation in 2 Indian families, one of which had shown linkage to markers on 19q13.3-q13.4, suggesting locus heterogeneity.

Westerman et al. (1999) found no apparent abnormality of the LKB1/STK11 gene in 7 of 19 predominantly Dutch PJS families. Jiang et al. (1999) identified a truncating STK11 alteration in only 1 of 10 unrelated PJS families and concluded that STK11 mutations account for only a proportion of PJS cases. Boardman et al. (2000) detected LKB1 mutations in only 2 probands with a family history of PJS and in 4 of 23 sporadic cases, suggesting significant genetic heterogeneity.

Olschwang et al. (2001) identified germline deleterious STK11/LKB1 mutations in 70% of 34 PJS families and concluded that up to 30% of PJS cases are caused by mutation in an unidentified gene. Buchet-Poyau et al. (2002) excluded the STK13, PRKCG, KLK10, and PSCD2 genes in the 19q13.3-q13.4 linkage region, as well as the STK11IP gene on chromosome 2q36, as candidates in family PJS07.

Le Meur et al. (2004) noted that STK11 germline mutations are detected in only 50 to 70% of PJS families and showed that large genomic deletions should be considered in families without detectable point mutations. In contrast, Amos et al. (2004) found no large deletions by real-time PCR in PJS participants without STK11 point mutations.

Alhopuro et al. (2008) searched for mutations in the MYH11 gene in patients with colorectal cancer, PJS, and juvenile polyposis and found a protein-elongating frameshift mutation in the germline of 1 individual with PJS. Frameshift mutations resulted in unregulated myosin molecules with constitutive motor activity.

### Molecular Genetics

Jenne et al. (1998) identified a novel gene encoding the serine/threonine kinase STK11 located 190 kb proximal to D19S886 on 19p13.3. In a 3-generation PJS family, they found an STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 segregating with the disease. Sequence analysis of STK11 in 4 unrelated PJS patients identified 3 nonsense mutations and 1 acceptor splice site mutation. All 5 germline mutations were predicted to disrupt the kinase domain.

Hemminki et al. (1998) identified truncating germline mutations in the LKB1 gene on chromosome 19p in multiple individuals with PJS. LKB1 has strong homology to the cytoplasmic Xenopus serine/threonine protein kinase XEEK1.

Mehenni et al. (1998) screened 9 PJS families of American, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Turkish, and Indian origin and detected 7 novel LKB1 mutations: 2 frameshift mutations, a 4-amino acid deletion, 2 amino acid substitutions, and 2 splicing errors. Expression of mutant LKB1 proteins (K78I, D176N, W308C, and L67P) showed loss of autophosphorylation activity in all mutants, providing evidence that elimination of LKB1 kinase activity is probably responsible for the PJS phenotype.

Gruber et al. (1998) confirmed linkage to 19p13.3 in the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949 and in 5 other families from the Johns Hopkins Polyposis Registry, and identified germline STK11 mutations in all 6 families by sequencing genomic DNA.

In the original Dutch family reported by Peutz, Westerman et al. (1999) found a germline LKB1 mutation, a T insertion at codon 66 in exon 1 resulting in frameshift and stop at codon 162, that cosegregated with the disease.

Westerman et al. (1999) performed mutation analysis in 19 predominantly Dutch PJS families and identified 12 novel LKB1/STK11 mutations in 12 families: 1 nonsense mutation, 3 frameshift deletions, 3 frameshift insertions, 2 acceptor splice site mutations, and 3 missense mutations. None occurred in more than 1 family, and several were shown to have arisen de novo.

Jiang et al. (1999) studied 10 unrelated PJS families by protein truncation test and genomic sequencing and identified a novel truncating deletion spanning STK11 exons 2 through 7 in a single patient.

Boardman et al. (2000) evaluated 5 kindreds with more than 2 affected members, 5 probands with only 1 other affected relative, and 23 individuals with sporadic PJS, using conformation-sensitive gel electrophoresis, direct sequencing, and long-range PCR. LKB1 alterations were found in 2 probands with a family history of PJS and in 4 of the 23 sporadic cases.

Miyaki et al. (2000) detected germline LKB1 mutations in 6 of 9 PJS families.

Olschwang et al. (2001) analyzed the STK11/LKB1 locus in 34 PJS families by searching for mutations and rearrangements in the coding sequence, allele-specific expression tests, and linkage studies, and identified germline deleterious mutations in 70% of cases.

In a family with typical features of PJS, including gastrointestinal hamartomas, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa, Le Meur et al. (2004) used quantitative multiplex PCR of short fluorescent fragments (QMPSF) of the 19p13 region to identify an approximately 250-kb heterozygous deletion removing the entire STK11 locus.

Amos et al. (2004) characterized STK11 mutations in 42 independent probands and detected mutations in 22 of 32 (69%) probands with PJS and in 0 of 10 probands referred to rule out PJS.

In a simplex case of PJS, Hernan et al. (2004) identified a de novo tyr246-to-ter (Y246X) mutation in exon 6 of the LKB1 gene; the mutation was not present in either parent.

In 2 sibs with PJS and primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified a germline STK11 mutation, c.890delG, encoding a truncated protein (p.Arg297fsX38). No germline mutations in the CDH1 or TP53 genes were detected.

Schumacher et al. (2005) studied STK11 genotyping and cancer risk in Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Olschwang et al. (2001) found that PJS families could be divided into 2 groups on the basis of the presence or absence of an identified STK11/LKB1 alteration, and that cancer risk differed markedly between the groups: PJS patients with no identified STK11/LKB1 mutation were at major risk for proximal biliary adenocarcinoma.

Amos et al. (2004) found that individuals with missense mutations in STK11 had a significantly later time to onset of first polypectomy (p = 0.04) and of other symptoms compared with those with truncating mutations or no detectable mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Using CGH of Peutz-Jeghers polyps combined with LOH analysis, Hemminki et al. (1997) identified a deletion site on distal 19p, providing molecular evidence of malignant potential in hamartomas. Jenne et al. (1998) concluded that germline STK11 mutations, probably in conjunction with acquired defects of the second allele in somatic cells, cause the manifestations of PJS. ⚠︎

Gruber et al. (1998) found LOH of 19p markers near STK11 in 70% of hamartomas and adenocarcinomas from PJS patients; haplotype analysis showed that the retained allele carried the germline mutation, confirming that STK11 is a tumor suppressor gene. LOH of 17p and 18q was identified in an adenocarcinoma but not in hamartomas, and adenocarcinomas with 17p LOH showed altered p53 by immunohistochemistry. None of 18 PJS tumors showed microsatellite instability, LOH on 5q near APC, or mutations in codons 12 or 13 of KRAS. The authors suggested that STK11 acts as an early gatekeeper regulating hamartoma development and that hamartomas may be precursors of adenocarcinoma.

Miyaki et al. (2000) examined 27 gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps from 10 patients in 9 PJS families. Somatic LKB1 mutations were found in 5 polyps and LOH at the LKB1 locus in 14 other polyps. Adenomatous lesions microdissected from hamartomatous polyps showed both beta-catenin mutation and 19p LOH, and a carcinomatous lesion in a gastric hamartomatous polyp contained a p53 mutation and LOH at the p53 locus in addition to LKB1 LOH and a beta-catenin mutation. KRAS mutations were detected in a few polyps, and no APC mutation or 5q LOH was found. The authors suggested that additional beta-catenin and p53 mutations convert hamartomatous polyps into adenomatous and carcinomatous lesions.

Entius et al. (2001) analyzed 39 polyps and 5 carcinomas from 17 PJS patients from 13 families. LOH at 19p was seen in 15 of 39 polyps and in all 5 carcinomas; 6 of 7 polyps from patients with cancer had LOH, compared with 9 of 31 polyps from the remaining patients (p = 0.01). No 5q LOH was observed, but an APC mutation was found in 4 of 5 carcinomas. No 17p LOH or p53 mutation was found; 1 carcinoma had a KRAS codon 12 mutation and another showed 18q LOH without loss of SMAD4 expression. The authors suggested that PJS-related tumors follow a pathway of carcinogenesis distinct from the adenoma-carcinoma sequence.

Hernan et al. (2004) studied LOH at the LKB1 locus in microdissected hamartomatous polyps from a patient with a de novo Y246X mutation; the results suggested a mechanism other than LOH in polyp formation. Shinmura et al. (2005) detected no inactivation of the wild-type STK11 allele by somatic mutation, chromosomal deletion, or promoter hypermethylation in gastric cancer from a PJS patient with a germline STK11 mutation.

Young et al. (1995) found that feminizing Sertoli cell tumors in boys with PJS showed increased transcription of the aromatase cytochrome P450 gene from promoter II, resulting in increased estrogen synthesis; transcripts from the placental-type promoters I.1 and I.2 were not detected at significant levels.

### Diagnosis

Mucocutaneous pigmentation resembling that of PJS has been reported in association with occult malignancy. Babin et al. (1978) reported oral hyperpigmentation responding to an occult malignancy. Gass and Glatzer (1991) described a 74-year-old man with occult carcinoma of the colon who developed pigmentation of the mouth and penis typical of PJS as the first manifestation of bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation. Eng et al. (1991) reported a 67-year-old man with esophageal adenocarcinoma and Peutz-Jeghers-like macules on the lips, face, nipples, and rectal mucosa, which showed atypical epidermal melanocytes resembling melanoma in situ.

Veraldi et al. (1991) described 4 patients with Laugier-Hunziker syndrome, aged 39 to 57 years, with pigmentation of the lower lip and hard palate, and in some cases of the buccal mucosa, upper lip, gums, soft palate, and fingers. Histopathology showed accumulation of melanin in basal keratinocytes and increased melanophages, without alterations of melanocytes.

DiLiberti et al. (1983) reported a 7.5-year-old boy with macrocephaly, hamartomatous intestinal polyps, and cafe-au-lait spots on the penis, which they considered to represent the Ruvalcaba-Myhre-Smith syndrome.

Amos et al. (2004) recommended that STK11 mutation analysis be restricted to individuals who meet PJS criteria or their close relatives, since no mutations were found in probands referred to rule out PJS, whereas direct sequencing yielded a high rate of point mutations in those meeting phenotypic criteria.

### Clinical Management

Burdick and Prior (1982) recommended that gastrointestinal surgery continue to be performed in symptomatic patients and that all patients be followed closely at regular intervals. Peloquin et al. (1981) likewise believed that all PJS patients deserve close long-term follow-up. Tweedie and McCann (1984) found that serum carcinoembryonic antigen was normal at presentation in a patient with metastatic colonic carcinoma and did not support its use as a screening test.

Foley et al. (1988) recommended endoscopic removal of even asymptomatic gastric, duodenal, and colonic polyps, and suggested that intraoperative endoscopy with polypectomy may prevent short bowel syndrome when surgery is needed; colonoscopic screening of patients and family members and surveillance for extraintestinal malignancy appeared warranted. ⚠︎

Young et al. (1995) performed bilateral orchiectomy in 2 boys with feminizing Sertoli cell tumors because neoplastic growth would likely result in sterility and continued elevation of estradiol threatened height potential.

Kato et al. (1998) treated labial lentigines in 2 children with PJS with ruby laser therapy, with excellent response and no sequelae or recurrence.

Westerman and Wilson (1999) concluded that, as genetic identification of asymptomatic gene carriers becomes possible, surveillance and screening protocols need to be developed for PJS patients and their relatives.

### Animal Model

Alhopuro et al. (2008) noted that the zebrafish meltdown (mlt) mutant, caused by a recessive ATPase-activating germline mutation in smooth-muscle myosin (smmhc/myh11), develops intestinal abnormalities reminiscent of human PJS and juvenile polyposis.

### History

Westerman et al. (1999) stated that the association of heredity, gastrointestinal polyposis, and mucocutaneous pigmentation was first recognized in 1921 by Peutz in a Dutch family. Keller et al. (2001) reviewed the contributions of Jan Peutz and Harold Jeghers to the recognition of this combination of polyposis and pigmentation of the skin and mucous membranes.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, EX4-5 DEL AND EX6-7 INV

In a 3-generation family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Jenne et al. (1998) found an STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 that segregated with the disease and was predicted to disrupt the kinase domain.

**.0002 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP INS, T, CODON 66

In the original Dutch family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome first recognized by Peutz in 1921, Westerman et al. (1999) found a germline LKB1 mutation, a T insertion at codon 66 in exon 1, resulting in frameshift and a stop at codon 162 in exon 4. The mutation cosegregated with the disease phenotype.

**.0003 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, EX2-7 DEL

In 1 of 10 unrelated families with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Jiang et al. (1999) identified a novel truncating deletion spanning STK11 exons 2 through 7, detected by protein truncation test and genomic sequence analysis. ⚠︎

**.0004 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 250-KB DEL

In a family with typical Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, including gastrointestinal hamartomas, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa, Le Meur et al. (2004) identified by QMPSF an approximately 250-kb heterozygous deletion removing the entire STK11 locus.

**.0005 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, TYR246TER [Tyr246X]

In a simplex case of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Hernan et al. (2004) identified a de novo nucleotide substitution at codon 246 in exon 6 of the LKB1 gene, resulting in a tyr246-to-ter (Y246X) mutation predicted to produce a truncated protein with loss of kinase activity. The mutation was not present in DNA from either parent.

**.0006 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP DEL, 890G [c.890delG; p.Arg297fsX38]

In 2 sibs with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome complicated by primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified a germline 1-bp deletion in the STK11 gene (c.890delG), encoding a truncated protein (p.Arg297fsX38).
