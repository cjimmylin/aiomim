### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the STK11 gene (602216) on chromosome 19p13.3.

### Description

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyposis and mucocutaneous melanin pigmentation, with melanin spots of the lips, buccal mucosa, and digits (Dormandy, 1957; Giardiello et al., 1987; Hemminki et al., 1997; Jenne et al., 1998). Patients have a markedly elevated risk of malignancy, including gastrointestinal cancer and extraintestinal cancers such as those of the breast, ovary, testis, and uterine cervix (Hemminki et al., 1997; Mehenni et al., 1997). Hemminki et al. (1998) described PJS as a rare hereditary disease that predisposes to benign and malignant tumors in many organ systems.

### Nomenclature

Bruwer et al. (1954) used the name Peutz-Jeghers syndrome for the combination of surface pigmentation and generalized intestinal polyposis.

### Clinical Features

Jeghers et al. (1949) described a syndrome of diagnostic significance consisting of generalized intestinal polyposis and melanin spots of the oral mucosa, lips, and digits.

Bartholomew et al. (1962) reported on intestinal polyposis associated with mucocutaneous pigmentation.

Sheward (1962) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in childhood with unusual radiologic features.

Keen and Murray (1962) reported a further family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Michalany and Ferraz (1962) reported Peutz syndrome in a mulatto family, with special reference to the histologic structure of the intestinal polyps.

Farmer et al. (1963) reported 3 cases illustrating the spectrum of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Christian et al. (1964) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with a functioning ovarian tumor.

Williams and Knudsen (1965) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome with metastasizing duodenal carcinoma.

McAllister et al. (1967) reported 17 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in 4 generations of a family.

Kieselstein et al. (1969) reported mucocutaneous pigmentation and intestinal polyposis in a family of Iraqi Jews with polycystic kidney disease; the report included a chromosome study.

Scully (1970) described sex cord tumor with annular tubules as a distinctive ovarian tumor of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Sommerhaug and Mason (1970) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome together with ureteral polyposis.

McKittrick et al. (1971) reported 2 cases of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, one with a 30-year follow-up.

Dodds et al. (1972) discussed gastrointestinal malignancy in association with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

McAllister and Richards (1977) reported 20 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in 5 generations of 1 family, the largest personal series in the literature at that time. They discussed how the spots on the lips and the polyps develop and relate to each other, and described a previously unreported case of colon intussusception. ⚠︎

Lin et al. (1977) described a patient with Peutz-Jeghers polyposis and metastasizing duodenal carcinoma but without cutaneous pigmentation; vitiligo in the patient was considered coincidental. They noted that of 14 reported cases of PJS associated with intestinal carcinoma, the tumor had been shown to originate in the polyp in only 2, and in their own patient they believed that the duodenal carcinoma had developed independently. They stated that duodenal carcinoma is the most commonly associated neoplasm.

Cochet et al. (1979) described metastasizing gastrointestinal carcinoma in a 56-year-old woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and her 29-year-old son. The mother died of duodenal carcinoma and the son of gastric carcinoma; both carcinomas developed in hamartomatous polyps and had extensive metastases. Both patients also had dysplastic areas within hamartomatous polyps, and the authors concluded that hamartomatous polyps may, in some cases, be precursors of digestive tract carcinomas.

Matuchansky et al. (1979) described a patient with PJS in whom an intestinal adenocarcinoma, with metastases to the omentum and celiac lymph nodes, was shown to originate in a jejunal hamartoma. The adenocarcinoma was closely intermingled with smooth muscle bands, and a well-defined transitional zone of malignant cells was seen in several glands of the degenerated polyp. Glands near the malignant areas had less-differentiated epithelium but were still mixed with nonstriated muscle bundles, which the authors considered strong evidence that the hamartomatous structures had dedifferentiated.

Cantu et al. (1980) described a 6-year-old boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and a unilateral feminizing Sertoli cell tumor. Endocrinologic studies showed consistently high plasma and urine estrogen levels with normal testosterone and dihydrotestosterone levels. The raised estrogen levels did not change with exogenous gonadotropin administration, indicating autonomous production. Histology of the nontumorous testicular tissue suggested that estrogens may have had both stimulating and atrophying effects on the seminiferous tubules. The authors concluded that gonadal tumors are an additional manifestation of the PJS gene in both male and female patients. ⚠︎

Riley and Swift (1980) reported a family in which the proband and her paternal grandmother had Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and bilateral breast carcinoma. An ovarian sex cord tumor with annular tubules was an incidental finding when oophorectomy was performed as treatment for the proband's premenopausal breast cancer. Noting that breast cancer had been reported before in patients with PJS, the authors suggested that the PJS gene may be associated with an increased risk of breast tumors.

Yamada et al. (1981) reported ultrastructural studies of the pigmented macules of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Peloquin et al. (1981) described a 49-year-old woman with PJS who first presented with deep thrombophlebitis of the leg, which appeared to be part of a paraneoplastic syndrome. She had an adenocarcinoma originating in a hamartoma of the terminal ileum, with ovarian and lymph node metastases; metastatic disease in the cervical lymph nodes responded temporarily to chemotherapy.

Burdick and Prior (1982) followed a family group of 10 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome for 27 years; 8 family members had 1 or more manifestations of the syndrome. Two members developed breast carcinomas, one arising in a fibroadenoma; both died, one of metastatic breast carcinoma and the other of a second primary malignancy, an adenocarcinoma of the jejunum arising in a Peutz-Jeghers polyp, with metastases in the mesenteric lymph nodes documented at postmortem. Three family members had benign ovarian tumors, 1 had a benign breast tumor, and 1 had a benign colloid thyroid nodule. The authors concluded that the exact risk of intestinal cancer in PJS was unknown but probably very small. ⚠︎

Solh et al. (1983) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with precocious puberty.

Parker and Knight (1983) reported a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who had obstructive jaundice due to a polyp in the common bile duct.

Lehur et al. (1984) described a woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who successively developed bilateral breast carcinoma and malignant transformation of a duodenal hamartomatous polyp, from which she died.

Tweedie and McCann (1984) described a 39-year-old man with PJS and a cecal adenocarcinoma that had metastasized widely to regional lymph nodes. The carcinoma was associated with several other colonic Peutz-Jeghers polyps that showed no dysplasia or malignancy, and the patient also had gastric and small intestinal polyps. The authors could not determine whether the carcinoma had arisen from a Peutz-Jeghers polyp, and stated that the risk of malignancy in PJS was unknown and would require follow-up of a defined PJS population compared with a matched general population sample.

Wilson et al. (1986) described a 6-year-old boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, gynecomastia, and multifocal and bilateral testicular tumors. From this case and other reports they concluded that males with PJS, as well as females, are at risk of developing gonadal tumors.

Banse-Kupin and Douglass (1986) reported a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in whom the characteristic pigmented macules developed within preexisting psoriatic plaques, at sites extremely unusual for the syndrome.

Bowlby (1986) reported a 16-year-old male with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, which the author believed to be the first reported association of PJS with carcinoma of the pancreas.

Giardiello et al. (1987) studied 31 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who were followed from 1973 to 1985. Cancer developed in 15 of the 31 patients (48%): gastrointestinal carcinomas in 4, nongastrointestinal carcinomas in 10, and multiple myeloma in 1. Adenomatous polyps of the stomach and colon occurred in 3 other patients. Cancers were diagnosed at a relatively young age but after the diagnosis of PJS (interval 25 +/- 20 years; range 1 to 64), and the observed development of cancer was 18 times greater than expected in the general population (P less than 0.0001), suggesting increased risk at gastrointestinal and nongastrointestinal sites.

Foley et al. (1988) reported a 49-year follow-up of the 'Harrisburg family,' whose 12 affected members made up the largest Peutz-Jeghers kindred reported at that time. One family member developed a duodenal carcinoma in a hamartoma with adenomatous changes, a progression in the duodenum not previously reported; gastrointestinal malignancy developed in 2 of the 12 affected members. Ten patients underwent 75 polypectomies, 1 patient developed short bowel syndrome, and 3 patients died in young adulthood. The authors concluded that PJS is not a benign disease and may be a premalignant condition.

Spigelman et al. (1989) found that among 72 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, malignant tumors developed in 16 (22%), of whom all but 1 died; there were 9 gastrointestinal and 7 nongastrointestinal tumors. The relative risks of death from gastrointestinal cancer and from all cancers were 13 and 9, respectively, and the chance of dying of cancer by age 57 years was 48%.

Coen et al. (1991) reported an aromatase-producing sex-cord tumor resulting in prepubertal gynecomastia.

Fernandez Seara et al. (1995) described a 15-day-old girl with generalized gastrointestinal polyposis resulting from Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Her manifestations included abdominal distention, hematemesis, bloody diarrhea, and edema, and she died at 1 year of age of multiple complications of her disease.

Young et al. (1995) reported multicentric Sertoli cell testicular tumors in 2 young boys with probable Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Each boy had prominent gynecomastia, rapid growth, and advanced bone age, and serum estradiol levels were markedly elevated; anti-mullerian hormone, measured in 1 boy, was in the normal range for age. Microscopy showed greatly enlarged seminiferous tubules packed with Sertoli-like cells and prominent eosinophilic basement membrane that formed hyalinized globules and microcalcifications. Electron microscopy showed laminated basement membranes and limited smooth endoplasmic reticulum.

In a literature review, Westerman and Wilson (1999) noted that the polyps in PJS carry risks of surgical emergencies such as small bowel intussusception and of chronic or acute bleeding, and that PJS is associated with both gastrointestinal and nongastrointestinal tumors, including rare tumors such as ovarian sex cord tumors with annular tubules, adenoma malignum of the cervix, and Sertoli cell tumors of the testis, indicating a general susceptibility to malignancy.

Westerman et al. (1999) studied the natural history of PJS in 6 generations of the original Dutch family described by Peutz. Clinical features in the family included gastrointestinal polyposis, mucocutaneous pigmentation, nasal polyposis, and rectal extrusion of polyps. Survival of affected members was reduced by intestinal obstruction and by the development of malignant disease.

Bergada et al. (2000) described a prepubertal boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, gynecomastia, and bilateral neoplastic Sertoli cell proliferation, in whom the only abnormal hormonal finding was increased serum inhibin B and pro-alpha C. They stated that gynecomastia in boys with PJS and Sertoli cell tumors of gonadal origin results from increased estrogen production due to increased aromatase activity within the testicular tumor.

Keller et al. (2002) presented molecular genetic evidence of an association between nasal polyposis and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Le Meur et al. (2004) reported a family with typical features of PJS, including gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa.

In a historical cohort of 51 individuals with PJS who completed questionnaires, Amos et al. (2004) found a median time to onset of gastrointestinal symptoms or polypectomy of 13 years, with wide variability. Gastric polyps were frequent, with a median age at onset of 16 years.

Shinmura et al. (2005) reported 2 sibs with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome complicated by primary gastric cancer.

### Inheritance

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait (Giardiello et al., 1987; Jenne et al., 1998). McAllister et al. (1967) reported 17 affected individuals in 4 generations of a family, and McAllister and Richards (1977) reported 20 patients in 5 generations.

In 5 families studied for linkage, Amos et al. (1997) found that every carrier of the linked haplotype expressed the PJS phenotype and that no noncarrier showed PJS features, which they took to indicate complete penetrance with no sporadic cases. Mehenni et al. (1997) described PJS as an autosomal dominant disease with variable expression and incomplete penetrance.

De novo mutations have been reported: Westerman et al. (1999) found that a number of the STK11 mutations in their predominantly Dutch families had arisen de novo, and Hernan et al. (2004) identified a de novo STK11 mutation in a simplex patient.

### Cytogenetics

Markie et al. (1996) found a pericentric inversion of chromosome 6 on karyotypic analysis in a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. FISH with YAC clones placed one breakpoint within the centromeric alphoid array, in a 1-cM interval between D6S257 and D6S402, and the other breakpoint in a 4-cM interval between D6S403 and D6S311. The estrogen receptor gene locus (ESR) was excluded from the 4-cM interval containing the distal breakpoint.

### Mapping

Hemminki et al. (1997) used comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) of Peutz-Jeghers polyps together with loss of heterozygosity (LOH) analysis to identify a deletion site. CGH, as described by Kallioniemi et al. (1992), maps DNA sequence copy number by chromosomal location across the genome by hybridizing differentially labeled test and normal reference DNA together to normal chromosome spreads. Linkage analysis in 12 families with markers from the deletion region demonstrated a high-penetrance locus in distal 19p, with a multipoint lod score of 7.00 at marker D19S886.

Amos et al. (1997) confirmed that PJS maps to the telomeric region of chromosome 19p and narrowed the locus. In 5 families there were no recombinants with marker D19S886, and the multipoint lod score at D19S886 was 7.52.

Mehenni et al. (1997) performed a genomewide linkage analysis in 6 PJS families (2 from Spain, 2 from India, 1 from the United States, and 1 from Portugal), comprising 93 individuals, of whom 39 were affected, 48 unaffected, and 6 of unknown status. They confirmed linkage to 19p13.3: marker D19S886 gave a maximum lod score of 4.74 at theta = 0.045, and multipoint analysis gave a lod score of 7.51 for the interval between D19S886 and 19pter.

Olschwang et al. (1998) analyzed 20 more PJS families and found that most were consistent with a PJS gene on 19p13.3, supporting a major PJS locus on that chromosome.

Gruber et al. (1998) confirmed linkage of PJS to 19p13.3 near STK11 in the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949 and in 5 other families.

### Heterogeneity

Hemminki et al. (1997) found no evidence of genetic heterogeneity among 12 Peutz-Jeghers families linked to distal 19p, and Amos et al. (1997) likewise found no evidence of heterogeneity among the 5 families they studied.

Mehenni et al. (1997) also found significant evidence for linkage to markers on 19q13.4; for example, D19S880 gave a maximum lod score of 3.8 at theta = 0.13. Most of this signal came from a single family, PJS07. The authors suggested that a potential second PJS locus on 19q13.4 may exist in a minority of families.

Olschwang et al. (1998) found that 3 of 20 PJS families were unlinked to 19p13.3, and none of the available polyps from these families showed allele loss at D19S886. There were no obvious clinicopathologic or ethnic differences between linked and unlinked families, and the authors suggested that there may be a minor locus (or loci) elsewhere.

Mehenni et al. (1998) found no LKB1 mutation in 2 Indian PJS families; in one of these they had previously detected linkage to markers on 19q13.3-q13.4, which suggested locus heterogeneity.

Westerman et al. (1999) found no apparent LKB1/STK11 abnormalities in 7 of 19 predominantly Dutch PJS families, which they suggested could reflect locus heterogeneity.

Jiang et al. (1999) screened 10 unrelated PJS families for germline STK11 alterations by protein truncation test and genomic sequencing and found a truncating deletion in only 1 patient; they concluded that STK11 mutations account for only a proportion of PJS cases. ⚠︎

Boardman et al. (2000) found LKB1 mutations in only 2 of 10 PJS probands with a family history and in 4 of 23 sporadic PJS cases, and concluded that PJS shows significant genetic heterogeneity and that other loci are involved.

Entius et al. (2001) found no LOH at 19p, or at the alternative candidate PJS loci 19q, 6p, and 6q, in a polyp from a patient without a germline STK11 mutation.

Olschwang et al. (2001) found that their data on 34 PJS families reinforced the hypothesis of a second PJS locus, and concluded that up to 30% of PJS cases are caused by mutation in an unidentified gene conferring high susceptibility to cancer.

Buchet-Poyau et al. (2002) studied family PJS07, which lacked STK11 mutations and showed linkage to 19q13.3-q13.4 between markers D19S180 and D19S254. Sequencing all exons and exon-intron junctions of PSCD2, KLK10, PRKCG, and STK13 in affected members found no pathogenic mutations, providing considerable evidence for exclusion of these genes as the second PJS locus; a very rare polymorphism resulting in a conserved lys-to-arg change was found in PSCD2. They also excluded STK11IP (LIP1) on 2q36 in this family, although it could be a candidate in other families without STK11 mutations. ⚠︎

Le Meur et al. (2004) noted that STK11 germline mutations are detected in only 50 to 70% of PJS families, which had suggested genetic heterogeneity of the disease. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jenne et al. (1998) identified a novel serine threonine kinase gene, STK11, located 190 kb proximal to marker D19S886 in the 19p13.3 Peutz-Jeghers linkage region. In a 3-generation PJS family they found an STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 that segregated with the disease. Sequence analysis of STK11 exons in 4 unrelated PJS patients identified 3 nonsense mutations and 1 acceptor splice site mutation. All 5 germline mutations were predicted to disrupt the kinase domain, and the authors concluded that germline STK11 mutations, probably together with acquired defects of the second allele in somatic cells, cause PJS.

Hemminki et al. (1998) identified truncating germline mutations in multiple individuals with PJS in LKB1, a gene on chromosome 19p that had been identified before but not mapped. LKB1 was strongly homologous to XEEK1, a cytoplasmic serine/threonine protein kinase in Xenopus, and more weakly similar to many other protein kinases. The authors concluded that PJS was the first cancer-susceptibility syndrome found to be caused by inactivating mutations in a protein kinase.

Mehenni et al. (1998) screened 9 PJS families of American, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Turkish, and Indian origin for LKB1 mutations and found 7 novel mutations: 2 frameshift mutations, a 4-amino acid deletion, 2 amino acid substitutions, and 2 splicing errors. Mutant LKB1 proteins K78I, D176N, W308C, and L67P, when expressed, had all lost autophosphorylation activity. No LKB1 mutation was found in 2 Indian families.

Gruber et al. (1998) identified germline STK11 mutations by sequencing genomic DNA in all 6 families from the Johns Hopkins Polyposis Registry that they studied, including the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949.

In the original Dutch family described by Peutz, Westerman et al. (1999) found that a mutant LKB1 allele carrying a T insertion at codon 66 in exon 1, resulting in frameshift and a stop at codon 162 in exon 4, cosegregated with the disease phenotype.

Westerman et al. (1999) performed mutation analysis in 19, predominantly Dutch, PJS families and identified 12 novel LKB1/STK11 mutations in 12 families: 1 nonsense mutation, 3 frameshift deletions, 3 frameshift insertions, 2 acceptor splice site mutations, and 3 missense mutations. No mutation occurred in more than 1 family, and a number had arisen de novo. Four polymorphisms were also detected.

Jiang et al. (1999) identified a novel truncating deletion spanning STK11 exons 2 through 7 in a single patient among 10 unrelated PJS families, along with several known polymorphisms. LOH studies in PJS polyps from 4 patients identified an allelic deletion of D19S886 in another patient.

Boardman et al. (2000) evaluated 5 kindreds with more than 2 affected members, 5 PJS probands with only 1 other affected family member, and 23 individuals with sporadic PJS, using conformation sensitive gel electrophoresis followed by direct sequencing, with long-range PCR to detect larger insertions or deletions. They found LKB1 alterations in 2 probands with a family history of PJS and in 4 of the 23 sporadic cases.

Miyaki et al. (2000) detected germline LKB1 mutations in 6 of 9 PJS families. In 27 gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps from 10 patients, they found somatic LKB1 mutations in 5 polyps and LOH at the LKB1 locus on 19p in 14 other polyps. The polyps showed no APC mutation or 5q LOH, and K-ras mutations were detected in only a few polyps.

Olschwang et al. (2001) analyzed the STK11/LKB1 locus in 34 PJS families by mutation and rearrangement screening of the coding sequence, allele-specific expression tests, and linkage studies, and identified germline deleterious STK11 mutations in 70% of cases.

Using quantitative multiplex PCR of short fluorescent fragments (QMPSF) of the 19p13 region, Le Meur et al. (2004) found an approximately 250-kb heterozygous deletion removing the entire STK11 gene in a family with PJS. This was the first reported complete germline deletion of STK11, and the authors suggested that large genomic deletions should be considered in PJS families without detectable STK11 point mutations.

Amos et al. (2004) detected STK11 mutations, both missense and truncating, in 22 of 32 (69%) PJS probands and in none of 10 probands referred to rule out PJS. Real-time PCR found no large deletions in mutation-negative patients.

In a simplex patient with PJS, Hernan et al. (2004) identified a substitution of cytosine for guanine at codon 246 in exon 6 of the LKB1 gene, resulting in a tyr246-to-ter (Y246X) mutation. The mutation was not present in either parent and therefore occurred de novo.

In 2 sibs with PJS and primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified a germline 1-bp deletion in the STK11 gene (c.890delG) encoding a truncated protein (Arg297fsTer38); no germline mutations were found in CDH1 or TP53. In gastric cancer tissue from 1 patient, there was no inactivation of the wild-type STK11 allele by somatic mutation or chromosomal deletion and no hypermethylation at the 5-prime CpG site of STK11. ⚠︎

Alhopuro et al. (2008) screened MYH11 (160745) in patients with colorectal cancer, PJS, and juvenile polyposis. They found a germline protein-elongating frameshift mutation in 1 individual with PJS, and somatic frameshift mutations in 55% of colorectal cancers with microsatellite instability; 2 somatic missense mutations, R501L and K1044N, were found in 1 microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer. The frameshift and missense mutations all produced unregulated molecules with constitutive motor activity, similar to the mutant myosin underlying the zebrafish meltdown (mlt) phenotype. The authors suggested that MYH11 mutations appear to contribute to human intestinal neoplasia.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Olschwang et al. (2001) found that cancer risk differed markedly between PJS families with and without an identified STK11 alteration; patients without an identified STK11 mutation were at major risk for proximal biliary adenocarcinoma, a tumor infrequent in the general population.

Amos et al. (2004) found that individuals with PJS carrying missense mutations of STK11 had a significantly later time to onset of first polypectomy (p = 0.04) and of other symptoms than those with truncating mutations or no detectable mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Spigelman et al. (1989) concluded that there was evidence for a hamartoma/carcinoma sequence in Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, suggesting that the gene locus involved is relevant to the development of malignancy in general.

In testicular tumor tissue from 2 boys with PJS, Young et al. (1995) found increased transcription of the aromatase cytochrome P450 gene from promoter II, the promoter used in normal ovary and testis, at levels comparable to corpus luteum, leading to increased estrogen synthesis. Transcripts from the placental-type promoters I.1 and I.2 were not found at significant levels. ⚠︎

Hemminki et al. (1997) suggested that the deletions/LOH at 19p in Peutz-Jeghers polyps pointed to a tumor suppressor locus and provided molecular evidence of malignant potential in hamartomas.

Mehenni et al. (1998) found that the mutant LKB1 proteins K78I, D176N, W308C, and L67P had all lost autophosphorylation (kinase) activity, and concluded that loss of LKB1 kinase activity is probably responsible for the PJS phenotypes.

Gruber et al. (1998) found LOH of 19p markers near STK11 in 70% of hamartomas and adenocarcinomas from PJS patients; haplotype analysis showed that the retained allele carried the germline mutation, confirming that STK11 is a tumor suppressor gene. They proposed that STK11 acts as an early gatekeeper regulating hamartoma development. LOH of 17p and 18q was found in an adenocarcinoma but not in hamartomas, implying that these are late events, and adenocarcinomas with 17p LOH showed altered p53 by immunohistochemistry. None of 18 PJS tumors showed microsatellite instability, LOH at 5q near APC, or mutations in K-ras codons 12 or 13. The authors suggested that hamartomas may be precursors of adenocarcinoma.

Miyaki et al. (2000) found both a beta-catenin mutation and 19p LOH in adenomatous lesions microdissected from hamartomatous polyps. A carcinomatous lesion in a gastric polyp had a p53 mutation and p53 LOH in addition to LKB1 LOH and a beta-catenin mutation. The authors suggested that PJS polyps develop when the second LKB1 allele is inactivated by somatic mutation or LOH, and that further beta-catenin and p53 mutations convert hamartomas into adenomatous and carcinomatous lesions.

Entius et al. (2001) analyzed 39 polyps and 5 carcinomas from 17 PJS patients in 13 families and found LOH at 19p13.3 (the STK11/LKB1 locus) in 15 of the 39 hamartomatous polyps and in all 5 carcinomas. Six of 7 polyps from patients with cancer showed LOH, compared with 9 of 31 polyps from the other patients (p = 0.01). They concluded that STK11 acts as a tumor suppressor and may be involved early in PJS tumorigenesis, and suggested that LOH in polyps be tested as a biomarker to predict cancer. In the carcinomas they found no LOH at 5q, but APC mutations in 4 of 5; no 17p LOH or p53 mutation; an activating KRAS codon 12 mutation in 1; and 18q LOH without loss of SMAD4 expression in another. The authors suggested that these differences from the colorectal adenoma-carcinoma sequence point to a distinct pathway of carcinogenesis.

Hernan et al. (2004) studied DNA from microdissected gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps of a PJS patient with a de novo STK11 Y246X mutation for LOH at the LKB1 locus using real-time fluorescence PCR genotyping; the results suggested a mechanism other than LOH in the formation of the hamartomatous polyps.

### Diagnosis

Babin et al. (1978) reported a case in which melanocytes of the oral and laryngeal mucosa caused hyperpigmentation in response to an occult malignancy, and discussed how to distinguish this from other disease processes that cause oral hyperpigmentation. ⚠︎

DiLiberti et al. (1983) described a 7.5-year-old boy with macrocephaly, hamartomatous intestinal polyps, and cafe-au-lait spots on the penis, and proposed that this triad was a separate disorder, the Ruvalcaba-Myhre-Smith syndrome, distinct from other hamartomatous polyposis conditions. ⚠︎

Tweedie and McCann (1984) noted that serum CEA was normal at presentation in their patient with PJS and metastasizing colonic adenocarcinoma, as in one other reported case, and concluded that CEA determination as a screening test in high-risk groups was not supported.

Eng et al. (1991) reported a 67-year-old white man with esophageal adenocarcinoma who had Peutz-Jeghers-like macules on the lips, face, nipples, and rectal mucosa; the macules contained atypical, pleomorphic large melanocytes in the epidermis resembling the neoplastic cells of melanoma in situ.

Gass and Glatzer (1991) described a 74-year-old man with an occult carcinoma of the colon who developed acquired pigmentation of the mouth and penis typical of PJS as the first sign of bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation. They suggested that the pigmented lesions may come from activation of occult melanocytic nevus cells in response to a hormone-producing carcinoma or another oncogenic stimulus. ⚠︎

Veraldi et al. (1991) described 4 adults, aged 39 to 57 years, with Laugier-Hunziker syndrome, all of whom had pigmentation of the lower lip and hard palate, some also with pigmentation of the buccal mucosa, upper lip, gums, soft palate, and fingers. Histopathology showed melanin accumulated in basal layer keratinocytes and more melanophages in the papillary dermis, with unaltered melanocytes. ⚠︎

Westerman et al. (1999) concluded that the diverse array of mutations, the apparent high mutation rate, and a possible second PJS locus make diagnostic or predictive genetic testing in individual patients difficult.

Having detected STK11 mutations in 22 of 32 PJS probands but in none of 10 probands referred to rule out PJS, Amos et al. (2004) concluded that STK11 testing should be restricted to individuals meeting PJS criteria or their close relatives.

### Clinical Management

Joishy et al. (1979) reported a Malaysian patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome whose successive intussusceptions required extensive small bowel resection, and reviewed the literature on diagnosing and managing complications of the syndrome.

Griffith and Bisset (1980) reported 3 cases of PJS presenting in early childhood and considered the need for surgical intervention.

Peloquin et al. (1981) reviewed the literature on metastatic PJS and concluded that, although the rate of metastatic disease is low, all patients with PJS deserve close long-term follow-up.

Burdick and Prior (1982) recommended continuing gastrointestinal surgery in symptomatic patients and close follow-up of all patients at regular intervals.

Foley et al. (1988) recommended that even asymptomatic gastric, duodenal, and colonic polyps be removed endoscopically, that intraoperative endoscopy with polypectomy be used when surgery is necessary to prevent short bowel syndrome, and that colonoscopic screening and surveillance for extraintestinal malignancy appeared warranted. ⚠︎

Young et al. (1995) performed bilateral orchiectomy in 2 boys with PJS and feminizing Sertoli cell tumors because tumor growth would most likely cause sterility and continued high estradiol levels threatened to reduce height potential. ⚠︎

Kato et al. (1998) treated labial lentigines in 2 children with PJS with ruby laser therapy, with excellent response and no sequelae or recurrence, and suggested that this therapy is safe and suitable for labial lentigines in children with PJS.

Westerman and Wilson (1999) concluded that because genetic identification of asymptomatic gene carriers was becoming possible, surveillance and screening protocols needed to be developed for PJS patients and their relatives.

### Animal Model

Alhopuro et al. (2008) noted that the zebrafish meltdown (mlt) phenotype, caused by a recessive ATPase-activating germline mutation in smooth-muscle myosin (smmhc/myh11), develops intestinal abnormalities reminiscent of human Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and juvenile polyposis.

### History

Westerman et al. (1999) followed up the original Dutch family described by Peutz in 1921 for more than 78 years, across 6 generations, and concluded from the morbidity and mortality in the family that PJS is not a benign disease.

Keller et al. (2001) reviewed the history of the combination of intestinal polyposis with pigmentation of the skin and mucous membranes, including the work of Jan Peutz and Harold Jeghers. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, EX4-5 DEL AND EX6-7 INV

In a 3-generation family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Jenne et al. (1998) found a heterozygous STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 that segregated with the disease.

**.0002 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 3 NONSENSE MUTATIONS

Sequence analysis of STK11 exons in 4 unrelated patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome by Jenne et al. (1998) identified 3 heterozygous nonsense mutations (and 1 acceptor splice site mutation); the specific changes were not given in the evidence. The authors predicted that the mutations disrupt the kinase domain.

**.0003 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, ACCEPTOR SPLICE SITE MUTATION

Among 4 unrelated patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Jenne et al. (1998) identified a heterozygous acceptor splice site mutation in STK11 in 1 patient (the other 3 had nonsense mutations).

**.0004 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, TRUNCATING MUTATIONS

Hemminki et al. (1998) identified truncating germline mutations in the LKB1 (STK11) gene on chromosome 19p in multiple individuals affected by Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

**.0005 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, LYS78ILE [p.K78I]

In a study of PJS families of American, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Turkish, and Indian origin, Mehenni et al. (1998) expressed the mutant LKB1 protein lys78-to-ile (K78I) and found that it had lost autophosphorylation activity. The paper did not state which family carried this change.

**.0006 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, ASP176ASN [p.D176N]

Mehenni et al. (1998) expressed the mutant LKB1 protein asp176-to-asn (D176N), from their study of PJS families, and found that it had lost autophosphorylation activity. The paper did not state which family carried this change. ⚠︎

**.0007 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, TRP308CYS [p.W308C]

Mehenni et al. (1998) expressed the mutant LKB1 protein trp308-to-cys (W308C), from their study of PJS families, and found that it had lost autophosphorylation activity. The paper did not state which family carried this change. ⚠︎

**.0008 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, LEU67PRO [p.L67P]

Mehenni et al. (1998) expressed the mutant LKB1 protein leu67-to-pro (L67P), from their study of PJS families, and found that it had lost autophosphorylation activity. The paper did not state which family carried this change. ⚠︎

**.0009 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, GERMLINE MUTATIONS IN 6 JOHNS HOPKINS FAMILIES

Gruber et al. (1998) identified germline STK11 mutations by sequencing genomic DNA in all 6 PJS families they studied from the Johns Hopkins Polyposis Registry, including the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949; these families showed linkage to 19p13.3 near STK11. In tumors, LOH near STK11 retained the allele carrying the germline mutation.

**.0010 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP INS, T, CODON 66

In the original Dutch family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome described by Peutz, Westerman et al. (1999) found a heterozygous T insertion at codon 66 in exon 1 of the LKB1 (STK11) gene, resulting in frameshift and a stop at codon 162 in exon 4. The mutation cosegregated with the disease phenotype. Affected members had gastrointestinal polyposis, mucocutaneous pigmentation, nasal polyposis, and rectal extrusion of polyps, and survival was reduced by intestinal obstruction and malignant disease. ⚠︎

**.0011 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 12 MUTATIONS IN DUTCH FAMILIES

In 12 of 19, predominantly Dutch, PJS families, Westerman et al. (1999) identified 12 novel LKB1/STK11 mutations: 1 nonsense mutation, 3 frameshift deletions, 3 frameshift insertions, 2 acceptor splice site mutations, and 3 missense mutations. None occurred in more than 1 family, and a number had arisen de novo.

**.0012 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, EX2-7 DEL

Among 10 unrelated PJS families screened by protein truncation test and genomic sequencing, Jiang et al. (1999) identified a novel heterozygous truncating deletion spanning STK11 exons 2 through 7 in a single patient. ⚠︎

**.0013 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, MUTATIONS IN 2 FAMILIAL PROBANDS

Boardman et al. (2000) found genetic alterations in LKB1 in 2 of 10 PJS probands who had a family history of PJS.

**.0014 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, MUTATIONS IN 4 SPORADIC CASES

Boardman et al. (2000) detected LKB1 mutations in 4 of 23 individuals with sporadic PJS.

**.0015 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, GERMLINE MUTATIONS IN 6 PJS FAMILIES

Miyaki et al. (2000) detected germline LKB1 mutations in 6 of 9 PJS families studied; the patients' gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps showed somatic LKB1 mutations or LOH at the LKB1 locus. ⚠︎

**.0016 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, DELETERIOUS GERMLINE MUTATIONS

Olschwang et al. (2001) identified germline deleterious STK11/LKB1 mutations in 70% of 34 PJS families, whose features included hamartomatous gastrointestinal polyps and perioral pigmented lesions. ⚠︎

**.0017 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 250-KB DEL

In a family with typical features of PJS, including gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa, Le Meur et al. (2004) used QMPSF of the 19p13 region to identify an approximately 250-kb heterozygous deletion removing the entire STK11 locus, the first reported complete germline deletion of STK11.

**.0018 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, MISSENSE AND TRUNCATING POINT MUTATIONS

Amos et al. (2004) detected STK11 point mutations, missense and truncating, in 22 of 32 (69%) PJS probands. Carriers of missense mutations had a significantly later onset of first polypectomy (p = 0.04) and of other symptoms than those with truncating mutations or no detectable mutation.

**.0019 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, TYR246TER [p.Y246*]

In a simplex patient with PJS and gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, Hernan et al. (2004) identified a heterozygous substitution of cytosine for guanine at codon 246 in exon 6 of the LKB1 (STK11) gene, resulting in a tyr246-to-ter (Y246X) mutation. Neither parent carried the mutation, which therefore arose de novo. LOH analysis of the patient's polyps suggested a mechanism other than LOH in polyp formation. ⚠︎

**.0020 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP DEL, 890G [c.890delg; p.R297fs]

In 2 sibs with PJS complicated by primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified a novel heterozygous germline 1-bp deletion in the STK11 gene (c.890delG), encoding a truncated protein (Arg297fsTer38). No germline mutations of CDH1 or TP53 were found. Gastric cancer tissue showed no inactivation of the wild-type allele by somatic mutation or chromosomal deletion and no hypermethylation at the 5-prime CpG site of STK11. ⚠︎
