### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the STK11 gene (602216) on chromosome 19p13.3.

### Description

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by hamartomatous polyps of the gastrointestinal tract and mucocutaneous melanin deposits (Giardiello et al., 1987). The melanin spots occur on the lips, buccal mucosa, and digits (Hemminki et al., 1997). Patients have an increased risk for various neoplasms, including gastrointestinal cancer (Jenne et al., 1998). Gastrointestinal tumors and cancers of the breast, ovary, testis, and uterine cervix appear to be overrepresented (Hemminki et al., 1997). Mehenni et al. (1997) described the disorder as having variable expression and incomplete penetrance, and Mehenni et al. (1998) noted that the increased risk of benign and malignant tumors applies both to the gastrointestinal tract and to tissues outside it. Hemminki et al. (1998) described PJS as a rare hereditary disease that predisposes to benign and malignant tumors in many organ systems.

### Nomenclature

Bruwer et al. (1954) used the designation Peutz-Jeghers syndrome for the association of surface pigmentation with generalized intestinal polyposis. Dormandy (1957) also used the name Peutz-Jeghers syndrome for the combination of gastrointestinal polyposis and mucocutaneous pigmentation.

### Clinical Features

Jeghers et al. (1949) described a syndrome of generalized intestinal polyposis with melanin spots of the oral mucosa, lips, and digits. They considered the syndrome to be of diagnostic significance.

Bartholomew et al. (1962) reported on intestinal polyposis associated with mucocutaneous pigmentation.

Sheward (1962) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in childhood with unusual radiologic features.

Keen and Murray (1962) reported a further family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Michalany and Ferraz (1962) reported the syndrome in a family described as mulatto, with particular attention to the histologic structure of the intestinal polyps.

Farmer et al. (1963) reported 3 cases illustrating the spectrum of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Christian et al. (1964) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with a functioning ovarian tumor.

Williams and Knudsen (1965) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome with a metastasizing duodenal carcinoma.

Kieselstein et al. (1969) described a family of Iraqi Jews with mucocutaneous pigmentation and intestinal polyposis in which polycystic kidney disease also occurred. They also performed a chromosome study.

Scully (1970) described sex cord tumor with annular tubules as a distinctive ovarian tumor of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Sommerhaug and Mason (1970) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome together with ureteral polyposis.

McKittrick et al. (1971) reported 2 cases of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, one of which had been followed for 30 years.

Dodds et al. (1972) reported on gastrointestinal malignancy in Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Brigg et al. (1976) reported unusual manifestations of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

McAllister and Richards (1977) reported 20 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in 5 generations, which they described as the largest personal series in the literature. They presented pedigree charts and discussed how the lip spots and intestinal polyps develop and relate to each other. They also presented a previously unreported case of colon intussusception in a patient with the syndrome, and discussed its clinical presentation and malignant potential.

Lin et al. (1977) reported a patient with Peutz-Jeghers polyposis and a metastasizing duodenal carcinoma. The patient did not have the skin pigmentation typical of the syndrome. The patient also had vitiligo, which the authors considered coincidental.

Cochet et al. (1979) described a 56-year-old woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and her affected 29-year-old son, both of whom developed metastasizing gastrointestinal carcinoma. The mother died of duodenal carcinoma and the son died of gastric carcinoma. In both, the carcinoma developed in hamartomatous polyps and had metastasized extensively. The authors stated that patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome have a slight, though definite, increased risk of gastrointestinal carcinoma.

Joishy et al. (1979) reported a patient from Malaysia with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, described as the first case from that country. The patient had successive intussusceptions that required extensive small bowel resection.

Matuchansky et al. (1979) described a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who had an intestinal adenocarcinoma with spread to the omentum and the celiac lymph nodes. Histology showed that the cancer arose in a jejunal hamartoma. The adenocarcinoma was closely intermixed with smooth muscle bands, and several glands of the polyp showed a clear transitional zone of malignant cells. Near the malignant areas, the glands of the polyp had less-differentiated epithelium but were still mixed with nonstriated muscle bundles. The authors took this as strong evidence of dedifferentiation of the hamartomatous structures.

Cantu et al. (1980) described a 6-year-old boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and a unilateral feminizing Sertoli cell tumor. Histopathologic study of the nontumorous testicular tissue showed changes in the seminiferous tubules, which the authors suggested reflected stimulating and atrophying effects of estrogens, acting directly or indirectly. They concluded that gonadal tumors are an additional manifestation of the Peutz-Jeghers syndrome gene in both male and female patients.

Riley and Swift (1980) described a family in which the proband and her paternal grandmother both had Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and bilateral breast carcinoma. The proband's breast cancer was premenopausal, and an ovarian sex cord tumor with annular tubules was found incidentally at the oophorectomy performed to treat it. Noting earlier reports of breast cancer in Peutz-Jeghers patients, the authors suggested that the Peutz-Jeghers gene may be associated with an increased risk of breast tumors.

Griffith and Bisset (1980) reported 3 cases of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome presenting in early childhood and considered the need for surgical intervention.

Yamada et al. (1981) carried out ultrastructural studies of the pigmented macules of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

Peloquin et al. (1981) reported a 49-year-old woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome whose first symptom was deep thrombophlebitis of the leg, which appeared to be part of a paraneoplastic syndrome. She had an adenocarcinoma arising in a hamartoma of the terminal ileum, with metastases to the ovary and lymph nodes. She later developed cervical lymph node metastases, which responded to chemotherapy for a time. ⚠︎

Burdick and Prior (1982) followed a family group of 10 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome for 27 years; 8 family members had 1 or more manifestations of the syndrome. One member died after a bypass procedure for a nonresectable jejunal carcinoma, and postmortem examination showed an adenocarcinoma of the jejunum arising in a Peutz-Jeghers polyp, with metastases in the mesenteric lymph nodes.

Solh et al. (1983) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome associated with precocious puberty.

Parker and Knight (1983) reported Peutz-Jeghers syndrome causing obstructive jaundice due to a polyp in the common bile duct.

Lehur et al. (1984) reported a woman with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who developed bilateral breast carcinoma and then malignant transformation of a duodenal hamartomatous polyp. She died of the duodenal malignancy.

Tweedie and McCann (1984) described a 39-year-old man with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome who developed a cecal adenocarcinoma with wide metastasis to regional lymph nodes. Several other colonic Peutz-Jeghers polyps showed no dysplasia or malignancy, and it could not be determined whether the carcinoma had arisen from a Peutz-Jeghers polyp. The patient also had gastric and small intestinal polyps.

Wilson et al. (1986) described a 6-year-old boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, gynecomastia, and multifocal, bilateral testicular tumors. Citing this and earlier reports, the authors suggested that males with PJS, like females, are at risk of developing gonadal tumors.

Banse-Kupin and Douglass (1986) reported a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in whom the characteristic pigmented macules developed within preexisting psoriatic plaques, at sites extremely unusual for the syndrome. They believed this to be the first such case in the English literature.

Bowlby (1986) reported a 16-year-old male with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and carcinoma of the pancreas, which to the author's knowledge was the first reported case of pancreatic carcinoma in association with the syndrome.

Giardiello et al. (1987) followed 31 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome from 1973 to 1985. Histopathologically confirmed cancer developed in 15 of the 31 (48%): 4 had gastrointestinal carcinomas, 10 had nongastrointestinal carcinomas, and 1 had multiple myeloma. Three other patients developed adenomatous polyps of the stomach and colon. The cancers were diagnosed when patients were relatively young but after PJS had been diagnosed, with an interval of 25 +/- 20 years (range, 1 to 64) between the 2 diagnoses. By relative-risk analysis, cancer developed 18 times more often than expected in the general population (P less than 0.0001). The authors suggested that patients with PJS have an increased risk of cancer at both gastrointestinal and nongastrointestinal sites.

Foley et al. (1988) reported 49 years of follow-up of the 'Harrisburg family,' one of the original Peutz-Jeghers families reported by Jeghers; with 12 affected members, it was the largest Peutz-Jeghers kindred reported. Ten patients underwent a total of 75 polypectomies, 1 patient developed short bowel syndrome, and 3 patients died in young adulthood. Two of the 12 affected members developed gastrointestinal malignancy; one had a duodenal carcinoma arising in a hamartoma with adenomatous changes, a progression in the duodenum not previously reported. The authors concluded that PJS is not a benign disease and suggested that it may be a premalignant condition.

Spigelman et al. (1989) reported that malignant tumors developed in 16 (22%) of 72 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, and all but 1 of these 16 patients died. Nine tumors were gastrointestinal and 7 were nongastrointestinal. The relative risk of death was 13 (95% CI 2.7-38.1) from gastrointestinal cancer and 9 (95% CI 4.2-17.3) from all cancers. The chance of dying of cancer by age 57 years was 48%.

Fernandez Seara et al. (1995) reported a 15-day-old girl with generalized gastrointestinal polyposis resulting from Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Her manifestations included abdominal distention, hematemesis, bloody diarrhea, and edema. She died at 1 year of age of multiple complications of her disease.

Young et al. (1995) described 2 young boys with probable Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and multicentric Sertoli cell testicular tumors. Both had prominent gynecomastia, rapid growth, advanced bone age, and markedly elevated serum estradiol. Serum anti-mullerian hormone, measured in 1 boy, was in the normal range for age. Bilateral orchiectomy was performed in both because tumor growth would likely cause sterility and continued high estradiol threatened their adult height potential. Microscopically, the tumors consisted of greatly enlarged seminiferous tubules packed with ovoid Sertoli-like cells; a prominent eosinophilic basement membrane surrounded the tubules and extended between the cells, forming hyalinized ovoid globules and microcalcifications. Electron microscopy showed layered basement membranes around adjacent cells and ovoid cells with abundant cytoplasm and limited smooth endoplasmic reticulum. Four similar cases in boys with PJS had been reported previously.

Westerman et al. (1999) reported a follow-up of more than 78 years of the original Dutch family described by Peutz in 1921, studying the natural history of PJS across 6 generations by interviews, physical examination, chart review, and histologic review. Clinical features included gastrointestinal polyposis, mucocutaneous pigmentation, nasal polyposis, and rectal extrusion of polyps. Intestinal obstruction and malignant disease reduced the survival of affected family members, and the authors suggested that PJS is not a benign disease.

In a review, Westerman and Wilson (1999) noted that the risks from polyps in PJS include surgical emergencies such as small bowel intussusception and chronic or acute bleeding from the polyps. They also noted that PJS has been associated with both gastrointestinal and nongastrointestinal tumors, although it was not clear whether these cancers arise from the polyps; the frequent occurrence of rare extraintestinal cancers, such as sex cord tumors with annular tubules of the ovary, adenoma malignum of the cervix, and Sertoli cell tumors of the testis, suggested a general susceptibility to cancer.

Bergada et al. (2000) described a prepubertal boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, gynecomastia, and bilateral neoplastic Sertoli cell proliferation.

Le Meur et al. (2004) reported a family with typical features of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, including gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa.

In a historical cohort of 51 individuals with PJS who completed self-administered questionnaires, Amos et al. (2004) found a median time to onset of gastrointestinal symptoms or polypectomy of 13 years, with wide variability. Gastric polyps were frequent, with a median age at onset of 16 years; the authors suggested that the common occurrence of gastric polyps may facilitate chemopreventive studies.

Shinmura et al. (2005) described 2 sibs with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome complicated by primary gastric cancer.

### Other Features

In the family reported by Burdick and Prior (1982), 2 members developed breast carcinoma, 1 of which arose in a fibroadenoma. Both died, 1 of metastatic breast carcinoma and the other of a second primary malignancy, an adenocarcinoma of the jejunum arising in a Peutz-Jeghers polyp. In addition, 3 family members had benign ovarian tumors, 1 had a benign breast tumor, and 1 had a benign colloid thyroid nodule.

Keller et al. (2002) reported molecular genetic evidence of an association between nasal polyposis and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

In the 6-year-old boy with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and a feminizing Sertoli cell tumor, Cantu et al. (1980) found consistently high estrogen levels in plasma and urine, with normal testosterone and dihydrotestosterone levels. Estrogen levels did not change in correlation with administration of exogenous gonadotropin, which the authors took as evidence of autonomous estrogen production. ⚠︎

Young et al. (1995) found increased transcription of the aromatase cytochrome P450 gene in testicular tumor tissue from both boys with PJS. Transcription used promoter II, which drives aromatase expression in normal ovary and testis, and transcript levels were similar to those in corpus luteum, resulting in increased estrogen synthesis. Transcripts from the placental-type aromatase promoters I.1 and I.2 were not detected at significant levels.

In a prepubertal boy with PJS, gynecomastia, and bilateral Sertoli cell proliferation, Bergada et al. (2000) found that the only abnormal hormonal finding was increased serum concentrations of inhibin B and Pro-alpha C. They noted that gynecomastia in boys with PJS and Sertoli cell tumors results from increased estrogen production due to increased aromatase activity within the testicular tumor.

### Inheritance

McAllister et al. (1967) reported 17 patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome in 4 generations, and McAllister and Richards (1977) reported 20 patients in 5 generations.

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome is inherited as an autosomal dominant disorder (Giardiello et al., 1987; Jenne et al., 1998). Mehenni et al. (1997) described the disorder as showing variable expression and incomplete penetrance. In contrast, in 5 families studied by Amos et al. (1997), all carriers of the at-risk haplotype expressed the Peutz-Jeghers phenotype and no noncarriers showed Peutz-Jeghers sequelae; the authors concluded that penetrance was complete and that there were no sporadic cases. Westerman et al. (1999) showed that a number of STK11 mutations in Dutch PJS families had arisen de novo, and Hernan et al. (2004) identified a de novo STK11 mutation in a simplex case.

### Cytogenetics

Markie et al. (1996) found a pericentric inversion of chromosome 6 on karyotype analysis of a patient with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Using FISH with YAC clones containing chromosome 6 genetic markers and a centromeric alphoid repeat probe, they localized one breakpoint within the alphoid array, in a 1-cM interval between D6S257 and D6S402, and the other in a 4-cM interval between D6S403 and D6S311. The estrogen receptor gene locus (ESR) was excluded from the second interval.

### Mapping

Hemminki et al. (1997) used comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) of Peutz-Jeghers polyps, together with loss of heterozygosity (LOH) studies, to search for a putative tumor suppressor locus. Linkage analysis in 12 families, using markers from the deletion site, showed a high-penetrance locus in distal 19p, with a multipoint lod score of 7.00 at marker D19S886.

Amos et al. (1997) confirmed linkage of PJS to the telomeric region of chromosome 19p and refined the localization. In the 5 families examined, there were no recombinants with marker D19S886, and the multipoint lod score at D19S886 was 7.52.

Mehenni et al. (1997) performed a genomewide linkage analysis in 6 PJS families (2 from Spain, 2 from India, 1 from the United States, and 1 from Portugal) comprising 93 individuals, 39 of whom were affected. Marker D19S886 gave a maximum lod score of 4.74 at theta = 0.045, and multipoint analysis gave a lod score of 7.51 for the interval between D19S886 and 19pter, confirming a PJS locus on 19p13.3.

Olschwang et al. (1998) analyzed 20 further PJS families, beyond 12 families previously found to be compatible with linkage to D19S886, and found that most were consistent with a PJS gene on 19p13.3. They concluded that 19p13.3 holds a major PJS locus.

Gruber et al. (1998) studied 6 PJS families from the Johns Hopkins Polyposis Registry, including the family originally described by Jeghers in 1949, and confirmed linkage to 19p13.3, near the STK11 gene.

### Heterogeneity

Hemminki et al. (1997) found no evidence of genetic heterogeneity for the distal 19p locus in their linkage analysis of 12 Peutz-Jeghers families, and Amos et al. (1997) likewise found no evidence of heterogeneity among the 5 families they studied.

Mehenni et al. (1997) found that markers on 19q13.4 also showed significant evidence for linkage in PJS families (maximum lod score of 3.8 at theta = 0.13 for D19S880), mostly contributed by a single family, PJS07. They suggested that a potential second PJS locus on 19q13.4 exists in a minority of families.

Olschwang et al. (1998) found that 3 of 20 PJS families were unlinked to 19p13.3, and none of the available polyps from these families showed allele loss at D19S886. Linked and unlinked families showed no obvious clinicopathologic or ethnic differences. The authors suggested that there may be a minor PJS locus or loci elsewhere in the genome.

Mehenni et al. (1998) found no LKB1 (STK11) mutation in 2 Indian PJS families, one of which they had previously found to show linkage to markers on 19q13.3-q13.4, suggesting locus heterogeneity.

Westerman et al. (1999) found no apparent abnormalities of the LKB1/STK11 gene in 7 of 19 PJS families, which they suggested could reflect locus heterogeneity.

Jiang et al. (1999) studied LOH in PJS polyps from 4 patients and found an allelic deletion of D19S886 in a patient other than the one carrying an STK11 deletion. Because germline STK11 mutations were found in only 1 of 10 families, the authors suggested that STK11 mutations account for only a proportion of PJS cases.

Boardman et al. (2000) screened LKB1 in 5 PJS kindreds with more than 2 affected members, 5 probands with only 1 other affected relative, and 23 individuals with sporadic PJS, using conformation-sensitive gel electrophoresis, direct sequencing, and long-range PCR for larger insertions or deletions. Mutations were found in only 2 probands with a family history of PJS and in 4 of 23 sporadic cases. The authors suggested significant genetic heterogeneity for PJS and involvement of other loci.

Entius et al. (2001) found that a polyp from a PJS patient without a germline STK11/LKB1 mutation showed no LOH at 19p or at 3 alternative PJS candidate loci (19q, 6p, and 6q).

Olschwang et al. (2001) found that PJS families fell into 2 groups depending on whether an STK11/LKB1 alteration had been identified, strengthening the hypothesis of a second PJS locus. They concluded that up to 30% of PJS patients have the disorder because of mutation in an unidentified gene.

Buchet-Poyau et al. (2002) noted that STK11 mutations cannot be detected in a considerable number of PJS patients. They studied family PJS07, which lacked STK11 mutations and in which multipoint linkage analysis had given significant lod scores for a second potential locus on 19q13.3-q13.4, between markers D19S180 and D19S254. They screened all exons and exon-intron junctions of 4 candidate genes in the region, PSCD2, KLK10, PRKCG, and STK13, after first determining the genomic structure of PSCD2 and PRKCG. No pathogenic mutation was found in affected individuals; the only change was a very rare PSCD2 polymorphism causing a conserved lys-to-arg substitution. They also excluded the STK11-interacting protein gene STK11IP (LIP1), which maps to 2q36, as a candidate in this family, although they noted that it could still be a good candidate in other families without STK11 mutations. ⚠︎

Le Meur et al. (2004) noted that detection of STK11 germline mutations in only 50 to 70% of PJS families had suggested genetic heterogeneity, but that their finding of a complete STK11 deletion indicated that large genomic deletions may account for some mutation-negative families. Amos et al. (2004), however, found no large deletions by real-time PCR quantitation of DNA in PJS participants without STK11 point mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Jenne et al. (1998) identified a novel human gene encoding the serine threonine kinase STK11, located 190 kb proximal to marker D19S886 in the PJS linkage region on 19p13.3. In a 3-generation PJS family, they found an STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 that segregated with the disease. By sequence analysis of STK11 exons in 4 unrelated PJS patients, they identified 3 nonsense mutations and 1 acceptor splice site mutation. All 5 germline mutations were predicted to disrupt the function of the kinase domain. The authors concluded that germline STK11 mutations, probably in conjunction with acquired defects of the second allele in somatic cells, cause PJS.

Hemminki et al. (1998) found truncating germline mutations in LKB1, a gene on chromosome 19p, in multiple individuals with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. LKB1 had already been identified but not mapped, and was strongly homologous to XEEK1, a cytoplasmic serine/threonine protein kinase from Xenopus, and more weakly similar to many other protein kinases. The authors concluded that PJS was the first cancer-susceptibility syndrome found to be caused by inactivating mutations in a protein kinase.

Mehenni et al. (1998) screened 9 PJS families of American, Spanish, Portuguese, French, Turkish, and Indian origin for LKB1 mutations and found 7 novel mutations: 2 frameshift mutations, 1 four-amino-acid deletion, 2 amino acid substitutions, and 2 splicing errors. Expressed mutant LKB1 proteins carrying the K78I, D176N, W308C, and L67P substitutions had all lost autophosphorylation (kinase) activity, and the authors concluded that loss of LKB1 kinase activity is probably responsible for the PJS phenotypes.

Gruber et al. (1998) sequenced genomic DNA and identified germline STK11 mutations in all 6 PJS families studied, including the original Jeghers family.

In the original Dutch PJS family first described by Peutz, Westerman et al. (1999) identified a novel germline LKB1 mutation that cosegregated with the disease: a T insertion at codon 66 in exon 1, causing a frameshift and a stop at codon 162 in exon 4.

Westerman et al. (1999) performed mutation analysis of LKB1/STK11 in 19 predominantly Dutch PJS families and identified 12 novel mutations in 12 families: 1 nonsense mutation, 3 frameshift deletions, 3 frameshift insertions, 2 acceptor splice site mutations, and 3 missense mutations. No mutation occurred in more than one family, and a number were shown to have arisen de novo. Four polymorphisms were also detected.

Jiang et al. (1999) screened 10 unrelated PJS families for germline STK11 changes using the protein truncation test and genomic DNA sequencing. They found a novel truncating deletion covering STK11 exons 2 through 7 in a single patient, as well as several known polymorphisms.

Boardman et al. (2000) found LKB1 mutations in 6 of 33 PJS probands: 2 of 10 probands with a family history of PJS and 4 of 23 individuals with sporadic PJS.

Miyaki et al. (2000) studied 27 gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps from 10 patients in 9 PJS families and found germline LKB1 mutations in 6 families. Somatic LKB1 mutations were found in 5 polyps, and LOH at the LKB1 locus on 19p was seen in 14 other polyps.

Olschwang et al. (2001) analyzed the STK11/LKB1 locus in 34 PJS families, searching the coding sequence for mutations and rearrangements and also using allele-specific expression tests and linkage studies. They found germline deleterious STK11/LKB1 mutations in 70% of cases.

In a family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Le Meur et al. (2004) used quantitative multiplex PCR of short fluorescent fragments (QMPSF) of the 19p13 region to identify an approximately 250-kb heterozygous germline deletion removing the entire STK11 locus. They stated that this was the first description of a complete germline deletion of STK11 and suggested that large genomic deletions should be considered in PJS families without detectable STK11 point mutations.

Amos et al. (2004) characterized STK11 mutations in 42 independent probands and detected mutations in 22 of 32 (69%) probands with PJS and in none of 10 probands referred to rule out PJS. Real-time PCR quantitation of DNA failed to detect large deletions in PJS participants without STK11 mutations. The authors concluded that direct sequencing of STK11 yields a high rate of point mutations in individuals who meet phenotypic PJS criteria.

Hernan et al. (2004) identified a de novo heterozygous germline nonsense mutation in exon 6 of the STK11/LKB1 gene (Y246X) in a simplex case of PJS. The mutation was not found in either parent.

In 2 sibs with PJS and primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified a novel germline STK11 frameshift mutation (890delG) by sequencing. No germline mutations in CDH1 or TP53 were detected.

Prompted by the zebrafish meltdown (mlt) mutant, which carries a recessive ATPase-activating smooth-muscle myosin mutation and develops intestinal abnormalities reminiscent of PJS and juvenile polyposis, Alhopuro et al. (2008) screened patients with colorectal cancer, PJS, and juvenile polyposis for mutations in the MYH11 gene (160745). They found a germline protein-elongating frameshift mutation in MYH11 in one individual with PJS. Functional evaluation of MYH11 frameshift mutations showed that they resulted in unregulated myosin molecules with constitutive motor activity, similar to the mutant myosin underlying the zebrafish mlt phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Olschwang et al. (2001) found that cancer risk differed markedly between PJS families with and without an identified STK11/LKB1 mutation. Patients without an identified mutation were at major risk for proximal biliary adenocarcinoma, a tumor that is infrequent in the general population.

Amos et al. (2004) found that individuals with missense mutations in STK11 had a significantly later time to onset of first polypectomy (p = 0.04) and of other symptoms compared with those with truncating mutations or no detectable mutation.

Schumacher et al. (2005) studied STK11 genotyping in relation to cancer risk in PJS.

### Pathogenesis

Lin et al. (1977) stated that duodenal carcinoma is the neoplasm most often associated with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Of 14 reported cases of the syndrome with intestinal carcinoma that they reviewed, the tumor was shown to arise in a polyp in only 2, and they believed that the carcinoma in their own patient developed independently of the polyps.

In contrast, Cochet et al. (1979) found dysplastic areas within the hamartomatous polyps of both of their patients, and concluded that hamartomatous polyps may in some cases be precursors of digestive tract carcinomas, supporting the malignant potential of Peutz-Jeghers polyps.

Spigelman et al. (1989) reported evidence for a hamartoma-carcinoma sequence in PJS and suggested that the gene locus involved is relevant to the development of malignancy in general.

Hemminki et al. (1997) concluded that their CGH and LOH findings in Peutz-Jeghers polyps pointed to a putative tumor suppressor locus and gave molecular evidence that hamartomas have malignant potential.

In hamartomas and adenocarcinomas from PJS patients, Gruber et al. (1998) found LOH of 19p markers near STK11 in 70% of tumors, and haplotype analysis showed that the retained allele carried the germline mutation, confirming that STK11 is a tumor suppressor gene. They concluded that STK11 acts as an early gatekeeper regulating hamartoma development and suggested that hamartomas may be precursors of adenocarcinoma. LOH of 17p and 18q was found in an adenocarcinoma but not in hamartomas, suggesting that these are late events; adenocarcinomas with 17p LOH also showed altered p53 on immunohistochemistry. None of the 18 PJS tumors showed microsatellite instability, LOH on 5q near APC, or mutations in K-ras codons 12 or 13.

Westerman and Wilson (1999) noted that, because of the general susceptibility to cancer in PJS, the STK11 gene is thought to act as a tumor suppressor.

Miyaki et al. (2000) suggested that gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps in PJS develop through inactivation of LKB1 by a germline mutation plus somatic mutation or LOH of the unaffected allele, and that additional mutations of beta-catenin and p53 convert hamartomatous polyps into adenomatous and carcinomatous lesions. Adenomatous lesions microdissected from hamartomatous polyps had both a beta-catenin mutation and 19p LOH. A carcinomatous lesion in a gastric hamartomatous polyp had a p53 mutation and LOH at the p53 locus in addition to LKB1 LOH and a beta-catenin mutation. K-ras mutations were found in a few polyps, but no APC mutations or 5q LOH were found.

Entius et al. (2001) analyzed 39 polyps and 5 carcinomas from 17 patients from 13 PJS families for LOH at 19p13.3. LOH was found in 15 of the 39 polyps and in all 5 carcinomas; 6 of 7 polyps from patients with cancer had LOH, compared with 9 of 31 polyps from the other patients (p = 0.01). They concluded that STK11/LKB1 acts as a tumor suppressor gene and may be involved in early stages of PJS tumorigenesis. No LOH at 5q (APC) was found, but APC mutations were present in 4 of 5 carcinomas. No 17p LOH or p53 mutation was seen, although p53 immunostaining was present in 1 carcinoma and focally in 3 polyps; 1 carcinoma had an activating K-ras codon 12 mutation, and another showed 18q LOH without loss of SMAD4 expression. The authors suggested that differences from the colorectal adenoma-carcinoma sequence indicate a distinct pathway of carcinogenesis in PJS. ⚠︎

Using real-time fluorescence PCR genotyping with fluorescent resonance energy transfer on DNA from microdissected gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps of a PJS patient with a de novo Y246X mutation, Hernan et al. (2004) obtained results suggesting that hamartomatous polyps form by a mechanism other than LOH at the LKB1 locus.

Similarly, Shinmura et al. (2005) detected no inactivation of the wildtype STK11 allele by somatic mutation, chromosomal deletion, or 5-prime CpG hypermethylation in the gastric cancer of a sib with PJS and a germline STK11 mutation.

Alhopuro et al. (2008) suggested that unregulated MYH11 may affect cellular energy balance or disturb cell lineage decisions in tumor progenitor cells, and that MYH11 mutations appear to contribute to human intestinal neoplasia.

### Diagnosis

Babin et al. (1978) reported a patient with oral hyperpigmentation in response to an occult malignancy, noting that melanocytes in the oral and laryngeal mucosa can respond in this way, and discussed how to distinguish this from other disease processes.

Tweedie and McCann (1984) found that serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was normal at presentation in their patient with PJS and metastatic colonic adenocarcinoma, as in one other reported case, and concluded that their report did not support the use of CEA as a screening test in such high-risk groups. ⚠︎

Gass and Glatzer (1991) described a 74-year-old man with an occult carcinoma of the colon who developed pigmentation of the mouth and penis typical of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome as the first manifestation of bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation. They suggested that the uveal disorder may result from activation of occult melanocytic nevus cells in response to a hormone-producing carcinoma or another common oncogenic stimulus.

Veraldi et al. (1991) described 4 adults (2 men and 2 women aged 39 to 57 years) with Laugier-Hunziker syndrome. All had pigmentation of the lower lip and hard palate; 2 also had pigmentation of the buccal mucosa, and another had pigmentation of the upper lip, gums, soft palate, and fingers of both hands. Histology showed melanin accumulation in basal-layer keratinocytes and increased melanophages in the papillary dermis, and electron microscopy showed many mature melanosomes in basal keratinocytes and melanophages, without alterations of the melanocytes.

Eng et al. (1991) described a 67-year-old white man with esophageal adenocarcinoma who presented with Peutz-Jeghers-like melanotic macules on the lips, face, nipples, and rectal mucosa. The macules contained atypical, pleomorphic large melanocytes in the epidermis resembling the neoplastic cells of melanoma in situ.

Westerman et al. (1999) noted that the diverse array of mutations, apparently high mutation rate, and possible second PJS locus make diagnostic or predictive genetic testing in individual patients difficult. Amos et al. (2004) concluded that STK11 mutation analysis should be restricted to individuals who meet PJS criteria or their close relatives.

### Clinical Management

After reviewing the literature on metastatic PJS, Peloquin et al. (1981) concluded that metastatic disease is uncommon but that all patients should have close long-term follow-up.

Burdick and Prior (1982) concluded that the exact risk of intestinal cancer in PJS is unknown but probably very small. They recommended that gastrointestinal surgery continue to be performed in symptomatic patients and that all patients be followed closely at regular intervals.

Tweedie and McCann (1984) noted that the cancer risk in PJS was unknown and proposed a UK national registry of PJS patients to determine it.

Foley et al. (1988) recommended endoscopic removal of gastric, duodenal, and colonic polyps even when asymptomatic, and, when surgery is needed, intraoperative endoscopy with polypectomy to help prevent short bowel syndrome. They proposed that colonoscopic screening of patients and family members may be beneficial and that surveillance for extraintestinal malignancy appeared warranted.

Kato et al. (1998) treated labial lentigines in 2 children with PJS with ruby laser therapy, with an excellent response, no recurrence, and no sequelae. They suggested that ruby laser therapy is safe and suitable for this purpose. ⚠︎

Westerman and Wilson (1999) concluded that carriers of the PJS gene are at increased risk of cancer both inside and outside the gastrointestinal tract, and that surveillance and screening protocols need to be developed for patients and their relatives as genetic identification of asymptomatic carriers becomes possible.

### History

Westerman et al. (1999) noted that Peutz first recognized the association of heredity, gastrointestinal polyposis, and mucocutaneous pigmentation in a Dutch family in 1921. Keller et al. (2001) reviewed the history of Jan Peutz and Harold Jeghers, after whom the syndrome is named. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, EX4-5 DEL AND EX6-7 INV

In a 3-generation family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Jenne et al. (1998) found an STK11 allele with a deletion of exons 4 and 5 and an inversion of exons 6 and 7 that segregated with the disease.

**.0002 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, LEU67PRO [p.L67P]

Mehenni et al. (1998) found 7 novel LKB1 mutations, including 2 amino acid substitutions, in 9 families with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Expressed mutant LKB1 protein carrying a leu67-to-pro (L67P) substitution had lost autophosphorylation (kinase) activity.

**.0003 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, LYS78ILE [p.K78I]

Mehenni et al. (1998) expressed mutant LKB1 protein carrying a lys78-to-ile (K78I) substitution and found that it had lost autophosphorylation (kinase) activity. They concluded that loss of LKB1 kinase activity is probably responsible for the PJS phenotypes.

**.0004 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, ASP176ASN [p.D176N]

Mehenni et al. (1998) expressed mutant LKB1 protein carrying an asp176-to-asn (D176N) substitution and found that it had lost autophosphorylation (kinase) activity.

**.0005 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, TRP308CYS [p.W308C]

Mehenni et al. (1998) found 7 novel LKB1 mutations, including 2 amino acid substitutions, in 9 families with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome. Expressed mutant LKB1 protein carrying a trp308-to-cys (W308C) substitution had lost autophosphorylation (kinase) activity.

**.0006 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP INS, T, CODON 66

In the original Dutch family with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome described by Peutz in 1921, Westerman et al. (1999) identified a germline insertion of a T at codon 66 in exon 1 of the LKB1 gene, causing a frameshift and a stop at codon 162 in exon 4. The mutation cosegregated with the disease, and the authors concluded that an inactivating germline LKB1 mutation is involved in the PJS phenotype in this, the oldest known affected kindred.

**.0007 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, EX2-7 DEL

Among 10 unrelated families with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome screened by the protein truncation test and genomic sequencing, Jiang et al. (1999) found a novel truncating germline deletion covering exons 2 through 7 of the STK11 gene in a single patient.

**.0008 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 250-KB DEL

In a family with typical features of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, including gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps, breast cancers, and melanin spots of the oral mucosa, Le Meur et al. (2004) used quantitative multiplex PCR of short fluorescent fragments (QMPSF) of the 19p13 region to identify an approximately 250-kb heterozygous germline deletion removing the entire STK11 locus.

**.0009 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, TYR246TER [p.Y246*]

In a simplex case of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, Hernan et al. (2004) identified a de novo heterozygous germline mutation in exon 6 of the STK11 gene, described as a substitution of cytosine for guanine at codon 246, resulting in a tyr246-to-ter (Y246X) substitution. The mutation was not found in either parent and was predicted to produce a truncated protein with presumed loss of kinase activity. LOH studies of microdissected hamartomatous polyps from the patient suggested that the polyps formed by a mechanism other than LOH.

**.0010 PEUTZ-JEGHERS SYNDROME**  
STK11, 1-BP DEL, 890G [c.890delg; p.R297fs]

In 2 sibs with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome complicated by primary gastric cancer, Shinmura et al. (2005) identified by sequencing a novel germline 1-bp deletion in the STK11 gene (c.890delG), predicted to encode a truncated protein (Arg297fsTer38). No inactivation of the wildtype STK11 allele by somatic mutation, chromosomal deletion, or 5-prime CpG hypermethylation was detected in the gastric cancer.
