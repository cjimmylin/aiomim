### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia-4 (CNSHA4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GPI gene (172400).

### Description

Glucosephosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency causes hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. In his review, Arnold (1979) noted that GPI deficiency was first reported in 1967 and that patients from all over the world have a typical nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with hemolytic crises during acute infections. Some patients also have neurological involvement, including myopathy and mental retardation (Schroter et al., 1985; Kugler et al., 1998). Paglia and Valentine (1974) also reviewed hereditary GPI deficiency.

### Clinical Features

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) described 8 children (5 living and 3 deceased) from 2 families in Kentucky and Indiana with severe hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia caused by GPI deficiency. All of the affected children were severely anemic in early life. The 3 deaths occurred in young patients who did not receive adequate blood transfusions or whose parents would not permit splenectomy. Growth and development were surprisingly normal, and no patient died of infection.

Rotteveel et al. (1977) reported a patient from a Dutch family with GPI deficiency, type Nijmegen. The nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia presented in the neonatal period. The degree of hemolysis stayed about the same over the years, but trivial infections often triggered increased hemolysis that required blood transfusions.

Galand et al. (1978) described a 6-year-old girl with GPI deficiency and mild hemolytic anemia.

Kahn et al. (1978) described patients from 2 families with severe chronic hemolytic anemia due to deficient GPI variants. The parents of the patients were consanguineous, and the patients were considered true homozygotes for the mutated GPI genes. ⚠︎

Zanella et al. (1978) reported a patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia who had a new abnormal GPI, deficiency of reduced glutathione (GSH), GSH instability, and altered carbohydrate composition of the erythrocyte membrane. ⚠︎

Isacchi et al. (1979) described a 5-year-old Italian girl, born to first-cousin parents, with severe congenital hemolytic anemia and proven red cell GPI deficiency.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) reported the first child of unrelated, healthy Caucasian parents who had GPI deficiency with severe hemolysis and hydrops fetalis and died at 3 hours of age. A second affected child was diagnosed prenatally (see DIAGNOSIS).

Arnold et al. (1980) reported a 1-year-old German boy, born to consanguineous parents, with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia and recurrent hemolytic crises caused by GPI deficiency. Because of the parental consanguinity, the patient was considered a true homozygote.

Zanella et al. (1980) described a new variant of GPI associated with severe hemolytic anemia, mental retardation, and muscular hypotonia.

Arnold et al. (1981) described a 5-year-old boy of Italian origin who had a severe hemolytic crisis. His hemolysate showed hemizygous glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency together with heterozygous GPI deficiency. The authors concluded that only the G6PD deficiency seemed to be responsible for the hemolytic crisis. They noted that this was the fourth family reported with combined deficiency of these 2 enzymes, whose genes lie on different chromosomes.

Arnold et al. (1983) described a 13-year-old German girl with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia caused by GPI deficiency, with recurrent hemolytic crises that usually followed a febrile infection. She required a blood transfusion during a crisis only once.

Schroter et al. (1985) described a propositus with generalized GPI deficiency who had a complex syndrome involving erythrocytes (congenital hemolytic anemia), granulocytes (decreased production of superoxide anion and reduced bactericidal activity in vitro), and the neuromuscular system (myopathy and mental retardation).

Ravindranath et al. (1987) reported GPI deficiency as a cause of hydrops fetalis.

Shalev et al. (1993) reported GPI deficiency in a 23-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish man who showed the typical clinical course of the disorder, with chronic hemolysis that improved after splenectomy. The authors stated that this was the first report of GPI deficiency in a patient of Jewish descent. ⚠︎

Kugler et al. (1998) studied 2 families with severe GPI deficiency. One patient (GPI Homburg) had nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with additional neurological deficits, whereas 2 affected sibs in the second family (GPI Calden) showed no neurological symptoms.

### Biochemical Features

Detter et al. (1968) studied inherited variations in human phosphohexose isomerase (GPI). Paglia et al. (1969) reported that defective hexosephosphate isomerization occurred in both human erythrocytes and leukocytes. Blume et al. (1972) characterized 2 new variants of GPI deficiency associated with hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. Nakashima et al. (1973) carried out electrophoretic and kinetic studies of GPI in 2 unrelated Japanese families with GPI deficiency.

In the 2 families from Kentucky and Indiana, Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that the abnormal GPI molecule was heat labile and was present in neutrophils and lymphocytes as well as in erythrocytes.

Paglia et al. (1975) studied a Mexican kindred with a unique erythrocyte GPI phenotype, which they called GPI Valle Hermoso. The isozyme of the proband showed decreased activity and marked thermal instability, but its kinetics, pH optimum, and starch gel electrophoretic pattern were normal.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a new variant of GPI deficiency in a patient, studied the kinetics and properties of the enzyme, and compared their data with the other variants reported up to that time.

In a separate report, Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a new case of GPI deficiency in a Dutch family, with enzyme activity reduced to 20 to 25% of normal. The mutant enzyme was markedly thermolabile, losing 90% of its activity after 1 hour at 45 degrees C. Its pH optimum and electrophoretic migration were normal, and its Km for F-6-P and its Ki values for the competitive inhibitors 2,3-DPG and 6-PG were within the normal range. Because this variant differed from all known variants, the authors proposed the name GPI-Utrecht.

On enzyme studies, Rotteveel et al. (1977) found that GPI deficiency in their Dutch patient was caused by a double heterozygous state for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles. The parents and grandparents each carried one of these deficient alleles but showed no sign of hemolysis, such as shortened red cell survival.

Galand et al. (1978) found that the deficient GPI enzyme in their patient had profoundly decreased red cell activity, a normal electrophoretic phenotype, normal isoelectric point, normal optimum pH, molecular instability, and a clearly decreased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate.

Kahn et al. (1978) found that in both of their GPI variants the main cause of the enzyme deficiency was molecular instability of the mutant GPI, with nearly normal catalytic activity. GPI 'Paris' showed slow electrophoretic migration and drastically altered substrate affinity (decreased for glucose-6-phosphate and increased for fructose-6-phosphate). GPI 'Enfants malades' was an unstable variant with slightly reduced electrophoretic mobility, an abnormal pH-activity curve, and an abnormal ratio of maximal velocity in the backward (fructose-6-phosphate to glucose-6-phosphate) versus forward direction.

Zanella et al. (1978) found that the only functional abnormality of the mutant GPI in their patient appeared to be marked instability to heat, urea, and guanidine-HCl. Incubation of normal and patient GPI with oxidizing and reducing agents suggested that the abnormality was in the SH groups of the mutant enzyme.

In a review, Arnold (1979) noted that the biochemical properties of the deficient GPI enzymes vary widely, so that many well-characterized enzymes had been designated as different variants. Alterations of physicochemical properties exceeded kinetic aberrations, and all defective variants were more or less unstable.

Isacchi et al. (1979) found that the variant in their Italian patient, GPI Roma, was slightly heat-unstable and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. The white cells of the patient showed a single predominant fast-migrating GPI form, whereas red cells showed this fast band plus a major band migrating as normal GPI and an additional slow band. The authors showed that this could be ascribed to postsynthetic events modifying the charge of the mutant enzyme.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found that the healthy heterozygous parents of their patient each had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity.

Arnold et al. (1980) found that the deficient GPI enzyme in their German patient had altered electrophoretic mobility, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate and for the competitive inhibitor 6-phosphogluconate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates. Electrophoresis after freezing and thawing of the hemolysate suggested that the subunit alteration affected conversion of the main band into secondary bands. The authors designated the variant GPI Augsburg.

Zanella et al. (1980) found that the defective GPI enzyme in their patient showed increased affinity for fructose-6-phosphate, decreased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, altered electrophoretic and isoelectrofocusing patterns, and a left shift of the precipitin curve, but was stable to heat, urea, guanidine-HCl, and storage. Optimum pH, Ki for 6-phosphogluconic acid and erythrose-4-phosphate, molecular weight, GPI-related antigen concentration, immunodiffusion pattern, and immunoinactivation were normal. The authors stated that this was the first example of a stable mutant GPI associated with severe hemolytic anemia, enzyme instability having been present in all previously reported cases.

Arnold et al. (1983) found that the deficient GPI enzyme in their 13-year-old German patient showed altered electrophoretic migration, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates. They designated it a new variant, GPI-Kaiserslautern.

Schroter et al. (1985) characterized a new GPI variant, GPI Homburg, with very low specific activity in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle tissue, nearly normal stability, normal kinetic properties, and decreased electrophoretic mobility.

Bonne-Tamir et al. (1987) studied phosphoglucose isomerase (PGI, or GPI) and 16 other biochemical genetic markers in an inbred Israeli-Arab family that had previously been described with hereditary adenylate kinase (AK) and G6PD deficiency. They found a rare PGI*3 allele in 11 of 32 family members tested. Its electrophoretic mobility was similar to that of the PGI*3 allele found in Indian populations, but unlike the Indian allele it had very low specific activity and heat stability.

Shalev et al. (1993) found that the GPI variant in their Ashkenazi Jewish patient moved slowly on electrophoresis, showed only 1 of the 2 bands normally present, and was extremely thermolabile. They proposed the name 'GPI Mount Scopus.'

### Inheritance

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) concluded that the anemia due to GPI deficiency was expressed as an autosomal recessive trait, although the enzyme variant could be detected in hematologically normal heterozygotes.

Paglia et al. (1975) concluded that the proband in their Mexican kindred was apparently homozygous for a mutant autosomal allele that governs production of the GPI Valle Hermoso isozyme.

In the Dutch family reported by Rotteveel et al. (1977), the patient was a double heterozygote for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles, and the parents and grandparents who each carried one deficient allele showed no sign of hemolysis.

Family studies by Zanella et al. (1978) suggested that their propositus was doubly heterozygous, having inherited from the mother a gene producing an inactive enzyme and from the father a gene with a structural alteration that made the encoded GPI markedly labile.

Arnold (1979) stated that GPI deficiency is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, with half of the patients being homozygotes and the others compound (double) heterozygotes.

Isacchi et al. (1979) showed that both first-cousin parents of their affected girl were heterozygous for the GPI Roma variant, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

Arnold et al. (1983) concluded from family studies that their patient was doubly heterozygous for GPI deficiency.

Bonne-Tamir et al. (1987) concluded that the distribution of the PGI*3(Israel) allele (11 of 32 members tested) in an inbred Israeli-Arab family indicated autosomal codominant inheritance of the allele.

### Mapping

Using biochemical variants in mice, Hutton (1969) showed linkage between the hemoglobin beta-chain locus and the Gpi locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Beutler et al. (1974) reported GPI deficiency due to a variant they named GPI Elyria.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) reported that genetic and electrophoretic data pointed to a double heterozygous state in their patient with GPI deficiency.

Galand et al. (1978) concluded that their 6-year-old patient was a double (compound) heterozygote for a 'silent gene' inherited from the mother and an abnormal enzyme inherited from the father. Because the paternal abnormal enzyme had undescribed characteristics, they proposed the name GPI-Mytho for this variant.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found that both parents of their affected child had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity, and qualitative studies of their red and white cells showed that each was heterozygous for a different GPI-deficiency variant allele. Placental studies showed that the propositus was a compound heterozygote.

Bonne-Tamir et al. (1987) designated the variant in their Israeli-Arab family PGI*3(Israel) and suggested that it was a different, unstable mutation from the Indian PGI*3 allele. They also suggested that, together with the AK and G6PD deficiencies in the family, it seemed to be associated with severe anemia.

Walker et al. (1993) reported DNA sequence abnormalities in patients with GPI deficiency.

In a neurologically affected patient with GPI deficiency (GPI Homburg), Kugler et al. (1998) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the GPI gene: a 59A-C transversion resulting in a his20-to-pro (H20P) substitution and a 1016T-C transition resulting in a leu339-to-pro (L339P) substitution. The authors suggested that the insertion of proline was likely to affect folding and activity of the enzyme. In 2 sibs with severe GPI deficiency without neurological symptoms (GPI Calden), Kugler et al. (1998) identified 1166A-G (H389R) and 1549C-G (L517V) mutations in the GPI gene, located at the subunit interface. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kahn et al. (1978) could not prove a clear relation between the kinetic abnormalities of their mutant GPI variants and the metabolic changes of GPI-deficient red cells or the severity of hemolysis.

Kugler et al. (1998) proposed that GPI mutations causing incorrect folding destroy both catalytic (GPI) and neurotrophic (neuroleukin) activities, leading to neurological symptoms (GPI Homburg), whereas alterations that allow correct folding retain neurotrophic properties (GPI Calden).

### Pathogenesis

Paglia et al. (1975) found that, unlike in previously known cases, leukocyte and plasma GPI activities were unimpaired in their proband with GPI Valle Hermoso. They suggested that the structural alteration mainly made the enzyme unstable without greatly reducing its catalytic effectiveness, so that cells able to continue synthesizing protein could compensate. However, density-gradient fractionation of the affected erythrocytes did not show age-related losses of GPI.

Kahn et al. (1978) emphasized a possible role of impairment of the hexose monophosphate pathway in the reduced viability of GPI-deficient red cells.

Arnold (1979) reported that GPI activity diminishes progressively in deficient red cells, leading to a rise in glucose-6-phosphate concentration and, after in vitro aging, to dramatic impairment of glycolysis and concomitant hemolysis. The metabolic block was attributed to the diminished GPI activity itself rather than to inhibition of hexokinase by high glucose-6-phosphate.

Schroter et al. (1985) suggested that the clinical syndrome in their patient resulted from generalized GPI deficiency due to decreased specific activity of the variant enzyme, which could not be compensated by increased de novo synthesis of GPI protein even in cells with active protein synthesis, such as granulocytes and muscle cells.

### Diagnosis

Blume and Beutler (1972) described a screening procedure for the detection of GPI deficiency.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) made a prenatal diagnosis of GPI deficiency by studying amniotic cells obtained by amniocentesis at 28 weeks in the second pregnancy of the mother of their deceased hydropic patient. The second child was a compound heterozygote at the GPI locus, indistinguishable from the first.

### Clinical Management

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that in patients with GPI deficiency, splenectomy generally abolished the requirement for blood transfusion, and no patient required regular transfusion after puberty.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) reported that their prenatally diagnosed second affected child was successfully treated with immediate exchange transfusion and later blood transfusions.

### Animal Model

Merkle and Pretsch (1993) described the first 2 mutations causing hereditary GPI deficiency with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia in a nonhuman mammal, the mouse. As in humans, the hemolytic syndrome (diminished erythrocyte number, lower hematocrit and hemoglobin, reticulocytosis, increased plasma bilirubin, and increased liver- and spleen-somatic indices) was manifested only in homozygous mutants; heterozygotes showed no hematologic or physiologic differences from wildtype. Allowing for the young erythrocyte population, homozygous mutant erythrocytes had increased glucose-6-phosphate, normal or decreased glycolytic metabolites distal to the block, and apparently reduced ATP and glycolytic rate. Homozygous anemic mice showed hepatosplenomegaly, adaptations to hypoxia (elevated heart-somatic index and, in one line, lung-somatic index), and marked reduction of body weight and increased lethality correlated with the degree of tissue enzyme deficiency, which the authors attributed to reduced glycolytic capability of the whole organism. Merkle and Pretsch (1993) concluded that the similarity of the physicochemical and kinetic properties of the mutant murine GPI proteins to human GPI-deficiency allozymes, together with comparable metabolic and physiologic consequences, supported these mouse mutants as excellent models for human GPI deficiency.

### History

Baughan et al. (1968) reported hereditary hemolytic anemia associated with GPI deficiency, describing it as a new enzyme defect of human erythrocytes.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI ELYRIA

**.0002 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI VALLE HERMOSO

Paglia et al. (1975) described a GPI variant, which they called GPI Valle Hermoso, in a proband from a Mexican kindred who was apparently homozygous for the mutant autosomal allele. The isozyme showed decreased activity and marked thermal instability, with normal kinetics, pH optimum, and starch gel electrophoretic pattern. Leukocyte and plasma GPI activities were unimpaired in the proband.

**.0003 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI UTRECHT

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a patient from a Dutch family with GPI deficiency, with enzyme activity reduced to 20 to 25% of normal. The mutant enzyme was markedly thermolabile, losing 90% of its activity after 1 hour at 45 degrees C, but had normal pH optimum, electrophoretic migration, Km for F-6-P, and Ki values for 2,3-DPG and 6-PG. Because the variant differed from all known variants, the authors proposed the name GPI-Utrecht.

**.0004 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI MYTHO

Galand et al. (1978) described a 6-year-old girl with GPI deficiency and mild hemolytic anemia who was a compound heterozygote for a 'silent gene' inherited from her mother and an abnormal enzyme inherited from her father. Because the paternal abnormal enzyme had undescribed characteristics, they proposed the name GPI-Mytho. The deficient enzyme in the patient had profoundly decreased red cell activity, normal electrophoretic phenotype, isoelectric point, and optimum pH, molecular instability, and a clearly decreased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate.

**.0005 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI PARIS

Kahn et al. (1978) described GPI 'Paris' in a patient with severe chronic hemolytic anemia born to consanguineous parents and considered a true homozygote. The main cause of the enzyme deficiency was molecular instability, with nearly normal catalytic activity; the variant showed slow electrophoretic migration and drastically altered substrate affinity (decreased for glucose-6-phosphate and increased for fructose-6-phosphate). ⚠︎

**.0006 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI ENFANTS MALADES

Kahn et al. (1978) characterized GPI 'Enfants malades' in a patient with severe chronic hemolytic anemia born to consanguineous parents and considered a true homozygote. It was an unstable variant with slightly reduced electrophoretic mobility, an abnormal pH-activity curve, and an abnormal ratio of maximal velocity in the backward (fructose-6-phosphate to glucose-6-phosphate) versus forward direction. ⚠︎

**.0007 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI ROMA

Isacchi et al. (1979) described GPI Roma in a 5-year-old Italian girl with severe congenital hemolytic anemia whose first-cousin parents were both heterozygous for the variant. The variant was slightly heat-unstable and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. White cells showed a single predominant fast-migrating GPI form, whereas red cells showed additional bands, which the authors ascribed to postsynthetic events modifying the charge of the mutant enzyme.

**.0008 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**

(1980) described GPI Augsburg in a 1-year-old German boy, born to consanguineous parents and considered a true homozygote, who had chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises. The enzyme had altered electrophoretic mobility, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate and 6-phosphogluconate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates.

**.0009 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**

The deficient enzyme showed altered electrophoretic migration, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates.

**.0010 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**
