### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia-4 (CNSHA4), due to glucose phosphate isomerase deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI). ⚠︎

### Description

Glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency is an autosomal recessive cause of hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. Patients have chronic hemolysis, with hemolytic crises during acute infections; severe cases may present with hydrops fetalis, and some patients have additional neurologic impairment. Splenectomy ameliorates the hemolysis. The deficient enzyme variants vary widely in their biochemical properties, and most are unstable (Arnold, 1979; Shalev et al., 1993; Kugler et al., 1998). ✗

### Clinical Features

Baughan et al. (1968) described hereditary hemolytic anemia associated with glucosephosphate isomerase deficiency as a new enzyme defect of human erythrocytes. Paglia et al. (1969) reported defective hexosephosphate isomerization in human erythrocytes and leukocytes.

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) observed 8 children (5 living, 3 deceased) with severe hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia caused by GPI deficiency in 2 families from Kentucky and Indiana. All affected children were severely anemic in early life. The 3 deaths occurred in young patients who did not receive adequate blood transfusions or whose parents refused splenectomy. Splenectomy generally abolished the requirement for transfusion, and no patient required regular transfusion after puberty. Growth and development were surprisingly normal, and no patient died of infection. The abnormal enzyme was heat labile and was present in neutrophils and lymphocytes as well as erythrocytes.

Paglia et al. (1975) studied a Mexican kindred with a variant designated GPI Valle Hermoso. The proband was apparently homozygous for a mutant autosomal allele producing an isozyme with decreased activity, marked thermal instability, normal kinetics and pH optimum, and normal starch gel electrophoretic pattern. Unlike previously known cases, leukocyte and plasma GPI activities were unimpaired.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a new case of GPI deficiency in a Dutch family, designated GPI-Utrecht. Enzyme activity was decreased to 20 to 25% of normal, and the enzyme showed pronounced thermolability, with a 90% loss of activity after 1 hour at 45 degrees C; pH optimum, electrophoretic migration, and the Km for fructose-6-phosphate were normal. In a separate report, Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a new variant in which genetic and electrophoretic data pointed to a double heterozygous state in the patient.

Rotteveel et al. (1977) reported a patient from a Dutch family with GPI deficiency type Nijmegen, which presented as nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia in the neonatal period. Hemolysis remained of the same degree over the years, but trivial infections often triggered increased hemolysis requiring blood transfusion. The patient was doubly heterozygous for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles; the presence of 1 of these alleles in the parents and grandparents was not accompanied by any sign of hemolysis.

Galand et al. (1978) observed mild hemolytic anemia due to GPI deficiency in a 6-year-old girl. The variant, designated GPI-MYTHO, had profoundly decreased red cell activity, normal electrophoretic phenotype, molecular instability, and a clearly decreased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. The proband was a double heterozygote for a 'silent gene' inherited from the mother and an abnormal enzyme from the father.

Kahn et al. (1978) described 2 new deficient GPI variants, GPI 'Paris' and GPI 'Enfants malades,' in patients with severe chronic hemolytic anemia. The parents were consanguineous, and the patients were homozygous for the mutant GPI genes. In both cases the main cause of the reduced enzyme activity was molecular instability, catalytic activity being nearly normal.

Zanella et al. (1978) reported a patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia associated with a new abnormal GPI, reduced glutathione (GSH) deficiency and instability, and altered carbohydrate membrane composition. The only functional abnormality of the mutant enzyme appeared to be marked instability to heat, urea, and guanidine-HCl. Family studies suggested that the proband was doubly heterozygous for a maternal gene producing an inactive enzyme and a paternal gene causing marked lability of the enzyme.

Isacchi et al. (1979) found red cell GPI deficiency due to a new variant, 'GPI Roma,' in a 5-year-old Italian girl with severe congenital hemolytic anemia. Her parents were first cousins and were heterozygous for the variant. The variant was slightly unstable to heat and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) reported GPI deficiency with severe hemolysis and hydrops fetalis in the first child of unrelated, healthy Caucasian parents; the child died at 3 hours. Both parents had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity, and each was heterozygous for a different GPI-deficient variant allele; studies of the placenta showed that the proband was a compound heterozygote. A second child, diagnosed prenatally and indistinguishable from the first at the GPI locus, was successfully treated by immediate exchange transfusion and subsequent blood transfusions.

Arnold et al. (1980) found GPI deficiency to be the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 1-year-old German boy. Because his parents were consanguineous, the patient was considered a true homozygote. The deficient enzyme, designated GPI Augsburg, showed altered electrophoretic behavior, pronounced thermolability, and increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate and for the competitive inhibitor 6-phosphogluconate.

Zanella et al. (1980) described a new GPI variant associated with hemolytic anemia, mental retardation, and muscular hypotonia. The defective enzyme had increased affinity for fructose-6-phosphate and decreased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, but was stable under all conditions tested (heat, urea, guanidine-HCl, and storage). This was the first example of a stable mutant GPI associated with severe hemolytic anemia; enzyme instability had been present in all previously reported cases.

Arnold et al. (1981) observed a severe hemolytic crisis in a 5-year-old boy of Italian origin who had hemizygous deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) and heterozygous deficiency of GPI. Only the G6PD deficiency appeared to be responsible for the hemolytic crisis.

Arnold et al. (1983) found GPI deficiency to be the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 13-year-old German girl. The crises usually occurred after a febrile infection, and the patient required blood transfusion only once during a crisis. Family studies indicated that she was doubly heterozygous for the deficiency. The variant, designated GPI-Kaiserslautern, showed altered electrophoretic migration, pronounced thermolability, and increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate.

Schroter et al. (1985) described a patient with a complex syndrome involving erythrocytes (congenital hemolytic anemia), granulocytes (decreased production of superoxide anion and reduced bactericidal activity in vitro), and the neuromuscular system (myopathy, mental retardation). The GPI variant, designated GPI Homburg, had very low specific activity in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle tissue, nearly normal stability, normal kinetic properties, and decreased electrophoretic mobility. The authors suggested that the syndrome resulted from generalized GPI deficiency that could not be compensated by increased de novo synthesis even in cells with active protein synthesis.

Ravindranath et al. (1987) reported GPI deficiency as a cause of hydrops fetalis.

Bonne-Tamir et al. (1987) studied an inbred Israeli-Arab family previously described for hereditary deficiency of adenylate kinase and G6PD. A rare PGI*3 allele was observed in 11 of 32 members tested. Unlike the electrophoretically similar PGI*3 allele found in Indian populations, this allele, designated PGI*3(Israel), had very low specific activity and heat stability, and together with the adenylate kinase and G6PD deficiencies appeared to be associated with severe anemia.

Shalev et al. (1993) diagnosed GPI deficiency in a 23-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish man with the typical clinical course of the disorder. The variant, designated GPI Mount Scopus, showed slow electrophoretic mobility, presence of only 1 of the 2 bands normally present, and extreme thermolability. The authors believed this to be the first report of GPI deficiency in a patient of Jewish descent.

### Biochemical Features

Blume et al. (1972) characterized 2 new variants of GPI deficiency with hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia, Nakashima et al. (1973) performed electrophoretic and kinetic studies of GPI in 2 Japanese families with GPI deficiency, and Beutler et al. (1974) described a variant designated GPI Elyria.

In a review of known variants, Arnold (1979) noted that the biochemical properties of the deficient enzymes vary widely, that modification of physicochemical properties surpasses kinetic aberrations, and that all defective variants are more or less unstable. Progressive loss of activity leads to a rise in glucose-6-phosphate concentration and, in red cells aged in vitro, to a dramatic impairment of glycolysis with concomitant hemolysis. The metabolic block was attributed to the diminished GPI activity itself rather than to inhibition of hexokinase by high glucose-6-phosphate.

Contrary to the general rule of enzyme instability, Zanella et al. (1980) and Schroter et al. (1985) described GPI variants that were stable or nearly stable. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) stated that the anemia of GPI deficiency is expressed as an autosomal recessive trait, although the enzyme variant can be detected in hematologically normal heterozygotes. Arnold (1979) noted that about half of patients are homozygotes and the others double heterozygotes. Consanguinity of the parents of homozygous patients was reported by Kahn et al. (1978), Isacchi et al. (1979), and Arnold et al. (1980).

### Molecular Genetics

Walker et al. (1993) reported DNA sequence abnormalities in human GPI deficiency.

Kugler et al. (1998) analyzed DNA from 2 patients with severe GPI deficiency, 1 with additional neurologic deficits, and their families. The neurologically affected patient (GPI Homburg) was compound heterozygous for a 59A-C transversion resulting in a his20-to-pro (H20P) substitution and a 1016T-C transition resulting in a leu339-to-pro (L339P) substitution in the GPI gene; the authors suggested that the introduction of proline was likely to affect folding and activity of the enzyme. In a second family (GPI Calden), 2 affected sibs without neurologic symptoms carried a 1166A-G transition (H389R) and a 1549C-G transversion (L517V), located at the subunit interface.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kahn et al. (1978) found no clear relation between the kinetic abnormalities of the mutant GPI variants and either the metabolic changes in GPI-deficient red cells or the severity of hemolysis.

Noting that GPI is identical to neuroleukin, which has neurotrophic and lymphokine properties, Kugler et al. (1998) proposed that mutations leading to incorrect folding destroy both the catalytic (GPI) and neurotrophic (neuroleukin) activities, leading to neurologic symptoms as in GPI Homburg, whereas alterations at the active site that allow correct folding retain the neurotrophic properties of the molecule, as in GPI Calden.

### Pathogenesis

Kahn et al. (1978) emphasized the possible role of impairment of the hexose monophosphate pathway in the reduced viability of GPI-deficient red cells. In studies of the variant associated with GSH abnormality, Zanella et al. (1978) found that incubation experiments with oxidizing and reducing agents seemed to indicate that the abnormality resided in the SH groups of the mutant enzyme.

Isacchi et al. (1979) found a single predominant fast-migrating GPI form in white blood cells of a patient with GPI Roma, whereas red cells additionally showed a major band migrating as normal GPI and a slow band; they attributed this to postsynthetic events modifying the charge of the mutant enzyme.

### Diagnosis

Blume and Beutler (1972) described a screening procedure for the detection of GPI deficiency.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) made a prenatal diagnosis of GPI deficiency by examination of amniotic cells obtained by amniocentesis at 28 weeks in the second pregnancy of a couple whose first child had died of the disorder.

### Clinical Management

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that splenectomy generally abolished the requirement for blood transfusion. Whitelaw et al. (1979) successfully treated a prenatally diagnosed affected infant by immediate exchange transfusion and subsequent blood transfusions.

### Animal Model

Merkle and Pretsch (1993) described 2 mouse mutations causing hereditary GPI deficiency associated with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. The hemolytic syndrome, characterized by decreased erythrocyte number, hematocrit, and hemoglobin, increased reticulocytes and plasma bilirubin, and increased liver- and spleen-somatic indices, was manifested only in homozygous mutants; heterozygotes showed no hematologic differences from wildtype. Homozygous mutant erythrocytes had increased glucose-6-phosphate concentration and apparently reduced ATP concentration and glycolytic rate. Homozygous anemic mice showed hepatosplenomegaly, adaptations to hypoxia, marked reduction of body weight, and increased lethality, the latter 2 correlated with the degree of enzyme deficiency in tissues. The authors concluded that these mutants are excellent animal models for the human disease.
