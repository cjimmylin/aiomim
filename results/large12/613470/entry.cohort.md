## ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4; CNSHA4

*Alternative titles; symbols*

HEMOLYTIC ANEMIA, NONSPHEROCYTIC, DUE TO GLUCOSE PHOSPHATE ISOMERASE DEFICIENCY

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia-4 (CNSHA4), due to glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GPI gene (172400).

### Description

Glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency is an unusual autosomal recessive cause of hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. It most often presents as chronic hemolysis that improves after splenectomy (Shalev et al., 1993). Hemolytic crises occur during acute infections (Arnold, 1979). Paglia and Valentine (1974) reviewed hereditary GPI deficiency.

### Nomenclature

Mutant GPI enzymes have been named as separate variants, often after the place where the patient was found. Arnold (1979) noted that many well-characterized deficient enzymes had been named in this way. Van Biervliet et al. (1975) proposed the name GPI-Utrecht for a variant that differed from all known GPI variants. Galand et al. (1978) proposed the name GPI-MYTHO for a new abnormal enzyme variant with characteristics not described before. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Baughan et al. (1968) described hereditary hemolytic anemia associated with deficiency of glucosephosphate isomerase (GPI) as a new enzyme defect of human erythrocytes.

Nakashima et al. (1973) carried out electrophoretic and kinetic studies of GPI in 2 unrelated Japanese families with GPI deficiency.

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) observed 8 children with severe hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia caused by GPI deficiency in 2 families from Kentucky and Indiana. Five of the children were living and 3 had died. All affected children were severely anemic in early life, and some needed transfusions before splenectomy. The 3 deaths occurred in young patients who did not receive adequate blood transfusions or whose parents refused to permit splenectomy. Growth and development were surprisingly normal, and no patient died of infection. ⚠︎

Paglia et al. (1975) studied a Mexican kindred. The proband had reduced erythrocyte GPI activity with marked thermal instability of the enzyme. Unlike in previously known cases, leukocyte and plasma GPI activities were normal. The variant was designated GPI Valle Hermoso.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a new variant of GPI deficiency in a patient whose genetic and electrophoretic data pointed to a double heterozygous state. In a separate report, Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described GPI deficiency in a patient from a Dutch family whose enzyme activity was 20 to 25% of normal (GPI-Utrecht).

Rotteveel et al. (1977) described the clinical course of a patient from a Dutch family with GPI deficiency type Nijmegen. The disorder presented in the neonatal period as nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. Trivial infections often triggered increased hemolysis that required blood transfusions.

Galand et al. (1978) observed mild hemolytic anemia in a 6-year-old girl whose red cell GPI activity was profoundly reduced.

Kahn et al. (1978) described 2 patients with severe chronic hemolytic anemia due to red cell GPI deficiency. The parents of both patients were consanguineous. The patients carried 2 new deficient variants, GPI Paris and GPI Enfants malades.

Zanella et al. (1978) reported a patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia associated with a new abnormal GPI. The patient also had deficiency and instability of reduced glutathione (GSH) and altered carbohydrate composition of the erythrocyte membrane.

In a review of GPI deficiency, Arnold (1979) stated that patients had typical nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia, with hemolytic crises during acute infections.

Isacchi et al. (1979) reported a 5-year-old Italian girl with severe congenital hemolytic anemia due to red cell GPI deficiency. They found a new variant, which they called GPI Roma. Her parents were first cousins.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) reported GPI deficiency with severe hemolysis and hydrops fetalis in the first child of unrelated, healthy Caucasian parents. The child died at 3 hours of age. In the second pregnancy, an affected child was diagnosed prenatally. This child was a compound heterozygote at the GPI locus, indistinguishable from the first child, and had transfusion-dependent anemia. ⚠︎

Arnold et al. (1980) found GPI deficiency to be the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 1-year-old German boy. His parents were consanguineous.

Zanella et al. (1980) described a new GPI variant in a patient with severe hemolytic anemia, mental retardation, and muscular hypotonia.

Arnold et al. (1983) found GPI deficiency to be the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 13-year-old German girl. The crises usually followed a febrile infection. Only once did she need a blood transfusion during a crisis.

Schröter et al. (1985) described a patient with a complex syndrome involving erythrocytes, granulocytes, and the neuromuscular system. He had congenital hemolytic anemia. His granulocytes produced less superoxide anion and had reduced bactericidal activity in vitro. He also had myopathy and mental retardation. GPI activity was very low in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle. The variant was designated GPI Homburg.

Ravindranath et al. (1987) reported GPI deficiency as a cause of hydrops fetalis.

Shalev et al. (1993) diagnosed GPI deficiency in a 23-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish man who had the typical clinical course of the disorder, with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. The variant was designated GPI Mount Scopus.

Kugler et al. (1998) studied 2 families with severe GPI deficiency and nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. One patient (GPI Homburg) also had neurologic deficits. In the second family (GPI Calden), the 2 affected sibs showed no neurologic symptoms.

### Other Features

Arnold et al. (1981) reported a 5-year-old boy of Italian origin with a severe hemolytic crisis. He had hemizygous glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency together with heterozygous GPI deficiency. The authors noted that this was the fourth reported family with combined deficiency of these 2 enzymes. They concluded that only the G6PD deficiency seemed to be responsible for the hemolytic crisis.

Bonné-Tamir et al. (1987) studied an inbred Israeli-Arab family previously described with hereditary deficiency of adenylate kinase (AK) and G6PD. They found a rare phosphoglucose isomerase allele, PGI*3(Israel), with reduced specific activity. The authors suggested that this allele, together with the AK and G6PD deficiencies, seemed to be associated with severe anemia.

### Biochemical Features

Paglia et al. (1969) reported defective hexosephosphate isomerization in both erythrocytes and leukocytes in humans.

Blume et al. (1972) characterized 2 new variants of GPI deficiency associated with hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia.

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that the abnormal isomerase molecule was heat labile and was present in neutrophils and lymphocytes as well as in erythrocytes.

Paglia et al. (1975) found that the GPI Valle Hermoso variant had decreased activity and marked thermal instability. Its kinetics, pH optimum, and starch gel electrophoretic pattern were normal.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) found that the mutant enzyme in their Dutch patient was markedly thermolabile, losing 90% of its activity after 1 hour at 45 degrees C. Its pH optimum and electrophoretic migration were normal, as were its Km for fructose-6-phosphate and its Ki values for the competitive inhibitors 2,3-DPG and 6-PG.

Galand et al. (1978) found that the GPI-MYTHO enzyme had profoundly decreased red cell activity and was molecularly unstable. Its electrophoretic phenotype, isoelectric point, and pH optimum were normal, but its Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate was clearly decreased.

Kahn et al. (1978) found that the main cause of reduced activity in both GPI Paris and GPI Enfants malades was molecular instability of the mutant enzyme, with nearly normal catalytic activity. GPI Paris migrated slowly on electrophoresis and had markedly altered substrate affinity, decreased for glucose-6-phosphate and increased for fructose-6-phosphate. GPI Enfants malades had slightly reduced electrophoretic mobility, an abnormal pH-activity curve, and an abnormal ratio of maximal velocity in the backward direction. ⚠︎

Zanella et al. (1978) found that the only functional abnormality of their mutant GPI appeared to be marked instability to heat, urea, and guanidine-HCl. Incubation of normal and mutant GPI with oxidizing and reducing agents seemed to indicate that the abnormality lay in the SH groups of the mutant enzyme.

Arnold (1979) found that the biochemical properties of deficient GPI enzymes varied widely. Changes in physicochemical properties outweighed kinetic abnormalities, and all defective variants were more or less unstable.

Isacchi et al. (1979) found that GPI Roma was slightly unstable to heat and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. The patient's white blood cells contained a single main fast-migrating GPI form. Her red cells showed this form plus a major band that migrated like normal GPI and an extra slow band. The authors attributed the extra bands to postsynthetic events that changed the charge of the mutant enzyme.

Arnold et al. (1980) found that GPI Augsburg had altered electrophoretic mobility and pronounced thermolability. It had increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate and for the competitive inhibitor 6-PG, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates. Electrophoresis after freezing and thawing of the hemolysate suggested that the genetic change in the subunit affected the process that converts the main band into the secondary bands.

Zanella et al. (1980) found that their variant GPI had increased affinity for fructose-6-phosphate, decreased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, and altered electrophoretic and isoelectrofocusing patterns. The precipitin curve was shifted to the left. Unlike previously described variants, the enzyme was stable to heat, urea, guanidine-HCl, and storage. Its pH optimum, Ki values, molecular weight, and concentration of GPI-related antigen were normal.

Arnold et al. (1983) found that GPI-Kaiserslautern had altered electrophoretic migration, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates, indicating a new variant.

Schröter et al. (1985) found that GPI Homburg had very low specific activity in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle tissue. It had nearly normal stability, normal kinetic properties, and decreased electrophoretic mobility.

Bonné-Tamir et al. (1987) found that the PGI*3(Israel) allele moved on electrophoresis like the PGI*3 allele found in Indian populations. Unlike the Indian allele, it had very low specific activity and heat stability. The authors concluded that it seemed to be a different, unstable mutation.

Shalev et al. (1993) found that GPI Mount Scopus had slow electrophoretic mobility and was extremely thermolabile. Only one of the 2 bands normally present was seen.

### Inheritance

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) concluded that the anemia was expressed as an autosomal recessive trait, although the enzyme variant could be detected in hematologically normal heterozygotes.

Rotteveel et al. (1977) found that the proband's parents and grandparents who carried 1 GPI-deficient allele showed no sign of hemolysis, such as shortened red cell survival.

Arnold (1979) noted that GPI deficiency is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. About half of reported patients were homozygous and the rest were double (compound) heterozygotes.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found that both healthy parents of their affected children had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity. Each parent was heterozygous for a different GPI-deficient variant allele. This was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance, with the affected children being compound heterozygotes.

In the inbred Israeli-Arab family studied by Bonné-Tamir et al. (1987), the rare PGI*3 allele was present in 11 of 32 family members tested, indicating autosomal codominant inheritance of the electrophoretic variant.

### Mapping

Using biochemical variants in mice, Hutton (1969) showed that the hemoglobin beta-chain locus and the glucosephosphate isomerase locus are linked.

### Molecular Genetics

Before molecular analysis was available, the genetic basis of GPI deficiency was inferred from enzyme and family studies. Beutler et al. (1974) reported a variant of GPI deficiency that they called GPI Elyria.

Paglia et al. (1975) concluded that the proband of their Mexican kindred was apparently homozygous for a mutant autosomal allele, GPI Valle Hermoso.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) found that genetic and electrophoretic data pointed to a double heterozygous state in their patient.

From enzyme studies, Rotteveel et al. (1977) concluded that the GPI deficiency in their patient was caused by a double heterozygous state for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles. Each deficient allele was present in the parents and grandparents. ⚠︎

Galand et al. (1978) found that their patient was a double heterozygote. She had inherited a 'silent gene' from her mother and an abnormal enzyme, GPI-MYTHO, from her father.

Kahn et al. (1978) concluded that, because the parents were consanguineous, their 2 patients were true homozygotes for the mutated GPI genes (GPI Paris and GPI Enfants malades).

Family studies by Zanella et al. (1978) suggested that their patient was doubly heterozygous. The maternal gene produced an inactive enzyme, and the paternal gene caused a structural alteration that made the encoded enzyme markedly labile.

Isacchi et al. (1979) showed that both parents of their patient with GPI Roma, who were first cousins, were heterozygous for the variant.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) showed by tests on the placenta that their first affected patient was a compound heterozygote. Each parent was heterozygous for a different GPI-deficient variant. The second affected child was also a compound heterozygote.

Arnold et al. (1980) concluded that their patient with GPI Augsburg was a true homozygote because his parents were consanguineous. Arnold et al. (1983) found from family studies that their patient with GPI-Kaiserslautern was doubly heterozygous for the deficiency.

Walker et al. (1993) reported DNA sequence abnormalities in human GPI deficiency.

Kugler et al. (1998) analyzed DNA from 2 patients with severe GPI deficiency and their families. The patient with neurologic deficits (GPI Homburg) was compound heterozygous for 2 missense mutations in the GPI gene: H20P and L339P. In the GPI Calden family, the 2 affected sibs, who had no neurologic symptoms, carried the missense mutations H389R and L517V. Both of these changes lie at the subunit interface.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Zanella et al. (1980) reported the first example of a stable mutant GPI associated with severe hemolytic anemia. They noted that enzyme instability had been present in all previously reported cases.

Kugler et al. (1998) suggested that GPI mutations causing incorrect folding destroy both the catalytic (GPI) and the neurotrophic (neuroleukin) activities of the protein, leading to neurologic dysfunction. Examples are the proline insertions H20P and L339P in GPI Homburg. They proposed that changes at the active site that still allow correct folding, as in GPI Calden, preserve the neurotrophic properties.

### Pathogenesis

Paglia et al. (1975) suggested that the structural change in GPI Valle Hermoso mainly made the enzyme unstable without greatly reducing its catalytic effectiveness. This would allow cells that can keep making protein to compensate. However, density-gradient fractionation of affected erythrocytes did not show age-related loss of GPI.

Kahn et al. (1978) could not establish a clear relationship between the kinetic abnormalities of their mutant GPI variants and either the metabolic changes in GPI-deficient red cells or the severity of hemolysis. They suggested that impairment of the hexose monophosphate pathway might help reduce the viability of GPI-deficient red cells.

Arnold (1979) reported that GPI activity in deficient red cells fell progressively, raising the glucose-6-phosphate concentration. After the cells aged in vitro, glycolysis was severely impaired and hemolysis followed. The metabolic block was attributed to the reduced GPI activity itself, not to inhibition of hexokinase by the high glucose-6-phosphate.

Schröter et al. (1985) suggested that the syndrome in their patient resulted from generalized GPI deficiency caused by the reduced specific activity of the variant enzyme. They proposed that increased de novo synthesis of GPI protein could not make up for this deficiency, even in cells with active protein synthesis such as granulocytes and muscle cells.

### Diagnosis

Blume and Beutler (1972) described a screening procedure for detecting GPI deficiency.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) made a prenatal diagnosis of GPI deficiency in the mother's second pregnancy by examining amniotic cells obtained by amniocentesis at 28 weeks.

### Clinical Management

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that splenectomy generally abolished the need for blood transfusion in GPI deficiency, and no patient required regular transfusion after puberty.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) successfully treated a prenatally diagnosed compound heterozygous GPI-deficient infant with immediate exchange transfusion followed by blood transfusions.

### Population Genetics

Detter et al. (1968) reported inherited variations in human phosphohexose isomerase.

Shalev et al. (1993) stated that, to their knowledge, theirs was the first report of GPI deficiency in a patient of Jewish descent.

### Animal Model

Merkle and Pretsch (1993) described the first 2 mutations causing hereditary GPI deficiency with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia in a nonhuman mammal, the mouse. As in humans, only homozygous mutants developed the hemolytic syndrome. Its features were reduced erythrocyte count, lower hematocrit and hemoglobin, reticulocytosis, raised plasma bilirubin, and hepatosplenomegaly. Heterozygous mutant mice showed no hematologic or other physiologic differences from wildtype mice.

Erythrocytes from the homozygous mutant mice had increased glucose-6-phosphate, normal or decreased levels of downstream glycolytic metabolites, and apparently reduced ATP and glycolytic rate. The anemic mice showed adaptations to hypoxia, including an elevated heart-somatic index. They also had markedly reduced body weight and increased lethality, both correlated with the degree of enzyme deficiency in tissues. Merkle and Pretsch (1993) attributed these findings to reduced glycolytic capability of the whole organism rather than to secondary effects of the erythrocyte defect.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, VALLE HERMOSO

Paglia et al. (1975) studied a Mexican kindred with GPI deficiency. The proband was apparently homozygous for a mutant autosomal allele, which they named GPI Valle Hermoso. The encoded isozyme had decreased activity and marked thermal instability, but normal kinetics, pH optimum, and starch gel electrophoretic pattern. Unlike in previously known cases, leukocyte and plasma GPI activities were not impaired.

**.0002 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, NIJMEGEN

Rotteveel et al. (1977) reported a patient from a Dutch family with GPI deficiency type Nijmegen. The patient had nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia that presented in the neonatal period, and infections triggered increased hemolysis that required transfusion. Enzyme studies showed a double heterozygous state for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles. Heterozygous carriers among the parents and grandparents showed no sign of hemolysis.

**.0003 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, SILENT ALLELE

Galand et al. (1978) reported a 6-year-old girl with mild hemolytic anemia and profoundly reduced red cell GPI activity. She was a double heterozygote: she had inherited a 'silent gene' from her mother and the abnormal enzyme GPI-MYTHO from her father.

**.0004 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, MYTHO

Galand et al. (1978) found the abnormal GPI-MYTHO enzyme, inherited from the father, in a 6-year-old girl with mild hemolytic anemia. She had inherited a silent allele from her mother. The enzyme had profoundly decreased red cell activity and was molecularly unstable. Its electrophoretic phenotype, isoelectric point, and pH optimum were normal, but its Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate was clearly decreased.

**.0005 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, PARIS

Kahn et al. (1978) found GPI Paris in a patient with severe chronic hemolytic anemia. Because the parents were consanguineous, the patient was a true homozygote. GPI Paris migrated slowly on electrophoresis and had drastically altered substrate affinity, decreased for glucose-6-phosphate and increased for fructose-6-phosphate. The main cause of reduced activity was molecular instability.

**.0006 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, ENFANTS MALADES

Kahn et al. (1978) found GPI Enfants malades in a patient with severe chronic hemolytic anemia. Because the parents were consanguineous, the patient was a true homozygote. The variant had slightly reduced electrophoretic mobility, an abnormal pH-activity curve, and an abnormal ratio of maximal velocity in the backward direction. Its reduced activity was mainly due to molecular instability. ⚠︎

**.0007 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, INACTIVE ENZYME ALLELE

Zanella et al. (1978) reported a patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia, GSH deficiency and instability, and altered erythrocyte membrane carbohydrate composition. Family studies suggested that the patient was doubly heterozygous. The maternal gene produced an inactive enzyme, and the paternal gene produced a markedly labile enzyme. ⚠︎

**.0008 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, LABILE ENZYME ALLELE

In the patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia reported by Zanella et al. (1978), family studies suggested that the paternal gene caused a structural alteration that made the encoded enzyme markedly labile. It was present together with a maternal gene producing an inactive enzyme. The mutant GPI was markedly unstable to heat, urea, and guanidine-HCl, and the abnormality appeared to lie in the SH groups of the enzyme.

**.0009 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, ROMA

Isacchi et al. (1979) found GPI Roma in a 5-year-old Italian girl with severe congenital hemolytic anemia and red cell GPI deficiency. Her parents were first cousins, and both were proven heterozygous for the variant. The enzyme was slightly unstable to heat and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. In red cells it showed extra electrophoretic bands, which the authors attributed to postsynthetic modification of its charge.

**.0010 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, DEFICIENT ALLELE, PATERNAL (WHITELAW FAMILY)

Whitelaw et al. (1979) studied the children of unrelated, healthy Caucasian parents. Each parent had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity and was heterozygous for a different GPI-deficient variant. The first child had severe hemolysis and hydrops fetalis and died at 3 hours; tests on the placenta showed compound heterozygosity. The second child, diagnosed prenatally, was an indistinguishable compound heterozygote and was treated successfully with exchange transfusion and blood transfusions. This entry is the paternal allele.

**.0011 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, DEFICIENT ALLELE, MATERNAL (WHITELAW FAMILY)

This is the maternal GPI-deficient allele in the 2 compound heterozygous children reported by Whitelaw et al. (1979). The first child had hydrops fetalis and died at 3 hours. The second, diagnosed prenatally, had transfusion-dependent anemia. The mother had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity, and her variant differed from the father's. ⚠︎

**.0012 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, AUGSBURG

Arnold et al. (1980) found GPI Augsburg in a 1-year-old German boy with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia and recurrent hemolytic crises. Because his parents were consanguineous, he was a true homozygote. The enzyme had altered electrophoretic mobility and pronounced thermolability. It had increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate and 6-PG, and slightly changed pH optima.

**.0013 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, KAISERSLAUTERN

Arnold et al. (1983) found GPI-Kaiserslautern in a 13-year-old German girl with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia and recurrent hemolytic crises after febrile infections. Family studies indicated that she was doubly heterozygous for the deficiency. The enzyme had altered electrophoretic migration, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates.

**.0014 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, HOMBURG

Schröter et al. (1985) described GPI Homburg in a patient with congenital hemolytic anemia, impaired granulocyte function, myopathy, and mental retardation. The variant had very low specific activity in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle. It had nearly normal stability, normal kinetic properties, and decreased electrophoretic mobility.

Kugler et al. (1998) showed that the neurologically affected patient with GPI Homburg was compound heterozygous for 2 missense mutations, H20P and L339P.

**.0015 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, MOUNT SCOPUS

Shalev et al. (1993) found GPI Mount Scopus in a 23-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish man with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. The variant had slow electrophoretic mobility and was extremely thermolabile. Only one of the 2 bands normally present was seen. ⚠︎

**.0016 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, HIS20PRO [c.59A>C; p.H20P]

Kugler et al. (1998) studied a patient (GPI Homburg) with severe GPI deficiency, nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia, and neurologic deficits. The patient was compound heterozygous for a 59A-C transversion in the GPI gene, resulting in a his20-to-pro (H20P) substitution, and an L339P mutation. The authors suggested that proline insertions causing incorrect folding destroy both the catalytic and neurotrophic activities of the protein.

**.0017 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, LEU339PRO [c.1016T>C; p.L339P]

In the patient with GPI Homburg, who had severe GPI deficiency and neurologic deficits, Kugler et al. (1998) identified a 1016T-C transition in the GPI gene, resulting in a leu339-to-pro (L339P) substitution. It was present in compound heterozygosity with H20P. The authors proposed that this misfolding mutation abolished both the catalytic and the neurotrophic activities of the protein.

**.0018 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, HIS389ARG [c.1166A>G; p.H389R]

Kugler et al. (1998) found a 1166A-G transition in the GPI gene, resulting in a his389-to-arg (H389R) substitution, together with an L517V mutation in the GPI Calden family. The 2 affected sibs had severe GPI deficiency without neurologic symptoms. Both mutations lie at the subunit interface. The authors proposed that changes that still allow correct folding keep the neurotrophic properties of the protein. ⚠︎

**.0019 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, LEU517VAL [c.1549C>G; p.L517V]

In the GPI Calden family, in which 2 sibs had severe GPI deficiency without neurologic symptoms, Kugler et al. (1998) identified a 1549C-G transversion in the GPI gene, resulting in a leu517-to-val (L517V) substitution, together with H389R. Both mutations are located at the subunit interface. ⚠︎
