### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia-4 (CNSHA4), due to glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GPI gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency is an autosomal recessive cause of congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. Patients have chronic hemolysis, with hemolytic crises during acute infections, and splenectomy can improve the anemia (Arnold, 1979; Shalev et al., 1993). Some patients also have neurologic involvement, such as mental retardation, myopathy, or muscular hypotonia (Zanella et al., 1980; Schroter et al., 1985; Kugler et al., 1998).

### Clinical Features

Baughan et al. (1968) reported hereditary hemolytic anemia associated with GPI deficiency as a new enzyme defect of human erythrocytes. Paglia et al. (1969) reported defective hexosephosphate isomerization in human erythrocytes and leukocytes.

Blume et al. (1972) characterized 2 new variants of GPI deficiency in patients with hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. Nakashima et al. (1973) carried out electrophoretic and kinetic studies of GPI in 2 Japanese families with GPI deficiency. Beutler et al. (1974) described a GPI-deficient variant named GPI Elyria.

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) observed 8 children (5 living and 3 deceased) from 2 families in Kentucky and Indiana with severe hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia caused by GPI deficiency. All affected children were severely anemic early in life. The 3 deaths occurred in young patients who did not receive adequate blood transfusions or whose parents refused to permit splenectomy. Splenectomy generally removed the need for blood transfusion, and no patient needed regular transfusion after puberty. Growth and development were surprisingly normal, and no patient died of infection.

Paglia et al. (1975) studied a Mexican kindred in which the proband appeared to be homozygous for a mutant allele encoding a GPI variant called GPI Valle Hermoso. This variant had decreased activity and marked thermal instability. Unlike in previously known cases, GPI activity in leukocytes and plasma was not impaired.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a new GPI-deficient variant. Genetic and electrophoretic data indicated that their patient was a double heterozygote. In another report, Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described GPI deficiency in a Dutch family, with enzyme activity reduced to 20 to 25% of normal. They named this variant GPI Utrecht.

Rotteveel et al. (1977) reported a Dutch patient with GPI deficiency type Nijmegen. The patient had nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia that presented in the neonatal period. The degree of hemolysis stayed the same over the years, but trivial infections often triggered increased hemolysis that required blood transfusion. The patient was doubly heterozygous for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles. Parents and grandparents carrying one of these alleles showed no sign of hemolysis, including no shortened red cell survival.

Galand et al. (1978) observed mild hemolytic anemia due to GPI deficiency in a 6-year-old girl. She was a double heterozygote for a 'silent gene' inherited from her mother and an abnormal enzyme inherited from her father, designated GPI Mytho.

Kahn et al. (1978) described 2 new GPI-deficient variants, GPI Paris and GPI Enfants Malades, in patients with severe chronic hemolytic anemia. In both cases the parents were consanguineous, so the patients were true homozygotes.

Zanella et al. (1978) reported a patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia associated with a new abnormal GPI, GSH (reduced glutathione) deficiency and instability, and altered carbohydrate composition of the membrane. Family studies suggested that the propositus was doubly heterozygous for a maternal gene producing an inactive enzyme and a paternal gene causing marked lability of the encoded enzyme.

Isacchi et al. (1979) found red cell GPI deficiency due to a new variant, GPI Roma, in a 5-year-old Italian girl with severe congenital hemolytic anemia. Her parents were first cousins and were shown to be heterozygous for the variant.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found GPI deficiency with severe hemolysis and hydrops fetalis in the first child of unrelated, healthy Caucasian parents. The child died at 3 hours of age. Each parent had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity and was heterozygous for a different deficiency-associated GPI variant allele, and tests on the placenta showed that the propositus was a compound heterozygote. In the second pregnancy, GPI deficiency was diagnosed prenatally. That child, a compound heterozygote indistinguishable from the first, was successfully treated with immediate exchange transfusion followed by blood transfusions.

Arnold et al. (1980) found GPI deficiency as the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 1-year-old German boy. His parents were consanguineous, and he was a true homozygote for a variant designated GPI Augsburg.

Zanella et al. (1980) described a GPI variant associated with hemolytic anemia, mental retardation, and muscular hypotonia. It was the first stable mutant GPI found in association with severe hemolytic anemia.

Arnold et al. (1983) found GPI deficiency as the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 13-year-old German girl. The crises usually followed a febrile infection, and she needed blood transfusion only once during a crisis. Family studies indicated that she was doubly heterozygous, and the variant was designated GPI Kaiserslautern.

Schroter et al. (1985) described a patient with a GPI variant, GPI Homburg, that had very low specific activity in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle tissue. The patient had a complex syndrome affecting erythrocytes (congenital hemolytic anemia), granulocytes (decreased superoxide anion production and reduced bactericidal activity in vitro), and the neuromuscular system (myopathy and mental retardation). The authors suggested that the syndrome resulted from generalized GPI deficiency that could not be compensated by increased de novo synthesis of GPI protein, even in cells with active protein synthesis such as granulocytes and muscle cells.

Ravindranath et al. (1987) reported GPI deficiency as a cause of hydrops fetalis.

Shalev et al. (1993) diagnosed GPI deficiency in a 23-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish man whose clinical course was typical of the disorder. The variant, which they named GPI Mount Scopus, was the first reported in a patient of Jewish descent.

### Other Features

Arnold et al. (1981) observed a severe hemolytic crisis in a 5-year-old boy of Italian origin. He had hemizygous deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) and heterozygous deficiency of GPI. The authors noted that this was the fourth family reported with combined deficiency of these 2 enzymes, and they concluded that only the G6PD deficiency appeared to be responsible for the hemolytic crisis.

Bonne-Tamir et al. (1987) studied an inbred Israeli Arab family previously described as having hereditary deficiencies of adenylate kinase and G6PD. They found a rare allele, PGI*3(Israel), in 11 of 32 family members tested, with autosomal codominant inheritance. The allele's electrophoretic mobility was similar to that of the PGI*3 allele in Indian populations, but unlike the Indian allele it had very low specific activity and heat stability. Together with the adenylate kinase and G6PD deficiencies, it appeared to be associated with severe anemia.

### Biochemical Features

Detter et al. (1968) studied inherited variation in human phosphohexose isomerase.

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that the abnormal isomerase was heat labile and was present in neutrophils and lymphocytes as well as erythrocytes.

GPI Valle Hermoso had normal kinetics, pH optimum, and starch gel electrophoretic pattern. Paglia et al. (1975) suggested that the structural change mainly made the enzyme unstable without greatly reducing catalytic efficiency, so cells still able to synthesize protein could compensate. However, they did not find age-related loss of GPI when affected erythrocytes were fractionated by density gradient.

GPI Utrecht showed pronounced thermolability, losing 90% of its activity after 1 hour at 45 degrees C. Its pH optimum, electrophoretic migration, Km for fructose-6-phosphate, and Ki for the competitive inhibitors 2,3-DPG and 6-PG were normal (Van Biervliet et al., 1975).

GPI Mytho had markedly decreased red cell activity, a normal electrophoretic phenotype, a normal isoelectric point and pH optimum, molecular instability, and a clearly decreased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate (Galand et al., 1978).

Kahn et al. (1978) found that the main cause of reduced activity in GPI Paris and GPI Enfants Malades was molecular instability, with nearly normal catalytic activity. GPI Paris migrated slowly on electrophoresis and had markedly altered substrate affinity: affinity for glucose-6-phosphate was decreased and affinity for fructose-6-phosphate was increased. GPI Enfants Malades had slightly reduced electrophoretic mobility, an abnormal pH-activity curve, and an abnormal ratio of maximal velocity in the reverse direction to that in the forward direction. The authors could not establish a clear relationship between the kinetic abnormalities and either the metabolic changes in the red cells or the severity of hemolysis. They emphasized that impairment of the hexose monophosphate pathway might reduce the viability of GPI-deficient red cells.

In the variant described by Zanella et al. (1978), the only functional abnormality appeared to be marked instability to heat, urea, and guanidine-HCl. Incubation experiments with oxidizing and reducing agents suggested that the abnormality lay in the SH groups of the mutant enzyme.

GPI Roma was slightly unstable to heat and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. The patient's white blood cells contained a single predominant fast-migrating GPI form. Her red cells showed this fast band together with a major band migrating like normal GPI and an additional slow band. Isacchi et al. (1979) attributed this pattern to postsynthetic events that modified the charge of the mutant enzyme.

Arnold (1979) reviewed the known variants. The biochemical properties of the deficient enzymes varied widely, and changes in physicochemical properties exceeded kinetic abnormalities. All defective variants were more or less unstable. Their activity declined progressively, raising glucose-6-phosphate concentration, and red cells aged in vitro showed marked impairment of glycolysis with accompanying hemolysis. Arnold (1979) concluded that the metabolic block was caused by the reduced GPI activity itself, not by inhibition of hexokinase by high glucose-6-phosphate.

GPI Augsburg showed altered electrophoretic behavior, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate and for the competitive inhibitor 6-PG, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates (Arnold et al., 1980).

The variant described by Zanella et al. (1980) had increased affinity for fructose-6-phosphate, decreased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, altered electrophoretic and isoelectric focusing patterns, and a leftward shift of the precipitin curve. It was stable under all conditions tested (heat, urea, guanidine-HCl, and storage). Instability had been present in all previously reported cases.

GPI Kaiserslautern showed altered electrophoretic migration, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates (Arnold et al., 1983).

GPI Homburg had nearly normal stability, normal kinetic properties, and decreased electrophoretic mobility (Schroter et al., 1985).

GPI Mount Scopus had slow electrophoretic mobility, only 1 of the 2 bands normally present, and extreme thermolability (Shalev et al., 1993).

### Inheritance

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that the anemia was inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, although the enzyme variant could be detected in hematologically normal heterozygotes. In a review, Arnold (1979) stated that the disease is autosomal recessive, with half of patients homozygous and the others double heterozygotes. Shalev et al. (1993) also described GPI deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder.

### Mapping

Hutton (1969) reported linkage of the hemoglobin beta-chain and glucosephosphate isomerase loci in mice.

### Molecular Genetics

Walker et al. (1993) reported DNA sequence abnormalities in human GPI deficiency.

Kugler et al. (1998) analyzed DNA from 2 families with severe GPI deficiency. The neurologically affected patient with GPI Homburg (Schroter et al., 1985) was compound heterozygous for 2 mutations in the GPI gene, 59A-C (H20P) and 1016T-C (L339P). Because both introduce proline, the authors considered them likely to affect folding and enzyme activity. In the second family, 2 affected sibs without neurologic symptoms (GPI Calden) carried the mutations 1166A-G (H389R) and 1549C-G (L517V), both located at the subunit interface.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kugler et al. (1998) noted that GPI is identical to neuroleukin, which has neurotrophic and lymphokine properties. They proposed that mutations causing misfolding destroy both the catalytic (GPI) and neurotrophic (neuroleukin) activities, producing hemolytic anemia with neurologic dysfunction, as in GPI Homburg. In contrast, mutations that allow correct folding would preserve neurotrophic function, as in GPI Calden, in which the sibs had no neurologic symptoms.

### Diagnosis

Blume and Beutler (1972) described a screening procedure for detecting GPI deficiency.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) made a prenatal diagnosis of GPI deficiency by examining amniotic cells obtained by amniocentesis at 28 weeks of pregnancy.

### Clinical Management

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that splenectomy generally abolished the need for blood transfusion. Shalev et al. (1993) noted that chronic hemolysis in GPI deficiency is improved by splenectomy. Whitelaw et al. (1979) successfully treated a prenatally diagnosed affected newborn with immediate exchange transfusion followed by blood transfusions.

### Animal Model

Merkle and Pretsch (1993) described the first 2 mutations causing hereditary GPI deficiency with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia in a nonhuman mammal, the mouse. As in humans, the hemolytic syndrome appeared only in homozygous mutants. It included reduced erythrocyte number, hematocrit, and hemoglobin; increased reticulocytes and plasma bilirubin; and increased liver- and spleen-somatic indices. Heterozygotes did not differ hematologically from wildtype. Allowing for the young erythrocyte population, homozygous mutant erythrocytes had increased glucose-6-phosphate and normal or decreased levels of glycolytic metabolites downstream of the block, and ATP concentration and glycolytic rate also appeared reduced. Homozygous anemic mice had hepatosplenomegaly and adaptations to hypoxia, as well as markedly reduced body weight and increased lethality, both correlating with the degree of enzyme deficiency in tissues. The authors attributed these findings to reduced glycolytic capacity of the whole organism. ⚠︎

### History

Paglia and Valentine (1974) reviewed hereditary GPI deficiency. Arnold (1979) noted that the first report of GPI deficiency was in 1967 and that many patients from around the world had since been described.
