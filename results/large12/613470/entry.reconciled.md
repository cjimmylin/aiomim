## ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4; CNSHA4

*Alternative titles; symbols*

HEMOLYTIC ANEMIA, NONSPHEROCYTIC, DUE TO GLUCOSE PHOSPHATE ISOMERASE DEFICIENCY

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia-4 (CNSHA4), due to glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GPI gene (172400) on chromosome 19q13.11. ⚠︎

### Description

Glucose phosphate isomerase (GPI) deficiency is an autosomal recessive cause of hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. It most often presents as chronic hemolysis that improves after splenectomy (Shalev et al., 1993). In a review, Arnold (1979) noted that patients had a typical nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia, with hemolytic crises during acute infections. Baughan et al. (1968) described hereditary hemolytic anemia with GPI deficiency as a new enzyme defect of human erythrocytes, and Paglia and Valentine (1974) reviewed hereditary GPI deficiency.

### Nomenclature

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) found that the variant in their Dutch patient differed from all known GPI variants and proposed the name GPI-Utrecht. Galand et al. (1978) proposed the name GPI-MYTHO for a new abnormal enzyme variant with characteristics not described before. Arnold (1979) noted that many well-characterized deficient enzymes had been named as separate variants.

### Clinical Features

Nakashima et al. (1973) performed electrophoretic and kinetic studies of GPI in 2 unrelated Japanese families with GPI deficiency. ⚠︎

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) observed 8 children (5 living, 3 deceased) with severe hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia caused by GPI deficiency in 2 families from Kentucky and Indiana. All affected children were severely anemic in early life, and some depended on transfusion before splenectomy. Three deaths occurred in young patients who either did not receive adequate blood transfusions or whose parents refused to allow splenectomy. Growth and development were surprisingly normal, and no patient died of infection.

Rotteveel et al. (1977) reported the clinical course of a Dutch patient with GPI deficiency type Nijmegen. The disorder was a nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia that presented in the neonatal period. Trivial infections often triggered increased hemolysis that required blood transfusions.

Galand et al. (1978) observed GPI deficiency with mild hemolytic anemia in a 6-year-old girl. Her red cell GPI activity was profoundly decreased.

Kahn et al. (1978) described 2 patients with severe chronic hemolytic anemia and red cell GPI deficiency. Each patient's parents were consanguineous.

Zanella et al. (1978) reported a patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia associated with an abnormal, unstable GPI. The patient also had reduced erythrocyte glutathione (GSH) with GSH instability, and an altered carbohydrate composition of the red cell membrane. ⚠︎

Isacchi et al. (1979) reported a 5-year-old Italian girl with severe congenital hemolytic anemia and red cell GPI deficiency. Her parents were first cousins.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found GPI deficiency with severe hemolysis and hydrops fetalis in the first child of unrelated, healthy Caucasian parents. The child died at 3 hours. The second child of the couple was diagnosed prenatally and had transfusion-dependent anemia. ⚠︎

Arnold et al. (1980) found GPI deficiency to be the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 1-year-old German boy whose parents were consanguineous.

Zanella et al. (1980) described a patient with severe hemolytic anemia, mental retardation, and muscular hypotonia associated with a new, stable GPI variant.

Arnold et al. (1983) found GPI deficiency to be the cause of chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia with recurrent hemolytic crises in a 13-year-old German girl. Her crises usually followed a febrile infection, and only once did she need a blood transfusion during a crisis.

Schroter et al. (1985) reported a patient with a complex syndrome due to generalized GPI deficiency (GPI Homburg). It involved erythrocytes (congenital hemolytic anemia), granulocytes (decreased superoxide anion production and reduced bactericidal activity in vitro), and the neuromuscular system (myopathy and mental retardation). GPI activity was very low in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle.

Ravindranath et al. (1987) reported GPI deficiency as a cause of hydrops fetalis.

Shalev et al. (1993) diagnosed GPI deficiency in a 23-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish man with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia and the typical clinical course of the disorder. ⚠︎

Kugler et al. (1998) studied 2 families with severe GPI deficiency and nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. One patient (GPI Homburg) also had neurologic deficits. In the second family (GPI Calden), the 2 affected sibs had no neurologic symptoms. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Arnold et al. (1981) reported a 5-year-old boy of Italian origin with a severe hemolytic crisis. He had hemizygous glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and heterozygous GPI deficiency. They noted that this was the fourth reported family with combined deficiency of these 2 enzymes, and concluded that only the G6PD deficiency seemed to be responsible for the hemolytic crisis.

### Biochemical Features

Paglia et al. (1969) reported defective hexosephosphate isomerization in human erythrocytes and leukocytes. Blume et al. (1972) characterized 2 new variants of GPI deficiency associated with hereditary nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia.

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that the abnormal isomerase molecule was heat labile. It was present in neutrophils and lymphocytes as well as in erythrocytes.

Paglia et al. (1975) found that the erythrocyte GPI variant in a Mexican kindred (GPI Valle Hermoso) had decreased activity and marked thermal instability. Its kinetics, pH optimum, and starch gel electrophoretic pattern were normal. Unlike in previously known cases, leukocyte and plasma GPI activities were not impaired.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) found GPI activity decreased to 20 to 25% of normal in a Dutch patient. The mutant enzyme was markedly thermolabile, losing 90% of its activity after 1 hour at 45 degrees C. Its pH optimum, electrophoretic migration, Km for fructose-6-phosphate, and Ki values for the competitive inhibitors 2,3-DPG and 6-PG were normal.

Galand et al. (1978) found that the deficient enzyme in their patient (GPI-MYTHO) had a normal electrophoretic phenotype, normal isoelectric point, and normal pH optimum. It showed molecular instability and a clearly decreased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate.

Kahn et al. (1978) found that in 2 deficient variants, GPI Paris and GPI Enfants malades, the main cause of reduced activity was molecular instability of the mutant enzyme, with nearly normal catalytic activity. GPI Paris showed slow electrophoretic migration and drastically altered substrate affinity: decreased for glucose-6-phosphate and increased for fructose-6-phosphate. GPI Enfants malades showed slightly reduced electrophoretic mobility, an abnormal activity-versus-pH curve, and an abnormal ratio of maximal velocity in the backward direction. ⚠︎

Zanella et al. (1978) found that the only functional abnormality of their mutant GPI appeared to be marked instability to heat, urea, and guanidine-HCl. Incubation of normal and mutant GPI with oxidizing and reducing agents suggested that the abnormality resided in the SH groups of the mutant enzyme.

In a review, Arnold (1979) found that the biochemical properties of deficient GPI enzymes varied widely. Changes in physicochemical properties outweighed kinetic abnormalities, and all defective variants were more or less unstable.

Isacchi et al. (1979) found that the GPI Roma variant was slightly unstable to heat and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. The patient's white blood cells contained a single main fast-migrating GPI form. Her red cells had, in addition, a major band migrating like normal GPI and an extra slow band. The authors attributed these bands to postsynthetic events that changed the charge of the mutant enzyme.

Arnold et al. (1980) found that the deficient enzyme in their patient, which they designated GPI Augsburg, showed altered electrophoretic mobility and pronounced thermolability. It had increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) and for the competitive inhibitor 6-PG, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates. Electrophoresis after freezing and thawing of the hemolysate suggested that the genetic change in the subunit affected the process that converts the main band into the secondary bands.

Zanella et al. (1980) found that their variant GPI had increased affinity for fructose-6-phosphate, decreased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, altered electrophoretic and isoelectric focusing patterns, and a left shift of the precipitin curve. However, it was stable to heat, urea, guanidine-HCl, and storage. Its pH optimum, Ki values, molecular weight, and GPI-related antigen concentration were normal.

Arnold et al. (1983) found that the deficient enzyme in their patient, GPI Kaiserslautern, showed altered electrophoretic migration, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for glucose-6-phosphate, and slightly changed pH optima for both substrates, indicating a new variant.

Schroter et al. (1985) found that GPI Homburg had very low specific activity in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle tissue. It had nearly normal stability, normal kinetic properties, and decreased electrophoretic mobility.

Bonne-Tamir et al. (1987) studied phosphoglucose isomerase (PGI) in an inbred Israeli-Arab family previously described with hereditary deficiencies of adenylate kinase (AK) and G6PD. They observed a rare PGI*3 allele in 11 of 32 family members tested, indicating autosomal codominant inheritance. The allele, designated PGI*3(Israel), moved on electrophoresis like the PGI*3 allele found in Indian populations. Unlike that allele, it had very low specific activity and heat stability, and the authors concluded that it seemed to be a different, unstable mutation. Together with the AK and G6PD deficiencies, it seemed to be associated with severe anemia in the family.

Shalev et al. (1993) found that the GPI variant in their patient, designated GPI Mount Scopus, showed slow electrophoretic mobility and extreme thermolability. Only 1 of the 2 bands normally present was seen.

### Inheritance

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) concluded that the anemia was expressed as an autosomal recessive trait, although the enzyme variant could be detected in hematologically normal heterozygotes.

Rotteveel et al. (1977) found that the proband's parents and grandparents, each carrying 1 GPI-deficient allele, showed no sign of hemolysis, such as shortened red cell survival.

Kahn et al. (1978) and Arnold et al. (1980) reported patients with consanguineous parents who were considered true homozygotes. Isacchi et al. (1979) showed that both parents of their patient, who were first cousins, were heterozygous for the variant.

Arnold (1979) noted that GPI deficiency is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. About half of reported patients were homozygous and the rest were double heterozygotes.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found that the healthy parents of affected children each had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity. Each parent was heterozygous for a different GPI-deficient variant allele, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance with compound heterozygosity in the children.

### Molecular Genetics

Beutler et al. (1974) reported a variant of GPI deficiency that they called GPI Elyria.

Paglia et al. (1975) concluded that the proband of a Mexican kindred was apparently homozygous for a mutant autosomal allele, GPI Valle Hermoso. The allele governed production of an isozyme with decreased activity and marked thermal instability.

Van Biervliet et al. (1975) described a new variant of GPI deficiency. Genetic and electrophoretic data pointed to a double heterozygous state in the patient.

Rotteveel et al. (1977) found by enzyme studies that the GPI deficiency in their Dutch patient (type Nijmegen) was caused by double heterozygosity for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles.

Galand et al. (1978) found that their 6-year-old patient was doubly heterozygous for a 'silent gene' inherited from her mother and an abnormal enzyme (GPI-MYTHO) inherited from her father.

Kahn et al. (1978) concluded that their 2 patients, whose parents were consanguineous, were true homozygotes for the mutant GPI genes encoding GPI Paris and GPI Enfants malades, respectively.

From family studies, Zanella et al. (1978) suggested that their patient was doubly heterozygous for 2 GPI alleles. The maternal allele produced an inactive enzyme, and the paternal allele caused a structural alteration that made the encoded enzyme markedly labile.

Isacchi et al. (1979) attributed red cell GPI deficiency in a 5-year-old Italian girl to a new variant, GPI Roma. Both of her parents, who were first cousins, were proven heterozygous for this variant.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) showed by tests on the placenta that their first, hydropic patient was a compound heterozygote at the GPI locus. Each parent was heterozygous for a different GPI-deficient allele. The couple's second child was also a compound heterozygote and was indistinguishable from the first.

Arnold et al. (1980) concluded that their patient, whose parents were consanguineous, was a true homozygote for the GPI Augsburg variant.

Family examination by Arnold et al. (1983) indicated that their 13-year-old German patient was doubly heterozygous for the deficiency (GPI Kaiserslautern).

Walker et al. (1993) reported DNA sequence abnormalities in human GPI deficiency.

Kugler et al. (1998) analyzed DNA from 2 patients with severe GPI deficiency and from their families. The patient with neurologic deficits (GPI Homburg; Schroter et al., 1985) was compound heterozygous for 2 missense mutations in the GPI gene, his20-to-pro (H20P) and leu339-to-pro (L339P). In the second family (GPI Calden), the mutations identified in the 2 affected sibs, who had no neurologic symptoms, were his389-to-arg (H389R) and leu517-to-val (L517V). Both lie at the subunit interface.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Zanella et al. (1980) reported the first example of a stable mutant GPI associated with severe hemolytic anemia. They noted that enzyme instability had been present in all previously reported cases.

Kugler et al. (1998) suggested that GPI mutations causing incorrect folding destroy both the catalytic (GPI) and the neurotrophic (neuroleukin) activities, leading to neurologic dysfunction. Examples were the proline insertions H20P and L339P in GPI Homburg. They proposed that changes at the active site that still allow correct folding, as in GPI Calden, preserve the neurotrophic properties.

### Pathogenesis

Paglia et al. (1975) suggested that the structural change in GPI Valle Hermoso mainly made the enzyme unstable without greatly reducing its catalytic effectiveness. Cells that can keep making protein could then compensate. However, density-gradient fractionation of affected erythrocytes did not show an age-related loss of GPI.

Kahn et al. (1978) could not show a clear relationship between the kinetic abnormalities of the mutant GPI variants and either the metabolic changes in GPI-deficient red cells or the severity of hemolysis. They suggested that impairment of the hexose monophosphate pathway might help reduce the viability of GPI-deficient red cells.

Arnold (1979) reported that GPI activity in deficient red cells fell progressively, raising the G6P concentration. After the cells aged in vitro, glycolysis was severely impaired and hemolysis followed. The metabolic block was attributed to the reduced GPI activity itself rather than to inhibition of hexokinase by the high G6P.

Schroter et al. (1985) suggested that the syndrome in their patient resulted from generalized GPI deficiency caused by the decreased specific activity of the variant enzyme. Increased de novo synthesis of GPI protein could not make up for this, even in cells with active protein synthesis such as granulocytes and muscle cells.

### Diagnosis

Blume and Beutler (1972) described a screening procedure for detecting GPI deficiency.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) made a prenatal diagnosis of GPI deficiency by examining amniotic cells obtained by amniocentesis at 28 weeks in the mother's second pregnancy.

### Clinical Management

Hutton and Chilcote (1974) found that splenectomy generally abolished the need for blood transfusion in GPI deficiency. No patient required regular transfusion after puberty.

Whitelaw et al. (1979) successfully treated a prenatally diagnosed compound heterozygous GPI-deficient infant by immediate exchange transfusion followed by blood transfusions.

### Population Genetics

Detter et al. (1968) reported inherited variations in human phosphohexose isomerase.

Shalev et al. (1993) stated that, to their knowledge, theirs was the first report of GPI deficiency in a patient of Jewish descent.

### Animal Model

Merkle and Pretsch (1993) described the first 2 mutations causing hereditary GPI deficiency with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia in a nonhuman mammal, the mouse. As in humans, the hemolytic syndrome appeared only in homozygous mutants. It included a reduced erythrocyte count, lower hematocrit and hemoglobin, reticulocytosis, raised plasma bilirubin, and hepatosplenomegaly. Heterozygous mutants did not differ hematologically or physiologically from wildtype mice.

Merkle and Pretsch (1993) found that erythrocytes from homozygous mutant mice had increased glucose-6-phosphate, normal or decreased levels of downstream glycolytic metabolites, and apparently reduced ATP and glycolytic rate. The anemic homozygotes showed adaptations to hypoxia, including an elevated heart-somatic index. They also had markedly reduced body weight and increased lethality, both correlated with the degree of enzyme deficiency in tissues. The authors attributed these findings to a reduced glycolytic capability of the whole organism rather than to secondary effects of the erythrocyte defect.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI VALLE HERMOSO

Paglia et al. (1975) studied a Mexican kindred with an unusual erythrocyte GPI phenotype, GPI Valle Hermoso. The proband was apparently homozygous for a mutant autosomal allele. The encoded isozyme had decreased activity and marked thermal instability, with normal kinetics, normal pH optimum, and a normal starch gel electrophoretic pattern. Unlike in previously known cases, leukocyte and plasma GPI activities were not impaired.

**.0002 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI NIJMEGEN

Rotteveel et al. (1977) reported a Dutch patient with GPI deficiency type Nijmegen and nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia that presented in the neonatal period. Trivial infections often increased hemolysis to the point of requiring transfusion. Enzyme studies showed double heterozygosity for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles. Carriers of 1 deficient allele among the parents and grandparents showed no sign of hemolysis.

**.0003 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, SILENT ALLELE

Galand et al. (1978) reported a 6-year-old girl with mild hemolytic anemia and profoundly decreased red cell GPI activity. She was doubly heterozygous for a 'silent gene' inherited from her mother and an abnormal enzyme, GPI-MYTHO, inherited from her father.

**.0004 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI MYTHO

Galand et al. (1978) found that a 6-year-old girl with mild hemolytic anemia was doubly heterozygous for an abnormal GPI enzyme inherited from her father, which they named GPI-MYTHO, and a 'silent gene' inherited from her mother. The variant enzyme had a normal electrophoretic phenotype, isoelectric point, and pH optimum. It showed molecular instability and a clearly decreased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate.

**.0005 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI PARIS

Kahn et al. (1978) described a patient with severe chronic hemolytic anemia whose parents were consanguineous. The patient was considered a true homozygote for a deficient GPI variant, GPI Paris. The enzyme had slow electrophoretic migration and drastically altered substrate affinity (decreased for glucose-6-phosphate, increased for fructose-6-phosphate). Molecular instability, with nearly normal catalytic activity, was the main cause of the reduced activity.

**.0006 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI ENFANTS MALADES

Kahn et al. (1978) described a patient with severe chronic hemolytic anemia whose parents were consanguineous. The patient was considered a true homozygote for a deficient GPI variant, GPI Enfants malades. The enzyme showed slightly reduced electrophoretic mobility, an abnormal activity-versus-pH curve, and an abnormal ratio of maximal velocity in the backward direction. Molecular instability was the main cause of the reduced activity. ⚠︎

**.0007 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, INACTIVE-ENZYME ALLELE

Zanella et al. (1978) reported a patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia, an unstable GPI, GSH deficiency and instability, and altered red cell membrane carbohydrate composition. Family studies suggested double heterozygosity for a maternal allele producing an inactive enzyme and a paternal allele causing marked lability of the encoded enzyme. ⚠︎

**.0008 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, LABILE-ENZYME ALLELE

In the patient with congenital nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia reported by Zanella et al. (1978), family studies suggested that the paternal allele caused a structural alteration that made the encoded enzyme markedly labile, together with a maternal allele producing an inactive enzyme. The mutant GPI was markedly unstable to heat, urea, and guanidine-HCl. Experiments with oxidizing and reducing agents suggested that the abnormality resided in the SH groups of the enzyme.

**.0009 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI ROMA

Isacchi et al. (1979) found red cell GPI deficiency due to a new variant, GPI Roma, in a 5-year-old Italian girl with severe congenital hemolytic anemia. Her parents, who were first cousins, were proven heterozygous for the variant. The enzyme was slightly unstable to heat and had a slightly increased Michaelis constant for fructose-6-phosphate. Its electrophoretic pattern differed between white and red cells, which the authors attributed to postsynthetic modification of the mutant enzyme.

**.0010 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, DEFICIENCY ALLELE, PATERNAL (WHITELAW FAMILY)

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found that 2 children of unrelated, healthy Caucasian parents were compound heterozygotes for 2 different GPI-deficient alleles. Each parent carried one allele and had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity. The first child had severe hemolysis and hydrops fetalis and died at 3 hours. The second child was diagnosed prenatally and treated by immediate exchange transfusion and later blood transfusions.

**.0011 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, DEFICIENCY ALLELE, MATERNAL (WHITELAW FAMILY)

Whitelaw et al. (1979) found the maternal GPI-deficient allele in compound heterozygous state, together with a different paternal allele, in 2 sibs. The first had hydrops fetalis and died at 3 hours, and the second had transfusion-dependent anemia. The healthy heterozygous mother had 50% of normal red cell GPI activity. ⚠︎

**.0012 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**

Because his parents were consanguineous, he was considered a true homozygote for the deficient variant GPI Augsburg. The enzyme showed altered electrophoretic mobility, pronounced thermolability, increased affinity for G6P and 6-PG, and slightly changed pH optima.

**.0014 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI HOMBURG

Schroter et al. (1985) described a patient with congenital hemolytic anemia, impaired granulocyte function, myopathy, and mental retardation due to a GPI variant, GPI Homburg. The enzyme had very low specific activity in erythrocytes, granulocytes, and muscle, with nearly normal stability, normal kinetics, and decreased electrophoretic mobility. Kugler et al. (1998) found that this patient was compound heterozygous for the H20P and L339P mutations.

**.0015 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, GPI MOUNT SCOPUS

Shalev et al. (1993) diagnosed GPI deficiency in a 23-year-old Ashkenazi Jewish man with chronic nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia. His enzyme variant, designated GPI Mount Scopus, showed slow electrophoretic mobility, only 1 of the 2 bands normally present, and extreme thermolability. ⚠︎

**.0016 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, HIS20PRO [c.59A>C; p.H20P]

Kugler et al. (1998) found a 59A-C transversion in the GPI gene, resulting in a his20-to-pro (H20P) substitution, in compound heterozygosity with L339P in the patient with GPI Homburg. This patient had severe GPI deficiency, nonspherocytic hemolytic anemia, and neurologic deficits. The authors suggested that proline insertions causing incorrect folding destroy both the catalytic and the neurotrophic activities of the protein. ⚠︎

**.0017 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, LEU339PRO [c.1016T>C; p.L339P]

In the patient with GPI Homburg, who had severe GPI deficiency with neurologic deficits, Kugler et al. (1998) found a 1016T-C transition in the GPI gene, resulting in a leu339-to-pro (L339P) substitution, in compound heterozygosity with H20P.

**.0018 ANEMIA, CONGENITAL, NONSPHEROCYTIC HEMOLYTIC, 4**  
GPI, HIS389ARG [c.1166A>G; p.H389R]

In the GPI Calden family, Kugler et al. (1998) identified a 1166A-G transition in the GPI gene, resulting in a his389-to-arg (H389R) substitution, together with L517V. The 2 affected sibs had severe GPI deficiency without neurologic symptoms. Both mutations are located at the subunit interface.
