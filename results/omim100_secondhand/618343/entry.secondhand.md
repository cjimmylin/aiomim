### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (PMGEDSV) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL3A1 gene (120180) on chromosome 2q32.

### Description

Polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (PMGEDSV) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic COL3A1 mutations. It is defined by structural brain anomalies: bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone type, cerebellar cysts, and abnormalities of the white matter. Some patients have developmental delay and seizures. Some patients also have connective tissue features of vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, including thin, translucent skin, arterial dissection or rupture, and tissue fragility, and these can cause early death (Jorgensen et al., 2015; Vandervore et al., 2017; Horn et al., 2017).

### Clinical Features

Plancke et al. (2009) reported a young girl from a consanguineous family with healthy parents who had recessive Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and met the vascular EDS criteria for laboratory testing. Jorgensen et al. (2015) gave further details of this patient. She developed acute abdominal pain, and during surgery to repair a necrotic and perforated jejunum, extreme fragility of the intestinal arteries and tissue was found. She died of multisystem failure 6 days after the intervention, at the age of 11 years. Along with features characteristic of EDS type IV, she had delayed motor development, pulmonary valve stenosis, and absence epilepsy. Brain MRI showed diffuse cortical dysplasia, mainly frontal, and ventricular dilatation.

Jorgensen et al. (2015) reported a brother and sister with features of vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome in a family with marked clinical variability. The proband was born with right-sided clubfoot and developed a unilateral hydrocele of the testis. He had crowded teeth and retrognathia. He was otherwise healthy until he died unexpectedly at 15 years of age, after a week of backache. Autopsy showed 3 dissections: a transverse dissection of the thoracic aorta at the level of the ligamentum arteriosum, and separate dissections of the right renal artery and the left femoral artery. There had been extensive bleeding into the thoracic and abdominal cavities, and the aortic wall showed cystic medial necrosis. His brain looked normal on gross examination, but neither brain imaging nor histologic examination of the brain was performed. Both sibs had pale, translucent skin with visible subcutaneous vessels and facial features characteristic of EDS type IV: a small lower jaw, prominent eyes, a pinched nose, and a thin upper lip. The sister had normal motor and language development. When evaluated at 19 years of age, she reported several episodes of spontaneous rupture of small superficial vessels. She had needed surgery for rupture of the medial meniscus and of the anterior cruciate ligament after sports trauma. She also had intermittent stomach aches, diarrhea, and occasional blood in the stool, as well as migraine with aura. She had small joint hypermobility in the hands but a Beighton score of zero. Imaging showed a dissection of the right internal carotid artery and an ectatic left internal carotid artery, which on follow-up 1 year later had a dissection flap. She also had a stenotic process in the right external iliac artery. Brain MRI showed cerebral cortical dysplasia with bilaterally thickened frontoparietal cortices, small gyri, and findings consistent with pachy micropolygyria. Her cognitive function and neurologic examination were normal, and she had not had seizures. The mother, who was heterozygous for one of the variants, had thin skin with visible subcutaneous vessels and reduced elasticity of the ascending aorta. She did not have the facial features of EDS type IV. The father, heterozygous for the other variant, was clinically normal at age 63.

Vandervore et al. (2017) studied a family with 2 affected and 2 unaffected sibs. The affected sibs had a brain phenotype on MRI similar to that associated with GPR56 mutations: cobblestone-like cortical malformation, white matter changes, and cerebellar cysts. The authors also reviewed brain imaging of 2 previously reported individuals with biallelic COL3A1 mutations and found a similar brain phenotype. They concluded that cobblestone-like malformation was present in all 3 families reported to date. ⚠︎

Horn et al. (2017) reported 2 unrelated families. The first patient was a 3-year-old girl with developmental delay who was born with bilateral clubfoot, joint laxity, and dysmorphic facial features. At 2 years of age she had an aneurysmal brain hemorrhage. Cerebral MRI showed profound cerebral abnormalities, including bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone variant. In the second family, 2 affected sibs had cobblestone-like cortical malformation, cerebellar cysts, white matter abnormalities, developmental delay, and seizures. Horn et al. (2017) noted that all families with biallelic COL3A1 variants had specific structural brain anomalies, and some had a severe Ehlers-Danlos syndrome phenotype. They characterized the brain phenotype as bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone variant, cerebellar microcysts, and abnormalities of the white matter. These features resembled the neurologic manifestations of individuals with autosomal recessive GPR56 mutations.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of PMGEDSV in the families reported by Plancke et al. (2009), Jorgensen et al. (2015), Vandervore et al. (2017), and Horn et al. (2017) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Plancke et al. (2009) found a homozygous mutation in a girl born to healthy consanguineous parents, each of whom carried the mutation. Jorgensen et al. (2015) found compound heterozygous variants in affected sibs; each variant was inherited from a different parent. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Plancke et al. (2009) sequenced the entire COL3A1 cDNA in a girl with recessive Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. She was homozygous for a 1-bp duplication (c.479dupT) that resulted in a premature termination codon (Lys161GlnfsTer45). Studies of genomic DNA showed that she inherited the mutation from each parent. Expression analysis by RT-PCR, quantitative PCR, immunohistochemistry, and Western blot showed strong mRNA decay and no type III collagen in the proband. The heterozygous parents were healthy and had no manifestations of vascular EDS.

In a brother and sister with vascular EDS, Jorgensen et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL3A1 gene by sequencing: a c.1786C-T transition in exon 26, resulting in an arg596-to-ter (R596X) substitution, and a c.3851G-A transition in exon 50, resulting in a gly1284-to-glu (G1284E) substitution in the carboxyl-terminal propeptide. The sister also had polymicrogyria. The father was heterozygous for the R596X mutation and the mother for the G1284E mutation, which confirmed that the variants were in trans. An unaffected brother carried neither variant. The proband had no variants in FBN1, TGFBR1, or TGFBR2. Cultured dermal fibroblasts from the affected sister made a reduced amount of type III procollagen, and all of its chains had abnormal electrophoretic mobility consistent with overmodification. Fibroblasts from the father made a reduced amount of apparently normal type III procollagen. Fibroblasts from the mother made mostly normal type III procollagen with a small amount of abnormal procollagen showing subtle overmodification. None of the paternal relatives heterozygous for the R596X variant had complications or clinical features consistent with vascular EDS.

Vandervore et al. (2017) performed exome analysis in a family with 2 affected and 2 unaffected sibs. They identified a homozygous c.145C-G transversion in exon 2 of the COL3A1 gene, resulting in a pro49-to-ala (P49A) substitution. They also performed functional assays on dermal fibroblasts.

Horn et al. (2017) identified biallelic COL3A1 mutations in 2 unrelated families. A 3-year-old girl was compound heterozygous for a nonsense mutation (c.1282C-T; R428X) and a 1-bp deletion (c.2057delC) causing a frameshift (Pro686LeufsTer105); both variants were novel. In the second family, 2 affected sibs were homozygous for the P49A mutation (c.145C-G).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jorgensen et al. (2015) concluded that biallelic COL3A1 sequence variants have a significantly worse outcome than heterozygous null or missense variants, and that frontoparietal polymicrogyria may be an added feature of the phenotype. They noted that biallelic variants explained the marked clinical variation within their family. The earliest death was from extensive aortic dissection at age 15 years, whereas other family members in their eighties had no complications.

Vandervore et al. (2017) suggested that the variable phenotype among patients with biallelic COL3A1 mutations shows that genetic alterations in different domains of type III procollagen can lead to different outcomes.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (1997) inactivated the Col3a1 gene in mouse embryonic stem cells by homologous recombination. About 10% of homozygous mutant mice survived to adulthood, but they lived much shorter lives than wildtype mice. The main cause of death was rupture of the major blood vessels, similar to patients with type IV Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Ultrastructural analysis of tissues from mutant mice showed that type III collagen is essential for normal collagen I fibrillogenesis in the cardiovascular system and other organs.

Jeong et al. (2012) noted that collagen III is a ligand for GPR56, mutations in which cause bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria. They found that Col3a1-null mice had a cobblestone-like cortical malformation, with breakdown of the pial basement membrane, neuronal overmigration, abnormal attachment of radial glial endfeet, and marginal zone heterotopias. The ectopias contained neurons from both deep and superficial cortical layers, as well as misplaced Cajal-Retzius cells and calbindin-positive interneurons. The pial basement membrane was properly formed at embryonic day (E) 10.5. Regional breakdown with concurrent neuronal overmigration was seen in about half of E11.5 embryos and in all embryos older than E12.5. Cortical ectopias were found in 23 of 28 null mice and in none of the wildtype or heterozygous mice. Meningeal fibroblasts from null mice showed no obvious defects, and the expression and glycosylation of alpha-dystroglycan were not affected. Jeong et al. (2012) suggested that collagen III regulates cortical development independently of the dystroglycan pathway.
