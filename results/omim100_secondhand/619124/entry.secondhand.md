### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene, which encodes the glutamate decarboxylase isoform GAD67, on chromosome 2q.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Seizures begin in the neonatal or early infantile period and are often epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. Early EEG often shows a suppression-burst pattern or hypsarrhythmia. Affected individuals have severe to profound developmental delay and intellectual disability with abnormal muscle tone. Some patients also have non-neural features, including cleft palate, omphalocele, joint contractures, pes equinovarus, scoliosis, and dysmorphic facial features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish (2 families), Iranian, and Brazilian origin. Seizures began in the first 2 months of life in all patients. All 10 patients with available early history had seizure onset in the first month of life. Seizures at onset were mainly epileptic spasms (6 patients) or myoclonic seizures (5 patients). Seven of 8 patients with EEG recordings in the neonatal period had a suppression-burst pattern or a variant with interburst attenuation. Two patients whose first EEG was recorded later had hypsarrhythmia. Six patients eventually achieved seizure control with antiepileptic drugs. All living patients had severe psychomotor delay, and intellectual disability was profound in all who could be assessed. Neurologic features included axial hypotonia, spasticity, tetraparesis, dystonia, and hyperkinetic movements. Head circumference was normal in all but 1 patient. Brain MRI was normal in 5 patients. Abnormal findings included hypoplastic corpus callosum with dilated subarachnoid spaces and progressive cerebral and cerebellar atrophy in 1 patient, and mild cerebral atrophy with ventricular enlargement at age 6 years in another patient. Seven patients had cleft palate, 6 had joint contractures, 5 had pes equinovarus, and 4 had scoliosis. Two sibs had omphalocele, and 3 patients had facial dysmorphic features without clear common elements. Four patients died before 4 years of age. One of these, born at 29 weeks' gestation, died at 9 days of age.

Among the patients reported by Chatron et al. (2020), the affected Egyptian sibs (Family B) had additional congenital anomalies. The sister had liver-containing omphalocele, jejunal atresia, a branchial fistula, multiple skin hemangiomas, camptodactyly, rocker-bottom feet, and a large clitoris with hypoplastic labia. She died at 2 years of age of failure to thrive and respiratory infection. Her brother had omphalocele, bilateral talipes equinovarus, and arthrogryposis, and died at 4 years of age of pulmonary infection and severe growth retardation. The proband of a Turkish family (Family E) also had Hirschsprung disease, cervical and axillary webbing, nystagmus, pale optic disks, failure of a hearing test at 1 year of age, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, and grade 1 nephrocalcinosis. One Turkish patient (Family C) had conductive hearing loss and recurrent fever episodes.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian, and Turkish ancestry. In the text, 4 patients were stated to be born to consanguineous parents. All were born at term after a normal pregnancy. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures with or without impaired awareness. Two patients had epileptic spasms, 4 had focal non-motor seizures, and 5 had bilateral motor seizures. Seizures were pharmacologically controlled in 3 patients and drug-resistant in 3. EEG at onset showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Cranial MRI was normal in all but 2 patients: 1 had slight ventricular enlargement and 1 had moderate global atrophy. All patients had severe developmental delay, and most did not achieve speech. One patient developed simple speech, had moderate intellectual disability, and remained seizure-free on medication. Five patients had reduced muscle strength. Four were unable to sit or crawl or had limited head control, and 2 depended on a nasogastric tube because of dysphagia. Four patients had slight dysmorphic facial features, and only 1 had cleft palate. That patient also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus, and hearing impairment. Other features without clear common elements included hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, and arthrogryposis of the lower limbs. Metabolic workups were normal.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) described a female infant with mild malformations and neonatal-onset seizures. Her EEG showed a burst-suppression pattern. †

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 is autosomal recessive. Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) found that bi-allelic GAD1 variants segregated with the disease in affected families, consistent with recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) studied 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with DEE89 using whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing. Every affected individual carried a homozygous GAD1 variant, and in each case this was the only likely pathogenic variant retained after filtering. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease in all families. Four families carried truncating variants. These were a splice site mutation (c.1414-1G-C) and a 5-bp deletion causing a frameshift (c.812_816delTTAAG). The fourth truncating variant was the nonsense mutation R531X, which was shared by an Iranian family and a Turkish family. cDNA analysis of the splice site mutation showed no normal mRNA product and 3 aberrant isoforms. In an Egyptian family, the authors found an in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA; K232del). In a cell-free assay, the mutant protein was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. In a Brazilian family, they found a missense mutation (E509K) in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that this change would disrupt salt bridges. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) studied 6 patients from 6 unrelated families with DEE89 using whole-exome sequencing. Patients in 5 families carried homozygous GAD1 variants. Three of these were missense variants (N564S, F324C, and T347M), 1 was a nonsense variant (Y29X), and 1 was a frameshift variant (c.568delC). An African American patient was compound heterozygous for R531X and a frameshift variant (c.670delC). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with a recessive mode of inheritance. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability for F324C and N564S, and broken hydrogen bonds in the pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate (PLP)-binding domain for T347M. On this basis, the authors proposed a loss-of-function mechanism.

In a female infant with DEE89, von Hardenberg et al. (2020) identified a novel homozygous splice site variant in the GAD1 gene. †

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) noted phenotypic variability between 2 families carrying the same homozygous R531X mutation. In particular, pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and cleft palate were absent in the Iranian family. The authors suggested that genetic background or environmental factors affecting fetal movement might explain the difference.

Neuray et al. (2020) questioned whether a previously reported homozygous S12C variant in the N-terminal domain of GAD67 causes autosomal recessive spastic cerebral palsy. They noted conflicting pathogenicity predictions for this variant and other candidate genes in the linked interval. They suggested that variants affecting the PLP-binding and C-terminal domains cause a more severe deficiency of GAD67 activity.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) suggested that cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures in patients with DEE89 are secondary to reduced fetal movements caused by severe central nervous system involvement. This proposal was based on similar findings in Gad1-null mice. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) reported that seizures responded well to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 patients who received it. They noted that this response appeared to depend on seizure type, because patients with epileptic spasms seemed to benefit in particular.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) started treatment with vigabatrin combined with a ketogenic diet in a female infant with DEE89 in her second month of life, after the genetic diagnosis. Seizures stopped clinically within 2 days, and at 7 months of age her growth and psychomotor development were unremarkable. The authors proposed this combination as an effective treatment for the disorder. †

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking Gad67. The mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67-null mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%. The brain showed no discernible defects.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 2 sibs with DEE89 born to Algerian first-cousin parents (Family A). Both were homozygous for a G-to-C transversion in intron 14 of the GAD1 gene (c.1414-1G-C), which was predicted to abolish the acceptor splice site of exon 15. cDNA analysis of the parents' lymphoblastoid cells showed no normal mRNA product. Instead there were 3 aberrant isoforms: one with skipping of exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delAGA; p.Lys232del]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 2 sibs with DEE89 born to Egyptian first-cousin parents (Family B). Both had omphalocele and talipes equinovarus, and the sister also had cleft palate. The proband was homozygous for a 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA), which deleted a highly conserved lysine (K232del). The variant was absent from public databases. In silico analysis predicted that it would disrupt splicing. In a cell-free expression system, the mutant protein was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype, so loss of function could not be formally demonstrated.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, 812TTAAG [c.812_816delTTAAG; p.Val271Aspfs*9]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 2 sibs with DEE89 and cleft palate born to Turkish first-cousin parents (Family C). Both were homozygous for a 5-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene (c.812_816delTTAAG), which caused a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9).

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; p.Arg531*]

Chatron et al. (2020) found a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution, in affected members of 2 families with DEE89: an Iranian family (Family D) and a Turkish family (Family E). The authors suggested a common haplotype because the families came from nearby regions. Pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and cleft palate were absent in Family D but present in Family E.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported an African American patient with DEE89 (Family C) who was compound heterozygous for the R531X mutation and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). R531X was reported only in the heterozygous state in gnomAD.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.Glu509Lys]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported a Brazilian girl with DEE89 born to first-cousin parents (Family F). She was homozygous for a c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution at an evolutionarily highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. The variant was absent from gnomAD. Structural modeling predicted loss of salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389.
