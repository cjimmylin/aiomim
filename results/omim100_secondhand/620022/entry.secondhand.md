### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene on chromosome 20q13.

### Description

Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive collagenopathy caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in the COL9A3 gene, which encodes the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) and Rad et al. (2022) found that the features seen in all reported patients were high myopia and moderate to severe or profound sensorineural hearing loss. Skeletal involvement, including epiphyseal or spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, varied more between patients, as did midface hypoplasia, vitreoretinal degeneration, retinal detachment, and cataract (Rad et al., 2022). Cleft palate had not been reported (Markova et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Faletra et al. (2014) described a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. According to the summaries by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) and Rad et al. (2022), the family was of Moroccan origin with first-cousin parents and had 3 affected sibs aged 4, 11, and 16 years. The sibs had moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, moderate to high myopia, normal vitreous, midface hypoplasia, tibial rotation, mild irregularities of the capital femoral and metacarpal epiphyses, and pes planus. They also had moderate to severe intellectual disability, which Faletra et al. (2014) suggested was unrelated to the COL9A3 mutation (Hanson-Kahn et al., 2018). ⚠︎

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy whose parents were third cousins from India. He failed his newborn hearing screen. At age 5 months, audiologic evaluation found moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe sensorineural hearing loss on the left, from 250 through 4,000 Hz. The hearing loss stayed stable. A CT scan of the temporal bones at 7 months showed only mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months. By 2 years he had myopia of -6.75 OD and -8 OS, and by 12 years this had progressed to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS. No vitreous abnormalities were noted. Femoral and tibial bowing were present at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed flattening of the acetabula, slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, irregularity of the medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly. Growth parameters were normal. Speech was initially delayed, which was attributed to the hearing loss, and intelligence was normal. Autoimmune hypothyroidism was diagnosed at age 10 years. No hearing loss, myopia, or skeletal anomalies were reported in the parents or in his older sister.

Nixon et al. (2019) studied 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome caused by type IX collagen gene variants. One family had 2 patients with a COL9A3 variant. All 6 patients in the study were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and all had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy. Rad et al. (2022) and Markova et al. (2021) reported further details of the COL9A3 sibs. They were aged 18 and 20 years and had severe sensorineural hearing loss. The older sib had refraction of -23 D in each eye at age 20 years and severe arthropathy of the shoulders and hip. This sib required a wheelchair, had undergone surgery for scoliosis, and had narrowing of the articular space in both knees on radiographs; the younger sib had none of these signs. Rad et al. (2022) also stated that Nixon et al. (2019) observed mild Stickler phenotypes in carrier parents.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a boy born of healthy nonconsanguineous parents; the pregnancy was complicated by oligohydramnios and fetal growth retardation. At age 1 month he had adductor spasm, hip dysplasia confirmed by ultrasound, and bilateral absence of ossification centers. Congenital high bilateral myopia was found at 3 months, and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss of grade 3 to 4 was diagnosed at 11 months. He had delayed speech development. Spine radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, mild platyspondyly, and kyphosis with the apex at T11. Radiographs of the lower limbs at 3 years showed enlarged joint spaces, flattened and irregular femoral heads, broadened and shortened femoral necks, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. Ophthalmic assessment found peripheral pigment rearrangement, small whitish dystrophic foci, multiple vitreous floaters, and a preretinal membrane in the right eye; the electroretinogram was normal. When examined at 2 years 5 months, he had a valgus knee deformity, waddling gait, rapid fatigability, and pain in the lower limb joints. His height was 88 cm (25th-50th percentile). He had a slightly flattened nasal bridge, small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, high palate, lumbar lordosis, limited hip abduction, long fingers, prominent knee joints, genu valgum, and planovalgus foot deformity. Markova et al. (2021) considered this a more severe presentation than previously reported, with early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration.

Rad et al. (2022) reported 5 affected individuals from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families, all with first-cousin parents. The proband in family 1 was a 28-year-old woman. She had high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, and retinal pigmentary changes, together with severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss. She had knee pain, but joint examination and radiographs were normal, and she lacked typical Stickler craniofacial features. The 2 affected sibs in family 2 were aged 65 and 57 years. Both had high myopia and had undergone multiple vitreoretinal surgeries for recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachment due to advanced vitreoretinal degeneration. Both also had cataract, profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss, and a herniated cervical disc. The older sib was blind without light perception. The 2 affected sibs in family 3, a boy aged 11.8 years and his sister aged 3.1 years, had myopia, congenital moderate to severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss, and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia. They also had pes planus, a depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares; midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures were more pronounced in the sister. The boy had knee pain, genu valgum, irregular femoral epiphyses, flattened femoral heads, short metacarpals, and a broad big toe. None of the 5 patients had intellectual disability or short stature. Rad et al. (2022) noted that the older patients in family 2 had milder skeletal findings than the younger patients in family 3.

### Inheritance

Faletra et al. (2014) first reported autosomal recessive inheritance of Stickler syndrome type VI, in a family with homozygous COL9A3 mutation. Further consanguineous families supported recessive inheritance. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) found that the parents and unaffected sib of an affected boy were heterozygous carriers, and Rad et al. (2022) described 3 families with first-cousin parents. Markova et al. (2021) found compound heterozygous mutations in trans in a patient from a nonconsanguineous family. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a homozygous loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene (c.1176_1198del; p.Gln393CysfsTer25). ⚠︎

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene in a boy with Stickler syndrome born to consanguineous parents. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination of translation. It was not found in public SNP databases. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant and showed that both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. Earlier sequencing and deletion/duplication analysis of COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and of specific exons of COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3, had been negative.

Nixon et al. (2019) identified homozygous variants in type IX collagen genes in 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome; 1 family with 2 affected patients carried a COL9A3 variant. Rad et al. (2022) identified this variant as a homozygous nonsense mutation (c.1411C-T; arg471-to-ter, R471X).

In a boy with Stickler syndrome born to nonconsanguineous parents, Markova et al. (2021) used targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes to identify compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (R90X) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (R577X) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variants and showed that they were in trans. The proband's healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants.

Rad et al. (2022) used exome sequencing in 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with Stickler syndrome and identified a different novel homozygous loss-of-function variant in the COL9A3 gene in each proband. The variants were a 10-bp deletion causing a frameshift in exon 2 (c.107_116del; p.Pro36ArgfsTer49), a nonsense variant in exon 23 (c.1204C-T; R402X), and a 1-bp deletion in exon 7 (c.355delC). The 10-bp deletion had an allele frequency of 0.00001390 in gnomAD, and the other 2 variants were not found in public databases. Segregation was examined in available family members by Sanger sequencing.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported that patients with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to loss-of-function mutations in COL9A1, COL9A2, or COL9A3 had similar phenotypes, all with high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and mild to severe hearing loss. At that time, vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not with COL9A3 mutations. They also noted that these patients differed from those with recessive Stickler syndrome due to COL11A1 mutations, who had severe to profound hearing loss and cleft palate but no reported midface hypoplasia or orthopedic problems. Vitreoretinal abnormalities were later described in COL9A3 patients by Nixon et al. (2019), Markova et al. (2021), and Rad et al. (2022).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 23-BP DEL, NT1176 [c.1176_1198del; p.Gln393CysfsTer25]

In affected members of a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a homozygous loss-of-function 23-bp deletion (c.1176_1198del) in the COL9A3 gene, resulting in a frameshift and premature termination (Gln393CysfsTer25). According to the summary by Rad et al. (2022), the family was Moroccan with first-cousin parents and had 3 affected sibs. ⚠︎

**.0002 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 1-BP DUP, 650C [c.650dupC]

In a boy with Stickler syndrome whose parents were third cousins from India, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC, NM_001853.3) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene by exome sequencing. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination and loss of the COL9A3 gene product. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant and showed that both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. The variant was not found in public SNP databases. Rad et al. (2022) gave 2 different protein-level names for this variant: p.Pro218AlafsTer49 in one table and p.Gly217TrpfsTer50 in another.

**.0003 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG471TER [c.1411C>T; p.Arg471Ter]

Nixon et al. (2019) identified a homozygous COL9A3 variant in 2 sibs with recessive Stickler syndrome. All patients in that study had high myopia, congenital megalophthalmos, hypoplastic vitreous, and sensorineural hearing loss. According to Rad et al. (2022), the variant was a c.1411C-T transition in exon 28 resulting in an arg471-to-ter (R471X) substitution. ⚠︎

**.0004 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG90TER [c.268C>T; p.Arg90Ter]

In a boy with Stickler syndrome born to nonconsanguineous parents, Markova et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL9A3 gene by targeted next-generation sequencing. One was a c.268C-T transition in exon 5 resulting in an arg90-to-ter (R90X) substitution, and the other was R577X. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variants were in trans, and the variants were classified as pathogenic under ACMG criteria.

**.0005 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG577TER [c.1729C>T; p.Arg577Ter]

In a boy with Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL9A3 gene. One was a c.1729C-T transition in exon 30 resulting in an arg577-to-ter (R577X) substitution, and the other was R90X. Segregation analysis showed that the variants were in trans.

**.0006 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 10-BP DEL, NT107 [c.107_116del; p.Pro36ArgfsTer49]

In a 28-year-old Iranian woman with Stickler syndrome whose parents were first cousins (family 1), Rad et al. (2022) identified a homozygous 10-bp deletion (c.107_116del, NM_001853.3) in exon 2 of the COL9A3 gene by exome sequencing. The deletion caused a frameshift (Pro36ArgfsTer49). The variant had an allele frequency of 0.00001390 in gnomAD, with 2 carriers.

**.0007 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG402TER [c.1204C>T; p.Arg402Ter]

In 2 Iranian sibs with Stickler syndrome born of a first-cousin marriage (family 2), Rad et al. (2022) identified a homozygous c.1204C-T transition in exon 23 of the COL9A3 gene by exome sequencing, resulting in an arg402-to-ter (R402X) substitution. The sibs, aged 65 and 57 years, had high myopia, recurrent retinal detachment, cataract, and profound sensorineural hearing loss. The variant was not reported in public databases.

**.0008 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 1-BP DEL, 355C [c.355delC; p.Leu119SerfsTer9]

In 2 Iranian sibs with Stickler syndrome and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia whose parents were first cousins (family 3), Rad et al. (2022) identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion (c.355delC) in exon 7 of the COL9A3 gene by exome sequencing. The deletion caused a frameshift, given as Leu119SerfsTer9 in the text and Leu119SerfsTer10 in the table. The variant was not reported in public databases.
