### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene (112264) on chromosome 8p21.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII is an autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder caused by deficient activity of the procollagen I C-terminal propeptide endopeptidase BMP1. Reported patients have had recurrent fractures, which in some started at or before birth. Some also had bone deformities, short stature, and umbilical hernia (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012; Cho et al., 2015). Bone density has varied between reports. It was described as low in the original Egyptian sibs (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012) but as increased or normal in other patients (Asharani et al., 2012; Valencia et al., 2014; Fahiminiya et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Munns et al. (2004) described 3 unrelated children with an apparently novel bone fragility disorder associated with an idiopathic mineralization defect. Recurrent lower limb fractures started when the children began bearing weight. The patients had none of the phenotypic, radiologic, or histomorphometric features classically associated with OI, idiopathic juvenile osteoporosis, or mild autosomal dominant osteopetrosis. Radiographs showed increased metaphyseal trabeculation and normal to increased cortical thickness, with no evidence of rickets or osteomalacia. Areal and volumetric bone mineral density (BMD) of the lumbar spine showed no major alteration. In 1 patient, peripheral quantitative computed tomography (pQCT) of the radius showed elevated cortical thickness and elevated total and trabecular volumetric BMD. Iliac bone biopsies showed a paucity of the birefringent pattern of normal lamellar bone. Histomorphometry showed osteomalacia with a prolonged mineralization lag time and a decreased mineral apposition rate. There was no biochemical evidence of abnormal calcium or phosphate metabolism, and mutation analysis of type I collagen was negative. Munns et al. (2004) concluded that this was a bone fragility disorder of moderate severity associated with accumulation of osteoid due to an intrinsic mineralization defect.

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) studied 2 sibs with severe autosomal recessive OI from a consanguineous Egyptian family. Their parents were first cousins. The proband was a 15-year-old girl. A fracture of the left tibia was noted after a normal full-term vaginal delivery, and she subsequently had 10 to 15 spontaneous fractures per year in the upper and lower limbs. Her features included triangular face, broad forehead, wide palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, faint blue sclerae, long philtrum, thin lips, and prominent ears. Her gross motor development was delayed and she could not stand unsupported. Mental development was normal, and she had no dentinogenesis imperfecta. She had a relatively large thorax compressing the abdomen and a large umbilical hernia (5 cm wide and 7 cm long) with abdominal contents. Other features included generalized hypotonia, muscle wasting, nocturnal enuresis, and generalized hirsutism. Her skin stretched normally, with no bruising or scars. Severe generalized bone deformities were painful to touch and included deformed clavicles, bowed and angulated upper limb bones, bowed femora, and severely angulated leg bones. She also had arachnodactyly, hyperextensibility of the elbow, wrist, and interphalangeal joints, kyphoscoliosis, and pectus carinatum. Radiographs showed deformed long bones with multiple fractures and callus formation, lack of bone modeling, serpentine thin tibiae and fibulae, S-curve thoracolumbar scoliosis with platyspondyly, generalized decreased bone density, and wormian bones. At 13 years of age, her weight was -3.8 SD, length -11.5 SD, and head circumference -3.0 SD. DEXA at 15 years showed borderline osteoporosis at the hip and spine (Z-scores -2.22 and -2.13, respectively).

The proband's 5-year-old brother had bilateral femoral bowing detected on fetal ultrasound and fractures of both femora at birth (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012). He had about 15 fractures per year. His facial features resembled his sister's, and like her he had normal mentality, no dentinogenesis imperfecta, and was unable to stand unsupported. He also had generalized bone deformities, joint hyperextensibility, arachnodactyly, and a large thorax with a large umbilical hernia (4 cm wide and 5 cm long). Radiographs showed wormian bones, deformed and unmodeled long bones with fractures, scoliosis, platyspondyly, and a wide symphysis pubis. His weight was -3.7 SD and length -7.2 SD, with normal head circumference. DEXA at 5 years showed borderline osteoporosis at the spine (Z-score -1.63) and osteoporosis at the left femur (Z-score -2.75). Audiologic and cardiovascular examinations were unremarkable in both sibs. Serum calcium and phosphate were normal, and alkaline phosphatase was slightly high. Both sibs were diagnosed with OI type III according to the classification of Sillence et al. (1979). Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) noted that the patients did not have the increased skin stretchability and fragility seen in Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.

Asharani et al. (2012) reported a consanguineous family in which 2 sibs had increased BMD and multiple recurrent fractures. ⚠︎

Valencia et al. (2014) described an additional patient with OI and normal bone density.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) studied 4 unrelated children with the bone fragility phenotype that had been described by Munns et al. (2004), which mainly involved lower extremity fractures and a mineralization defect in bone tissue but not at the growth plate. pQCT showed elevated volumetric cortical BMD in all 4 individuals. In iliac bone samples, histomorphometry and quantitative backscattered electron imaging showed that mineralization began late at bone formation sites but that the mineralized matrix was hypermineralized. ⚠︎

Cho et al. (2015) reported a Korean patient with OI. The patient had an umbilical hernia, frequent fractures, markedly short stature, delayed motor development, scoliosis, and dislocation of the radial head with a bowed radius and ulna.

### Inheritance

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) established autosomal recessive inheritance of OI13. In a consanguineous Egyptian family, 2 affected sibs were homozygous for a BMP1 mutation, and their unaffected parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous carriers. Asharani et al. (2012) also found a homozygous BMP1 mutation in a consanguineous family.

### Mapping

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) performed genomewide homozygosity mapping with SNP arrays in 2 affected sibs and 2 unaffected sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with autosomal recessive OI. The affected sibs were not homozygous at the CRTAP, PPIB, SERPINH1, SP7, or SERPINF1 loci, and only 1 of them was homozygous at LEPRE1 and FKBP10. Two homozygous regions were present in both patients and absent in the unaffected sibs. One was a 35.4-Mb region around the centromere of chromosome 3 containing 151 genes. The other was a 10.27-Mb region on chromosome 8p containing 84 genes, including BMP1.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with severe autosomal recessive OI, Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the BMP1 gene (F249L). The mutation was found by sequencing BMP1, a candidate gene in a region identified by homozygosity mapping. It segregated with the disorder in the family. It was not found in 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin or in 884 chromosomes from European controls. No mutations were found in COL1A1 or COL1A2 in 1 of the patients. Phe249 lies in the protease domain and is conserved in all members of the astacin family of metalloproteases. Supernatants from patient fibroblasts contained less pN-alpha-1(I) relative to pC-alpha-1(I) and less free C-propeptide (PICP) than controls, indicating reduced processing of the procollagen I C-propeptide. Fibroblasts from a heterozygous sib showed slightly impaired processing. Overexpressing wildtype mTLD (the long BMP1 isoform) in NIH3T3 cells and human primary fibroblasts led to C-terminal processing of most proalpha-1(I), whereas overexpressing the mutant protein did not. Only wildtype mTLD improved processing in patient fibroblasts.

Using whole-exome sequencing and filtering for homozygous stretches, Asharani et al. (2012) identified a homozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene (c.34G-C; G12R) in a consanguineous family with increased BMD and multiple recurrent fractures. The mutation lies within the BMP1 signal peptide. It impaired secretion of the protein and altered its posttranslational modification. ⚠︎

In a patient with OI and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) identified a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene, the same mutation reported by Asharani et al. (2012). Western blot analysis of the patient's dermal fibroblasts showed decreased levels of both alternatively spliced BMP1 products and abnormal cleavage of the type I procollagen C-propeptide.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing on 4 unrelated children with frequent fractures and a mineralization defect in bone tissue. Three were homozygous for a variant in BMP1 (c.*241T-C) affecting the polyadenylation signal of the transcript that encodes the short isoform, BMP1-1. Skin fibroblasts from these individuals had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein. The fourth child was compound heterozygous for the c.*241T-C variant and a variant in exon 15 (c.2107G-C) that affected splicing of both BMP1-1 and the long isoform, BMP1-3. Both genotypes were associated with impaired cleavage of the procollagen type I C-propeptide: fibroblast supernatants contained less free C-propeptide than controls. Fahiminiya et al. (2015) concluded that isolated lack of BMP1-1 causes bone fragility in children.

Using whole-exome sequencing in a Korean patient with OI, Cho et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous variants in the BMP1 gene: c.808A-G, which caused a missense change (M270V), and c.1297G-T, which caused skipping of exon 10. A zebrafish assay showed that both variants were hypofunctional.

### Pathogenesis

Valencia et al. (2014) used fluorescence and electron microscopy to study cultured fibroblasts from 2 patients: one homozygous for the BMP1 c.34G-C mutation and one previously reported patient homozygous for the c.747C-G mutation (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012). Fibroblasts from both patients showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix. Valencia et al. (2014) concluded that BMP1 is essential for the formation of human type I collagen fibrils.

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) found that pepsin-treated procollagens from patient fibroblasts had normal migration patterns for collagen types I, III, and V, indicating that the collagens were not overmodified. Levels of mTLD and BMP1 protein were similar in control and mutant fibroblasts. The authors suggested that the patients were more severely affected than patients with C-propeptide cleavage-site mutations in COL1A1 or COL1A2 because BMP1 deficiency impairs C-terminal processing of both procollagen I chains at the same time.

### Clinical Management

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) started cyclic intravenous bisphosphonate treatment in 2 affected sibs.

### Animal Model

Asharani et al. (2012) characterized the zebrafish bone mutant 'frilly fins,' which carries lesions in bmp1a. Genetic, biochemical, and histologic analyses showed that Bmp1a is critically required for generating mature collagen in bone, downstream of osteoblast maturation. This showed that BMP1 function in bone formation is conserved across species. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**  
BMP1, PHE249LEU [c.747C>G; p.Phe249Leu; NM_006129.4:c.747C>G]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with severe autosomal recessive OI (OI13), Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in exon 6 of the BMP1 gene. The mutation results in a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution at a residue in the protease domain that is conserved across the astacin family of metalloproteases. The unaffected parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous. The mutation was not found in 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin or in 884 chromosomes from European controls. Patient fibroblasts showed reduced processing of the procollagen I C-propeptide. When overexpressed in NIH3T3 cells and in human fibroblasts, mutant mTLD did not increase C-terminal processing of proalpha-1(I), whereas wildtype mTLD did.

Valencia et al. (2014) found impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix of cultured fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for the c.747C-G mutation.

**.0002 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**  
BMP1, GLY12ARG [c.34G>C; p.Gly12Arg]

In a consanguineous family in which 2 sibs had increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures (OI13), Asharani et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.34G-C transversion in the BMP1 gene, resulting in a gly12-to-arg (G12R) substitution. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and filtering for homozygous stretches. It lies within the signal peptide and impaired secretion of the protein and altered its posttranslational modification. ⚠︎

In a patient with OI and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) identified a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene. The patient's dermal fibroblasts had decreased levels of both alternatively spliced BMP1 products, abnormal cleavage of the type I procollagen C-propeptide, and impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils.

**.0003 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**  
BMP1, *241T-C [c.*241T>C]

In 3 unrelated children with frequent fractures, mainly of the lower extremities, and a mineralization defect in bone tissue (OI13), Fahiminiya et al. Skin fibroblasts had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein and released less free procollagen type I C-propeptide than controls. A fourth child was compound heterozygous for the c.*241T-C variant and a c.2107G-C variant in exon 15 of BMP1.

**.0004 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**
