### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder. Saunders et al. (2015) described it in a girl with progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia, seizures, poor weight gain, and lactic acidosis. Her muscle showed abnormal mitochondrial ultrastructure, and PNPLA8 protein was absent. Later reports of patients with biallelic PNPLA8 variants described a wider range of neurologic disease. At one end were congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early death. At the other were childhood-onset neurodegeneration and adolescent- or adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with peripheral neuropathy (Shukla et al., 2018; Burnyte et al., 2023; Abdel-Hamid et al., 2024; Nakamura et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) reported a 23-year-old man with mitochondrial myopathy. He had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy appeared. His venous lactate levels were elevated, and at one stage of the illness he developed severe lactic acidosis. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids, which histochemistry and thin-layer chromatography confirmed. The metabolic defect was not defined.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl (CMH193) who was evaluated for suspected mitochondrial myopathy with dystonia, abnormal gait, and an abnormal muscle biopsy. She was asymptomatic in her first year and met all developmental milestones, although her parents noted toe-walking. At age 2 she had a positive Gowers sign, and by age 3 she could no longer walk. She could sit independently for only 10 minutes because of fatigue. Her fine motor skills declined and her speech became dysarthric. She had proximal muscle weakness, tight heel cords, and generalized mildly increased tone. Over several years her proximal weakness increased, she developed dysmetria, and her hypotonicity progressed to spasticity of different opposing muscle groups. She later developed complex partial seizures with recurrent left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. Coenzyme Q10 supplementation increased her appetite but not her weight, and she kept a very lean body habitus. She also developed significant lactic acidosis that required bicarbonate supplementation. Her anion gap had been consistently elevated (17 mmol/L) since age 5. At age 7, blood lactic acid (3.8-5.6 mmol/L; normal, 0.7-2.1) and pyruvic acid (0.18-0.32 mmol/L; normal, 0.08-0.16) were elevated, with ratios of 17.5-21.1. Cerebrospinal fluid studies at age 6 showed 5-methyltetrahydrofolate deficiency. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtly increased signal in the medial globus pallidus bilaterally, which was no longer seen 2 years later. A quadriceps biopsy at 4 years showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic fibers of all subtypes and no ragged red fibers. Electron microscopy showed mitochondria with marked disarray of cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions. There were also subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggesting degenerating mitochondria, and an increase in secondary lysosomes containing residual bodies. SDH and COX activities in muscle were normal, as was electron transport chain function.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls. Patient 1 was born to consanguineous Indian parents. She had congenital microcephaly (head circumference 29 cm, -4 SD) and clonic seizures starting on day 2 of life, with spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak.

Patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018) was born to nonconsanguineous parents of Indian/Fijian origin. She developed normally until about 13 months of age and then regressed, with episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures. She developed acquired microcephaly, and at age 15 years her head circumference was 49 cm (-4.7 SD). Other features included choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, and contractures. Over a decade, brain MRI showed volume loss in the cerebellum, brainstem, and parietal and occipital lobes, with thinning of the corpus callosum. MR spectroscopy showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. On electron microscopy, muscle showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria surrounded by membrane whorls, with disrupted cristae. Activities of electron transport chain complexes I-IV were normal. She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, elevated blood lactate on several occasions, and elevated growth and differentiation factor-15 (1293 pg/ml).

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset, severe, and progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) reported 2 adult sibs whose symptoms began at about 14 years of age, with ataxia and progressive sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. Cognition was preserved over time. The brother was unable to walk without assistance from age 17 and became nonambulant at 44 years. At age 30 he had marked axial ataxia, tetraparesis, distal muscle wasting, absent tendon reflexes in the lower limbs, foot deformities, and bilateral Babinski signs. At age 45 he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, and his Mini-Mental State Examination score was 30/30. Brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. His sister, examined at age 21, had severe axial ataxia, appendicular dysmetria, and axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, with normal cognition. Neither sib had visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver dysfunction.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 had progressive microcephaly, infantile-onset seizures, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a distinct phenotype within a spectrum defined by age at onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals from 12 unrelated families with biallelic PNPLA8 variants. These included a family previously reported by Denommé-Pichon et al. (2022) without details. Common features were central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). The 8 patients from families 1 through 8 had severe disease. Features in this group included congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7 of 7), peripheral spasticity (7 of 8), and seizures beginning within 7 months of age (8 of 8). Their brain imaging showed a simplified gyral pattern (6 of 6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7 of 7), and severe progressive atrophy of the cerebral cortex (5 of 5). By contrast, 4 patients from families 11 and 12 had milder disease. All 4 learned to walk and later lost milestones, 3 had ataxia, and 3 had mild or no intellectual disability. Their MRI showed progressive cerebellar atrophy with little or no loss of cerebral cortical volume. Patients from families 9 and 10 were of intermediate severity. Nakamura et al. (2024) concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum, ranging from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy to childhood-onset neurodegeneration.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance was supported by Saunders et al. (2015), who found that the PNPLA8 mutations in their patient had been inherited from carrier parents. Shukla et al. (2018) and Nakamura et al. (2024) found the same pattern in additional families. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Shukla et al. (2018) stated that the PNPLA8 gene is located on chromosome 7q31.

### Molecular Genetics

Saunders et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing in a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis and in her parents. The patient was compound heterozygous for 2 frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4), numbered on NM_001256011.1. Sanger sequencing confirmed both mutations, and each was inherited from a carrier parent. Two healthy sibs carried neither mutation. Neither variant was found in the Exome Sequencing Project or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle showed a dramatic decrease in the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 bands. The authors noted that the patient's clinical presentation, muscle histology, and mitochondrial ultrastructure closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice.

Shukla et al. (2018) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 unrelated girls with a neurodegenerative mitochondrial phenotype. Patient 1 was homozygous for a nonsense mutation in PNPLA8, c.517G-T (glu173-to-ter, E173X; NM_001256007.1). Patient 2 was compound heterozygous for c.2275_2276delCT (p.Leu759AlafsTer4) and c.419C-A (ser140-to-ter, S140X). Segregation analysis showed that the variants were inherited from carrier parents.

Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous missense variant, c.1874A-G, in the patatin domain of PNPLA8. The patient had prenatal-onset severe neurodegeneration.

In 2 sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous PNPLA8 variants: a novel 1-bp deletion, c.2269del (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10), and the previously reported c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4) (NM_001256007.3). The c.2275_2276del variant was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but exome and Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles. The c.2269del variant was not found in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, or gnomAD databases. Both truncating variants were in the last coding exon. The authors suggested that such transcripts might escape nonsense-mediated decay and retain partial enzyme activity, which could explain the milder phenotype.

Using exome sequencing, Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) identified a loss-of-function variant in PNPLA8, c.1231C-T (arg411-to-ter, R411X), in one family and a missense variant, c.1559T-A (val520-to-asp, V520D), in a second family.

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified 14 PNPLA8 variants, 12 of them novel, in 14 individuals from 12 unrelated families. The variants comprised 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense variants, and each had a population frequency below 0.001%. In patient-derived lymphoblastoid cells, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant caused skipping of exons 6 to 8. Fibroblasts from the same patient showed a significant reduction in 77-kD PNPLA8 protein. Cerebral organoids derived from this patient's induced pluripotent stem cells, as well as PNPLA8-knockout organoids, had fewer basal radial glial cells and upper-layer neurons. Treatment with lysophosphatidic acid partly restored the number of basal radial glial cells in the patient-derived organoids. PNPLA8-knockout neural progenitor cells had reduced amounts of lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid. Patient fibroblasts had significantly reduced maximal mitochondrial respiration. Nakamura et al. (2024) also found homozygous N-terminal frameshift variants, which affect only the largest (88-kD) protein isoform, in 2 Iranian individuals with optic atrophy or nonsyndromic hearing loss. They considered these variants to be of undetermined significance.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) suggested that the position of a PNPLA8 variant, rather than its type, correlates strongly with age at disease onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) analyzed 25 affected individuals from 20 families. Biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were found specifically in the severe group, which had congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. Patients of intermediate severity carried combinations of a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent C-terminal frameshift p.Leu759Alafs*4. All patients with the mild late-onset movement-disorder phenotype had biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757Glnfs*10 or p.Leu759Alafs*4). In fibroblasts, a variant from a severely affected patient (p.Glu296Lysfs*6) had a null effect. Cells carrying p.Leu759Alafs*4 from a mildly affected patient showed weak expression of the 77-kD protein, suggesting residual function. By contrast, fibroblasts from the previously reported severely affected patient who was homozygous for V520D had PNPLA8 protein levels similar to those of controls.

### Clinical Management

In the patient reported by Mastaglia et al. (1980), the myopathy improved markedly after prednisone and thiamine were started, and the improvement was maintained after both drugs were withdrawn.

In the patient of Saunders et al. (2015), a multivitamin containing iron, carbidopa-levodopa, and leucovorin initially improved symptoms. The lactic acidosis required bicarbonate supplementation.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that loss of iPLA2-gamma (Pnpla8) in mice caused profound changes in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism and mitochondrial phospholipid homeostasis. These changes included a markedly elevated hippocampal cardiolipin content, with a shift toward shorter-chain molecular species, increased oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species, and increased ceramides. Electron microscopy showed enlarged heteromorphic lamellar structures, identified as degenerating mitochondria, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. The mutant mice had autophagy and cognitive dysfunction.
