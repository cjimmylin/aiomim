### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative mitochondrial disorder. It is caused by loss of the mitochondrial calcium-independent phospholipase A2-gamma encoded by PNPLA8. The first reported patient had childhood-onset progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia, seizures, poor weight gain, and lactic acidosis (Saunders et al., 2015). Later reports widened the phenotype to a continuum. At the severe end are congenital microcephaly, early-onset seizures, spasticity, simplified gyral pattern, and pontocerebellar hypoplasia, with death in infancy in some patients. At the mild end are childhood- or adolescent-onset regression, ataxia, peripheral neuropathy, and progressive cerebellar atrophy with mild or no intellectual disability (Shukla et al., 2018; Burnyte et al., 2023; Abdel-Hamid et al., 2024; Nakamura et al., 2024). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) reported a 23-year-old man with a mitochondrial myopathy. Twenty-two years before the skeletal myopathy appeared, he had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that resulted in cardiac insufficiency. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and severe lactic acidosis developed at one stage of the illness. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and accumulation of neutral lipids; the lipid accumulation was confirmed histochemically and by thin-layer chromatography. The myopathy improved markedly after prednisone and thiamine were started, and the improvement persisted after these drugs were withdrawn. The metabolic defect was not defined.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl evaluated for suspected mitochondrial myopathy with dystonia, abnormal gait, and an abnormal muscle biopsy. She was born at term with a weight of 3.1 kg (25th percentile). She was asymptomatic in the first year and met all developmental milestones, although her parents noted toe-walking. At age 2 she had a positive Gowers sign, and by 3 years she could no longer walk. Her fine motor skills declined and her speech became dysarthric. Examination showed proximal muscle weakness, tight heel cords, and generalized mildly increased tone. Over several years she developed increasing proximal weakness and dysmetria, and hypotonicity progressed to spasticity. She later developed complex partial seizures with recurrent left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. She remained very lean despite an increased appetite on CoQ10. She developed significant lactic acidosis requiring bicarbonate supplementation, and her anion gap had been consistently elevated (17 mmol/L) since age 5. At age 7, blood lactic acid was 3.8 to 5.6 mmol/L (normal, 0.7-2.1) and pyruvic acid was 0.18 to 0.32 mmol/L (normal, 0.08-0.16), with lactate:pyruvate ratios of 17.5 to 21.1 (normal, less than 20). Cerebrospinal fluid showed deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle increased signal in the medial globus pallidus bilaterally; this was no longer visible 2 years later. Creatine phosphokinase and plasma acylcarnitines were normal, as were mtDNA tRNA sequencing and mtDNA depletion studies. A quadriceps biopsy at 4 years showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic fibers of all subtypes and no ragged red fibers. Electron microscopy showed mitochondria with marked disarray of cristae, globular dense osmiophilic inclusions, and subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes. There was also an increase in secondary lysosomes containing residual bodies that appeared to derive from mitochondria. SDH and COX activities in muscle were normal, and electron transport chain function was not affected.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls. Patient 1 was born to consanguineous parents of Indian origin. At birth her weight was 2410 g (-2 SD), length 48 cm (-1 SD), and head circumference 29 cm (-4 SD). She had clonic seizures from the second day of life, spasticity of all 4 limbs, and overriding cranial sutures. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak.

Patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018) was born to nonconsanguineous parents of Indian/Fijian origin. She developed normally until about 13 months of age and then regressed, with several episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures that were difficult to control. Her development stagnated at an 8-month level. She was nonverbal, required a gastrostomy tube, and needed continuous positive airway pressure at night because of central hypotonia and muscle weakness. She developed acquired microcephaly; at age 15 years her head circumference was 49 cm (-4.7 SD). Other features included choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, and severe contractures. Serial brain MRI showed progressive volume loss in the cerebellum, brainstem, and parietal and occipital lobes, together with thinning of the corpus callosum. MRS showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. On electron microscopy, muscle biopsy showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria with surrounding membrane whorls, disrupted cristae, and electron-dense deposits. Activities of complexes I to IV were normal. Blood lactate was elevated on several occasions, and at age 14 years GDF15 was elevated (1293 pg/ml; normal, less than 750 pg/ml). She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, severely reduced visual evoked potential amplitudes, and obstructive sleep apnea with restrictive lung disease.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset severe and progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) reported 2 adult sibs. The brother, aged 49 years, developed lower limb weakness and distal muscle atrophy at age 14, accompanied by ataxia and gait disturbance. He could not walk without assistance from age 17. Later he developed tetraparesis, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, and marked dysmetria. He was nonambulant from age 44. At 45 years he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, and his Mini-Mental State Examination score was 30/30. Nerve conduction studies showed sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, and brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. His sister developed walking difficulty and lower limb weakness at age 14 years. At age 21 she had distal limb muscle atrophy, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, and severe axial ataxia and appendicular dysmetria. Her cognition was normal, and nerve conduction studies showed axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy. Neither sib had cognitive decline or visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver dysfunction.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 had progressive microcephaly, infantile-onset seizures, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a distinct phenotype, in addition to the previously described congenital, childhood-onset, and adult-onset ataxia-neuropathy presentations.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals from 12 unrelated families with neurologic disease and biallelic PNPLA8 variants. These included additional data on 1 family previously reported without details by Denommé-Pichon et al. (2022). Common features were central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). Eight patients (families 1-8) formed a severe group with congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7 of 7), peripheral spasticity (7 of 8), and seizures beginning within 7 months of age (8 of 8). On neuroimaging, this group had a simplified gyral pattern (6 of 6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7 of 7), and severe progressive cerebral cortical atrophy (5 of 5). Four patients (families 11-12) had relatively mild features: they achieved walking (4 of 4), had ataxia (3 of 4), regression of milestones (4 of 4), and mild or no intellectual disability (3 of 4), and seizures were unique or absent (4 of 4). Their brain MRI showed little or no cerebral volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Two patients had intermediate severity. Combining their patients with 11 previously reported individuals, Nakamura et al. (2024) concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum, ranging from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy to childhood-onset neurodegeneration. In patient 1, fibroblasts showed significantly reduced maximal respiration, but mitochondrial morphology was normal.

### Inheritance

Saunders et al. (2015) found that the PNPLA8 variants in their patient were inherited from carrier parents. Shukla et al. (2018) confirmed segregation in 2 families, one of them consanguineous. Nakamura et al. (2024) showed segregation in 12 families. These findings were consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Saunders et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing in a girl with suspected mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis and in her healthy parents. The patient was compound heterozygous for 2 frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4), numbered on NM_001256011.1. The variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and were inherited from carrier parents. Two healthy sibs carried neither variant. Both variants were absent from the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project and from an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot of patient muscle showed a marked decrease of the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 protein bands.

Shukla et al. (2018) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 unrelated girls. Patient 1, born to consanguineous parents, carried a homozygous nonsense mutation (c.517G-T; Glu173Ter) in PNPLA8. Patient 2 was compound heterozygous for c.2275_2276delCT (p.Leu759AlafsTer4) and c.419C-A (Ser140Ter). Sanger sequencing confirmed the variants, and segregation analysis showed that the parents were carriers. ⚠︎

Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation (c.1874A-G) in the patatin domain of PNPLA8 in a patient with prenatal-onset severe neurodegeneration.

Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 adult sibs with adolescent-onset ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy. Both sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 PNPLA8 mutations: a novel 1-bp deletion (c.2269del; p.Thr757GlnfsTer10) and the previously reported c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4). The c.2275_2276del variant was maternally inherited. Paternal DNA was not available, but sequencing data showed that the 2 variants were in trans. The c.2269del variant was not present in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, or gnomAD databases.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing in 3 patients from 2 families. They identified a loss-of-function mutation in PNPLA8 (c.1231C-T; Arg411Ter) in family I and a missense mutation (c.1559T-A; Val520Asp) in family II.

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified biallelic PNPLA8 variants in 14 individuals from 12 unrelated families with neurologic disease. Of the 14 variants, 12 were novel: 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense. All had frequencies below 0.001% in population databases. In patient 1, the splice variant c.1625+3_1625+6del caused skipping of exons 6 to 8, and the 77-kD PNPLA8 protein was markedly reduced in his fibroblasts. A frameshift variant from a severely affected patient (Glu296LysfsTer6) also resulted in a null effect. Fibroblasts from a mildly affected patient with Leu759AlafsTer4 showed weak expression of the 77-kD protein. Fibroblasts from the previously reported patient homozygous for Val520Asp showed protein levels similar to controls despite the severe phenotype. Nakamura et al. (2024) also identified 2 individuals from 2 unrelated Iranian families in a non-neurologic cohort. One had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss. Each was homozygous for a different N-terminal frameshift variant that affects only the largest (88-kD) isoform. In the optic atrophy family, an affected sib of the proband was heterozygous, so the authors considered the significance of these variants undetermined.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that the c.2275_2276del variant had previously been reported in compound heterozygosity with another truncating variant in a patient with severe neurodegeneration in early childhood. By contrast, their sibs, who carried it with c.2269del, had a less severe phenotype. Both of the sibs' truncating variants lie in the last coding exon of PNPLA8. The authors suggested that such transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and retain partial enzyme activity. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) suggested that disease onset correlates strongly with the site of the variant rather than its type.

Nakamura et al. (2024) analyzed 25 affected individuals from 20 families. Biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were found specifically in the severe group, which had congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. Patients of intermediate severity carried a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent C-terminal frameshift variant Leu759AlafsTer4. All patients with the mild late-onset movement disorder phenotype carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (Thr757GlnfsTer10 or Leu759AlafsTer4). The authors concluded that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting the 77- and 74-kD isoforms are sufficient to cause the severe developmental and progressive phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Nakamura et al. (2024) generated cerebral organoids from PNPLA8-knockout induced pluripotent stem cells and from cells of a patient. Both showed a smaller subventricular zone-like region and fewer basal radial glial cells and SATB2-positive upper-layer neurons; deep-layer neurons and basal intermediate progenitors were not affected. Spatial transcriptomics of patient-derived organoids showed altered expression of genes related to the cell cycle, basal radial glia, and neural differentiation in apical radial glial cells. PNPLA8-deficient neural progenitor cells had reduced lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid. Adding lysophosphatidic acid partly restored the number of basal radial glial cells in patient-derived organoids. Mitochondrial morphology and oxidative phosphorylation were largely unaffected in PNPLA8-knockout neural progenitor cells. The authors concluded that biallelic null variants in PNPLA8 cause microcephaly by reducing the abundance of basal radial glial cells.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that mice lacking calcium-independent phospholipase A2-gamma (iPLA2-gamma) had profound changes in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism. These included elevated cardiolipin content with a shift to shorter-chain molecular species, decreased plasmenylethanolamine, increased oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine, and increased ceramides. Electron microscopy showed enlarged, degenerating mitochondria and ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. The mice had autophagy and cognitive dysfunction.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that the mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities in their patient's muscle closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice. Features of the null mice cited by the authors included mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, enhanced insulin sensitivity, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 334AATT [c.334_337delAATT; p.Asn112HisfsX29]

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a girl with mitochondrial myopathy, seizures, poor weight gain, and lactic acidosis. She was compound heterozygous for a 4-bp deletion in the PNPLA8 gene (c.334_337delAATT, NM_001256011.1), which causes a frameshift (Asn112HisfsTer29), and for a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG; Leu659AlafsTer4). Exome sequencing identified the mutations, and they segregated from carrier parents. The variant was absent from the Exome Sequencing Project and from an internal database of 1,913 samples. PNPLA8 protein was markedly reduced in the patient's muscle.

**.0002 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis. She was compound heterozygous for a 2-bp deletion in the PNPLA8 gene, which they designated c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4) on NM_001256011.1, and for a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT). Nakamura et al. (2024) included this patient among those carrying the recurrent C-terminal frameshift variant Leu759AlafsTer4. ⚠︎

Shukla et al. (2018) identified compound heterozygosity for c.2275_2276delCT (p.Leu759AlafsTer4) and c.419C-A (Ser140Ter) in PNPLA8 in a girl of Indian/Fijian origin. She had regression after 13 months of age, seizures, acquired microcephaly, lactic acidosis, and progressive cerebellar atrophy. Both parents were carriers.

Burnyte et al. (2023) identified this mutation (c.2275_2276del), inherited from the mother, in 2 adult sibs with adolescent-onset ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy. It was in trans with a 1-bp deletion (c.2269del; p.Thr757GlnfsTer10).

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that patients with biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants, including Leu759AlafsTer4, had a relatively mild phenotype. Fibroblasts from a mildly affected patient with Leu759AlafsTer4 showed weak expression of the 77-kD PNPLA8 protein.
