### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding titin (TTN) on chromosome 2q31.

### Description

Myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9), also called hereditary myopathy with early respiratory failure (HMERF), is an autosomal dominant muscle disease with onset in adulthood. Muscle weakness may be predominantly proximal, distal, or axial, and early respiratory muscle weakness is a key feature. It can lead to ventilatory failure while patients can still walk. Muscle biopsy shows myofibrillar abnormalities, including eosinophilic inclusions or cytoplasmic bodies, rimmed vacuoles, and accumulation of myofibrillar proteins (Edstrom et al., 1990; Chinnery et al., 2001; Pfeffer et al., 2012; Pfeffer et al., 2014).

### Nomenclature

Pfeffer et al. (2014) found that patients with the TTN p.C30071R mutation met clinical and pathologic criteria for myofibrillar myopathy (MFM). They proposed that HMERF caused by TTN mutations be classified as MFM-titinopathy.

### Clinical Features

Edstrom et al. (1990) described 16 patients from 7 pedigrees with an unusual limb-girdle myopathy of adult onset. All patients had prominent respiratory muscle weakness early in the disease. In many of them, an acute respiratory incapacity was the reason for the first neurologic examination. Other early symptoms were weakness of the neck flexors and sometimes of the foot extensors. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed a myopathy. Serum muscle enzyme concentrations were normal or slightly elevated. On light microscopy, muscle biopsies showed plaques that stained strongly with rhodamine-conjugated phalloidin, a specific marker for F-actin. On electron microscopy, these plaques consisted of moderately dense thin filaments and were related to Z-disc splitting or formed extensions from Z-discs. The authors suggested that these changes indicated defective myofibrillogenesis. All patients were described as having proximal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, with early involvement of the neck flexors and respiratory muscles, especially the diaphragm. ⚠︎

Chinnery et al. (2001) reported an autosomal dominant myopathy in a family spanning 3 generations. They clinically assessed 24 family members, 11 of whom were affected, and studied muscle pathology in 7 affected individuals, including 1 at autopsy. The disease presented in mid-adult life with weakness of the tibialis anterior. Affected individuals in the second generation presented earlier, and their disease progressed faster, than those in the first generation. Lung function tests showed progressive global respiratory muscle weakness that was detectable from the time of presentation, with preferential involvement of the diaphragm in some cases. Weakness of the hip girdle and shoulder girdle developed later. Clinical and pathologic features correlated closely. Clinically unaffected muscles showed minimal pathologic change. Moderately affected muscles showed fiber splitting, eosinophilic inclusions, and vacuoles with basophilic rims. In severely affected muscles, fat and fibrous connective tissue had replaced muscle fibers. The inclusions were Congophilic and reacted with antibodies to desmin, beta-amyloid, and phosphorylated tau protein.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) studied 31 carriers of a TTN mutation, followed for up to 31 years. They included members of the family reported by Chinnery et al. (2001), a second family, and a third familial case. The disease could present with predominantly distal, proximal, or respiratory muscle weakness. Age at onset varied widely, from early adulthood to a mild phenotype in advanced age. In nearly all patients, weakness began earlier and was more severe in the lower limbs. Seven patients also had axial muscle weakness. Respiratory function declined gradually over time. No patient developed cardiomyopathy despite up to 31 years of follow-up. Muscle histopathology most often showed eosinophilic inclusions (inclusion bodies) and rimmed vacuoles, but findings were nonspecific in a minority of patients.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) described 5 British families with the TTN p.C30071R mutation. Onset was usually in the fourth or fifth decade. Presenting symptoms included tripping and falling, myalgia, and difficulty walking. CK values ranged from normal to 1195 IU/L. The disease progressed slowly and involved proximal, distal, and axial muscles. Only 1 patient lost ambulation, after about 25 years of disease. Ten of 11 patients had abnormal pulmonary function tests indicating respiratory muscle weakness. Four patients needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation about 20 to 30 years after onset. Features not previously documented in this condition were scapular winging, often asymmetric; contractures of the elbow and/or Achilles tendon; variable spinal rigidity; and muscle hypertrophy, including of the calf and quadriceps. One patient had mild left ventricular impairment, possibly related to respiratory dysfunction, and another had supraventricular tachycardia. Two mutation carriers reported only mild myalgia and had mild or very mild weakness. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic or dystrophic changes. Some biopsies had rimmed vacuoles and eosinophilic inclusions that appeared dark blue on Gomori trichrome staining. Desmin, myotilin, p62, and ubiquitin had accumulated in the cytoplasm to varying degrees in 6 of 7 biopsies. In 3 biopsies, the cytoplasmic aggregates had a 'cheetah skin' appearance, particularly on myotilin staining. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Birchall et al. (2005) performed muscle MRI in 7 patients with autosomal dominant myopathy with early respiratory failure, aged 37 to 66 years, 4 of whom were male. Imaging showed selective early involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus muscles that could not be detected clinically. Rates of progression differed between closely related muscles. The authors considered these findings specific to the disorder and useful for early noninvasive diagnosis in individuals at risk.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) performed MRI in 21 affected patients and found semitendinosus involvement in 20, including 3 who had no symptoms. Pfeffer et al. (2014) also found early involvement of the semitendinosus and peroneus longus muscles in patients with TTN variants. This pattern was present in a presymptomatic carrier of the p.C30071R mutation.

### Inheritance

Edstrom et al. (1990) found evidence of autosomal dominant inheritance in most of the pedigrees they studied. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also observed by Nicolao et al. (1999) in 2 Swedish families and by Chinnery et al. (2001) in a 3-generation family.

### Mapping

Nicolao et al. (1999) performed a genomewide linkage screen in 2 Swedish families with autosomal dominant myopathy, proximal weakness, early respiratory failure, and characteristic cytoplasmic bodies in muscle biopsies. They found linkage to chromosome 2q24-q31, with a maximum combined 2-point lod score of 4.87 at theta = 0 for marker D2S1245. Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 17-cM region between markers D2S2384 and D2S364. Affected individuals in both families shared an identical haplotype, suggesting a common origin.

In the family originally reported by Chinnery et al. (2001), Pfeffer et al. (2012) used genomewide linkage analysis together with whole-exome sequencing. They identified a likely causal TTN variant within a shared 2.93-Mb haplotype on chromosome 2.

### Heterogeneity

Xiang et al. (1999) studied a French family whose clinical and muscle biopsy findings resembled those of the Swedish families. Linkage to chromosome 2q24-q31 was completely excluded. A genomewide scan gave a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.11, the highest achievable in this family, for markers in a 4-cM region between D2S1272 and D2S1260. The authors suggested that the disease gene in this family probably lies on chromosome 2q21.

Chinnery et al. (2001) found that the disorder in their family was not linked to any of the known distal myopathy loci.

### Molecular Genetics

Lange et al. (2005) reported that a human mutation in the protein kinase domain of titin (TTN) causes hereditary muscle disease by disrupting a signaling pathway involving nbr1, p62/SQSTM1, and MuRF2 (see PATHOGENESIS). Pfeffer et al. (2014) referred to this kinase domain mutation as p.R32450W and noted that it had been found in 2 families previously designated as having HMERF. ⚠︎

In the family originally reported by Chinnery et al. (2001), Pfeffer et al. (2012) combined genomewide linkage analysis with whole-exome sequencing. They identified a heterozygous g.274375T-C mutation in the TTN gene, resulting in a cys30071-to-arg (C30071R) substitution. The mutation segregated with the phenotype in 21 members of the original family, in 9 members of a second family with the same selective pattern of muscle involvement on MRI, and in a third familial case with a similar phenotype. ⚠︎

Pfeffer et al. (2014) studied 127 undiagnosed probands whose clinical presentation was compatible with myofibrillar myopathy. They used Sanger sequencing of the 119th fibronectin-3 (FN3) domain of TTN and of the kinase domain region around position p.32450. The heterozygous p.C30071R mutation was found in 5 unrelated probands (3.9%), 4 of whom had a clinical diagnosis of MFM. In 3 families, the mutation segregated with disease. Two other probands carried novel heterozygous variants in the same FN3 domain: p.P30091L (g.274436C-T) and p.N30145K (g.274599C-G). Both variants affected highly conserved residues and were absent from 400 ethnically matched control chromosomes. The p.P30091L variant was also present in the healthy 61-year-old brother of the proband. The authors therefore considered it possibly neutral, although they could not exclude a pathogenic variant with variable penetrance. Segregation of p.N30145K could not be tested. No patient had a mutation at position p.32450.

### Pathogenesis

Lange et al. (2005) found that the titin kinase domain interacts with the zinc-finger protein nbr1 through a conformation that can be induced mechanically. Nbr1 targets the ubiquitin-associated protein p62/SQSTM1 to sarcomeres. p62 in turn interacts with MuRF2, a muscle-specific E3 ligase that also binds the transactivation domain of serum response factor (SRF). Mechanical inactivity induced translocation of MuRF2 to the nucleus, which reduced nuclear SRF and repressed transcription. A human mutation in the titin kinase domain caused hereditary muscle disease by disrupting this pathway.

### Diagnosis

Pfeffer et al. (2012) recommended considering this disease in patients with adult-onset proximal or distal myopathy and early respiratory failure, even when muscle pathology is nonspecific. They suggested muscle MRI as an initial investigation and, if the findings are positive, screening of TTN. They noted that whole-exome sequencing offers a reliable and cost-effective approach, provided the gene is adequately captured. Pfeffer et al. (2014) recommended sequencing the 119th FN3 domain of TTN as part of the diagnostic workup for myofibrillar myopathy, particularly when there is early respiratory involvement and supportive muscle MRI.

### Clinical Management

Pfeffer et al. (2014) noted that MFM-titinopathy did not appear to be associated with major cardiac involvement. Because of titin's role in myocardial function, however, they recommended cardiac surveillance for all patients with TTN mutations.

### Population Genetics

Pfeffer et al. (2014) found the TTN p.C30071R mutation in 3.9% of 127 undiagnosed UK probands with suspected MFM, and in 5.5% of those with a clinical diagnosis of MFM. All 8 British families with this mutation shared a core haplotype of 171 kb, and the authors attributed the mutation to a British founder effect. The small size of the shared haplotype suggested an ancient mutation.
