### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by an expanded intronic ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat insertion in the STARD7 gene on chromosome 2.

### Description

Familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is a disorder characterized by cortical myoclonic tremor, usually beginning in the second decade of life, and overt myoclonic or generalized tonic-clonic seizures (Corbett et al., 2019). The myoclonus is predominantly distal, semicontinuous, and rhythmic. Some affected individuals also have complex partial (focal) seizures and mild cognitive impairment (Guerrini et al., 2001). Neurophysiologic studies indicate cortical hyperexcitability with features of cortical reflex myoclonus (Guerrini et al., 2001; Crompton et al., 2012).

### Nomenclature

Guerrini et al. (2001) called the disorder in their family autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME). They regarded it as a new epilepsy syndrome, separate from familial adult myoclonic epilepsy linked to chromosome 8q23.3-q24, because of the different phenotype and the different locus. De Falco et al. (2003) reported Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME) linked to the same region of chromosome 2 and suggested that BAFME and ADCME might be allelic. De Fusco et al. (2014) found no ADRA2B mutation in BAFME/FAME patients and suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME could be classified as 2 distinct clinical entities. However, Corbett et al. (2019) later found the same STARD7 repeat expansion in all chromosome 2-linked families they tested, including the ADCME families that carried the ADRA2B variant. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) described a pedigree in which 8 individuals had a nonprogressive disorder with onset between 12 and 50 years of age. All patients had predominantly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 also had complex partial seizures. Most individuals had seizures only rarely and had normal cognition, but 3 individuals with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All patients had both frontotemporal and generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Neurophysiologic study suggested that the myoclonus was cortical in origin. In 5 patients, frequency analysis showed coherence between EMG and EEG over the contralateral rolandic area at 8 to 25 Hz, with EEG activity preceding EMG activity by 8 to 15 ms. In 2 individuals, coherence with the ipsilateral EEG was consistent with transcallosal spread. The C-reflex at rest was enhanced, and somatosensory and visual evoked potentials were of high amplitude. Compared with controls, the resting motor threshold to transcranial magnetic stimulation was significantly reduced and the post-motor evoked potential silent period was significantly shortened. Guerrini et al. (2001) concluded that these findings indicated diffuse cortical hyperexcitability with a high propensity for intra- and interhemispheric spread.

Labauge et al. (2002) described a 4-generation European kindred with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy. Ten living and 3 deceased relatives were affected. Mean age at onset in the 10 living patients was 41 years (range, 30-60 years), and 8 of the 13 affected individuals had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Electrophysiologic studies in 5 patients confirmed the diagnosis.

De Falco et al. (2003) reported a clinical and electrophysiologic study of 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy.

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. Eleven members, aged 24 to 56 years (mean, 39.2 years), had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study showed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected members. Giant somatosensory evoked potential components and an enhanced long-loop reflex I were also found in 3 presymptomatic members, who developed hand tremor and upper limb myoclonus after 1.5 years of follow-up.

Suppa et al. (2009) studied an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy. Affected members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neuropsychological testing showed visuospatial impairment, which the authors attributed possibly to temporal lobe dysfunction. Neurophysiologic findings suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex with normal sensorimotor integration. In 4 affected members compared with controls, Suppa et al. (2009) found a significantly lower resting motor threshold, a shorter cortical silent period, and reduced short-interval intracortical inhibition. ⚠︎

Crompton et al. (2012) described a 6-generation kindred of European descent ascertained in New Zealand and Australia with 55 affected members. All had fine hand tremor, which typically began in adolescence (median age, 15 years; range, 4-60 years). Proximal myoclonus was present in 44 of 55 (80%) and began later than the hand tremor (median age, 17 years; range, 5-60 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range, 18-76 years). The seizures occurred around sleep onset after severe generalized myoclonus. Neurophysiologic testing confirmed features of cortical reflex myoclonus. Crompton et al. (2012) concluded that the most common phenotype in this family was a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, combined with subtle proximal myoclonus.

De Fusco et al. (2014) described a second ADCME family from Tuscany (family B), a 4-generation kindred with 5 affected members, 4 of them living. Its phenotype closely resembled that of the family reported by Guerrini et al. (2001) (family A). All patients had postural myoclonus of the hands and upper limbs. Cortical tremor was the presenting symptom in all and began between 18 and 50 years of age. All but 1 individual had epilepsy, consisting of rare generalized tonic-clonic seizures that were sometimes precipitated by visual stimuli. Two patients also had drug-resistant focal motor or complex partial seizures. One individual had borderline cognitive function, and the others had normal intelligence. All patients had generalized or focal paroxysmal EEG activity. Jerk-locked back-averaged EEG, somatosensory evoked potentials, and long-loop reflexes were consistent with cortical reflex myoclonus. There was no history of cardiovascular disease in any ADCME patient. The 2 oldest ADRA2B variant carriers developed age-related dementia. The authors noted that myoclonus often worsens in advanced age in ADCME.

Corbett et al. (2019) summarized clinical data on 22 FAME2 families. Most were Italian, and the others were of Australian/New Zealand, Sephardic Jewish (Iraqi and Israeli), South African, French/Spanish, French, and Syrian origin. The frequency of generalized tonic-clonic seizures ranged from 15 to 100% among the families. In the large Australian/New Zealand family, median age at onset fell over successive generations: 30 years in generation III (range, 14-60 years; n = 6), 17 years in generation IV (range, 8-50 years; n = 30), and 12 years in generation V (range, 4-19 years; n = 16). Corbett et al. (2019) took this as evidence of anticipation.

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) found that the pattern of inheritance in their family was autosomal dominant with high penetrance. Labauge et al. (2002) and Madia et al. (2008) also reported autosomal dominant inheritance.

### Mapping

By genomewide linkage analysis in the family with ADCME, Guerrini et al. (2001) identified a 12.4-cM critical region between markers D2S2161 and D2S1897 on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for marker D2S2175, and multipoint lod scores reached 3.74 around D2S2175. Linkage to the familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus on chromosome 8q23.3-q24 was excluded.

De Falco et al. (2003) found linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy.

In the family from Naples, Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p, with a maximum lod score of 5.9.

Madia et al. (2008) genotyped 53 affected individuals from 5 families from southern Italy, 1 of which had not been reported before. They found significant linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2, with a maximum cumulative lod score of 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388. A haplotype, '5332,' of the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113, and D2S2175 segregated with the disease in all 5 families. This indicated that the same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, was segregating in these families.

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the 2p11.1-q12.2 locus in a Spanish family with cortical tremor and epilepsy and excluded candidate genes in the region. †

In an Italian family, Suppa et al. (2009) excluded linkage to 8q24. The patients shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the pericentromeric region of chromosome 2.

In the large New Zealand/Australian family, Crompton et al. (2012) narrowed the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

### Heterogeneity

Labauge et al. (2002) excluded the 8q24 locus for familial adult myoclonic epilepsy/benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy in a European family with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy. They concluded that the disorder is genetically heterogeneous. Guerrini et al. (2001) and Suppa et al. (2009) also excluded linkage to 8q24 in Italian families. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of 2 ADCME families from Tuscany, including the family reported by Guerrini et al. (2001), De Fusco et al. (2014) identified an in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG; p.H225_L229delinsQ225_F_G_R228). The variant replaces 5 amino acids (HGGAL) with 4 new residues (QFGR) in the third intracellular loop of the receptor. It was found by direct sequencing of candidate genes in the critical region. The variant cosegregated completely with the disease in family A. It was absent from 575 unrelated Tuscan controls and from the dbSNP, Exome Variant Server, and 1000 Genomes Project databases. The 2 families shared the same disease haplotype across the entire critical region, suggesting a common ancestor. Targeted capture and next-generation sequencing of all coding regions in the critical region in 2 affected members of family A identified the ADRA2B variant as the only relevant change. In transfected cells, the mutant receptor localized normally to the plasma membrane and underwent arrestin-3-dependent internalization. However, it did not form a stable complex with spinophilin after 10 minutes of epinephrine stimulation. In Xenopus oocytes, coexpression of spinophilin did not raise the EC50 for epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling through the mutant receptor, as it did for the wildtype receptor. De Fusco et al. (2014) concluded that the variant had a gain-of-function effect. They found no ADRA2B mutations in families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy from southern Italy that mapped to the same locus.

Corbett et al. (2019) analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from affected individuals in 3 families (Australian/New Zealand, Italian, and French/Spanish) using the repeat expansion detection methods ExpansionHunter and exSTRa. In all FAME samples tested, they found an expanded ATTTT repeat with an inserted ATTTC repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene. The ATTTC repeat was not present in whole-genome sequencing data from 69 controls. Using a repeat-primed PCR assay, they detected the expansion in 137 of 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported FAME2 families. Of 24 tested family members without a FAME diagnosis, 2 carried the expansion; both were from younger generations and likely presymptomatic. Among 72 additional individuals with clinical similarity to FAME, the expansion was found in 20 of 20 familial cases from 6 families and in 1 of 52 singleton cases. It was also found in 8 of 13 individuals within the families whose diagnosis was uncertain. It did not amplify in any of 28 controls. In total, the expansion segregated in 158 of 158 typically affected individuals from 22 pedigrees. Long-read sequencing indicated that the pathogenic repeat had the structure (AAATG)n(AAAAT)n on the forward strand of chromosome 2. In 1 individual, a SMRT read contained 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats and an Oxford Nanopore read contained 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats, suggesting somatic variation in repeat size. A second individual carried 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats, a total length of 4,645 bp. The expansion was present in the 2 families carrying the ADRA2B indel reported by De Fusco et al. (2014). Corbett et al. (2019) concluded that this strongly suggested the ADRA2B allele is not causative. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In a preliminary genotype-phenotype analysis of 5 southern Italian families, Madia et al. (2008) found only minor clinical differences among patients carrying the founder disease haplotype. They suggested that expressivity of the founder mutation is not markedly influenced by other factors.

### Pathogenesis

In patient-derived fibroblasts, Corbett et al. (2019) found no difference in STARD7 transcript or protein levels between patients and controls. RNA-seq data contained no reads with ATTTC repeats, despite robust expression of STARD7. The authors suggested that the repeat sequence itself is pathogenic, independent of any effect on the coding sequence of the gene. They noted that STARD7 expression might be regulated differently in nonproliferating brain cells.

### Diagnosis

Crompton et al. (2012) noted that the most common phenotype in their large family, a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor with subtle proximal myoclonus, could cause the disorder to be underrecognized. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) found that their repeat-primed PCR assay with an ATTTC repeat primer had a sensitivity of 100% for FAME in all families with linkage or suggestive linkage to chromosome 2. Because the endogenous ATTTT repeat varies naturally in length, it was not suitable for mutation screening.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found evidence that a common founder haplotype was shared by 5 FAME2 families from southern Italy. Corbett et al. (2019) noted that FAME2 is the most frequently observed linked locus among Caucasian individuals with the disorder, whereas linkage to chromosome 8 has so far been limited to Asian individuals.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2**  
STARD7, ATTTC INS/EXP [(AAATG)n(AAAAT)n, intron 1 ATTTC repeat insertion with ATTTT repeat expansion]

In 158 of 158 typically affected individuals from 22 FAME2 pedigrees, Corbett et al. (2019) identified an expanded ATTTT pentanucleotide repeat with an inserted, expanded ATTTC repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene. The pedigrees included 16 previously reported families and were recruited worldwide. The expansion was first detected by repeat expansion analysis of whole-genome sequencing data and then screened by repeat-primed PCR. It was absent from 69 control genomes and did not amplify in 28 control samples. Long-read sequencing showed the structure (AAATG)n(AAAAT)n on the forward strand of chromosome 2. Observed repeat counts included 274 to 345 AAATG and 387 to 390 AAAAT repeats in 1 patient, and 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats (4,645 bp) in another. The expansion did not alter STARD7 mRNA or protein levels in patient fibroblasts. It was also present in the 2 Tuscan ADCME families that carried an ADRA2B indel. ⚠︎
