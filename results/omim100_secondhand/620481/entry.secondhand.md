### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding thrombopoietin (THPO).

### Description

Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is an autosomal recessive disorder of hematopoiesis. It presents with severe thrombocytopenia and few or no megakaryocytes in the bone marrow. In some patients it progresses during childhood to trilineage bone marrow failure with pancytopenia (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018; Capaci et al., 2023). The disorder results from defective production or activity of thrombopoietin. Unlike CAMT due to MPL mutations, serum THPO levels are not markedly raised. Patients respond to thrombopoietin receptor agonists but not to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018; Capaci et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (2013) reported 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with idiopathic autosomal recessive aplastic anemia. Both parents and a heterozygous sib had reduced platelet counts, and a sib with the wildtype sequence had a normal platelet count. Pecci et al. (2018) later noted that bone marrow aplasia had been recognized at ages 16 and 28 years in the 2 patients and that 2 other sibs had died of aplastic anemia in adolescence.

Seo et al. (2017) reported 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families with severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to trilineage bone marrow failure. Four of the children received hematopoietic stem cell transplants. All 4 had poor graft function, with persistent severe cytopenias even after repeated transplants from different donors.

Pecci et al. (2018) reported 3 affected children of first-cousin parents of Egyptian origin. The proband, a boy, presented at 3.5 years of age with ecchymotic patches. He had preauricular skin tags and a left divergent squint. Blood counts showed very severe thrombocytopenia (platelets 3 x 10(9)/l), hyporegenerative anemia, and severe neutropenia, and platelet size was normal. The bone marrow was hypocellular with very rare immature megakaryocytes, and the karyotype was normal. Fanconi anemia and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria were excluded, and he was first diagnosed with idiopathic aplastic anemia. He went on to have recurrent major hemorrhages (epistaxis, gum bleeding, and rectal bleeding) and recurrent severe infections (febrile neutropenia or otitis media) that required multiple platelet and red cell transfusions. His brother had severe isolated thrombocytopenia (26 x 10(9)/l) at 7 months, with markedly decreased bone marrow megakaryocytes. From 1.6 years of age the brother had recurrent spontaneous bleeding that required platelet transfusions. A younger sister developed a hematoma at a vaccination site at 2 months of age and had anemia and severe thrombocytopenia. Blood cell counts were normal in the heterozygous parents. Pecci et al. (2018) noted that thrombocytopenia was the only feature present in all 3 patients. The trilineage aplasia in the oldest sib suggested that the hematologic picture worsens with age.

Capaci et al. (2023) reported a boy of Muslim Palestinian descent born to first-cousin parents. He presented at 3.5 years of age with easy bruising and thrombocytopenia (21 x 10(9)/L), with a normal mean platelet volume. His platelet count shortly after birth had been 70 x 10(9)/L. At 2 years of age he had severe thrombocytopenia (20 x 10(9)/L) and needed a platelet transfusion before meatotomy for urethral stenosis. Apart from mild occasional neutropenia, he had no other cytopenias. Two bone marrow biopsies showed normocellular marrow with severe hypoplasia of megakaryocytes, a few small immature forms, and mild dyserythropoiesis. Fanconi anemia was excluded, and the bone marrow karyotype was normal (46,XY). His heterozygous mother had mild thrombocytopenia that worsened during each pregnancy; her platelet counts were stable at about 80 x 10(9)/L and she had no bleeding. A heterozygous younger sib had platelet counts of 130 and 160 x 10(9)/L in infancy.

### Biochemical Features

Seo et al. (2017) found that the THPO mutations in their patients led to a lack of THPO in the patients' serum.

Pecci et al. (2018) measured serum THPO before romiplostim treatment and found normal levels: 10.9 pg/ml in the proband and 14.1 pg/ml in his brother. In 50 healthy subjects the range was 6.9 to 54.4 pg/ml. Serum THPO is markedly increased in bone marrow aplasia, including CAMT caused by MPL mutations, so the authors considered these levels inappropriately low. ⚠︎

Capaci et al. (2023) found a serum THPO concentration of 126 pg/mL in their proband, against a laboratory reference range of 15 to 80 pg/mL for children younger than 15 years. They considered this much lower than expected given the severely reduced megakaryocyte and platelet mass.

### Inheritance

Dasouki et al. (2013) found autosomal recessive inheritance of aplastic anemia in a Micronesian family, with mild thrombocytopenia in heterozygous family members. Pecci et al. (2018) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance of CAMT2: in a consanguineous family, the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for a THPO mutation and the unaffected parents were heterozygous. In the consanguineous family reported by Capaci et al. (2023), the mother and a sib were heterozygous for the familial mutation and had asymptomatic mild thrombocytopenia.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with autosomal recessive aplastic anemia, Dasouki et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.112C-T transition in the THPO gene. The change results in an arg38-to-cys (R38C) substitution, also called R17C when the 21-amino acid signal peptide is excluded, in the receptor-binding domain. In silico modeling predicted that the extra cysteine might disrupt normal disulfide bonding and impair receptor binding. In functional assays, medium containing the mutant protein had a 2- to 3-fold reduced ability to sustain proliferation of THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cells. Both parents and a sib were heterozygous and had reduced platelet counts. The authors concluded that R17C is a partial loss-of-function allele.

In 5 patients from 3 unrelated families with bone marrow failure, Seo et al. (2017) used exome and targeted sequencing to identify homozygous mutations in the THPO gene: R99W in affected children from 2 families and R157X in the affected child from the third family. Both mutations resulted in a lack of THPO in patient serum. The authors concluded that biallelic loss-of-function mutations in THPO cause bone marrow failure that does not respond to transplant because the mechanism lies outside the hematopoietic cells.

In the proband of an Egyptian family with CAMT, Pecci et al. (2018) found no variants in the MPL gene. Sequencing of THPO identified a homozygous c.355C-T transition, resulting in an arg119-to-cys (R119C) substitution. The 2 affected sibs were also homozygous, and the parents were heterozygous. The variant was not found in the dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, or ExAC databases. R119 lies in helix C of the MPL-binding domain and is conserved in orthologs and in human erythropoietin. In transfected HEK293T cells the mutant protein was expressed and was as stable as wildtype. However, its concentration in the culture supernatant was about 23-fold lower than wildtype, indicating defective secretion; the R38C mutant behaved the same way. At equal concentrations, R119C THPO had about 45 to 53% of wildtype activity in UT7-TPO proliferation assays. It also caused defective activation of STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT downstream of MPL.

In a Palestinian boy with CAMT born to consanguineous parents, Capaci et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.-323C-T transition in the THPO gene. In transcript variant 1, this change lies 45 nucleotides upstream of the transcription start site. The parents and a younger sib were heterozygous, and 2 healthy sibs were homozygous for the wildtype allele. The variant was listed in dbSNP but not in gnomAD, and it lies in a stretch of nucleotides conserved across species. No variants were found in MPL, MECOM, HOXA11, RBM8A, or genes associated with Fanconi anemia or other inherited bone marrow failure syndromes. In HEK293T cells the mutation reduced luciferase reporter activity by almost 80%. It abolished predicted binding sites for the transcription factors ETS1 and STAT4, and chromatin immunoprecipitation showed reduced binding of these factors to the mutant promoter. Knockdown of ETS1 and STAT4 reduced THPO expression in HepG2 liver cells.

### Diagnosis

Pecci et al. (2018) and Capaci et al. (2023) suggested that measuring serum THPO could help distinguish CAMT due to THPO mutations from CAMT due to MPL mutations and from other bone marrow failure syndromes. They noted that a normal serum THPO level in a patient with amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia strongly suggests a THPO defect. Capaci et al. (2023) recommended that molecular analysis of THPO, including its regulatory regions, be part of the routine diagnostic workup for suspected CAMT.

### Clinical Management

Seo et al. (2017) found that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation did not restore hematopoiesis in patients with THPO mutations. The 2 surviving patients improved in all 3 blood cell lineages after treatment with a THPO receptor agonist.

Pecci et al. (2018) treated all 3 affected sibs with romiplostim. The proband started at 4.4 years of age on 1 microgram/kg/week for 3 months, then 4 microgram/kg monthly. He improved in all 3 lineages, his mean neutrophil count rose from 0.7 to 1.7 x 10(9)/l, and he had no further spontaneous bleeding, infections, or transfusions over 6.5 years of follow-up. His brother, given 4 microgram/kg monthly, had platelet counts of 30 to 100 x 10(9)/l, stopped bleeding, and remained transfusion-independent over 6.3 years. Their sister also responded, with improved platelet counts and hemoglobin. No side effects were seen. Before romiplostim, the proband had not responded to cyclosporine A or oxymetholone.

Capaci et al. (2023) treated their patient with eltrombopag, first at 1.4 mg/kg/day and then at 2.8 mg/kg/day. His platelet count rose to 125 x 10(9)/L and his easy bruising resolved.
