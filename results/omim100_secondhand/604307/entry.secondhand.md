### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cataract-2 (CTRCT2), which can take several forms, with or without microcornea, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gamma-C-crystallin gene (CRYGC) on chromosome 2q33-q35.

### Description

CTRCT2 is an autosomal dominant congenital cataract caused by heterozygous mutation in the CRYGC gene. The lens opacities vary in form. Reported types include Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus, zonular pulverulent cataract, lamellar cataract, and nuclear cataract. Some families also have microcornea, nystagmus, and amblyopia (Lubsen et al., 1987; Ren et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2009; Guo et al., 2012). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family in which heterozygotes had a hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus.

Scott et al. (1994) described a 7-generation family in which 48 of 138 individuals were known to have autosomal dominant congenital cataracts of the pulverulent type. The authors examined 53 family members, and 28 of them had congenital cataracts. Of these patients, 19 eyes (8 right eyes and 11 left eyes) were unilateral and 9 were bilateral. On detailed slit-lamp examination, the clinically unaffected eye of patients with unilateral cataract showed no lens opacity. Severity ranged from a subtle unilateral zonular cataract with 20/20 visual acuity, through bilateral inner fetal nuclear pulverulent opacities with 20/16 visual acuity in both eyes, to dense unilateral and bilateral nuclear cataracts that required early surgery. When historic data on the unexamined members were included, the family had 48 affected members: 28 unilateral, 17 bilateral, and 3 obligate carriers who were not examined. Scott et al. (1994) noted that hereditary cataracts are typically symmetric, and that this family showed apparently random expression of the gene as either unilateral or bilateral cataract. ⚠︎

Ren et al. (2000) studied a previously described 7-generation family with 30 members affected by highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) described a family with several members who had primary congenital cataract of the nuclear type.

Zhang et al. (2009) reported a 4-generation Chinese family with 6 affected members. The family lived in a relatively isolated region of northern China. Affected individuals had bilateral congenital nuclear cataracts, with a central nuclear opacity involving the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nucleus of the lens. They also had congenital small eyeballs and corneas about 9 to 10 mm in diameter. No other ocular or systemic abnormalities were present. Every affected individual had the same poor visual acuity of hand movement in front of the eye, and all had nystagmus and amblyopia.

Li et al. (2012) studied a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract.

Guo et al. (2012) reported a 4-generation Chinese family with 6 affected members who had isolated bilateral nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The nuclear opacities involved the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nuclei, and corneal diameter was about 9 mm in all affected individuals. The proband was diagnosed with bilateral nuclear cataract and microcornea at age 8 years and underwent iridectomy on both eyes at that age. At age 38, his left eye underwent phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation; best corrected visual acuity was 0.08 in the right eye and counting fingers at 30 cm in the left eye. Two other affected members were first diagnosed at 6 months and 1 year of age. Intraocular pressure was normal, and all patients had nystagmus and amblyopia. The family had no history of related systemic abnormalities.

### Inheritance

The cataract in the family of Lubsen et al. (1987) occurred in heterozygotes. Scott et al. (1994) and Ren et al. (2000) described autosomal dominant transmission in a 7-generation family. Zhang et al. (2009) supported autosomal dominant inheritance by showing affected individuals in each of 4 generations and male-to-male transmission. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also seen in the families of Li et al. (2012) and Guo et al. (2012).

### Mapping

Lubsen et al. (1987) found 3 haplotypes (Q, R, and S) defined by polymorphic TaqI sites within the gamma-crystallin gene cluster in the Dutch and English population. They detected a fourth haplotype (P) in a family with Coppock-like cataract. Haplotype P was present in all affected family members and only in affected members. This showed close linkage between the cataract locus and the gamma-crystallin gene cluster (maximum lod score of 7.58 at theta = 0).

In a 7-generation family with autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts, Ren et al. (2000) localized the disorder to a 19-cM interval on chromosome 2q33-q35 that includes the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. Maximum lod scores were 4.56 (theta = 0.02) with D2S157, 3.66 (theta = 0.12) with D2S72, and 3.57 (theta = 0.052) with CRYG.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) obtained a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.29 at marker D2S325 (theta = 0.0). This marker closely flanks the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. Linkage and haplotype analyses placed the disease gene in a 19.04-cM region at 2q32.3-q35, bounded by D2S117 and D2S2382.

### Molecular Genetics

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) cloned and characterized the gamma-D-crystallin gene and the gamma-E-crystallin pseudogene from the chromosome carrying Coppock-like cataract. The gamma-D gene worked the same as its wildtype allele. The pseudogene, however, had a cluster of sequence changes in and around its TATA box. Together these changes raised the activity of the pseudogene promoter 10-fold, bringing its expression to 30% of that of the gamma-D gene. The authors proposed that reactivation of the pseudogene, with overexpression of a predicted 6-kD N-terminal gamma-crystallin fragment, could cause the Coppock-like cataract.

Heon et al. (1999) found that the variant in the gamma-E-crystallin pseudogene previously reported as the Coppock-like cataract mutation was a polymorphism, present in 23% of their control population. Further analysis of the original Coppock-like cataract family showed a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene: a thr5-to-pro (T5P) substitution. The mutation was not seen in a large control population. ⚠︎

In a 7-generation family with autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts linked to 2q33-q35, Ren et al. (2000) sequenced the pseudo-gamma-E-crystallin promoter region. The results suggested that activation of the pseudogene was unlikely to cause the cataracts in this family. Base changes in the TATA box were also found in unaffected controls. Sequencing of the gamma-D-crystallin gene showed no abnormalities. In exon 2 of the CRYGC gene, however, the authors found a 5-bp duplication on one allele of every affected family member. The duplication was absent from unaffected family members and controls. It disrupts the reading frame and was predicted to produce an unstable gamma-C-crystallin with 38 amino acids of the first 'Greek key' motif followed by 52 random amino acids.

Santhiya et al. (2002) reported novel mutations in the gamma-crystallin genes that cause autosomal dominant congenital cataracts. These included an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution in the CRYGC gene, which was associated with lamellar cataract. †

In affected members of a family with nuclear congenital cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) sequenced the CRYGA-CRYGD gene cluster and identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an R168W substitution. Healthy family members and 170 unrelated controls had a normal sequence. The authors concluded that R168W, which was listed as a SNP in the NCBI database, is a disease-causing mutation.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) sequenced the CRYGA-CRYGD gene cluster and identified a c.470G-A change in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene. The change was predicted to produce a stop codon at tryptophan-157 (W157X). It cosegregated with cataract in the family and was not found in 100 normal controls.

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) sequenced candidate genes and identified a heterozygous c.385G-T change in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. Molecular modeling and spectroscopic studies indicated that the mutation impaired the tertiary structure of the protein by modifying the hydrogen-bonding network in the C-terminal domain. The mutation markedly decreased the thermal stability of gamma-C-crystallin. It also substantially increased the protein's tendency to aggregate when stored at high concentration or exposed to heat or UV irradiation.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) sequenced 9 candidate genes and identified a c.471G-A transition in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a W157X substitution. The change was not found in unaffected family members or in 100 controls. The mutant protein was predicted to lack 18 C-terminal amino acids and part of the fourth Greek key motif. Guo et al. (2012) noted that the W157X change had previously been reported in another Chinese family with nuclear cataracts and microcornea, but resulting from a different nucleotide change (c.470G-A).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Zhang et al. (2009) noted that the opacities associated with CRYGC mutations, including Coppock-like (T5P), variable zonular pulverulent (5-bp duplication), lamellar and nuclear (R168W), and nuclear (W157X) cataracts, involve the lens nucleus. They considered this consistent with the expression of CRYGC mainly in the lens nucleus. Guo et al. (2012) observed that the 2 different nucleotide changes producing W157X (c.470G-A and c.471G-A) gave similar phenotypes of nuclear cataract and microcornea.

### Animal Model

Li et al. (2012) showed that the CRYGC G129C mutant caused vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens in zebrafish, reproducing the cataract phenotype seen in humans.

### History

The linkage of Coppock-like cataract to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster reported by Lubsen et al. (1987) was the first genetic evidence that a lesion in a crystallin gene could be the primary cause of a human cataract. Brakenhoff et al. (1994) proposed that activation of the gamma-E-crystallin pseudogene caused the Coppock-like cataract. Heon et al. (1999) later found that the pseudogene variant was a common polymorphism and identified a CRYGC missense mutation in the original Coppock-like cataract family. Ren et al. (2000) also concluded that pseudogene activation was unlikely to cause cataracts in their family. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CATARACT 2, COPPOCK-LIKE**  
CRYGC, THR5PRO [c.13A>C; p.Thr5Pro; T5P]

In the original family with Coppock-like cataract, which had previously been linked to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, Heon et al. (1999) identified a thr5-to-pro (T5P) missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The mutation was not seen in a large control population. ⚠︎

**.0002 CATARACT 2, ZONULAR PULVERULENT**  
CRYGC, 5-BP DUP, NT119 [c.119_123dupGCGGC; p.Cys42AlafsTer63]

In a 7-generation family with highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts, Ren et al. (2000) identified a 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The duplication was present on one allele of every affected family member and absent from unaffected family members and controls. It disrupts the reading frame and was predicted to produce an unstable protein with 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids.

**.0003 CATARACT 2, MULTIPLE TYPES**  
CRYGC, ARG168TRP [c.502C>T; p.Arg168Trp; R168W]

Santhiya et al. (2002) reported an arg168-to-trp (R168W) mutation in the CRYGC gene in association with autosomal dominant congenital lamellar cataract. †

In affected members of a family with nuclear congenital cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an R168W substitution. The variant was absent from healthy family members and 170 unrelated controls. The authors concluded that R168W is disease-causing rather than a SNP, and that it shows phenotypic heterogeneity.

**.0004 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR, WITH MICROCORNEA**  
CRYGC, TRP157TER, 470G-A [c.470G>A; p.Trp157Ter; W157X]

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a c.470G-A change in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with cataract in the family and was not found in 100 normal controls. It was predicted to truncate the protein at the end of the fourth Greek key motif.

**.0005 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR**  
CRYGC, GLY129CYS [c.385G>T; p.Gly129Cys]

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.385G-T change in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. The mutant protein had impaired tertiary structure, markedly decreased thermal stability, and an increased tendency to aggregate under stress. In zebrafish, the mutant caused lens vacuoles and incomplete lens denucleation, reproducing the cataract phenotype.

**.0006 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR, WITH MICROCORNEA**

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al.
