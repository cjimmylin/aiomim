## SYNDACTYLY, TYPE III; SDTY3

*Alternative titles; symbols*

SYNDACTYLY OF FINGERS IV AND V  
RING AND LITTLE FINGER SYNDACTYLY

### TEXT

This entry describes type III syndactyly (SDTY3), an autosomal dominant malformation in which the ring and little fingers are joined. Type III syndactyly can occur on its own or as part of oculodentodigital dysplasia (ODDD). A family with type III syndactyly was linked to the ODDD region on chromosome 6q22-q24, which contains the GJA1 gene. † ⚠︎

### Description

Type III syndactyly is a malformation of the hands in which the ring (fourth) and little (fifth) fingers are joined. It is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait and its expression varies (Johnston and Kirby, 1955; McKiernan and McCann, 1993; De Smet et al., 1994). The fifth finger often has a short middle phalanx (brachymesophalangia) (De Smet et al., 1994). Type III syndactyly can occur on its own or as part of oculodentodigital dysplasia (Gladwin et al., 1997; Paznekas et al., 2003). †

### Nomenclature

Type III syndactyly is also called the Johnston-Kirby type, after the family reported by Johnston and Kirby (1955). †

### Clinical Features

Johnston and Kirby (1955) reported a family with syndactyly of the ring and little fingers. It was one of the largest fully described pedigrees, with 7 affected males and 7 affected females over 5 generations. †

Opjordsmoen and Nyberg-Hansen (1980) reported a family from northern Norway with a hereditary neurologic disorder made up of spastic paraplegia, neurogenic bladder disturbance and syndactyly. Nine of 22 family members across 3 generations had these features. The bladder disturbance was an incomplete supranuclear bladder paresis (uninhibited neurogenic bladder). It was the main complaint and appeared early in the disease. The authors thought the family probably had an unusual form of hereditary spastic paraplegia.

Brueton et al. (1990) described a family with type III syndactyly whose facial appearance resembled that of oculodentodigital dysplasia. None of the other characteristic findings of oculodentodigital dysplasia were present. The authors discussed whether type III syndactyly and oculodentodigital dysplasia are separate genetic entities or part of one disease spectrum.

Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) reported a newborn boy with complete cutaneous syndactyly of fingers 3 to 5 on the left hand and fingers 4 and 5 on the right hand. His mother and maternal grandmother had been born with the same hand malformation. The mother had a thin nose and small teeth, and her eyes were normal. The grandmother's teeth had been very small, with enamel hypoplasia, and her eyes were normal. The authors considered that the family showed the variable expression of oculodentodigital dysplasia.

McKiernan and McCann (1993) reported a pedigree with 14 cases of familial syndactyly of the ring and little fingers. Three of the patients were treated surgically in the authors' unit. A fourth had been treated elsewhere earlier by the senior author. Clinical study showed that expression varied.

De Smet et al. (1994) described 5 new cases of isolated syndactyly of the ring and little fingers. A short middle phalanx of the fifth finger (brachymesophalangia) was a frequent associated finding. The authors stated that surgery is needed at a young age.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of type III syndactyly was shown by the 5-generation family of Johnston and Kirby (1955). It was also supported by the families reported by Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993), McKiernan and McCann (1993) and De Smet et al. (1994). Opjordsmoen and Nyberg-Hansen (1980) considered the inheritance autosomal dominant in their family with spastic paraplegia, neurogenic bladder disturbance and syndactyly. † ⚠︎

### Mapping

Gladwin et al. (1997) ran a genome-wide search in families with oculodentodigital syndrome and found linkage to polymorphic markers on chromosome 6q (pairwise Zmax = 9.37; theta = 0.001). They also analyzed a large family with type III syndactyly and atypical facial features. This analysis suggested that the locus for isolated type III syndactyly lies in the same region, 6q22-q24.

### Heterogeneity

Whether isolated type III syndactyly is a separate entity from oculodentodigital dysplasia has been discussed. Brueton et al. (1990) raised the question of whether the two are separate genetic entities or a single disease spectrum. Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) took their family with type III syndactyly and mild dental and nasal features as evidence that oculodentodigital dysplasia varies in expression. In contrast, De Smet et al. (1994) described type III syndactyly as an isolated malformation. Gladwin et al. (1997) noted that type III syndactyly occurs as part of oculodentodigital syndrome but has also been reported on its own. Their linkage data suggested that the two map to the same region of chromosome 6q.

### Molecular Genetics

Paznekas et al. (2003) noted that the connexin-43 gene, GJA1, lies on chromosome 6q22-q23 within the candidate region for oculodentodigital dysplasia. They found GJA1 mutations in all 17 families with oculodentodigital dysplasia that they screened: 16 different missense mutations and 1 codon duplication. They stated that type III syndactyly can occur in some cases of oculodentodigital dysplasia.

Richardson et al. (2004) reported that expression of Gja1 correlates with the phenotype seen in oculodentodigital syndrome/type III syndactyly.
