## SYNDACTYLY, TYPE III; SDTY3

*Alternative titles; symbols*

SYNDACTYLY OF FINGERS IV AND V  
RING AND LITTLE FINGER SYNDACTYLY

### TEXT

This entry describes type III syndactyly (SDTY3), an autosomal dominant hand malformation with syndactyly of the ring and little fingers. Linkage to chromosome 6q22-q24, the region containing the oculodentodigital dysplasia locus, has been suggested, but the papers cited here do not identify a causal gene for isolated type III syndactyly.

### Description

Type III syndactyly is syndactyly of the ring and little fingers (Johnston and Kirby, 1955). It is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with variable expression (McKiernan and McCann, 1993). Brachymesophalangia of the fifth finger is often present (De Smet et al., 1994). Type III syndactyly also occurs as part of oculodentodigital dysplasia (ODD), and how the isolated form relates to ODD has been discussed (Brueton et al., 1990; Schrander-Stumpel et al., 1993; Gladwin et al., 1997). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Johnston and Kirby (1955) reported syndactyly of the ring and little finger.

Opjordsmoen and Nyberg-Hansen (1980) reported a family from northern Norway with a hereditary neurologic disorder made up of spastic paraplegia, neurogenic bladder disturbances, and syndactyly. Nine of 22 family members across 3 generations had these features. The bladder disturbance was an incomplete supranuclear bladder paresis (uninhibited neurogenic bladder). It was the main complaint and appeared early in the disease. The authors thought the family probably had an unusual form of hereditary spastic paraplegia.

Brueton et al. (1990) described a family with type III syndactyly and a facial appearance resembling that of oculodentodigital dysplasia. None of the other characteristic findings of oculodentodigital dysplasia were present. The authors discussed the relationship between type III syndactyly and oculodentodigital dysplasia.

Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) reported a newborn boy with complete cutaneous syndactyly of fingers 3 to 5 on the left hand and fingers 4 and 5 on the right hand. His mother and maternal grandmother had been born with the same hand malformation. The mother had a thin nose and small teeth, and her eyes were normal. The grandmother had had very small teeth with enamel hypoplasia, and her eyes were normal. The authors considered that the family showed the variable expression of oculodentodigital dysplasia.

McKiernan and McCann (1993) reported a pedigree with 14 cases of familial syndactyly of the ring and little finger. Three were treated surgically in their unit, and a fourth had earlier been treated elsewhere by the senior author. Clinical study showed variable phenotypic expression.

De Smet et al. (1994) described 5 new cases of isolated syndactyly of the ring and small fingers. Brachymesophalangia of the fifth finger was a frequent association.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of type III syndactyly was described in the families reported by Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993), McKiernan and McCann (1993), and De Smet et al. (1994). Opjordsmoen and Nyberg-Hansen (1980) considered the inheritance of spastic paraplegia with neurogenic bladder and syndactyly in their family to be autosomal dominant.

### Mapping

Gladwin et al. (1997) used a genome-wide search to map oculodentodigital syndrome to chromosome 6q22-q24 (maximum pairwise lod score = 9.37 at theta = 0.001). Analysis of a large family with type III syndactyly and atypical facial features further suggested that isolated type III syndactyly also maps to this region.

### Heterogeneity

Type III syndactyly can occur as part of oculodentodigital dysplasia. Paznekas et al. (2003) found mutations in the GJA1 gene (connexin-43), located on chromosome 6q22-q23, in all 17 families with oculodentodigital dysplasia that they screened.

### Pathogenesis

Richardson et al. (2004) reported that expression of Gja1 correlates with the phenotype seen in oculodentodigital syndrome/type III syndactyly.

### Clinical Management

McKiernan and McCann (1993) treated some affected family members surgically. De Smet et al. (1994) stated that surgery is required at a young age.
