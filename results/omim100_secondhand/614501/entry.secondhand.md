### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures is an autosomal recessive disorder reported in the Old Order Amish. Its cardinal features are hypotonia, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, and a characteristic craniofacial appearance with skull abnormalities. Seizures begin in infancy or childhood and are often intractable. Some patients have congenital heart defects, upper airway abnormalities, feeding difficulties, and other congenital anomalies (Puffenberger et al., 2012; Ammous et al., 2021).

### Nomenclature

Puffenberger et al. (2012) called the disorder 'symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia.' Ammous et al. (2021) used the term 'SNIP1-related disorder.'

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described Old Order Amish children with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia, including 2 affected brothers. Affected neonates were hypotonic and fed poorly. Dysmorphic features that evolved over time included a bulbous nose, wide mouth and tongue, broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. Affected children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak. Many kept use of their hands to scoot, maneuver a wheelchair, or gesture. Some children responded behaviorally to language but did not engage socially or follow verbal commands. On examination, the children were typically subdued and had strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, a thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities, hypomyelination, and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular skull contour. Multifocal spike-wave discharges from central, occipital, and temporal regions typically began by 6 months of age. They were accompanied by focal or generalized seizures, which could manifest as dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, or generalized tonic-clonic events. A single patient could have multiple intractable seizure types. Some patients had subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, or hydrocele.

Ammous et al. (2021) clinically evaluated 35 of 51 identified affected individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families (19 males and 16 females, aged 1 month to 26 years). This group included 2 individuals previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). Six children died between ages 9 months and 11 years from infection, sudden cardiopulmonary arrest, or accidental drowning. Phenotypic variation was substantial, including within sibships. All individuals had severe global developmental delay and were nonverbal. All also had hypotonia, hyporeflexia, seizures, feeding difficulties, abnormal skull shape, high-arched palate, and a wide mouth with a cupid's bow upper lip. Craniofacial features included midface hypoplasia, a wide mouth with downturned corners and thin cupid's bow upper lip, large tongue, microretrognathia, malocclusion, a small upturned bulbous nose, long palpebral fissures, and proptosis in those with craniosynostosis. Three patients had Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate. Skull abnormalities ranged from normocephaly to severe multisuture craniosynostosis with a cloverleaf skull, seen in 5 individuals. They also included an irregular skull surface with variable calvarial thickness and bony dysplasia. Other features included brachydactyly, broad thumbs, single palmar crease, clubfoot, pes planus, toenail hypoplasia, and pectus excavatum. ⚠︎

In the patients of Ammous et al. (2021), seizures were focal or generalized (myoclonic, absence, tonic-clonic), intractable, and began in infancy or childhood. EEG was abnormal in all individuals assessed but showed no characteristic pattern. In some infants, seizures first presented as apnea or breath-holding spells, and subclinical seizures were detected in 6 individuals. Febrile illness, sleep deprivation, and hypoglycemic episodes could trigger or worsen seizures. No antiepileptic drug consistently controlled seizures. Some individuals had severe developmental regression after status epilepticus, and 2 were left with permanent hemiparesis. Individuals identified in late childhood or adolescence were often diagnosed with mixed-tone spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy. Behavioral problems were present in 75% and included irritability, sleep/wake dysregulation, autism spectrum disorder, and attention deficit disorder. Most affected individuals walked independently (age range 3 to 10 years), and some communicated with signs, gestures, and sounds. Neuroimaging was abnormal in all 18 individuals imaged. Findings included hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, hypomyelination, irregular cortical ribbon, Chiari malformation, absence of the septum pellucidum, hypoplastic optic nerves, and septooptic dysplasia. One individual had a cerebral arteriovenous malformation.

Ammous et al. (2021) found congenital heart defects in 60% of their patients. These included hypoplastic left heart syndrome, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation, aortic root dilatation, atrial and ventricular septal defects, patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary artery stenosis, and mitral valve regurgitation. Twelve percent developed cardiomyopathy (left ventricular noncompaction). Upper airway abnormalities in the neonatal period were common. These included laryngomalacia, pharyngomalacia, and subglottic stenosis, which caused a weak cry, stridor, and apnea and occasionally required tracheostomy. Poor feeding in the neonatal period was universal, and many patients needed a gastrostomy tube. Other features included gastroesophageal reflux, paraesophageal hernia, intestinal malrotation, hepatomegaly, umbilical and inguinal hernias, intrauterine growth retardation, failure to thrive, and spinal abnormalities (sacral dimple, tethered cord, scoliosis). Some patients had hypoglycemia, hypothyroidism, or hypothermia. Others had strabismus, horizontal nystagmus, myopia, chronic otitis media, or hearing loss; 21% failed the newborn hearing screen.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of this disorder in the Old Order Amish families reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012) and Ammous et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Ammous et al. (2021) found that all parents of affected individuals were heterozygous for the SNIP1 mutation.

### Mapping

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed autozygosity mapping with 10K SNP arrays in 6 affected Old Order Amish individuals. They mapped the disorder to a shared 6.6-Mb homozygous block on chromosome 1 containing 126 genes.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed genomewide SNP genotyping in 4 affected individuals from 2 Amish nuclear families. They identified a single shared region of homozygosity larger than 1 Mb: a 1.65-Mb region on chromosome 1p34.3 between markers rs6667450 and rs10889902 that contained 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in 3 affected Old Order Amish individuals from 2 sibships. Within the mapped region on chromosome 1, they identified a single novel homozygous variant, a c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. PolyPhen-2 predicted it to be probably damaging. When transiently overexpressed in mIMCD3 cells, wildtype mouse Snip1 localized to the nucleus in a punctate pattern. The corresponding mouse mutant, Snip1 glu353-to-gly, also localized to the nucleus but had a more aggregated appearance. On Western blot, the mutant protein was unstable, with band density 84.9 +/- 9.6% lower than wildtype, although transcript levels were comparable to wildtype.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed exome sequencing in 2 affected Amish individuals. The only potentially pathogenic variant they found in the 1p34.3 critical interval was the homozygous E366G mutation in exon 4 of SNIP1, which they confirmed by dideoxy sequencing. Targeted testing confirmed homozygosity for the mutation in 35 affected individuals. All parents were heterozygous, and all unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. The glu366 residue is highly conserved and lies close to the forkhead-associated domain at the C terminus. No unaffected individual in an in-house Amish control database of more than 5,000 individuals was homozygous for the variant.

### Pathogenesis

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-transcriptome sequencing of blood from 6 affected individuals in 4 sibships and compared them with sex-matched controls. They identified 75 significantly upregulated and 109 significantly downregulated genes. The most overrepresented pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, in which PARD3 was upregulated and F11R and RPS27A were downregulated. Of the most altered genes with a clinical phenotype association, 24 had a previously established association with neurologic disease. Five of these, including ROBO1, were associated with seizures, and ROBO1 was the most upregulated gene.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) recommended management aimed at preventing life-threatening complications and optimizing psychomotor development. Their recommendations included elective gastrostomy tube placement to support growth and limit pulmonary aspiration. They advised careful optimization of anticonvulsant drugs to treat apnea in infancy, maintain seizure control, and prevent status epilepticus and the developmental regression that follows it. They also advised early EEG in infants with suspected apneic episodes, neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis, and consideration of an upper gastrointestinal study, head ultrasound, and renal ultrasound. Further recommendations were monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism, yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments, and early supportive therapies. Behavioral therapy was beneficial in older children with autism spectrum disorder, and melatonin had been used successfully in some children with sleep dysregulation.

### Population Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) identified 5 carriers of the SNIP1 E366G mutation among 203 Old Order Amish controls (2.5%). In the same report, the population-specific allele frequency was given as 1.48% (6 of 406 chromosomes).

Ammous et al. (2021) found that the E366G mutation had an allele frequency of 0.5% in the Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in the Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish. In gnomAD (v3.1), its allele frequency was 0.001%, with 11 heterozygotes (10 Amish) and no homozygotes. They identified 51 affected Old Order Amish individuals in 27 sibships. Of these, 49 were confirmed to be homozygous for the mutation. The other 2 were not genotyped but had characteristic features and an affected sib who was confirmed homozygous.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, CRANIOFACIAL ABNORMALITIES, AND SEIZURES**  
SNIP1, GLU366GLY [NM_024700.4:c.1097A>G; p.Glu366Gly]

Puffenberger et al. (2012) studied 3 Old Order Amish patients from 2 sibships with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Using exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. The variant was the only novel homozygous variant in the mapped interval and was predicted to be probably damaging. The corresponding mouse mutant protein (glu353-to-gly) showed an aggregated nuclear distribution and was unstable in transfected mIMCD3 cells. The mutation was found in 5 of 203 Old Order Amish controls.

Ammous et al. (2021) studied 35 affected individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families and confirmed homozygosity for the E366G mutation in all of them. The mutation segregated with the disorder: all parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. The allele frequency was 0.5% to 1.4% in Amish populations, and no unaffected homozygotes were found.
