### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder. Like Wolfram syndrome-1, its diagnosis requires insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy. In the families first described, WFS2 differed from WFS1 because diabetes insipidus was absent and patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding (El-Shanti et al., 2000). Defective platelet aggregation has also been reported (Mozzillo et al., 2014).

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients from 4 consanguineous Jordanian families with the Wolfram syndrome phenotype. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding. ✗

Mozzillo et al. (2014) reported a 17-year-old Caucasian girl from Italy with WFS2, the first child of parents who reported that they were not consanguineous. From the age of 5 years she had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. At age 14 years, malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception were diagnosed, and she underwent resection of a duodenal segment with duodenal-jejunal anastomosis. At age 16 years she developed reduced visual acuity and dyschromatopsia. Fundus examination showed optic disc pallor with preserved pupillary light reflexes, and pattern visual evoked potentials and optic nerve caliber on MRI were normal. Non-autoimmune diabetes appeared at age 17 years and was treated with insulin. She also had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss at high frequencies, slow intestinal transit, and enterogastric bile reflux. After 5 years of follow-up, visual field testing, ERG, and OCT of the macula and retinal nerve fiber layer showed moderate involvement of the optic nerve in both eyes. The authors considered this compatible with optic neuropathy and excluded optic atrophy. Platelet aggregation to ADP was reduced and reversible at up to 10 microM, platelet secretion in response to ADP was absent, and aggregation in response to collagen, epinephrine, and ristocetin was normal. The other main coagulation tests were normal. At age 22 years she had diabetes mellitus, optic neuropathy with preserved visual acuity, no optic atrophy, and tinnitus related to the high-frequency hearing loss. Her heterozygous parents and brother had no ophthalmologic abnormalities, diabetes, or deafness, but each showed reversible defective platelet aggregation in response to ADP. Mozzillo et al. (2014) noted that defective aggregation to collagen, rather than to ADP, had been described previously in WFS2. They suggested that differences in experimental conditions or between patients might explain this discrepancy.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) reported 2 Italian sisters with WFS2. The first symptom was severe duodenal ulcers. Both sisters had diabetes mellitus, optic atrophy, hearing loss limited to the high frequencies, and polyuria. One of them developed clinical and biochemical signs of diabetes insipidus. Other features included hypogonadism, neurogenic bladder, mood disorder, rheumatologic disease (Lofgren syndrome and HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis), hypogammaglobulinemia, and microcytic anemia. †

### Inheritance

El-Shanti et al. (2000) described Wolfram syndrome as an autosomal recessive disorder and studied consanguineous families with WFS2. Autosomal recessive inheritance of WFS2 was supported by the finding of homozygous CISD2 mutations in affected individuals from consanguineous Jordanian families (Amr et al., 2007). In an Italian family, the affected proband was homozygous for a CISD2 deletion and her parents and brother were heterozygous (Mozzillo et al., 2014). In another Italian family, 2 affected sisters were homozygous for a CISD2 mutation and their parents were heterozygous (Rondinelli et al., 2015). † ⚠︎

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 4 families with the Wolfram syndrome phenotype. Using 3 microsatellite markers (D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366) linked to the chromosome 4p16.1 Wolfram syndrome locus, they significantly excluded linkage to that region in 3 of the 4 families. In the remaining family, the 2 affected individuals were homozygous for all 3 markers in the 4p16.1 region. In the other 3 families, El-Shanti et al. (2000) demonstrated linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24.

### Heterogeneity

By finding linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24 in families in which linkage to the 4p16.1 locus had been excluded, El-Shanti et al. (2000) provided evidence for genetic heterogeneity of Wolfram syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

Amr et al. (2007) studied affected members of 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome. The families had no mutations in the WFS1 gene and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus on 4q22-q25. In all 3 families, Amr et al. (2007) identified a homozygous mutation in the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene, a G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 (c.109G-C) predicting a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution. Although the mutation changes a conserved amino acid, the authors concluded that its pathogenic effect results from aberrant splicing: it eliminated exon 2 from the mRNA and introduced a premature stop codon. Screening of 377 probands with hearing loss identified no mutations in the WFS2 gene.

In an Italian girl with WFS2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) found that exon 2 of the CISD2 gene could not be amplified, whereas exons 1 and 3 were present. CISD2 cDNA sequencing showed a homozygous deletion of the whole of exon 2. SNP array analysis confirmed the deletion and showed that it was homozygous in the proband and heterozygous in her parents and brother. Mozzillo et al. (2014) restricted the maximum size of the deletion to 2,050 bp, spanning from intron 2 to intron 3. The parents reported that they were not consanguineous, but their grandparents had been born on the same small Italian island. Analysis of long runs of homozygosity did not reveal a parental relationship, but microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor. The authors noted that the codons for amino acids 102 to 106 lie within the deleted region and suggested that the deletion may produce a nonfunctional protein lacking the zinc-finger domain.

In 2 Italian sisters with WFS2, Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a homozygous mutation in the donor splice site of intron 1 of the CISD2 gene (c.103+1G-A). The mutation was predicted to cause skipping of exon 1. The parents were heterozygous for the mutation. †

### Pathogenesis

Amr et al. (2007) showed that the ZCD2-encoded protein, ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein), localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum but does not interact directly with wolframin, the WFS1 protein. Lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals had a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than control cells when stimulated with thapsigargin. Resting intracellular calcium concentrations did not differ.

### Diagnosis

Mozzillo et al. (2014) observed that their patient had optic neuropathy rather than optic atrophy. They argued that the 'asymptomatic' optic atrophy described in the Jordanian patients was not fully supported by the reported investigations and suggested that the eye disease in those patients was probably also optic neuropathy. Because optic atrophy is a main diagnostic criterion for Wolfram syndrome, they proposed that so-called WFS2 might not be a subtype of Wolfram syndrome. They called for further clinical reports to define its clinical spectrum and reassess its classification.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction accompanied by autophagic cell death. These changes preceded the earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration and led to a set of features suggesting premature aging. In these mice, Cisd2 localized mainly to mitochondria. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased in parallel with the development of the premature aging phenotype. Chen et al. (2009) concluded that the knockout mice supported the hypothesis that Wolfram syndrome is partly a mitochondria-mediated disorder, and they proposed that CISD2 mutation causes WFS2 in humans through mitochondrial dysfunction.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, GLU37GLN [c.109G>C; p.Glu37Gln; E37Q]

In affected members of 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome-2, Amr et al. (2007) identified a homozygous G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 of the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene, predicting a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution at a conserved residue. The mutation disrupted mRNA splicing, causing loss of exon 2 and introducing a premature stop codon. Amr et al. (2007) considered this aberrant splicing to be the basis of the disorder.

**.0002 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, EX2 DEL

In an Italian girl with Wolfram syndrome-2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a homozygous deletion of the whole of exon 2 of the CISD2 gene. The deletion was detected by failure of PCR amplification and by cDNA sequencing, and it was confirmed by SNP array analysis. Its maximum size was restricted to 2,050 bp, spanning from intron 2 to intron 3. Her parents and brother were heterozygous. Microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor, although the parents reported that they were not consanguineous. The heterozygous relatives had subclinical defective platelet aggregation in response to ADP.
