### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene on chromosome 13q12.

### Description

Cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is a rare autosomal recessive, early-onset neurodevelopmental disorder. Most reported patients have had encephalopathy, developmental delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, and dysarthric speech, often with nonprogressive cerebellar ataxia and cerebellar atrophy. More severe cases have optic atrophy, inability to walk, abnormal brain MRI, absent speech, and feeding difficulties. Quadrupedal locomotion has been reported in some patients (Onat et al., 2013; Mohamadian et al., 2020). Milder forms of congenital ataxia with near-normal cognition have also been reported (Damasio et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous family from Turkey in which 4 members had cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome with cerebellar atrophy and quadrupedal locomotion. ⚠︎

Mohamadian et al. (2020) reported 3 sibs, born to healthy first-cousin parents of Arab descent from a consanguineous Iranian family, who had nonprogressive cerebellar ataxia, severe hypotonia, intellectual disability, dysarthria, and cerebellar atrophy. The proband was a 9-year-old girl who had normal weight, height, and head circumference at birth. Her first noticeable sign was severe neonatal hypotonia, followed by delay in all psychomotor and developmental milestones. She used a wheelchair and could not walk, sit, or hold up her head, and she had had feeding difficulties since infancy. Her affected brother and sister were 17 and 15 years old and had similar features. All 3 patients had IQ levels of 20 to 40, delayed speech and language, dysarthria, and absent deep tendon reflexes. Hearing examination showed no significant impairment, and seizures and optic atrophy were not present. Metabolic investigations were normal. Brain MRI showed cerebellar atrophy in all 3 patients.

Damasio et al. (2021) described 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who were able to walk independently by age 4 years. After 16 years of follow-up, they had near-normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported Japanese sibs who developed athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. Both had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, and severely delayed development. One sib had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. The sibs later developed flattening of the auditory brainstem responses and areflexia. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or control the head, although some nonverbal communication was preserved. Symptoms did not progress during early development.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMRQ4 in the families reported by Onat et al. (2013) and Mohamadian et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Both families were consanguineous, and affected members carried homozygous ATP8A2 mutations. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Onat et al. (2013) noted an unrelated patient with impaired intellectual development and severe hypotonia who carried a de novo balanced translocation t(10; 13) that disrupted the ATP8A2 gene.

### Mapping

By homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4, Onat et al. (2013) found several shared homozygous regions, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Cerebellar hypoplasia with quadrupedal locomotion is genetically heterogeneous. Ozcelik et al. (2008) found mutations in the VLDLR gene in 2 consanguineous Turkish families with this phenotype, and the phenotype was not linked to chromosome 9p in 2 other families. Gulsuner et al. (2011) found a mutation in the WDR81 gene in a consanguineous Turkish family. They noted that WDR81 was the third gene implicated in quadrupedal locomotion in humans, after VLDLR and CA8.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4, Onat et al. (2013) identified a homozygous missense mutation (I376M) in the ATP8A2 gene. The mutation was found by targeted next-generation sequencing of an affected individual, followed by segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction approaches. It lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain. ⚠︎

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4, Mohamadian et al. (2020) identified a homozygous missense mutation (G447R) in the ATP8A2 gene. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It segregated with the disorder in the family and was absent from public databases and from 100 healthy, ethnically matched controls. Structural modeling predicted that the substitution reduces protein stability and alters the ligand-binding pocket.

In 2 sibs with a relatively mild congenital ataxia, Damasio et al. (2021) identified a homozygous intronic variant in the ATP8A2 gene (c.1580-18C-G) by exome sequencing. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the variant activated an alternative acceptor splice site. The resulting transcript retained 17 bp of intron 17, inserting 4 amino acids followed by a premature stop codon. The authors concluded that ATP8A2-related disorders include less severe forms of congenital ataxia.

In Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4, Narishige et al. (2022) identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the ATP8A2 gene (R581X and R720X) by whole-exome sequencing. The R581X variant had been reported previously, and R720X was novel.

### Diagnosis

Narishige et al. (2022) noted that, because symptoms did not progress early in development, CAMRQ4 can be hard to distinguish from dyskinetic cerebral palsy, especially in solitary cases. They suggested that visual and hearing impairment together with involuntary movements and severe developmental delay may be a clue to the diagnosis.

In affected members of a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4 and quadrupedal locomotion, Onat et al. (2013) identified a homozygous ile376-to-met (I376M) mutation in the ATP8A2 gene. The mutation was found by targeted next-generation sequencing, followed by segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction approaches. It lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain. Mohamadian et al. (2020) gave the nucleotide change as c.1128C-G. ⚠︎

**.0002 CEREBELLAR ATAXIA, IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT, AND DYSEQUILIBRIUM SYNDROME 4**  
ATP8A2, GLY447ARG [NM_016529.6:c.1339G>A; NP_057613.4:p.Gly447Arg]

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4, Mohamadian et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1339G-A transition in exon 14 of the ATP8A2 gene, resulting in a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the ATP-binding domain. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous carriers, and 1 unaffected sib had 2 wildtype alleles. The variant was absent from public databases and from 100 ethnically matched controls. In silico tools predicted it to be deleterious, and structural modeling predicted reduced protein stability and an altered ligand-binding pocket.

In 2 sibs with congenital ataxia, near-normal cognition, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia, Damasio et al. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the variant created an alternative acceptor splice site.
