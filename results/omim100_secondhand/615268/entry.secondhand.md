### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene on chromosome 13q12. ⚠︎

### Description

Cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is a rare, early-onset autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Its features include nonprogressive cerebellar ataxia, intellectual disability, hypotonia, dysarthria, and global developmental delay, often with cerebellar atrophy. Quadrupedal locomotion was described in the first reported family (Onat et al., 2013; Mohamadian et al., 2020). The phenotype varies widely. At the severe end are nonambulatory patients with involuntary movements, feeding difficulties, and visual and hearing impairment (Narishige et al., 2022). At the mild end is congenital ataxia with independent gait and near-normal cognition (Damasio et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous family from Turkey in which 4 members had cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium syndrome. The title of their report associated the condition with cerebellar atrophy and quadrupedal locomotion. Mohamadian et al. (2020) summarized the affected members of this family as having intellectual disability, mild cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, dysarthria, and truncal ataxia.

Mohamadian et al. (2020) reported 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family of Arab descent; the parents were first cousins. The proband was a 9-year-old girl who was born after an uncomplicated pregnancy with normal weight, height, and head circumference. Her first noticeable sign was severe neonatal hypotonia, after which she was delayed in all psychomotor and developmental milestones. She was wheelchair-bound, could not walk, sit, or hold her head up, and had feeding difficulties from infancy. Her affected brother and sister, aged 17 and 15 years, had similar features. All 3 patients had limited intellectual functioning, with IQ levels from 20 to 40, as well as delayed speech and language and dysarthria. Hearing examination showed no significant impairment, and deep tendon reflexes were absent. Metabolic investigations were normal. Brain MRI showed cerebellar atrophy in all 3 patients. They had no seizures and no optic atrophy. Counting these patients, Mohamadian et al. (2020) stated that 35 patients with CAMRQ4 had been identified.

Damasio et al. (2021) described 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who walked independently by 4 years of age. After 16 years of follow-up, they had near-normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported Japanese sibs who developed athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. Both had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, and severely delayed development. One sib also had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. Over time, both developed areflexia and flattening of the auditory brainstem responses. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or control the head, although some nonverbal communication was preserved. The symptoms did not progress in early development, so the authors noted that the disorder can be hard to distinguish from dyskinetic cerebral palsy, especially in solitary cases. They suggested that visual and hearing impairments combined with involuntary movements and severe developmental delay may point to CAMRQ4. Narishige et al. (2022) also reviewed 33 patients with CAMRQ4. Of these, 80 to 90% had nonambulatory status, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, and involuntary movements (choreoathetosis/dystonia). About 50% had optic atrophy, ophthalmoplegia, or other visual impairments. Overall, 88% were nonambulatory and 73% lacked sitting or head control. † ⚠︎

### Inheritance

In consanguineous families, CAMRQ4 segregated with homozygous ATP8A2 mutations (Onat et al., 2013; Mohamadian et al., 2020); in the Iranian family, the parents were heterozygous carriers (Mohamadian et al., 2020). Compound heterozygous mutations were also reported (Narishige et al., 2022). These findings are consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

In a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4, Onat et al. (2013) used homozygosity mapping and found that affected members shared several homozygous regions, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Ozcelik et al. (2008) studied 4 large consanguineous Turkish kindreds with quadrupedal gait, dysarthric speech, mental retardation, and varying degrees of cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia. In 2 of the families, they found mutations in the VLDLR gene. The phenotype in the other 2 families was not linked to chromosome 9p, which showed that hereditary disorders with quadrupedal gait are genetically heterogeneous. In a reply to commentaries, Ozcelik et al. (2008) again discussed the genetic heterogeneity of cerebellar hypoplasia with quadrupedal locomotion.

In a large consanguineous Turkish family with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation, and cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia linked to chromosome 17p13.1-p13.3, Gulsuner et al. (2011) identified a missense mutation in the WDR81 gene. They described WDR81 as the third gene implicated in human quadrupedal locomotion, after VLDLR and CA8.

Mohamadian et al. (2020) noted that 4 subtypes of CAMRQ had been identified, caused by mutations in the VLDLR, WDR81, CA8, and ATP8A2 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4. Targeted next-generation sequencing of an affected individual, followed by segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction approaches, identified a novel homozygous missense variant in the ATP8A2 gene, ile376-to-met (I376M). Mohamadian et al. (2020) gave the nucleotide change as c.1128C-G. The mutation lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain. Onat et al. (2013) reported that ATP8A2 is expressed mainly in brain, in particular cerebellum, and during development. They also described an unrelated individual with mental retardation and severe hypotonia who carried a de novo balanced translocation, t(10; 13), that disrupted ATP8A2. ⚠︎

In the proband of a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4, Mohamadian et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1339G-A transition in exon 14 of the ATP8A2 gene, predicting a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution. Karyotyping and chromosomal microarray analysis had found no pathogenic abnormality. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation. It was homozygous in all 3 affected sibs and heterozygous in both parents and 2 unaffected sibs; the remaining unaffected sib was homozygous wildtype. The variant was absent from public variant databases and from 100 healthy, ethnically matched controls. It lies in a highly conserved part of the ATP-binding cytoplasmic N domain. In silico structural modeling predicted reduced protein stability and changes to the ligand-binding pocket.

In 2 sibs with congenital ataxia, Damasio et al. (2021) used exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous variant in intron 17 of the ATP8A2 gene (c.1580-18C-G). Bioinformatics tools predicted that the variant creates a new acceptor splice site. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the variant produced an aberrant transcript that retained 17 bp of intron 17 through use of the alternative acceptor site. This inserted 4 amino acids and created a premature stop codon. The authors concluded that ATP8A2-related disorders include less severe forms of congenital ataxia.

In Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4, Narishige et al. (2022) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the ATP8A2 gene: c.1741C-T, predicting arg581-to-ter (R581X), and c.2158C-T, predicting arg720-to-ter (R720X). The R581X mutation had been reported before; the R720X mutation was novel.

### History

Several authors debated what causes quadrupedal locomotion in families with cerebellar hypoplasia. Herz et al. (2008) argued that quadrupedalism may be an epiphenomenon of neurodevelopmental malformation and severe truncal ataxia. Humphrey et al. (2008) pointed out that quadrupedalism occurs variably among individuals with mutations in the same gene. They suggested that contextual or environmental factors during development contribute to this outcome. In reply, Ozcelik et al. (2008) stressed the genetic heterogeneity of cerebellar hypoplasia with quadrupedal locomotion. They also argued that no environmental factors had forced the affected individuals to walk on all fours. †
