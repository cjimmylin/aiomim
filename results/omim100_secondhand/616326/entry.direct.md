### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN).

### Description

Congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 (CMS11) is an autosomal recessive disorder of neuromuscular transmission. It results from deficiency of the postsynaptic protein rapsyn, which clusters the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the motor endplate, and therefore causes endplate AChR deficiency (Ohno et al., 2002). Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients present in the first year of life and a few later in life. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis or other congenital malformations at birth, and intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises. Most patients respond well to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (Engel et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin who had congenital myasthenia with facial malformations. The malformations included an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was mostly limited to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive. Patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors and had no antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor. Repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz produced a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus did not evoke a repetitive compound muscle action potential. The authors noted that the facial abnormalities might either result from the neuromuscular defect or be primary and unrelated to it.

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in AChR subunit genes. Endplate studies in each patient showed reduced staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development.

Burke et al. (2003) identified 2 distinct phenotypes, early onset and late onset, among 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome and rapsyn mutations. The early-onset phenotype was often associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The late-onset phenotype developed in adolescence or adulthood and was at first mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency. At birth he had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity. Because of respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue, he spent a total of 71 days in the neonatal intensive care unit and 47 days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Imaging, metabolic tests, and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. A muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that the clinical features of rapsyn deficiency can suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but ocular ductions are intact in most patients. Apneic episodes can occur. Endplate studies show multiple synaptic contacts on single muscle fibers and poorly differentiated junctional folds. The endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that congenital myasthenia with facial malformations in Iraqi and Iranian Jews is transmitted as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) found that the rapsyn mutations causing endplate AChR deficiency were recessive.

### Mapping

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with a phenotypically distinct form of CMS (6 affected individuals). Four of the families were consanguineous, and all came from the same geographic region. Linkage was excluded to the regions of chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes and to the chromosome 17p region linked to familial infantile myasthenia. The authors suggested that a different gene caused CMS in these patients.

### Heterogeneity

In Iranian Jewish families with a distinct form of CMS, Menold et al. (1998) excluded linkage to the regions containing the AChR subunit genes and to the familial infantile myasthenia locus on 17p. They suggested that the clinical differences among CMS patients reflect locus heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in AChR subunit genes and identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for L14P in tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) 1 and N88K in TPR3. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. One patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5) that causes a frameshift in TPR5. In expression studies in HEK cells, none of the mutations impaired rapsyn self-association, but all 3 reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified 2 mutations in E-box sequences of the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with CMS. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation, which changes an E-box at -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8 were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. They carried a -38A-G mutation, which changes another E-box at -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without altering the CANNTG consensus. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, -38A-G gained binding affinity and -27C-G lost binding affinity for different components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. In luciferase reporter assays, both mutations reduced reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes.

Burke et al. (2003) identified RAPSN mutations in 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome. All 16 shared the N88K mutation.

Gaudon et al. (2010) studied 3 patients with a recessive CMS phenotype and RAPSN mutations. Familial analysis showed that direct sequencing had failed to detect one mutant allele in each. Allelic quantification of patient DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN. These rearrangements accounted for 15% of the authors' CMS patients with RAPSN mutations. The authors recommended single-nucleotide polymorphism markers and gene dosage analysis in addition to direct sequencing. ⚠︎

In a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency, Das et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 confirmed pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found 2 asymptomatic individuals who were homozygous for N88K. Their N88K-associated haplotype differed from the common haplotype at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination within the rapsyn gene might affect phenotypic expression of the disease.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that there are no genotype-phenotype correlations for rapsyn deficiency, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A-G E-box mutation have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe masticatory and facial muscle weakness, and hypernasal speech.

### Diagnosis

Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended single-nucleotide polymorphism markers and gene dosage analysis in addition to direct DNA sequencing, because multiexon RAPSN deletions are missed by sequencing. Das et al. (2014) highlighted whole-exome sequencing as a diagnostic tool after nonspecific results from other investigations.

### Clinical Management

Das et al. (2014) started a boy with rapsyn deficiency on pyridostigmine at 16 months of age. Muscle tone and strength improved dramatically, and his joint contractures steadily resolved. Four months after starting treatment he was beginning to bear weight, could sit unsupported, and could vocalize full words.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. Some gain additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some from adding ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) noted that congenital myasthenia gravis was among the disorders frequent in both the Iranian and Iraqi Jewish communities.

Haplotype analysis by Ohno et al. (2003) showed that the -38A-G RAPSN promoter mutation in Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin arose from a common founder.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) used 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb to study the N88K mutation. They identified a common haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes that was also present in all heterozygous N88K carriers, which supported a founder effect. Müller et al. (2004) reported that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder. Engel et al. (2015) likewise stated that Indo-Europeans carry a common N88K mutation in RAPSN.

Ohno et al. (2002) studied a patient with endplate AChR deficiency who had no AChR subunit mutations. The patient was compound heterozygous for a leu14-to-pro (L14P) mutation in TPR1 of rapsyn and the N88K mutation in TPR3. **.0002 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, ASN88LYS [N88K]

Ohno et al. (2002) identified an asn88-to-lys (N88K) mutation in TPR3 of rapsyn in 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. One was compound heterozygous for N88K and L14P, and one was compound heterozygous for N88K and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5). N88K diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn in HEK cells.

Burke et al. (2003) found the N88K mutation in all 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn mutations in their series. These patients had either early-onset or late-onset phenotypes.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found that N88K was associated with a common intragenic SNP haplotype, which supported a founder effect. They also found a discordant haplotype in 2 asymptomatic N88K homozygotes. Müller et al. (2004) reported that N88K derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

**.0003 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, 5-BP INS, 553 [553ins5]

Ohno et al. (2002) studied a patient with endplate AChR deficiency who was compound heterozygous for the N88K mutation and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5) in RAPSN. The insertion causes a frameshift in TPR5. In HEK cell expression studies, it diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

**.0004 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -27C-G [-27C>G]

Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -27C-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter region in a patient with CMS (patient 1). The mutation changes an E-box at -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying it was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. The mutation lost binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. It attenuated reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes and in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin, and it markedly attenuated the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter.

**.0005 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**

Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -38A-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter region in 7 patients with CMS (patients 2-8). The patients were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. The mutation changes an E-box at -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without altering the CANNTG consensus. Haplotype analysis showed a common founder. The mutation gained binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts and attenuated reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes.
