### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 (CMS11), associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RAPSN gene, which encodes rapsyn.

### Description

Congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 is an autosomal recessive disorder of neuromuscular transmission. It results from a deficiency of acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the motor endplate, caused by defects in rapsyn, a postsynaptic protein that concentrates and anchors AChR in the postsynaptic membrane (Ohno et al., 2002; Engel et al., 2015). Most patients present in the first year of life. Arthrogryposis and other congenital malformations occur in about one-third of patients, and intercurrent infections can trigger respiratory crises (Engel et al., 2015). Burke et al. (2003) distinguished an early-onset form from a late-onset form that begins in adolescence or adulthood.

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin, from 10 families, who had congenital myasthenia with facial malformations: an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was mostly limited to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive, and the patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. Antibodies to acetylcholine receptor were absent. Repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz produced a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus did not evoke a repetitive compound muscle action potential. The authors suggested that the facial abnormalities might be secondary to the neuromuscular defect, or might be primary and unrelated.

Burke et al. (2003) studied 16 unrelated patients with a congenital or hereditary myasthenic syndrome and rapsyn mutations, and identified 2 distinct phenotypes. Early-onset disease was frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. Late-onset disease began in adolescence or adulthood and was at first mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency. At birth he had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity. Respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue kept him for a total of 71 days in the neonatal intensive care unit and 47 days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Imaging, a battery of metabolic tests, and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. Muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) noted that most patients with rapsyn deficiency present in the first year of life and a few present later. Arthrogryposis at birth and other congenital malformations occur in nearly one-third of patients. Intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises that lead to anoxic encephalopathy. The clinical features can suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but ocular ductions are intact in most patients. Weakness occasionally has a limb-girdle and axial distribution.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that the form of congenital myasthenia with facial malformations seen in Iraqi and Iranian Jews is transmitted as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) identified recessive rapsyn mutations in patients with endplate AChR deficiency, and they were either homozygous or compound heterozygous.

### Mapping

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with 6 affected individuals, all diagnosed with a phenotypically distinct form of congenital myasthenic syndrome. Four of the families were consanguineous, and all came from the same geographic region. Linkage was excluded to the regions of chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes, and to the region of chromosome 17p to which familial infantile myasthenia had been localized. The authors concluded that a defect in a different gene was responsible for the disorder in these patients.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes, and identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for leu14-to-pro (L14P), located in tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) 1, and asn88-to-lys (N88K), located in TPR3. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. The fourth patient carried N88K and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5) that causes a frameshift in TPR5. In each case, endplate studies showed decreased staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development. Expression studies in HEK cells showed that none of the mutations hindered rapsyn self-association, but all 3 diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified 2 mutations in E-box elements of the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation that changes the E-box at -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying -27C-G was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8 were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. They were homozygous for a -38A-G mutation that changes another E-box at -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without altering the CANNTG consensus sequence. Haplotype analysis indicated that -38A-G arose from a common founder. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, -38A-G gained binding affinity and -27C-G lost binding affinity for different components of nuclear extracts from C2C12 myotubes. In luciferase reporter assays, both mutations attenuated reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes. The -27C-G mutation also attenuated expression in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin, and markedly reduced the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter. The authors predicted that the impaired transcriptional activity would reduce rapsyn expression and cause endplate AChR deficiency. ⚠︎

Burke et al. (2003) identified rapsyn mutations in 16 unrelated patients with a congenital or hereditary myasthenic syndrome. The N88K mutation was common to all of them.

Gaudon et al. (2010) studied 3 patients with a characteristic phenotype of recessive congenital myasthenic syndrome and RAPSN mutations. In each, family analysis showed that the mutation on one allele had not been detected by direct sequencing. Allelic quantification of patient DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN. These rearrangements accounted for 15% of the authors' patients with RAPSN mutations, and the authors recommended gene dosage methods in addition to direct sequencing. ⚠︎

In a 20-month-old boy with congenital myasthenic syndrome, Das et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 confirmed pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found that 3 affected individuals homozygous for N88K shared a common haplotype, whereas 2 asymptomatic N88K homozygotes carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. They suggested that recombination events within the rapsyn gene may affect phenotypic expression of the disease.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that there are no genotype-phenotype correlations in rapsyn deficiency, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A-G E-box mutation have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe weakness of the masticatory and facial muscles, and hypernasal speech.

### Pathogenesis

Ohno et al. (2002) described the structure of rapsyn, a 43-kD postsynaptic protein essential for clustering AChR at the endplate. Seven TPRs mediate self-association, a coiled-coil domain binds AChR, and a RING-H2 domain associates with beta-dystroglycan and links rapsyn to the subsynaptic cytoskeleton. Self-association of rapsyn precedes recruitment of AChR to rapsyn clusters.

Engel et al. (2015) noted that in rapsyn deficiency, multiple synaptic contacts appear on single muscle fibers. The endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds are not well differentiated. Depending on the mutation, rapsyn self-association or binding to AChR is hindered, agrin-MuSK-LRP4-mediated clustering of AChR is impeded, or rapsyn expression is decreased.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) noted that the late-onset phenotype was initially mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis, and that recognizing it should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy. Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended SNP markers and gene dosage analysis in addition to direct sequencing, particularly when genetic counseling is at issue. Das et al. (2014) highlighted whole-exome sequencing as a diagnostic tool in a patient whose earlier test results had been nonspecific and nondiagnostic.

### Clinical Management

Goldhammer et al. (1990) reported that their patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. Das et al. (2014) treated their patient with pyridostigmine starting at 16 months of age. Muscle tone and strength improved dramatically, and the joint contractures steadily resolved. Four months after treatment began, he was starting to bear weight, could sit unsupported, and could vocalize full words. Engel et al. (2015) noted that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors; some gain additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some from the addition of ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) noted that congenital myasthenia gravis was frequent in both the Iranian and the Iraqi Jewish communities.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) used 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb and found a common haplotype in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, supporting a founder effect. Müller et al. (2004) concluded that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Ohno et al. (2002) identified an asn88-to-lys (N88K) mutation in TPR3 of rapsyn in all 4 patients they studied with endplate AChR deficiency. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. One carried N88K with L14P, and one carried N88K with a 5-bp insertion (553ins5). Expression studies in HEK cells showed that the mutation did not hinder rapsyn self-association but diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Burke et al. (2003) found the N88K mutation in all 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn mutations whom they studied.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found that all heterozygous carriers of N88K shared a common haplotype, supporting a founder effect. They also identified 2 asymptomatic N88K homozygotes who carried a discordant haplotype. Müller et al. (2004) concluded that N88K derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.
