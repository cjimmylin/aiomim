### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ABCD syndrome (ABCDS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding the endothelin B receptor (EDNRB).

### Description

ABCD syndrome is an autosomal recessive neural crest disorder. Its name comes from its main features: albinism, a black lock of hair, a cell migration disorder of the neurocytes of the gut (aganglionosis, or Hirschsprung disease), and deafness (Gross et al., 1995; Verheij et al., 2002). Verheij et al. (2002) noted that the phenotype clearly overlaps with Shah-Waardenburg syndrome. Because they found an EDNRB mutation, they suggested that ABCD syndrome is not a separate entity but an expression of Shah-Waardenburg syndrome.

### Nomenclature

Gross et al. (1995) introduced the acronym ABCD for albinism, black lock, cell migration disorder of the neurocytes of the gut, and deafness.

### Clinical Features

Gross et al. (1995) reported a macrosomic newborn girl who had albinism, a black lock of hair at the right temporo-occipital region, and retinal depigmentation. Brainstem auditory-evoked potentials confirmed bilateral deafness. She also had a severe defect of intestinal innervation. Biopsy showed aganglionosis of the large intestine and a total absence of neurocytes and nerve fibers in the small intestine, which indicated a total lack of sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation. She died of intestinal dysfunction at 5 weeks of age. She was the 14th child of consanguineous Kurdish parents. Four of her sibs had the same syndrome and died a few days after birth. The other 9 sibs were well and had an unremarkable phenotype. Gross et al. (1995) concluded that this combination of albinism, black lock, deafness, and a total lack of intestinal neural innervation had not been reported before and represented a new neural crest syndrome.

### Inheritance

Gross et al. (1995) proposed autosomal recessive inheritance of ABCD syndrome. Their evidence was 5 affected sibs born to consanguineous Kurdish parents who had 9 unaffected children. Verheij et al. (2002) supported this by finding a homozygous EDNRB mutation in the index patient.

### Molecular Genetics

The ABCD syndrome phenotype overlaps with Shah-Waardenburg syndrome, so Verheij et al. (2002) screened DNA from the index patient of the family reported by Gross et al. (1995) for mutations in EDNRB, a gene known to be involved in Shah-Waardenburg syndrome. They found a homozygous nonsense mutation in exon 3 of EDNRB (R201X). They suggested that ABCD syndrome is not a separate entity but an expression of Shah-Waardenburg syndrome. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 ABCD SYNDROME**  
EDNRB, ARG201TER [R201X]

Verheij et al. (2002) found a homozygous nonsense mutation, arg201-to-ter (R201X), in exon 3 of the EDNRB gene. The carrier was the index patient with ABCD syndrome from the consanguineous Kurdish family reported by Gross et al. (1995). ⚠︎
