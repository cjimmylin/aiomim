### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10 (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene on chromosome 5. ⚠︎

### Description

The mild variant of congenital myopathy-10 (CMYO10B) is an autosomal recessive myopathy. Its onset ranges from infancy or childhood to adulthood. Features include predominantly axial and proximal muscle weakness, facial weakness, scoliosis, joint contractures, palatal abnormalities with hypernasal speech, dysphagia, and respiratory insufficiency that develops later in the disease course. Muscle biopsy can show a mixture of dystrophic and myopathic changes, including minicores, and myofibrillar alterations have also been described (Boyden et al., 2012; Harris et al., 2018; Croci et al., 2022). The disorder is milder than the severe early-onset form of MEGF10-related myopathy, which is characterized by areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (Takayama et al., 2016; Croci et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Boyden et al. (2012) described the disorder as a recessive congenital myopathy with minicores. Croci et al. (2022) referred to the milder chronic forms of MEGF10 myopathy, which have a more favorable course and later onset, as the mild variant of early-onset myopathy, areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (mvEMARDD).

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) studied a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an unclassified autosomal recessive myopathy. Weakness appeared by 12 months of age, starting in the neck. It involved the whole body but was mainly axial and proximal, and it stabilized in adolescence. Facial weakness was also present. Neck contractures were seen in infancy, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords had developed by adulthood. Serum creatine kinase measured before age 10 years was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis that was treated with spinal fusion. From age 10 to 11 years, all developed respiratory problems that were treated with nighttime bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation. One patient was born with a cleft palate, a second had a midline palatal ridge in adulthood, and the third had a normal-appearing palate. All 3 affected sibs had hypernasal speech. Muscle biopsies showed replacement of myofibers by fatty tissue, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers, which are features of muscular dystrophy. However, the biopsies also showed moth-eaten fibers and minicores, suggesting a congenital myopathy. Most atrophic fibers were type I, most hypertrophic fibers were type II, and type I fibers slightly predominated. Before genetic testing, the diagnosis had been given as limb-girdle muscular dystrophy or spinal muscular atrophy.

Boyden et al. (2012) also reported a Caucasian woman of distant Portuguese ancestry (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease and who had no affected relatives. Hypotonia appeared at several weeks of age, initially as a weak neck and floppy head. She had a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. As a child she had frequent acute pulmonary infections but no chronic breathing problems. At age 10 years, serum creatine kinase was mildly elevated at 302 U/L, and the mild scoliosis noted at that time had progressed to 20 degrees by age 12. She had mild, nonprogressive, mainly proximal weakness, including facial weakness. Muscle biopsy showed marked variation in fiber size, internalized nuclei, slightly increased endomysial connective tissue, and patches of reduced NADH activity. Electron microscopy confirmed minicores.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. In his fifties, while still ambulant, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents. He was born at 33 weeks' gestation, needed resuscitation for asphyxia, had a weak suck, and showed a congenital myopathic phenotype with elbow contractures and scoliosis from the first months of life. Motor development and intellect were normal. However, he developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement. The case report states that nocturnal noninvasive ventilation was needed from age 16 years, whereas the discussion of the same report states that respiratory support was needed from age 6. Serum creatine kinase, brain and spine MRI, ECG, and echocardiogram were normal. Nerve conduction was normal apart from reduced amplitude of the muscle evoked potential. On examination he had axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy and weakness, preserved facial expression, cleft palate, global areflexia, and contractures of the elbows and ankles. He walked without support with an equinovarus-supinated foot stance, hyperlordosis, and pelvic tilt, and he later needed a manual wheelchair for longer distances. He underwent bilateral Achilles tendon tenotomy and vertebral arthrodesis. From age 17 years he had swallowing difficulties and recurrent aspiration pneumonias. A muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: fiber size variation, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later biopsy showed myofibrillar alterations, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CMYO10B in the family reported by Boyden et al. (2012) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance: affected sibs were compound heterozygous for MEGF10 mutations that segregated with the disorder in the family. Croci et al. (2022) reported a patient born to consanguineous parents who was homozygous for a MEGF10 mutation, and both parents were carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) performed genomewide linkage analysis in a family with 3 affected sibs using a recessive model. They found positive lod scores at 16 loci, and 10 of these approached the theoretical maximum lod score of 1.329. The MEGF10 gene lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached this maximum.

### Molecular Genetics

Boyden et al. (2012) performed whole-genome sequencing in 1 affected member of a family with CMYO10B (family 1030) and filtered the variants using linkage data. In the 3 affected sibs they identified compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: C326R and C774R. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans and segregated with the disorder in all 6 family members. Neither mutation was present in public variant databases or in 715 and 645 genotyped controls, respectively. Both affected cysteine residues lie in atypical EGF-like domains and are conserved in all 43 species examined. Boyden et al. (2012) also screened MEGF10 in 190 additional patients with unexplained myopathy. They found a single heterozygous R71W mutation in a patient with a similar phenotype (subject 105-1), inherited from an unaffected parent. No second mutation was identified, including by sequencing of muscle cDNA. Knockdown of megf10 in zebrafish supported pathogenicity.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy who was compound heterozygous for 2 MEGF10 mutations: a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G) and a novel missense mutation (C810Y).

Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing in a 66-year-old man with adult-onset MEGF10 myopathy. They identified 2 novel mutations in the MEGF10 gene: an essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a missense mutation (c.352T-C, C118R).

Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing in an Italian man with CMYO10B born to consanguineous parents. They identified a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution, and confirmed it by Sanger sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD and affected a conserved residue. It was predicted to be damaging by several in silico tools and was classified as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. No functional studies were performed.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that nonsense and frameshift mutations in MEGF10 had been found to cause a severe congenital myopathy with diaphragmatic weakness, areflexia, and dysphagia, and that the patients with those mutations had a generally similar but more severe phenotype. In one such family, the C774R mutation was in compound heterozygosity with a frameshift mutation. Boyden et al. (2012) suggested that the milder disease in family 1030 might reflect residual MEGF10 function with the missense mutations, compared with total loss of detectable MEGF10 expression in the severe families. They also noted that minicores were prominent in their patients with missense mutations but had not been noted in the families with null mutations.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported 2 Japanese patients. One had the severe early-onset phenotype and was homozygous for a frameshift mutation (c.131_132del). The other had the milder phenotype and was compound heterozygous for a splice site mutation and a cysteine missense mutation. The authors concluded that these findings indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

In a review of 20 reported patients with MEGF10 mutations, Harris et al. (2018) confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. In patients with at least 1 null mutation, respiratory dysfunction began in the first year of life. In those with 2 missense mutations, respiratory dysfunction began at 10 years of age or much later.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that their patient, who was homozygous for a missense mutation, nevertheless had an early-onset myopathy with a progressive course. They proposed that the phenotypes associated with MEGF10 variants form a continuum. Because minicores appeared only in a later biopsy from their patient, they also proposed that minicores may be a consequence of a longstanding myofibrillar pathologic process that begins in infancy. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Holterman et al. (2007) found that mouse Megf10 was expressed in quiescent satellite cells and proliferating myoblasts but not in differentiated myofibers, and that it was upregulated in activated satellite cells. Overexpression of Megf10 in myoblasts increased proliferation and inhibited differentiation. Silencing of Megf10 in activated satellite cells or cultured myoblasts caused precocious differentiation, depletion of the self-renewing Pax7+/MyoD- population, and downregulation of components of Notch signaling. ⚠︎

Boyden et al. (2012) found that the C326R and C774R mutations replace cysteine residues that probably form disulfide bonds in atypical EGF-like domains. They suggested that these mutations likely cause loss of MEGF10 function. They hypothesized that excessive differentiation of satellite cells could then deplete the pool of self-renewing cells available to regenerate damaged muscle fibers, which might explain the dystrophic features seen on biopsy.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish using 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. The resulting morphants had unhatched eggs, curved or bent tails, impaired swimming, and reduced motility in response to touch. Morphants had reduced birefringence, disrupted somite boundaries, and gaps between myofibers. Electron microscopy showed widespread myofibrillar disorganization and reduced striation, consistent with a myopathic process.

In 3 sibs from a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family with CMYO10B, Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a c.976T-C transition in exon 10, resulting in a cys326-to-arg (C326R) substitution in EGF-like domain 6, and a cys774-to-arg (C774R) substitution. The mutations were found by whole-genome sequencing combined with linkage analysis. They were confirmed to be in trans and segregated with the disorder in the family. The C326R mutation was absent from 715 genotyped controls, and the affected residue is conserved in all 43 species examined.
