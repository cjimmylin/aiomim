### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple epiphyseal dysplasia-2 (EDM2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding the alpha-2 chain of type IX collagen (COL9A2) on chromosome 1p32.3-p33. ⚠︎

### Description

Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED) is a dominantly inherited chondrodysplasia characterized by mild short stature and early-onset osteoarthrosis (Briggs et al., 1994). EDM2 is a subtype of MED in which the hips are not markedly affected at an early age. Beginning in childhood, the disease causes pain and stiffness of the knees, ankles, elbows, and finger joints, and affected individuals are prone to early degenerative joint disease (Fiedler et al., 2002).

### Nomenclature

Fiedler et al. (2002) referred to this disorder as multiple epiphyseal dysplasia with relative hip sparing during childhood (EDM2).

### Clinical Features

Barrie et al. (1958) described multiple epiphyseal dysplasia in a British family. The knees were more severely involved than the hips, which were relatively spared. †

Versteylen et al. (1988) described 2 families that appeared to have multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. In both families, severe osteochondritis of the knees was strikingly common. The authors suggested that the fragmented and maldeveloped epiphyses characteristic of epiphyseal dysplasia caused the osteochondritis.

Fiedler et al. (2002) reported a family of 19 individuals over 4 generations, 12 of whom were affected with a variant of multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. Beginning in childhood, the disease caused pain and stiffness of the knees, ankles, elbows, and finger joints. Some adult patients had repeated episodes of free articular bodies that caused the joint to lock, and affected individuals were prone to early degenerative joint disease. The hips were not markedly affected at an early age. On electron microscopy, the diameter of collagen fibrils from free articular bodies removed arthroscopically from 2 patients did not differ obviously from that of normal articular cartilage.

Nakashima et al. (2005) reported a double-layered patella in a Japanese male with dominant multiple epiphyseal dysplasia who carried a heterozygous COL9A2 mutation. They concluded that a double-layered patella in multiple epiphyseal dysplasia is not exclusive to DTDST mutation. †

### Inheritance

Briggs et al. (1994) noted that multiple epiphyseal dysplasia is dominantly inherited. The 4-generation family with EDM2 reported by Fiedler et al. (2002) was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, and the heterozygous COL9A2 mutation in this family segregated with the disorder. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Briggs et al. (1994) performed linkage studies in a large family with MED. They excluded the chromosome 19 interval to which both pseudoachondroplasia and a form of MED had previously been linked. Instead, they identified an MED locus, EDM2, on the short arm of chromosome 1 (1p32.2-33), in a region containing the COL9A2 gene, which encodes the alpha-2 chain of type IX collagen. ⚠︎

Using fluorescence in situ hybridization, Warman et al. (1994) mapped the COL9A2 gene to chromosome 1p32.3-p33. Using a single-strand conformational polymorphism within the murine Col9a2 gene, they mapped the mouse gene to chromosome 4.

### Heterogeneity

Briggs et al. (1994) excluded linkage to the chromosome 19 MED/pseudoachondroplasia interval in a large MED family and mapped the disorder in that family to chromosome 1p. This showed that MED is genetically heterogeneous.

### Molecular Genetics

Muragaki et al. (1996) identified a heterozygous T-to-C transition at position +2 of the intron 3 splice donor site of the COL9A2 gene (IVS3DS+2T-C) in a family with EDM2. The mutation caused exon 3 to be skipped in COL9A2 mRNA. †

Fiedler et al. (2002) screened candidate regions in a 4-generation family with EDM2 and identified a G-to-C substitution at position -1 in the COL9A2 exon 3/intron 3 splicing region. They predicted that the change most probably alters splicing. Using automated sequencing and BsaWI digestion of amplicons, they confirmed the substitution in 8 clinically affected family members and in 3 presymptomatic young children. Fiedler et al. (2002) concluded, together with previous reports, that mutations causing skipping of exon 3 within the COL3 domain of the alpha-2 chain of type IX collagen may be relatively common in patients with an MED subtype in which the hips are not markedly affected at an early age.

Nakashima et al. (2005) identified a heterozygous COL9A2 mutation in a Japanese male with dominant MED and double-layered patella. †

### Diagnosis

Fiedler et al. (2002) suggested that, in patients with MED and their families, screening the candidate regions of COL9A2 for mutations may help to confirm the diagnosis, guide appropriate lifestyle advice, and support well-founded genetic counseling. ⚠︎
