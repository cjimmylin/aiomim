### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MLYCD) on chromosome 16.

### Description

Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism. It is characterized by malonic aciduria, developmental delay, seizures, hypoglycemia, and cardiomyopathy (Wightman et al., 2003). Other reported features include vomiting, hypotonia, metabolic acidosis, and mild mental retardation (Gao et al., 1999). Sacksteder et al. (1999) described the disorder as potentially lethal, with cardiomyopathy and developmental delay, and noted that several of its features resemble those of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disorders. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Brown et al. (1984) described the first patient with deficiency of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase, which they considered a mitochondrial enzyme. The patient was a 5-year-old boy with repeated episodes of vomiting, some of which needed hospital admission. The enzyme defect was first suspected because he persistently excreted malonic and methylmalonic acids in the urine. Disturbances of lipid metabolism were also shown. ⚠︎

Haan et al. (1986) described a second child, a boy with a more severe enzyme deficiency. He was mildly mentally retarded. During a urinary tract infection he presented with vomiting, a seizure, hypoglycemia, and mild metabolic acidosis. His urine contained increased amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, succinic, adipic, glutaric, and suberic acids. In cultured fibroblast extracts, mitochondrial malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity was 4% of the mean control value.

MacPhee et al. (1993) described 2 Scottish-born patients with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency whose consanguineous parents were of Scottish/Irish origin. Before this report, the disorder had been described in only 2 Australian children. The patients were investigated during episodes of vomiting and febrile convulsions, and both had developmental delay. Testing showed malonic aciduria and greatly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity. The authors presented urinary organic acid profiles obtained by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and compared the clinical and biochemical features of the Scottish and Australian patients.

De Wit et al. (2006) reported a girl with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency. Brain MRI showed white matter abnormalities together with diffuse pachygyria and periventricular heterotopia, consistent with a malformation of cortical development. She had a strikingly low appetite. The authors recommended brain MRI for patients with this deficiency, and metabolic screening for patients with unexplained malformations of cortical development.

Chapel-Crespo et al. (2019) reported the clinical, biochemical, and molecular characteristics of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, including long-term follow-up of 9 patients.

### Biochemical Features

Haan et al. (1986) tested the effect of diet in their patient. A high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet led to symptomatic hypoglycemia and moderate metabolic acidosis. It also caused large amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, succinic, adipic, glutaric, and suberic acids and 3-hydroxybutyrate to be excreted in the urine. On an isocaloric low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet, he excreted only relatively small amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, and succinic acids. Acute fat and lysine loads increased urinary malonic acid excretion without changing excretion of the other organic acids. The authors concluded that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase prevents malonyl-CoA from accumulating in the mitochondrion, and that the enzyme's importance shows most clearly when fat is the main metabolic fuel.

### Inheritance

Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is inherited as an autosomal recessive disorder (Wightman et al., 2003). MacPhee et al. (1993) reported affected children born to consanguineous parents. FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found homozygous mutations in 2 consanguineous patients, and the mutations segregated with the disorder in the families.

### Molecular Genetics

Gao et al. (1999) cloned the human MLYCD gene, which encodes malonyl-CoA decarboxylase. They identified the mutation causing the deficiency in a patient who had clinical features of the defect, malonic aciduria, and markedly reduced enzyme activity.

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) studied 2 consanguineous Scottish patients with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, previously reported by MacPhee et al. (1993). Using RT-PCR analysis of fibroblast RNA, they found a different homozygous MLYCD mutation in each patient. One was a nonsense mutation (S148X). The other was an intronic mutation (IVS4-14A-G) that created a new splice acceptor site, producing a 13-bp insertion in the mature RNA and a frameshift. Both mutations segregated with the disorder in the families and were absent from 100 unrelated normal individuals. ⚠︎

Sacksteder et al. (1999) found a severe mutation in the MLYCD gene (c.947-948delTT) in a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency. Recombinant MCD protein showed high intrinsic malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity, confirming that the gene encodes human malonyl-CoA decarboxylase.

Wightman et al. (2003) sequenced the MLYCD gene in 9 unrelated patients with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency and identified 16 of the 18 pathogenic alleles. They studied fibroblast lines from 8 of these patients and from 2 previously reported patients with homozygous MLYCD mutations. On Western blot, a 66-kD band was absent in 6 patients and substantially reduced in 3. One patient had increased protein levels with a prominent smeary 68- to 83-kD band. Immunocytochemistry of patient cell lines that expressed MLYCD showed apparent intracellular mislocalization of the protein. A mutation at the extreme N terminus (G3D) mislocalized the protein to the plasma membrane. Two other mutations both gave punctate perinuclear staining: a 25-base deletion between the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiation codons (M1 and M40), and a point mutation that abolished the second of these codons (M40T). None of these 3 mislocalizing mutations was predicted to affect catalytic function, so the authors concluded that correct subcellular localization of MLYCD is critical for normal function.

In a girl with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, de Wit et al. (2006) found a normal MLYCD genomic sequence but no messenger product. They suggested that the defect lay in transcription regulation.

### Pathogenesis

Sacksteder et al. (1999) found malonyl-CoA decarboxylase in both the cytoplasm and peroxisomes, and its mRNA was most abundant in cardiac and skeletal muscle. They proposed that the cytoplasmic enzyme regulates cytoplasmic malonyl-CoA levels and therefore mitochondrial fatty acid uptake and oxidation. They suggested that this role could explain why patients have features resembling mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disorders. They also proposed that the peroxisomal enzyme breaks down malonyl-CoA formed inside peroxisomes during beta-oxidation of odd-chain dicarboxylic fatty acids.

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting sequence (-SKL) in human malonyl-CoA decarboxylase. In subcellular fractionation of rat liver, the distribution of enzyme activity strongly suggested that the enzyme is located in peroxisomes as well as in mitochondria.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, SER148TER [c.442C>G; p.Ser148Ter; S148X]

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) studied a consanguineous Scottish patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, previously reported by MacPhee et al. (1993). Using RT-PCR analysis of fibroblast RNA, they found a homozygous 442C-G transversion in the MLYCD gene that creates a premature stop codon (ser148-to-ter; S148X) in the N-terminal half of the protein. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family and was absent from 100 unrelated normal individuals. ⚠︎

**.0002 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, IVS4AS, A-G, -14 [IVS4-14A>G]

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) studied a second consanguineous Scottish patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, previously reported by MacPhee et al. (1993). In this patient they found a homozygous A-to-G transition in intron 4 of the MLYCD gene (IVS4-14A-G). The change created a new splice acceptor site, causing a 13-bp insertion in the mature RNA and a frameshift that was predicted to truncate the protein. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family and was absent from 100 unrelated normal individuals. ⚠︎

**.0003 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, 2-BP DEL, 947TT [c.947_948delTT]

In a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, Sacksteder et al. (1999) found a severe mutation in the MLYCD gene: a 2-bp deletion (c.947-948delTT).

**.0004 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, GLY3ASP [c.8G>A; p.Gly3Asp]

In a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, Wightman et al. (2003) found a c.8G-A transition in the MLYCD gene, causing a gly3-to-asp (G3D) substitution at the extreme N terminus of the protein. On immunocytochemistry, the mutant protein mislocalized to the plasma membrane. The authors suggested that a new targeting signal may lie in a conserved 4-amino acid motif at the N terminus.

**.0005 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, MET40THR [c.119T>C; p.Met40Thr]

In a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, Wightman et al. (2003) found a c.119T-C transition in the MLYCD gene, causing a met40-to-thr (M40T) substitution. This change abolishes the second of the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiation codons. On immunocytochemistry, the mutant protein showed punctate perinuclear staining, suggesting it was mislocalized.
