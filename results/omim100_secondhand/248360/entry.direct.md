### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MLYCD gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MLYCD) deficiency is an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism. Its features include malonic aciduria, developmental delay, seizure disorder, hypoglycemia, and cardiomyopathy (Wightman et al., 2003). Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase converts malonyl-CoA to acetyl-CoA and CO(2) (Sacksteder et al., 1999; Gao et al., 1999).

### Clinical Features

Brown et al. (1984) described a child with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, an inborn error of metabolism that had not been recognized before. The child had repeated episodes of vomiting, some of which required hospitalization. The enzyme defect was first suspected because the child persistently excreted malonic and methylmalonic acids in the urine. Disturbances of lipid metabolism were demonstrated.

Haan et al. (1986) described a second child, a boy with a more severe deficiency of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase. He was mildly mentally retarded. During a urinary tract infection he presented with vomiting, a seizure, hypoglycemia, and mild metabolic acidosis. His urine contained increased amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, succinic, adipic, glutaric, and suberic acids. Mitochondrial malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity in extracts of his cultured fibroblasts was 4% of the mean control value.

MacPhee et al. (1993) described 2 Scottish-born patients with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency. Before this report, the disorder had been reported only in 2 Australian children. Both patients were the offspring of consanguineous parents of Scots/Irish origin. They were investigated during episodes of vomiting and febrile convulsions, and they also had developmental delay. Both had malonic aciduria and grossly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity.

de Wit et al. (2006) described a girl with MCD deficiency. Brain MRI showed white matter abnormalities, as well as diffuse pachygyria and periventricular heterotopia consistent with a malformation of cortical development. She had a strikingly low appetite. The authors recommended brain MRI for patients with MCD deficiency. They also suggested that unexplained malformations of cortical development should prompt metabolic screening.

Chapel-Crespo et al. (2019) reported the clinical, biochemical, and molecular characteristics of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, including long-term follow-up of 9 patients.

### Biochemical Features

Haan et al. (1986) carried out dietary studies in their patient. A high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet led to symptomatic hypoglycemia and moderate metabolic acidosis. On this diet the patient excreted large amounts of the same organic acids in the urine, together with 3-hydroxybutyrate. On an isocaloric low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet, he excreted only relatively small quantities of malonic, methylmalonic, and succinic acids. Acute fat and lysine loads increased urinary malonic acid excretion but did not change excretion of the other organic acids. Haan et al. (1986) suggested that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase prevents the accumulation of malonyl-CoA in the mitochondrion. They also suggested that the enzyme's importance is greatest when fat is the main metabolic fuel.

### Inheritance

Wightman et al. (2003) described MLYCD deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder. FitzPatrick et al. (1999) supported this pattern of inheritance by identifying homozygous MLYCD mutations in patients from consanguineous families. In those families, the mutations segregated appropriately.

### Molecular Genetics

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) studied fibroblast RNA by RT-PCR in the 2 consanguineous Scottish patients with MCD deficiency previously reported by MacPhee et al. (1993). They identified 2 different homozygous mutations in the MLYCD gene. The first was a 442C-G transversion that created a premature stop codon (S148X) in the N-terminal half of the protein. The second was an intronic mutation (IVS4-14A-G) that generated a new splice acceptor site, which led to a 13-bp insertion in the mature RNA and a frameshift predicted to truncate the protein. Both mutations segregated appropriately within the families and were absent in 100 unrelated normal individuals.

Sacksteder et al. (1999) found a severe mutation in the MCD gene (c.947-948delTT) in a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency. Recombinant MCD protein had high intrinsic malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity.

Gao et al. (1999) isolated and sequenced the human gene encoding malonyl-CoA decarboxylase. They identified the mutation causing the deficiency in a patient who had clinical features of the disorder, malonic aciduria, and markedly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity.

Wightman et al. (2003) carried out genomic sequencing of MLYCD in 9 unrelated patients and identified 16 of the 18 expected pathogenic alleles. Fibroblast cell lines were available from 8 of these patients and from 2 previously reported patients with homozygous MLYCD mutations. Western blot analysis with antisera raised to a C-terminal peptide detected a 66-kD band. This band was absent in 6 patients and substantially reduced in 3. One patient had increased protein levels with a prominent smeary band of 68 to 83 kD. In patient cell lines that expressed MLYCD, immunocytochemical analysis showed apparent intracellular mislocalization of the protein. A mutation at the extreme N terminus, c.8G-A (G3D), mislocalized the protein to the plasma membrane. Two other mutations both showed punctate perinuclear staining: a 25-base deletion between the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiating codons (M1 and M40), and a point mutation that ablated the second of these codons (c.119T-C, M40T). None of these 3 mislocalizing mutations was predicted to alter the catalytic function of the protein. Wightman et al. (2003) therefore concluded that correct subcellular localization of MLYCD is likely to be critical for its normal function.

In a girl with MCD deficiency and brain malformation, de Wit et al. (2006) found a normal genomic sequence of the MLYCD gene but no messenger product. They suggested that this reflected an abnormality of transcription regulation.

### Pathogenesis

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting sequence (-SKL) in human MCD. Subcellular fractionation of rat liver homogenates strongly suggested that MCD is localized to peroxisomes as well as mitochondria. Sacksteder et al. (1999) found MCD in both the cytoplasm and peroxisomes. They proposed that cytoplasmic MCD regulates cytoplasmic malonyl-CoA levels and thereby regulates mitochondrial fatty acid uptake and oxidation. They noted that this hypothesis is consistent with the features of MCD-deficient patients that resemble mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disorders. They also noted that MCD mRNA is most abundant in cardiac and skeletal muscle.

MLYCD, GLY3ASP [c.8G>A; p.G3D]

In a patient with MLYCD deficiency, Wightman et al. (2003) identified an extreme N-terminal mutation in the MLYCD gene, a c.8G-A transition resulting in a gly3-to-asp (G3D) substitution. Immunocytochemistry showed that the mutant protein mislocalized to the plasma membrane. The authors suggested that a new targeting signal may reside in a conserved 4-amino acid motif at the N terminus.
