### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the neurofilament light chain gene (NEFL) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is a hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy caused by mutation in the NEFL gene. It is marked by early onset, often with a severe clinical course, and by moderately to severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. Both dominant (heterozygous missense) and recessive (homozygous nonsense) NEFL mutations have been reported (Jordanova et al., 2003; Abe et al., 2009; Yum et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) noted that the nerve conduction velocities in their patients with a homozygous NEFL mutation (about 20 m/s) fell within the 'demyelinating' range under the usual 38 m/s cutoff. However, NEFL is not prominently expressed in myelinating Schwann cells, and nerve biopsy showed that the slowing was most likely due to an absence of large myelinated axons. They felt that 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) was a more fitting name for the disorder. They also suggested that, under OMIM nomenclature for recessive axonal neuropathies, their patients should be called CMT2B5.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) found that patients with NEFL mutations had early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. On electrophysiologic testing, nerve conduction velocities were moderately to severely slowed. A nerve biopsy from a patient with a de novo NEFL missense mutation showed axonal pathology, with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) reported 6 unrelated Japanese patients with CMT and NEFL mutations. All had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities, which the authors thought might be due to a loss of large-diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) reported a 2-generation Palestinian family in which 4 of 5 children of consanguineous parents (first cousins) had a severe, progressive neuropathy. Onset was at about 1.5 years, and initial symptoms were gait abnormality, toe walking, or delayed walking. All 4 affected children had hypotonia in infancy and early childhood, mildly delayed motor milestones, slowly progressive atrophy and weakness of the distal muscles of the feet and hands, and pes cavus. They also had moderate glove-and-stocking sensory impairment and absent ankle jerks. Ages at last report ranged from 13 to 23 years. One patient used a walker and had moderate proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait. The others were ambulatory with molded ankle-foot orthoses, and 1 had mild to moderate weakness of the proximal arm and shoulder girdle. CMT neuropathy scores ranged from 16 to 27. None had pathologic reflexes, palpably enlarged nerves, ulcerated feet, cranial neuropathies, or clinically evident hearing loss. One patient had learning difficulties. Brain MRI was normal in the 3 patients examined.

In the affected children, sensory nerve responses were almost all absent. Median and ulnar motor conduction velocities were severely slowed, ranging from 12 to 25 m/s, and amplitudes became progressively smaller with age. Peroneal and tibial responses were similarly slowed and progressively reduced, and eventually could not be obtained in any affected child. EMG showed severe denervation in distal muscles. Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, indicating subclinical involvement of central nervous system axons. The heterozygous parents and the unaffected sib had normal neurologic examinations and normal sural and peroneal nerve responses (Yum et al., 2009).

A sural nerve biopsy from 1 affected boy at age 16 years showed fewer myelinated axons than normal: 1300/mm, compared with about 6000/mm in normal controls. All myelinated axons were less than 3 microns in diameter, and many were irregular in shape. There were occasional clusters of regenerated axons, and some axons were partly surrounded by crescents of Schwann cell processes, but there was no evidence of actively degenerating axons. On electron microscopy, myelinated axons contained microtubules but no intermediate filaments. On immunostaining, axons were labeled by an antiserum against the N terminus of NFL but not by one against the C terminus. Axons were positive for NFM, NFH, peripherin, and alpha-internexin. The authors concluded that patients had indolent but progressive axonal loss (Yum et al., 2009).

### Inheritance

Jordanova et al. (2003) found NEFL mutations in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT, including a de novo missense mutation. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that NEFL missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype, whereas nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype. Yum et al. (2009) established autosomal recessive inheritance in a consanguineous family. In that family, 4 affected sibs were homozygous for an NEFL nonsense mutation, and both parents were heterozygous and asymptomatic. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for mutations in the NEFL gene. They found 6 disease-associated missense mutations and 1 in-frame 3-bp deletion, clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. One patient had a de novo missense mutation.

Abe et al. (2009) analyzed NEFL in 223 Japanese patients with CMT. Patients with the demyelinating form were negative for mutations in PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX, and patients with the axonal form were negative for mutations in MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS. In 5 unrelated patients, they found 4 heterozygous missense mutations: P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K. Another patient was homozygous for a nonsense mutation, E140X. The authors stated that this was the first report of a homozygous NEFL nonsense mutation.

In 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe, early-onset CMT, Yum et al. (2009) found a homozygous c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, predicted to cause a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation, and the unaffected sib did not carry it. Sequencing of PMP22, GJB1, MPZ, EGR2, PRX, LMNA, GDAP1, and MFN2 in 2 of the patients found no other mutations, and there was no PMP22 duplication or deletion. The mutation was not found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at a diagnostic laboratory. In SW-13 vim- cells, the E210X mutant did not form filaments. Instead it showed a diffuse, largely nuclear pattern, and it accumulated at much lower levels than wildtype NFL. Unlike the dominant mutant Q333P, E210X did not interfere with filament formation by coexpressed wildtype NFL or disrupt the vimentin network, consistent with a simple loss of function. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jordanova et al. (2003) suggested that the clinical variation seen in CMT depends on both the gene mutated and the specific type of mutation. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that NEFL nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype, whereas missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) concluded that the recessive NEFL E210X mutation causes a simple loss of function. In the sural nerve of a homozygous patient, myelinated axons were small and lacked intermediate filaments, even though NFM, NFH, peripherin, and alpha-internexin were present. This indicated that neither the truncated NFL protein nor the other axonal intermediate filament proteins could form intermediate filaments with NFM and NFH in myelinated peripheral nerve axons. The authors proposed that the markedly slowed conduction velocities were explained by the absence of large myelinated axons, which probably fail to reach their normal caliber. They concluded that neurofilaments are the main determinant of axonal caliber and conduction velocity, and that they are needed to maintain myelinated peripheral nerve axons. They also found low but detectable NFM and NFH immunoreactivity in the axons of Nefl-null mice.
