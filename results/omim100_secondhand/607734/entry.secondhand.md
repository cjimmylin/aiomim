### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the NEFL gene on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F is a hereditary motor and sensory peripheral neuropathy caused by mutation in the NEFL gene, which encodes the neurofilament light chain polypeptide. Onset is early and the clinical phenotype is often severe. Nerve conduction velocities are moderately to severely slowed (Jordanova et al., 2003). Both dominant and recessive inheritance have been reported (Abe et al., 2009; Yum et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) noted that the nerve conduction velocities in their patients with a homozygous NEFL mutation fell below the conventional cutoff of 38 m/s, so the neuropathy would be classed as 'demyelinating'. However, their histologic findings showed that the slowed conduction was best explained by the absence of large myelinated axons. They therefore considered the term 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) a better fit and suggested that, under OMIM nomenclature for recessive axonal neuropathies, their patients would be called CMT2B5. They also stressed that homozygous NEFL mutations should be considered when evaluating children with Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies and found NEFL mutations in some of them. Patients with NEFL mutations had early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. On electrophysiologic examination, nerve conduction velocities were moderately to severely slowed. Jordanova et al. (2003) reported the first nerve biopsy from a CMT patient with a de novo missense mutation in NEFL. It showed axonal pathology with clusters of regenerating axons and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) studied Japanese CMT patients with NEFL mutations, comprising 5 unrelated patients with heterozygous mutations and 1 patient with a homozygous mutation. All of them had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities, which the authors thought might be caused by a loss of large-diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) reported a 2-generation Palestinian family in which 4 of 5 children of consanguineous parents (first cousins) had a severe, progressive neuropathy that began in early childhood. Onset was at about 1.5 years of age. The index patient presented at age 2 years with toe walking. All 4 affected children had hypotonia in infancy and early childhood, mildly delayed motor milestones, slowly progressive atrophy and weakness of the distal muscles of the feet and hands, and bilateral pes cavus. All had moderate glove-and-stocking sensory impairment and absent ankle jerks. One patient used a walker and had moderate proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait. Another had mild to moderate weakness of the proximal arm and shoulder girdle. The other 3 were ambulatory with molded ankle-foot orthoses. CMT neuropathy scores ranged from 16 to 27. None had pathologic reflexes, palpably enlarged nerves, foot ulcers, cranial neuropathies, or clinically evident hearing loss. One patient had learning difficulties, and brain MRI was normal in the 3 patients examined. In the affected children, sensory responses were absent apart from 2 low-amplitude responses with slowed conduction. Median and ulnar motor conduction velocities were severely slowed, ranging from 12 to 25 m/s, and amplitudes became progressively smaller with age. Peroneal and tibial responses were similarly slowed, declined with age, and eventually could not be obtained in any affected child. EMG showed severe denervation in distal muscles. Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, indicating subclinical involvement of central nervous system axons. Both parents and the unaffected sib had normal neurologic examinations and nerve conduction studies. ⚠︎

Yum et al. (2009) examined a sural nerve biopsy taken from the index patient at age 16 years. The density of myelinated axons was reduced to 1,300/mm, compared with about 6,000/mm in controls. All myelinated axons were less than 3 microns in diameter, and many were irregular in shape. There were occasional clusters of regenerated axons, and some axons were partially surrounded by crescents of Schwann cell processes. There was no evidence of actively degenerating axons. Myelinated axons contained microtubules but no intermediate filaments. Axons were immunoreactive with an antiserum against the N terminus of the neurofilament light chain but not with one against the C terminus. NFM, NFH, peripherin, and alpha-internexin were present in the axons.

### Inheritance

Jordanova et al. (2003) found NEFL mutations in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT, including a de novo mutation. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that missense NEFL mutations cause a dominant phenotype, whereas nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype. Yum et al. (2009) reported autosomal recessive inheritance in a consanguineous family in which the affected children were homozygous for a nonsense mutation and both parents were heterozygous carriers. The parents were asymptomatic and had normal nerve conduction studies. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for mutations in the NEFL gene. They identified 6 disease-associated missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. One patient had a de novo missense mutation.

Abe et al. (2009) analyzed the NEFL gene in 223 Japanese CMT patients. Patients with the demyelinating form had tested negative for mutations in PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX, and those with the axonal form had tested negative for mutations in MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS. Abe et al. (2009) found 4 heterozygous missense mutations (P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K) in 5 unrelated patients. A sixth patient was homozygous for a nonsense mutation (E140X), which was the first homozygous nonsense mutation reported in NEFL.

In 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe early-onset neuropathy, Yum et al. (2009) found a homozygous nonsense mutation in the NEFL gene (E210X) by sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sib did not carry the mutation. Two of the affected patients had no mutations in PMP22, GJB1, MPZ, EGR2, PRX, LMNA, GDAP1, or MFN2, and no PMP22 duplication or deletion. Yum et al. (2009) expressed the mutation in SW-13 vim- cells. The mutant protein did not form an intermediate filament network and accumulated at much lower levels than wildtype. It did not interfere with filament formation by wildtype NEFL or with the vimentin network. These results were consistent with a simple loss of function. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) noted that NEFL is not prominently expressed by myelinating Schwann cells. In a patient with a homozygous NEFL nonsense mutation, myelinated axons lacked intermediate filaments and were all of small caliber. The authors concluded that the slowed nerve conduction was most likely explained by the absence of large myelinated axons rather than by primary demyelination. Because conduction velocities were already greatly slowed in early childhood, they suggested that myelinated axons probably fail to reach their normal calibers, although axonal atrophy could not be excluded. The authors also concluded that neurofilaments are required for the maintenance of myelinated peripheral nervous system axons.

In 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe, early-onset neuropathy, Yum et al. (2009) identified a homozygous c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, resulting in a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Motor conduction velocities in these patients were slowed to 12 to 25 m/s. Both parents were heterozygous carriers, and the unaffected sib did not carry the mutation. The mutation was not found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at a diagnostic laboratory. In a sural nerve biopsy from one patient, myelinated axons lacked intermediate filaments and did not react with an antibody against the C terminus of NEFL. In transfected SW-13 vim- cells, the mutant protein did not form filaments and did not disrupt filament assembly by wildtype NEFL or vimentin, consistent with loss of function. ⚠︎
