### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous loss-of-function mutation in the HPSE2 gene on chromosome 10q23-q24.

### Description

Urofacial syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are an unusual 'inverted' facial expression, in which patients appear to be crying when they try to smile or laugh, and failure of the urinary bladder to empty completely despite no anatomic bladder outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage (Ochoa and Gorlin, 1987; Daly et al., 2010). Patients present with enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and voiding dysfunction due to neurogenic bladder (Wang et al., 1997). Reflux of infected urine can damage the kidneys, and some severely affected patients become hypertensive and progress to end-stage renal disease (Derbent et al., 2009; Daly et al., 2010). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Elejalde (1979) used the designation 'the Ochoa syndrome' in the title of the first report, crediting Ochoa, who had made the observation but was not an author of the paper. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) proposed the term urofacial syndrome for the disorder. † ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families, one of which was consanguineous (first cousins). The patients had peculiar facies and gestures when smiling and crying. Urinary tract findings included hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal caliber of the urethra in the prostatic and membranous portions, urethral valves, and an abnormal bladder with trabeculation and diverticula, associated with severe hypertrophy of the mucosa with sclerotic changes. Elejalde (1979) concluded that the syndrome involved both the facial and the urinary developmental fields, and that the peculiar facies allowed early recognition of the condition.

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) reported 36 children, seen between 1965 and 1986, who had enuresis and urinary tract infection together with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic work-up showed mild neuropathic bladder in all cases, and most patients had severe urinary tract damage. They had no apparent neurologic condition and no mechanical obstruction to explain the urologic findings. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation. The authors recommended urologic evaluation for anyone with 'inversion' of facial expression. †

Chauve et al. (2000) reported a French family with urofacial syndrome. These were the first reported European cases.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 more European patients with urofacial syndrome. They noted that very few cases had been reported outside Colombia. They suggested that cases may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing goes unrecognized, and stressed that early diagnosis matters for managing the urinary problems.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported the first published case of urofacial syndrome in Turkey. The patient had been followed for recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux, and the syndrome was not diagnosed until age 16 years. The authors noted that the urinary problems usually lead to enuresis, urinary tract infection, and hydronephrosis, and that some severely affected patients become hypertensive and progress to end-stage renal disease.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 families from Turkey, with a median follow-up of 32 months (range, 2-44 months). Some affected individuals had only the facial phenotype or only the urinary tract phenotype. †

### Inheritance

From genetic analysis of 3 families, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant, with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that inheritance was autosomal recessive, based on occurrence in multiple sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio. Later mapping by homozygosity in affected individuals (Wang et al., 1997) and the finding of homozygous HPSE2 mutations (Daly et al., 2010) were consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) carried out a genome screen in 20 selected patients, 1 patient pool, and 3 control pools of unaffected relatives, combining homozygosity mapping with DNA pooling. After 36 randomly chosen markers had been analyzed, marker D10S677 was found to be linked to and associated with UFS, with a significant excess of homozygosity in patients compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)). Fine mapping with 10 additional flanking markers showed that all 31 patients were homozygous for 2 closely linked markers (D10S1726 and D10S198) about 5 cM telomeric to D10S677. Only 12% of unaffected relatives were homozygous for both markers (P less than 10(-19)). Recombination events placed the UFS gene within a 1-cM interval between D10S1433 and D10S603 on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) studied American patients of Irish heritage. Homozygosity mapping localized their disorder to the same region already mapped in Colombian families, showing genetic homogeneity. Using a physical map and new polymorphic markers, they narrowed the UFS gene to within one YAC clone of 1,410 kb. They located the GOT1 gene within the critical region and determined its genomic structure, but mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Chauve et al. (2000) performed haplotype analysis in a French family with UFS and compared the results with published data from Colombian and American patients. The results fit the same critical region on 10q23-q24 and supported genetic homogeneity.

Wang et al. (2003) built a genomic sequence map covering the whole UFS interval, which had previously been estimated at about 360 kb on 10q23-q24. They narrowed the disease interval to a 220-kb region containing the ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene. An extensive search of ACDP1 (coding region, 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, promoter, and exon/intron junctions) found no pathogenic mutation in UFS patients. The authors concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q was responsible in UFS patients from several ethnic groups.

### Heterogeneity

Daly et al. (2010) found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 of the UFS patients they studied, which pointed to genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all UFS patients they studied, who came from Colombia, the United States, and France. In Colombian patients they found a nonsense mutation in exon 11 (R506X), predicted to produce a truncated protein lacking the C-terminal 86 amino acids. Patients carrying this mutation shared the same haplotype across a region that included HPSE2, which is consistent with a founder effect. HPSE2 encodes a 592-amino acid protein with a domain homologous to the glycosyl hydrolase motif of heparanase (HPSE). RT-PCR showed high HPSE2 expression in facial muscle. The authors suggested that HPSE2 may be important for the coordinated action of the muscles involved in facial expression and in urine voiding. †

Daly et al. (2010) used whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual to define a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24. Within this region they found a homozygous 10-kb deletion in HPSE2 that removed exons 8 and 9. In 5 more unrelated families they found homozygous HPSE2 mutations, including exonic deletions (among them a deletion of exon 3), nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations (such as a 2-bp deletion, c.1465_1466delAA). These findings confirmed HPSE2 as the causative gene. HPSE2 was expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system, where the authors suggested it might help control facial expression and urinary voiding. It was also expressed in bladder smooth muscle, which fits a role in renal tract morphology and function. ⚠︎

It results in an arg506-to-ter (R506X) substitution and is predicted to produce a truncated protein lacking the C-terminal 86 amino acids. Patients carrying the mutation shared the same haplotype across a region that included HPSE2, which is consistent with a founder effect. †
