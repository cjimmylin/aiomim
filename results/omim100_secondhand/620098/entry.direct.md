### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental delay with variable intellectual disability and dysmorphic facies (DIDDF) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the JARID2 gene (601594) on chromosome 6p22.3.

### Description

Developmental delay with variable intellectual disability and dysmorphic facies (DIDDF) is a neurodevelopmental disorder. Features include developmental delay, cognitive impairment ranging from borderline intellectual functioning to severe intellectual disability, hypotonia, autistic features, and behavioral abnormalities. Dysmorphic facial features include high anterior hairline, broad forehead, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, depressed nasal bridge, bulbous nasal tip, and full lips (summary by Verberne et al., 2022). Some patients have palatal abnormalities and heart defects, and expressivity is variable (Cadieux-Dion et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Verberne et al. (2021) reported 16 individuals from 15 families with a deletion or single-nucleotide variant in the JARID2 gene. All had developmental delay and/or intellectual disability. They shared some overlapping clinical features, such as facial features, with individuals who had larger deletions involving JARID2.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported 7 individuals from 5 families with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants. The authors highlighted palatal abnormalities and heart defects as part of the phenotype. Some variants were inherited from mildly affected parents, showing variable expressivity.

Verberne et al. (2022) summarized the clinical features of 8 patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants, some of whom had been reported earlier. The group included a mother and her daughter (patients 7 and 6) who carried the same deletion. All 8 had developmental delay. Intellectual functioning ranged from normal (patient 6) through learning difficulties and borderline intellectual functioning (IQ 82) to mild intellectual disability and moderate intellectual disability. Two patients had a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder, and others had autistic features or behavioral abnormalities. Hypotonia was present in 2 patients, and gait disturbance in 2. Brain MRI showed a small posterior fossa cyst or mega cisterna magna in 1 patient and an arachnoid cyst in another. Variable dysmorphic features included broad forehead, high anterior hairline, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, midface hypoplasia, depressed nasal bridge, bulbous nasal tip, short philtrum, and full lips. One patient had prominent supraorbital ridges. Other anomalies were seen in single patients: submucous cleft palate with bifid uvula, pes planovalgus, cryptorchidism, congenital torticollis, kyphoscoliosis, a supernumerary tooth, and cafe-au-lait macules.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous JARID2 variants in most reported patients occurred de novo (Verberne et al., 2021; Cadieux-Dion et al., 2022). Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported JARID2 variants inherited from mildly affected parents, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with variable expressivity. Verberne et al. (2022) described a deletion that a daughter inherited from her mother, who had learning difficulties. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Baroy et al. (2013) reported 5 unrelated patients with de novo deletions, 0.1 to 4.8 Mb in size, in chromosome 6p22.3-p24.1. The deletions were found by array CGH in a cohort of about 3,600 patients ascertained for neurodevelopmental disorders. Patients 4 and 5 had non-overlapping deletions, and both deletions lay within the deletions of patients 1 to 3. Patients 1 to 4 shared a smallest region of overlap (SRO I) of 189 kb, which contained the JARID2 and DTNBP1 genes. Patient 4 had a 189-kb deletion that removed exons 3 to 18 of JARID2 and exons 7 to 10 of DTNBP1. Patients 1 to 4 had variable cognitive impairment, ranging from borderline IQ (74 in patient 4) to severe intellectual disability. They also had gait disturbance and similar facial dysmorphism, including prominent supraorbital ridges, deep-set eyes, dark infraorbital circles, and midface hypoplasia. Additional features were bulbous nasal tip, full lips, marked Cupid's bow, prominent or pointed chin, clinodactyly of the fifth fingers, and foot anomalies. Seizures occurred in 2 patients, an atrial septal defect in 1, and autism spectrum disorder in 1. JARID2 expression in leukocytes was significantly reduced in patients 1 to 4 compared with controls. Baroy et al. (2013) suggested that JARID2 haploinsufficiency might be a clinically recognizable neurodevelopmental syndrome. On review of photographs of 7 of 10 previously reported patients with deletions including JARID2, they confirmed a similar facial pattern.

### Molecular Genetics

Verberne et al. (2021) used chromosomal microarray analysis, exome sequencing, and GeneMatcher to identify deletions or single-nucleotide variants involving the JARID2 gene in 16 individuals from 15 families with developmental delay and/or intellectual disability. Several of the variants were predicted to cause haploinsufficiency through nonsense-mediated mRNA decay.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants in 7 individuals from 5 families. Some variants were inherited from mildly affected parents. The authors noted that intragenic JARID2 copy number variants were common and stressed the importance of exon-level analysis.

Verberne et al. (2022) studied 8 patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants. Six had deletions of at least 1 exon of JARID2, 1 had a frameshift variant (c.2866dupG), and 1 had a splice site variant (c.2731+1G-C). Peripheral blood DNA methylation profiling found a specific episignature that clearly separated these patients from 56 controls and from patients with other neurodevelopmental disorders. Three patients carrying JARID2 missense variants of uncertain significance (E644K and R788Q) clustered with controls. The authors considered these missense variants possibly benign.

Zahir et al. (2016) found that a copy number variant involving only exon 6 of JARID2, which encodes one of a series of repeated motifs, was benign. Its population frequency was more than 14%, although the authors suggested it could have a contributory effect.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL DELAY WITH VARIABLE INTELLECTUAL DISABILITY AND DYSMORPHIC FACIES**  
JARID2, 1-BP DUP, 2866G [NM_004973.4:c.2866dupG; p.(Glu956GlyfsTer72)]

Verberne et al. (2022) reported a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication (c.2866dupG, NM_004973.4) in the JARID2 gene in a 14-year-old boy (patient 4) with developmental delay and moderate intellectual disability. The duplication was predicted to cause a frameshift (Glu956GlyfsTer72). He also had autism spectrum disorder, behavioral abnormalities, high anterior hairline, short philtrum, and full lips. The variant was classified as pathogenic. His blood DNA methylation profile matched the JARID2 episignature.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL DELAY WITH VARIABLE INTELLECTUAL DISABILITY AND DYSMORPHIC FACIES**  
JARID2, 2731+1G-C [NM_004973.4:c.2731+1G>C]

Verberne et al. (2022) reported a de novo heterozygous splice site variant (c.2731+1G-C, NM_004973.4) in the JARID2 gene in a 5-year-old boy (patient 5). He had developmental delay, hypotonia, an arachnoid cyst, broad forehead, deeply set eyes, right cryptorchidism, and congenital torticollis. The variant was classified as likely pathogenic. His blood DNA methylation profile matched the JARID2 episignature.
