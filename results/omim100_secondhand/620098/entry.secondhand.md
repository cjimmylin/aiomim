### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental delay with variable intellectual disability and dysmorphic facies (DIDDF) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the JARID2 gene on chromosome 6p22.3.

### Description

Developmental delay with variable intellectual disability and dysmorphic facies (DIDDF) is a neurodevelopmental disorder. Its features include developmental delay, cognitive impairment ranging from borderline intellectual functioning to severe intellectual disability, hypotonia, autistic features, and behavioral abnormalities. Dysmorphic facial features include a high anterior hairline, broad forehead, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, depressed nasal bridge, bulbous nasal tip, and full lips (Verberne et al., 2022). Expressivity is variable, and some affected individuals inherited the variant from a mildly affected parent (Cadieux-Dion et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Barøy et al. (2013) described 4 unrelated patients with de novo deletions in chromosome 6p22.3 that included the JARID2 gene. Three of the patients were born to nonconsanguineous Norwegian or Norwegian/English parents and 1 to nonconsanguineous Lebanese parents. Patient 1 was a 15-year-old girl with moderate intellectual disability. Her vocabulary was 20 to 30 words, and she had hyperactivity, sleeping difficulties, an unsteady and broad-based gait, suggested dyspraxia, seizure-suspected syncope episodes, and shortening of several metacarpal and metatarsal bones. Patient 2 was a 4-year-old boy with moderate intellectual disability, severely delayed language, a broad-based and unsteady gait, poor eye contact, repetitive behavior, seizure-suspected syncope episodes in infancy, and an atrial septal defect that closed spontaneously. Patient 3 was a 6.5-year-old girl with severe intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, hyperactivity, sleeping difficulties, unsteady gait, neonatal hypotonia, and a large umbilical hernia. Patient 4 was a 6.5-year-old boy with borderline intellectual functioning (IQ 74), delayed language development, early-onset hypotonia, and problems with balance and coordination. The authors interpreted his gait as a sign of dyspraxia. All 4 patients had gait disturbance and similar dysmorphic facial features. The most characteristic were prominent supraorbital ridges, deep-set eyes, dark infraorbital circles, and midface hypoplasia. All 4 also had a bulbous nasal tip, full lips, a marked Cupid's bow, a prominent or pointed chin, and clinodactyly of the fifth fingers. Toe anomalies included syndactyly of toes, sandal gap, and pes planovalgus. Cerebral MRI in these patients did not show changes affecting the cerebellum. Patients 1 through 3 also had deletions of ATXN1 and had lower cognitive function than patient 4. Barøy et al. (2013) concluded that JARID2 haploinsufficiency might represent a clinically recognizable neurodevelopmental syndrome. ⚠︎

Verberne et al. (2021) reported 16 individuals from 15 families with a deletion or single-nucleotide variant involving JARID2. All had developmental delay and/or intellectual disability. They shared some clinical characteristics, including facial features, with individuals who had larger deletions involving JARID2.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported 7 individuals from 5 families with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants. They identified palatal abnormalities and heart defects as part of the phenotype. Some parents carrying the variants were mildly affected, which demonstrated variable expressivity.

Verberne et al. (2022) summarized the clinical features of 8 patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants, including a mother and daughter. All had developmental delay. Intellectual functioning ranged from normal (in the daughter, aged 6 years) through learning difficulties and borderline intellectual functioning to mild or moderate intellectual disability. Several patients had autistic features, and 2 had a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder. Other findings included behavior abnormalities, hypotonia, and gait disturbance. Facial features included broad forehead, high anterior hairline, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, bulbous nasal tip, short philtrum, and full lips. Other anomalies included submucous cleft palate with bifid uvula in 1 patient, a small posterior fossa cyst or mega cisterna magna in 1, an arachnoid cyst in 1, and kyphoscoliosis in 1.

### Inheritance

The deletions reported by Barøy et al. (2013) occurred de novo. Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported inheritance of JARID2 variants from mildly affected parents. Verberne et al. (2022) described a JARID2 deletion that was transmitted from a mother with learning difficulties to her daughter. These findings are consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with variable expressivity. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Barøy et al. (2013) studied a cohort of about 3,600 patients ascertained for neurodevelopmental disorders. Using array CGH, they identified 5 unrelated patients with de novo deletions in chromosome 6p22.3-p24.1 ranging in size from 116 kb to 4.8 Mb. The smallest region of overlap among patients 1 through 4 was 189 kb and contained the JARID2 and DTNBP1 genes. This region was defined by the deletion in patient 4, which removed exons 3 to 18 and the 3-prime UTR of JARID2 and exons 7 to 10 of DTNBP1. Patients 1 through 3 had larger deletions that also included ATXN1. Patient 5 had a nonoverlapping 116-kb deletion that included GMPR and part of ATXN1 but not JARID2; she had mild intellectual disability and behavioral abnormalities without the characteristic facial features. JARID2 expression in leukocytes from patients 1 through 4 was significantly lower than in controls. Barøy et al. (2013) considered a link between DTNBP1 haploinsufficiency and the patients' features unlikely and suggested that JARID2 was the critical gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Zahir et al. (2016) had previously found a copy number variant involving only exon 6 of JARID2, which occurred apparently de novo in 7 patients with intellectual disability. Exon 6 encodes one of a series of repeated motifs. After studying this variant in 2 cohorts, they concluded that the JARID2 exon 6 CNV is benign, with a population frequency greater than 14%, although it could have a contributory effect. ⚠︎

Verberne et al. (2021) used chromosome microarray analysis, exome sequencing, and GeneMatcher to identify 16 individuals from 15 families with DIDDF. All carried deletions or single-nucleotide variants in the JARID2 gene. Several of the variants were predicted to cause haploinsufficiency through nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. Verberne et al. (2021) concluded that JARID2 haploinsufficiency causes a clinically distinct neurodevelopmental syndrome.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants in 7 individuals from 5 families with DIDDF. They noted a high prevalence of intragenic JARID2 copy number variants and emphasized the importance of exon-level analysis.

Verberne et al. (2022) studied peripheral blood DNA methylation in 8 patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic heterozygous JARID2 variants. Six had deletions of at least 1 exon, 1 had a frameshift variant (c.2866dupG; p.Glu956GlyfsTer72), and 1 had a splice site variant (c.2731+1G-C). The variants had been identified by microarray analysis, whole-exome sequencing, or a gene panel. The variants were de novo in 6 patients, and 1 deletion was inherited by a girl from her mother. The authors identified a DNA methylation episignature of 150 probes that clearly separated these patients from controls. A support vector machine classifier based on this episignature gave all patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants scores near 1, and gave controls and patients with 57 other neurodevelopmental disorders low scores. Three patients with JARID2 missense variants of uncertain significance (R788Q in 2 unrelated patients and E644K in 1) clustered with controls and had scores near zero. The authors suggested that these missense variants might be benign, while noting that a negative episignature does not rule out pathogenicity.

### Diagnosis

Verberne et al. (2022) identified a sensitive and specific peripheral blood DNA methylation episignature for JARID2-related neurodevelopmental syndrome. They proposed using it as a biomarker to reclassify JARID2 variants of uncertain significance, and it was added to the EpiSign diagnostic test.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL DELAY WITH VARIABLE INTELLECTUAL DISABILITY AND DYSMORPHIC FACIES**  
JARID2, 1-BP DUP, 2866G [NM_004973.4:c.2866dupG; p.Glu956GlyfsTer72]

Verberne et al. (2022) reported a 14-year-old boy (patient 4) with DIDDF who carried a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication (c.2866dupG, NM_004973.4) in the JARID2 gene. The duplication results in a frameshift and premature termination (Glu956GlyfsTer72). He had moderate intellectual disability, behavior abnormalities, autism spectrum disorder, and a small posterior fossa cyst or mega cisterna magna. His facial features included a high anterior hairline, short philtrum, and full lips. The variant was classified as pathogenic. His blood DNA methylation profile clustered with the JARID2 episignature.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL DELAY WITH VARIABLE INTELLECTUAL DISABILITY AND DYSMORPHIC FACIES**  
JARID2, 2731+1G-C [NM_004973.4:c.2731+1G>C]

Verberne et al. (2022) reported a 5-year-old boy (patient 5) with DIDDF who carried a de novo heterozygous G-to-C transversion at a splice site (c.2731+1G-C, NM_004973.4) in the JARID2 gene. He had developmental delay, hypotonia, an arachnoid cyst, right cryptorchidism, and congenital torticollis. His facial features included a broad forehead and deeply set eyes. The variant was classified as likely pathogenic. His blood DNA methylation profile clustered with the JARID2 episignature.
