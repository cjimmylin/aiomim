### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis, also called intermediate cystinosis, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Late-onset (juvenile or adolescent) nephropathic cystinosis is a rare form of cystinosis. Cystinosis is a disorder of lysosomal cystine transport caused by mutations in the CTNS gene, which encodes cystinosin, an integral lysosomal membrane protein (Attard et al., 1999). In the late-onset form, symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age (Attard et al., 1999). Kidney involvement ranges from a proximal tubulopathy that is milder than in the infantile form to an apparent nephrotic syndrome, and renal failure can follow (Langman et al., 1985). The disorder is caused by one mild CTNS mutation combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or by homozygosity for a mild mutation (Thoene et al., 1999). †

### Nomenclature

Thoene et al. (1999) used the term 'intermediate cystinosis' for this form of the disease. Goldman et al. (1971) used the term 'adolescent cystinosis' and compared it with the infantile and adult forms. Attard et al. (1999) used the term 'late-onset cystinosis.'

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of the disease. They reported that the juvenile form is rare, accounting for about 5% of patients. Clinical symptoms appeared in adolescence, kidney dysfunction began at variable ages, and the disease eventually led to end-stage kidney disease. Goldman et al. (1971) also described a 'swan-neck' deformity of the proximal tubules, which is not specific to cystinosis. †

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis (multinucleation) of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis, together with an unusual clinical variant of the disease. Multinucleated cells were also seen in tubular epithelial and interstitial cells. The presence of multinucleated podocytes was considered helpful in diagnosis. †

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis. The renal symptoms of the juvenile form ranged from a proximal tubulopathy milder than in the infantile form to an apparent nephrotic syndrome. †

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with the intermediate form of cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) defined late-onset cystinosis by onset of symptoms between 12 and 15 years of age. They also studied 3 patients whose phenotype did not fit the classic definitions. In these patients, cystinosis began before age 7 years, but the disease course was milder than in the infantile nephropathic form.

### Inheritance

Thoene et al. (1999) and Attard et al. (1999) found that late-onset nephropathic cystinosis follows autosomal recessive inheritance. Thoene et al. (1999) found that the disease results from homozygosity for a mild CTNS mutation or from 1 mild mutation combined with a mutation that causes nephropathic cystinosis. Attard et al. (1999) described cystinosis as an autosomal recessive disease. Langman et al. (1985) reported affected sibs. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) identified CTNS mutations in 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis. Two of the mutations had not been described before. In these patients, the disease resulted either from 1 mild mutation combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis or from homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation. Transfection of cystinotic fibroblasts with normal cDNA partially corrected their phenotype, as did transfection with a cDNA derived from a mutation causing intermediate cystinosis. ⚠︎

Attard et al. (1999) screened patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, patients with late-onset cystinosis, and patients whose phenotype did not fit the classic definitions. They found 23 different CTNS mutations, 14 of which were novel. In 2 late-onset patients, the mutations affected regions of cystinosin that were predicted to be functionally unimportant, which the authors proposed accounted for the milder phenotype. In 3 patients with onset before 7 years of age but a milder course, the authors suggested that the missense mutations they carried allowed production of functional protein.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) compared mutations across cystinosis phenotypes. Of 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, 12 had 2 severely truncating CTNS mutations, consistent with a loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions, which were predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains and abolish protein function. In contrast, the mutations in 2 patients with late-onset disease affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin. Thoene et al. (1999) found that intermediate cystinosis results from at least 1 mild mutation, either combined with a mutation causing nephropathic cystinosis or in the homozygous state.
