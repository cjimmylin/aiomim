### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene.

### Description

Brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is an autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease. Rutten et al. (2024) described it as a triad of movement disorders beginning in mid-adulthood, numerous and massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and intracerebral hemorrhage. Rendu et al. (2019) reported neuropsychiatric features, cystic lesions in the basal ganglia, and a positive urine sulfite test in affected individuals. They linked the positive test to urinary accumulation of deaminated glutathione resulting from loss of NIT1 function.

### Nomenclature

Rendu et al. (2019) called the disorder in their family a familial deep cavitating state. They suggested that the basal ganglia cavities might correspond to the 'cerebral porosis' of the historical classification of lacunar brain lesions. Rutten et al. (2024) used the term NIT1-small vessel disease (NIT1-SVD).

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) reported 3 affected brothers from 1 family. The index patient (II.4) presented at 34 years of age with a sudden pure motor left hemiparesis, and a stroke was suspected. He had a history of nonsevere mental retardation. Two years earlier he had been admitted to a psychiatry unit for an acute psychotic episode followed by generalized seizures. CT and MRI showed small bilateral cystic lesions in the basal ganglia. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and echocardiography were normal. A dipstick urine test for sulfites was positive. Over a 20-year period his condition worsened, with severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia, and he died in 2011. One of his brothers (II.5) had a history of diabetes mellitus and had led an active professional life. At 58 years of age he was admitted to intensive care with a sudden coma and cardiovascular collapse. His CT and MRI showed the same basal ganglia abnormalities, described as a deep cavitating state, and his urine sulfite test was positive. He recovered progressively but had neuropsychological deterioration (BREF 14/18, MMSE 22/30). A third brother (II.7) had been diagnosed with schizophrenia 40 years earlier. His clinical examination was normal, but CT and MRI showed the same brain lesions, and his urine sulfite test was positive. The mother and the unaffected sibs who were tested had no symptoms and negative urine sulfite tests. The authors noted that the basal ganglia location distinguished these cavities from the cavitary lesions frequently reported in adults. They also excluded sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency, which they described as the only previously known disorders combining brain damage with a positive urinary sulfite test.

Rutten et al. (2024) studied 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees: 3 sibs and 4 single cases. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous and massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia. Three patients had a nonlobar intracerebral hemorrhage between 45 and 60 years of age, which was fatal in 2. At brain autopsy, large electron-dense deposits were seen in the walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries.

### Biochemical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) performed qualitative organic acid analysis on urine by GC/MS. All 3 affected brothers had an unusual compound whose mass spectrum matched a deaminated glutathione (dGSH) derivative, the same compound found in the urine of Nit1-knockout mice. The compound was undetectable in the mother and in the other sibs tested. The authors proposed that dGSH accumulation probably caused the positive urine sulfite test. In fibroblasts from patient II.5, cytoplasmic Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was unchanged, whereas mitochondrial MnSOD activity was increased twofold compared with controls. Rutten et al. (2024) reported that metabolic analysis of patient urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Rendu et al. (2019) proposed autosomal recessive inheritance based on 3 affected brothers who were compound heterozygous for NIT1 mutations. Rutten et al. (2024) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance, finding biallelic NIT1 variants in 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Rendu et al. (2019) performed exome sequencing in 2 affected brothers (II.5 and II.7). Both were compound heterozygous for 2 mutations in the NIT1 gene (NM_005600): c.457G-A, which causes a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution, and c.670dupA, which causes a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41). The c.670dupA mutation was inherited from the asymptomatic mother; the father's DNA was not available. Molecular cloning of a PCR fragment from patient II.5 that spanned both changes showed that they lay on different alleles. The asymptomatic sibs whose DNA was available carried neither change. The c.457G-A change affects the last nucleotide of exon 4, and sequencing of NIT1 transcripts from a skin biopsy of patient II.5 showed that it altered splicing. Western blot found no detectable NIT1 protein in fibroblasts from patient II.5.

Rutten et al. (2024) used exome sequencing to identify biallelic NIT1 variants in 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees. The first patients identified were 3 sibs who were compound heterozygous for a c.727C-T transition, causing an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and a c.198_199del, causing a premature stop (Ala68Ter). The other 4 patients were single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees, and all were homozygous for the R243W variant. Western blotting showed no NIT1 protein in patient fibroblasts.

### Pathogenesis

Rendu et al. (2019) noted that NIT1 is an amidase that recycles deaminated glutathione (dGSH) into 2-ketoglutarate and cysteinylglycine. They proposed that loss of NIT1 function leads to accumulation of dGSH, a possibly toxic metabolite. In their view, this could interfere with glutathione metabolism and, over the long term, cause redox damage. They cited the increased mitochondrial SOD activity in patient fibroblasts as consistent with an adaptive response to oxidative stress. They stated that the mechanisms underlying the neuronal dysfunction required further investigation.
