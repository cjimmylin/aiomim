### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder. It begins with infantile hypotonia that progresses to spasticity of the lower limbs or of all 4 limbs, and it is accompanied by severe intellectual disability, poor or absent speech, microcephaly, inability to walk, stereotypic laughter, and drooling. Some patients have seizures, short stature, and brain imaging abnormalities such as ventriculomegaly, cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, and white matter loss (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). The disorder results from deficiency of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4), of which AP4E1 encodes the epsilon subunit (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) described the disorder in their family as an autosomal recessive form of spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy. Because families with disruption of different AP-4 subunits had very similar clinical features, they proposed the term 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome' for the disorder caused by disruption of any of the 4 AP-4 subunits. Kong et al. (2013) noted that the disorders caused by mutation in the 4 AP-4 subunit genes had been designated SPG47 (AP4B1), SPG50 (AP4M1), SPG51 (AP4E1), and SPG52 (AP4S1), and regarded AP-4 deficiency syndrome as a subtype of hereditary spastic paraplegia.

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs, a female and a male, from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. Their condition had been described as cerebral palsy. Both were born at term after uneventful pregnancies and had microcephaly and hypotonia from birth or shortly after. The female (patient IV-4) developed spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia by age 1 year. She never developed speech or independent walking, had been wheelchair-bound since age 5 years, and began having generalized tonic-clonic seizures at that age. When examined at age 23 years, she had microcephaly (-3 SD), profoundly impaired cognition, adaptive skills below the 1-year-old level, drooling, and outbursts of stereotypic laughter. Her dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face with a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum with a full everted upper vermilion, prominent antihelices, and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was most pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing of the background. Her brother (patient IV-5) had developmental delay that was evident by age 1 year. He acquired some dysarthric words but never normal speech. He walked with support until increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. At age 22 years, he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia and hyperreflexia, stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, and severe cognitive deficits. His dysmorphic features and EEG were very similar to his sister's, and head CT showed moderate ventriculomegaly with slightly prominent cisterns. Both sibs had a Babinski sign, lacked sphincter control, and had normal eye and hearing evaluations. Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that these features closely resembled those of a previously reported sibship with AP4M1 mutation, except that epilepsy was present only in their family.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families with AP-4 deficiency. One family had a mutation in AP4E1 and the others had mutations in AP4S1 or AP4B1. Across the 3 families, the patients had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. Neonatal asphyxia was noted at delivery. When examined at age 3 years, the twins had marked developmental retardation, microcephaly (head circumference -2 SD and -2.5 SD), inability to walk unaided, abnormal speech, and severe intellectual disability. They smiled or laughed for no obvious reason and drooled abnormally. Both had early infantile muscular hypotonia and spastic paraplegia limited to the lower extremities; hypertonia, hyperreflexia, a Babinski sign, and seizures were absent. Both had a prominent bulbous nose, a wide mouth, and coarse features. Both were of short stature with low body weight and lacked sphincter control. Pseudobulbar signs included jaw jerk and gag reflex. CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. One twin had left-sided conductive deafness. Both twins had been vaccinated with BCG a few days after birth. At 9 months of age, each developed unilateral enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph nodes, which were removed surgically; biopsy showed acid-fast bacilli consistent with mycobacterial infection. Immunologic studies found no abnormality of the IL-12/IFN-gamma axis or of the oxidative burst.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 in the families reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) and Kong et al. (2013) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the family of Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), the homozygous deletion was found in both affected sibs, and the mother was a heterozygous carrier. The father was inferred to be a carrier because SNRPN methylation analysis showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15. In the family of Kong et al. (2013), both parents were heterozygous for the mutation and a healthy sib was homozygous wildtype.

### Heterogeneity

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that a homozygous mutation in AP4M1, which encodes another AP-4 subunit, had been reported in an autosomal recessive form of spastic cerebral palsy with clinical features similar to those in their AP4E1 family. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified mutations in AP4S1 and AP4B1, as well as AP4E1, in families with a clinically recognizable AP-4 deficiency syndrome. Kong et al. (2013) noted that mutations in AP4B1, AP4M1, AP4E1, and AP4S1 cause SPG47, SPG50, SPG51, and SPG52, respectively, and that the patients have a uniform clinical phenotype.

### Molecular Genetics

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used chromosomal microarray analysis in 2 sibs with SPG51 from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family and identified a homozygous deletion on chromosome 15q21.2. High-resolution array analysis sized the deletion at 192 kb, and FISH confirmed it. The deletion included the 5-prime ends of 2 genes, AP4E1 and SPPL2A. The authors could not completely exclude a contribution from loss of SPPL2A but proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main cause of the phenotype. Their reasoning was the clinical similarity to a family with an AP4M1 mutation and evidence that loss of any single AP-4 subunit disrupts the whole complex.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families with AP-4 deficiency using autozygosity mapping combined with either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. They identified 1 mutation in each of 3 AP-4 subunit genes. The AP4E1 mutation was a splice mutation (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA) that resulted in r.421_542del and was predicted to cause a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20).

Kong et al. (2013) performed whole-exome sequencing in Moroccan twins with SPG51 and mycobacterial disease and identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in AP4E1 (R1105X), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family. It was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls from 51 ethnic groups or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient cells, AP4E1 mRNA levels and splicing were normal. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of the AP-4 mu and beta subunits were also reduced. Assembly of the AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower than in controls, and the AP-4 binding partner tepsin was undetectable in patient fibroblasts. The authors found no defects in known genes for Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease and no other plausible candidate variants. They therefore proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease in the twins. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) restudied the Moroccan twins reported by Kong et al. (2013), using whole-exome sequencing and genomewide linkage analysis, and found that they were also homozygous for a splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene (c.733+1G-A) that caused complete skipping of exon 6. A different homozygous SPPL2A splice site mutation was found in an unrelated Turkish boy with BCG disease. Functional studies showed that both SPPL2A mutations caused complete SPPL2a deficiency with accumulation of the CD74 N-terminal fragment. Kong et al. (2018) concluded that autosomal recessive SPPL2a deficiency was a genetic cause of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease. They stated that, in addition, the twins had hereditary spastic paraplegia due to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18) that included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The deletion was detected by chromosomal microarray analysis, refined with a high-resolution array, and confirmed by FISH. The mother was a heterozygous carrier, and the father was inferred to be a carrier.

**.0002 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 4-BP DEL, NT542+1 [NM_007347.3:c.542+1_542+4delGTAA; r.421_542del; p.Glu181Glyfs*20]

In a consanguineous family with AP-4 deficiency, characterized by severe intellectual disability, absent speech, stereotypic laughter, hypotonia progressing to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, and growth retardation, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a splice mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The mutation resulted in r.421_542del and was predicted to cause a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). It was identified using autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger or exome sequencing. ⚠︎

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105X]

In identical twin girls, born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, who had SPG51 and BCG lymphadenitis, Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous, and the mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or 69 Moroccan controls. AP4E1 mRNA levels were normal in patient cells, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, the other AP-4 subunits were reduced, and assembly of the AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower than in controls. Kong et al. (2018) later found that the twins were also homozygous for a splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene, which they identified as a cause of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease. ⚠︎
