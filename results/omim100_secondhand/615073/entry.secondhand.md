### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene on chromosome 18p.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is a form of primary torsion dystonia, in which dystonia, with or without tremor, is the only neurologic sign. It usually begins in adulthood, although onset ranges from childhood to the sixth decade. Onset is most often in the neck. The dystonia frequently spreads to other sites, especially the cranial muscles, and speech is often affected (Bressman et al., 1994; Fuchs et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 members of 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected. Mean age at onset was 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7 affected members the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 28 individuals with definite dystonia and complete clinical information from 8 multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia who carried GNAL mutations. All were Caucasian of mixed European ancestry. Mean age at dystonia onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of carriers, and 93% had neck involvement at final examination. Most patients had progressed to dystonia at other sites, and only 46% had focal dystonia at last examination. Cranial involvement was present in 57% of carriers and speech involvement in 44%. No patient had brachial onset, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement. Other sites of onset included the legs, larynx, jaw, and tongue, and a few patients developed generalized dystonia. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation. Dystonia was inherited from the maternal and paternal sides equally, and Fuchs et al. (2013) saw no apparent phenotypic differences between maternally and paternally inherited cases.

### Inheritance

In the families studied by Fuchs et al. (2013), dystonia-25 was inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Heterozygous GNAL mutations segregated with the disease in all families for which DNA from multiple affected individuals was available.

### Mapping

In a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset, cervical-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia, Bressman et al. (1994) excluded linkage to the region containing the DYT1 locus on chromosome 9q34.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported that a previous whole-genome scan in a family with primary torsion dystonia (family P) had identified 3 regions of possible linkage, with lod scores of 1.5 to 2.1. The GNAL gene, in which Fuchs et al. (2013) found the causative mutation in this family, is located on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci.

### Heterogeneity

Bressman et al. (1994) concluded that a gene other than DYT1 is responsible for some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. In family P, which was of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage, they identified a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the GNAL gene (S293X) in a linkage region. The mutation segregated with dystonia in the family. In family D1 they identified a heterozygous missense mutation in GNAL (V137M) that segregated with the disease. Neither variant was found in about 3,500 European exomes in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project database, and V137M was not found in 572 control chromosomes.

Fuchs et al. (2013) then Sanger-sequenced the coding region of GNAL in 39 additional multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia of mixed European origin who were negative for DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. They found novel heterozygous GNAL mutations in 6 families (19%): a nonsense mutation (R21X), 2 frameshift mutations (S95fsX110 and R198fsX210), a missense mutation (E155K), an in-frame deletion of 3 amino acids (P102-V104del), and a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C). Bioinformatic analysis suggested that the c.274-5T-C change disrupts an acceptor splice site, but Fuchs et al. (2013) could not confirm this because GNAL expression was undetectable in patient lymphoblasts. None of the variants were found in dbSNP135, about 3,500 European exomes, or 572 control chromosomes. The mutations segregated with the disease in all families for which DNA from multiple affected individuals was available. The V137 and E155 residues were completely conserved in GNAL orthologs. In a cell-based BRET assay of Gα-olf coupling to the dopamine D1 receptor, the S293X mutant failed to support any receptor-driven responses. The E155K and V137M mutants showed intermediate phenotypes, largely consistent with impaired association with the G-beta-gamma subunits. A V16F variant found in a single control showed wildtype-like behavior. Fuchs et al. (2013) concluded that the functional assay suggested loss of function, and they predicted that the early truncating mutations would remove essential functional regions of the protein. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) could not discern any genotype/phenotype correlation with regard to mutation type among 8 families with GNAL mutations, and phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that GNAL encodes Gα-olf, a stimulatory G-protein alpha subunit. In striatal medium spiny neurons, Gα-olf replaces Gα-s and couples dopamine D1 receptors and adenosine A2A receptors to the activation of adenylyl cyclase type 5. Fuchs et al. (2013) proposed that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1 receptor and/or A2A receptor transmission as a cause of dystonia. In this model, the primary abnormality lies postsynaptically, in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSTONIA 25**  
GNAL, SER293TER [c.878C>A; p.Ser293*; p.S293X]

Fuchs et al. (2013) used exome sequencing in a family with primary torsion dystonia of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage (family P) and identified a heterozygous c.878C-A transversion in the GNAL gene, resulting in a ser293-to-ter (S293X) substitution. The mutation lay within a linkage region and segregated with dystonia in the family. It was not found in about 3,500 European exomes in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project database. In a BRET assay, the S293X mutant failed to support any dopamine D1 receptor-driven responses. Affected family members had ages at onset of 25 to 48 years. Onset was in the neck in most of them, and 2 had generalized dystonia.

**.0002 DYSTONIA 25**  
GNAL, VAL137MET [c.409G>A; p.Val137Met; p.V137M]

Fuchs et al. (2013) used exome sequencing in a family with primary torsion dystonia (family D1) and identified a heterozygous c.409G-A transition in the GNAL gene, resulting in a val137-to-met (V137M) substitution at a residue completely conserved in GNAL orthologs. The mutation segregated with the disease. It was not found in 572 control chromosomes or in about 3,500 European exomes. In a BRET assay, the mutant showed an intermediate phenotype, largely consistent with impaired association with G-beta-gamma subunits. The 7 affected family members had ages at onset of 7 to 50 years. Onset was in the neck in all but 1, who had leg-onset generalized dystonia.
