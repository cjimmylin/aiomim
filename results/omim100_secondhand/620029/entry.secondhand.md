### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene on chromosome 12p13.33. ⚠︎

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is characterized by global developmental delay or intellectual disability, hypotonia, and delayed or absent language. Many patients have orthopedic abnormalities, such as clubfoot, congenital finger contractures, adducted thumbs, hip dysplasia, kyphoscoliosis, syndactyly, or joint hypermobility. Most patients develop seizures, ranging from refractory neonatal- or infantile-onset epileptic encephalopathy to later-onset focal or generalized seizures. Unlike patients with Timothy syndrome, most patients do not have significant cardiac abnormalities or QT prolongation (Kosaki et al., 2018; Bozarth et al., 2018; Rodan et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old Japanese girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She had profound developmental and speech delay, delayed growth, craniofacial dysmorphism resembling that seen in Timothy syndrome, thin scalp hair, upper limb joint contractures, and several seizure-like episodes. QT prolongation was not found on repeated electrocardiograms. † ⚠︎

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (patient 1), born at 38 weeks' gestation to nonconsanguineous parents of English and Ukrainian ancestry, with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. At birth, her length was 40.6 cm (z-score -3.92). After birth, she had hypoglycemia (30 mg/dL), profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubbed feet, bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, and camptodactyly. Dysmorphic features included micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, very scant hair, and bilateral inverted nipples. She had mild optic nerve hypoplasia, severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, and anal stenosis needing sequential dilation. She needed a gastrostomy tube at 8 months because of severe dysphagia. Seizures began within a few hours of birth, with many subtle tonic seizures per day as well as prolonged tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures. At 3 months, she was diagnosed with epileptic tonic spasms that did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs. Clobazam, started at 7 months, substantially reduced her seizure burden. At 18 months, she still had multiple daily tonic seizures and focal seizures, had made no progress in developmental milestones, had profound hypotonia, could not visually track or fixate, and had a severely disturbed sleep-wake cycle. The first EEG, at 5 days of age, showed diffuse excessive fast activity mixed with other frequencies. This pattern and the lack of normal organization persisted beyond the neonatal period. Polysomnography at 5 months showed severe central and obstructive sleep apnea. Brain MRI at 7 days and 12 months of age was normal. Echocardiography and a 24-hour Holter monitor at 13 months were also normal.

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants and predominantly neurologic manifestations. Fourteen of these individuals (8 females and 6 males in the tabulated data, aged 10 months to 24 years at last examination) had de novo nontruncating variants. Prenatal findings included reduced fetal movement (3 of 14), bilateral clubfoot on prenatal ultrasound (2 of 14), and oligohydramnios (2 of 14). All 14 had a history of developmental delay or intellectual disability, and 2 had a learning disability after early delays. At last follow-up, 9 were nonverbal and 6 were nonambulatory. A 10-month-old infant had severe global developmental delay and could not babble, roll, or sit. One individual had a formal diagnosis of autism, and 6 others had autistic features. Twelve of the 14 had hypotonia, 4 had abnormal balance and coordination, 1 had bilateral vestibular areflexia, 1 had leg spasticity, and 2 had tremor. Ten of the 14 had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years of age. Both generalized and focal epilepsies were reported, including infantile spasms/West syndrome, which evolved into Lennox-Gastaut syndrome in 1 individual. Seizures were fever-sensitive in 3 individuals and medically refractory in 4. EEG findings varied, and 1 individual had excessive diffuse fast (beta) activity. Brain MRI was normal apart from an incidental arachnoid cyst in 1 individual and prominent cerebellar folia in another. Orthopedic abnormalities were present in 12 of the 14, including bilateral clubfoot with congenital finger contractures (2), persistently adducted thumbs (2 additional individuals), kyphosis or scoliosis (2), congenital hip dysplasia (3), syndactyly (2), and joint hypermobility (3). Other features included hyperopia (4) and strabismus (4). Two individuals were microcephalic, and 3 had short stature, including the 2 with clubfoot. Minor facial dysmorphisms were seen in several individuals, but no features were consistent across the group. Of the 11 individuals who had an ECG, 1 had mildly prolonged QTc, with significant QT prolongation and T-wave alternans on Holter monitoring, and 1 had a possible ventricular conduction delay. Echocardiograms were normal in 7 of 9 individuals.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous CACNA1C mutations found in patients with NEDHLSS reported by Kosaki et al. (2018), Bozarth et al. (2018), and Rodan et al. (2021) occurred de novo.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-year-old Japanese girl with NEDHLSS, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (R1024G).

In a girl with NEDHLSS and neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the CACNA1C gene (V1363M). The variant affected a highly conserved residue and was absent from the gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes databases. In a structural model of the protein, the variant was located in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV, facing the sixth transmembrane helix that lines the channel. Exome sequencing also found a de novo heterozygous terminal deletion of the MSH2 gene in this patient.

Rodan et al. (2021) identified de novo heterozygous nontruncating variants in the CACNA1C gene in 14 unrelated individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders: 13 had missense variants and 1 had an in-frame deletion (I743del). The cases were collected through GeneMatcher and ClinVar, and the variants were found by exome sequencing or an intellectual disability gene panel. None of the variants were present in the gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or Exome Variant Server databases. All missense variants affected highly conserved residues and were predicted to be damaging. Missense variants clustered in a linker segment of domain II (exon 13) and in domain IV (exon 35). Whole-cell patch clamp studies of 4 missense variants in HEK293 cells showed different effects on channel function. L1408V reduced current density to about 30% of control without changing protein expression. L657F significantly increased calcium current, with about 2-fold higher protein levels. L614P caused a modest increase in current that was not statistically significant. L614R had no significant effect. Larger currents with L614P and L657F were accompanied by a hyperpolarizing shift in the voltage dependence of activation. Rodan et al. (2021) noted that the L614R experiments used the CACNB2 accessory subunit, whereas the other variants were tested with CACNB3.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with heterozygous truncating CACNA1C variants, including 1 canonical splice variant. Three of these variants were paternally inherited and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families. Compared with individuals who had nontruncating variants, most individuals with truncating variants had essentially normal early motor development and walked independently. They had a more selective developmental impairment in expressive language: 10 of 11 had delayed expressive language. Autism was more common, affecting 5 of 11. Only 1 of 11 had a history of seizures, and only 3 had hypotonia. Family members carrying the same truncating variant showed variable penetrance and expressivity.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

CACNA1C, ARG1024GLY [p.Arg1024Gly]

In a 5-year-old Japanese girl with NEDHLSS, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous arg1024-to-gly (R1024G) mutation in the CACNA1C gene. She had cutaneous syndactyly, profound developmental and speech delay, pulmonary hypertension, upper limb joint contractures, and seizure-like episodes. She did not have QT prolongation. † ⚠︎

**.0002 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, VAL1363MET [NM_000719.6:c.4087G>A; p.Val1363Met]

In a girl with NEDHLSS and neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.4087G-A transition (c.4087G-A, NM_000719.6) in exon 33 of the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a val1363-to-met (V1363M) substitution in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV. The residue is highly conserved, and the variant was absent from the gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes databases. Functional studies were not performed. The patient had profound hypotonia, bilateral clubfeet, finger and toe syndactyly, camptodactyly, and refractory seizures, and her cardiac evaluation was normal.

**.0003 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, LEU614ARG [c.1841T>G; p.Leu614Arg]

In a 2.5-year-old boy (P7) with NEDHLSS, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1841T-G transversion in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu614-to-arg (L614R) substitution at the apex of the domain II voltage sensor. He had severe developmental delay, hypotonia, myoclonic and generalized tonic-clonic seizures beginning at 10 months, bilateral clubfoot, adducted thumbs, and strabismus. He was nonverbal and began walking at 3 years. In whole-cell patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells, the variant did not cause a statistically significant change in current or in voltage-dependent gating.

**.0004 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, LEU614PRO [c.1841T>C; p.Leu614Pro]

In a 24-year-old man (P8) with NEDHLSS, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1841T-C transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu614-to-pro (L614P) substitution. He had intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, fever-sensitive generalized epilepsy beginning at 1 year of age, and kyphosis. He was nonverbal and nonambulatory. In whole-cell patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells, the variant caused a modest current increase that was not statistically significant, along with a hyperpolarizing shift in the voltage dependence of activation. Protein levels were similar to wildtype.

**.0005 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, LEU657PHE [c.1969C>T; p.Leu657Phe]

In a 15-year-old girl (P9) with NEDHLSS, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1969C-T transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu657-to-phe (L657F) substitution in segment S5 of domain II. She had moderate to severe intellectual disability, language delay, behavioral arrest spells with or without head or limb jerking beginning at 6 years, small-appearing feet, and tapered fingers. In whole-cell patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells, the variant significantly increased calcium current, shifted the voltage dependence of activation in a hyperpolarizing direction, and raised protein levels about 2-fold, consistent with a gain of function.

**.0006 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, LEU1408VAL [c.4222C>G; p.Leu1408Val]

In a 6.8-year-old girl (P12) with NEDHLSS, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.4222C-G transversion in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu1408-to-val (L1408V) substitution. She had severe intellectual disability and hypotonia and was nonverbal and nonambulatory. Myoclonic seizures beginning at 6 months evolved into a generalized epilepsy syndrome. She also had kyphoscoliosis, coxa valga, strabismus, and a possible ventricular conduction delay. In whole-cell patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells, current density was reduced to about 30% of control with unchanged protein expression, consistent with a loss of function.

**.0007 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, VAL1411LEU [c.4231G>C; c.4231G>T; p.Val1411Leu]

Rodan et al. (2021) identified 2 different de novo heterozygous nucleotide changes in the CACNA1C gene that cause the same val1411-to-leu (V1411L) substitution in 2 unrelated patients with NEDHLSS. A 7.8-year-old girl (P13) with a c.4231G-C transversion had severe intellectual disability, hypotonia, and cortical visual impairment. She had infantile spasms beginning at 11 months that evolved into Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. She also had prolonged QT. A 10-month-old boy (P14) with a c.4231G-T transversion had severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, and generalized epilepsy beginning at 2 months. He also had bilateral congenital hip dysplasia, bilateral 2-3 toe syndactyly, bilateral adducted thumbs, and a borderline QTc of 450 msec.
