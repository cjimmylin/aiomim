### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene (PRPH2; also called RDS) on chromosome 6p. Homozygous mutation in PRPH2 has been found in patients with a severe early-onset form of the disorder. A digenic form of RP7 is caused by double heterozygosity for mutations in the PRPH2 and ROM1 genes. ⚠︎

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is a form of retinitis pigmentosa, a photoreceptor degeneration, caused by mutation in the gene that encodes peripherin/RDS, a photoreceptor-specific glycoprotein. It is usually inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with moderate severity, and its expression varies widely, even within families, where retinitis pigmentosa may occur alongside macular dystrophies (Kajiwara et al., 1991; Farrar et al., 1991; Weleber et al., 1993; Manes et al., 2015). Digenic inheritance has been described: only individuals heterozygous for mutations in both PRPH2 and ROM1 develop retinitis pigmentosa (Kajiwara et al., 1994). Homozygous PRPH2 mutations cause a severe early-onset retinal dystrophy with Leber congenital amaurosis or juvenile retinitis pigmentosa phenotypes (Wang et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Farrar et al. (1991) and Jordan et al. (1992) designated the autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa locus that they mapped to chromosome 6p in an Irish pedigree as RP6. The abstract of Farrar et al. (1991) also refers to this locus as RP5.

### Clinical Features

Farrar et al. (1991) studied a large Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. ⚠︎

Weleber et al. (1993) described 3 different phenotypes in 1 family that segregated a 3-bp deletion in the peripherin/RDS gene. The proband, a 75-year-old woman, presented at age 63 years with a profoundly abnormal electroretinogram (ERG) and adult-onset retinitis pigmentosa. Over 12 years the disease progressed dramatically, with marked loss of peripheral visual field. One daughter developed pattern macular dystrophy at age 31 years. At age 44 her ERG was moderately abnormal, but her clinical disease was limited to the macula. Another daughter presented with macular degeneration at age 42 years and over 10 years developed the clinical picture of fundus flavimaculatus. Her peripheral visual field was preserved, but her ERG was moderately abnormal. The son developed macular degeneration at age 44 years and had pericentral scotomas and a markedly abnormal ERG. Fluorescein angiography showed punctate pigment epithelial transmission defects. ⚠︎

Kikawa et al. (1994) reported autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa associated with bull's-eye maculopathy in patients carrying a mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene.

Wang et al. (2013) described 3 patients homozygous for PRPH2 mutations. Two had a clinical diagnosis of Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) and 1 had juvenile retinitis pigmentosa. All 3 had visual impairment within the first year of life, nondetectable or reduced ERGs, and a very similar maculopathy. The 2 patients with LCA also had nystagmus. In patient 1318, photographed at age 29 years, the fundus showed a prominent multilobulated central atrophic maculopathy surrounded by concentric rings of yellow deposits, with vessel narrowing and fine diffuse peripheral retinal changes. In patient 3256, photographed at age 66 years, the fundus showed peripheral and macular pigment deposits, choroidal sclerosis, vessel narrowing, optic disc pallor, and an extensive central atrophic maculopathy, and optical coherence tomography showed unusual globular lesions in the foveal region. In patient 741, examined at age 30 years, there was diffuse retinal dystrophy with retinal vessel narrowing, retinal pigment epithelium mottling and loss, and a multilobulated maculopathy. Visual acuity in this patient was 20/40. Family members heterozygous for these mutations were asymptomatic but had detectable maculopathy. The 56-year-old father of patient 741 had macular pattern dystrophy with clear-cut foveal changes and essentially normal visual acuity. At age 57 years, the mother of patient 1318 had 20/20 visual acuity, a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy, and retinal flecks. The 7-year-old son of patient 1318 showed a miniature form of foveal butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy.

Manes et al. (2015) studied the clinical features of French patients with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa due to PRPH2 mutations. Disease severity was moderate: 78% of eyes had a visual acuity of 1 to 0.5, and 52% of eyes retained more than 50% of the visual field. Some patients had characteristic vitelliform deposits or macular involvement. In some families, some members had pericentral retinitis pigmentosa or macular dystrophy and others had widespread retinitis pigmentosa. Manes et al. (2015) concluded that PRPH2 mutations cause highly variable phenotypes and moderate forms of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, including mild cases that might be underdiagnosed.

### Inheritance

Kajiwara et al. (1991) and Farrar et al. (1991) showed that mutations in the peripherin/RDS gene cosegregated with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with digenic inheritance, in which only individuals heterozygous for mutations in both ROM1 and peripherin/RDS developed retinitis pigmentosa. Wang et al. (2013) reported a severe early-onset form caused by homozygous PRPH2 mutations. In these families, heterozygous relatives were asymptomatic but had milder macular findings.

### Mapping

In a large Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, Farrar et al. (1991) found positive 2-point lod scores with 5 chromosome 6p markers: D6S89 (Z = 3.338 at theta = 0.10), D6S109 (Z = 3.932 at theta = 0.10), D6S105 (Z = 6.081 at theta = 0.00), HLA-DRA (Z = 4.364 at theta = 0.00), and RDS (Z = 5.376 at theta = 0.00). Overlapping multipoint analyses gave a maximum lod score of 6.6 at HLA-DRA. These results provided evidence for an additional autosomal dominant RP locus and implicated the human homolog of the mouse rds gene, peripherin-rds, as a candidate gene. ⚠︎

In the same Irish family, Jordan et al. (1992) found that an intragenic marker from the peripherin-RDS gene cosegregated with the disease locus with zero recombination (lod score of 5.46 at theta = 0.00). They mapped the peripherin-RDS gene relative to other 6p markers using the CEPH reference panel. A multipoint analysis that included these data gave a lod score of 8.21, maximizing at the peripherin-RDS locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Kajiwara et al. (1991) identified 3 mutations in the human homolog of the mouse rds gene (RDS) that cosegregated with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa in separate families.

In a large Irish pedigree with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa linked to 6p, Farrar et al. (1991) identified a 3-bp deletion in the peripherin gene. The deletion removed 1 of a pair of highly conserved cysteine residues in the predicted third transmembrane domain of peripherin. It segregated with the disease and was not found in unaffected controls.

In a family in which the mother had retinitis pigmentosa and her children had pattern macular dystrophy, fundus flavimaculatus, or macular degeneration, Weleber et al. (1993) identified a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene.

By screening families with autosomal dominant retinal dystrophies, Wells et al. (1993) found a deletion of a cysteine at codon 118/119 of the RDS gene in a family with retinitis pigmentosa. In other families with macular dystrophies, they found different RDS mutations (see GENOTYPE/PHENOTYPE CORRELATIONS).

Using nonradioisotopic SSCP, Kikawa et al. (1994) detected an asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene in autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa associated with bull's-eye maculopathy.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with mutations in 2 unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes, ROM1 and peripherin/RDS, in which only double heterozygotes developed retinitis pigmentosa. In these families, the leu185-to-pro (L185P) mutation in peripherin/RDS was inherited together with heterozygous mutations in ROM1. †

Wang et al. (2013) performed targeted next-generation sequencing of 163 retinal disease genes in 179 unrelated, prescreened patients with a clinical diagnosis of LCA or juvenile retinitis pigmentosa. Two patients with LCA, 1318 and 3256, were homozygous for previously reported PRPH2 missense mutations: c.637T-C (C213R) and c.554T-C (L185P), respectively. Wang et al. (2013) then sequenced PRPH2 in another 135 unsolved patients with LCA or juvenile retinitis pigmentosa. A third patient (741), who had juvenile retinitis pigmentosa, was homozygous for the L185P mutation. The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and segregation was studied in the families. The parents of patient 741 and the parents and son of patient 1318 were heterozygous. The unaffected brother of patient 1318 was homozygous wildtype. Wang et al. (2013) noted that the C213R mutation had been associated with autosomal dominant pattern dystrophy. The L185P mutation, together with a null ROM1 mutation, had been reported to cause digenic retinitis pigmentosa.

Manes et al. (2015) used Sanger sequencing to screen PRPH2 in a cohort of 310 French families with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. They identified 15 mutations, 6 novel and 9 previously reported, in 32 families. A novel recurrent mutation, leu254-to-gln (L254Q), caused protein aggregation when peripherin-2 was expressed in yeast, suggesting abnormal folding.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wells et al. (1993) found that RDS mutations can cause either retinitis pigmentosa or macular dystrophy. A cysteine deletion at codon 118/119 was associated with retinitis pigmentosa. Three families with similar macular dystrophy had mutations at codon 172: arginine was replaced by tryptophan in 2 families and by glutamine in 1. A stop codon at position 258 was found in a family with adult vitelliform macular dystrophy. Wells et al. (1993) suggested that certain amino acids in peripherin-RDS may have different functional significance in cones and rods.

Weleber et al. (1993) found that a single 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene produced clinically disparate phenotypes within 1 family: retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus.

Wang et al. (2013) found that patients homozygous for PRPH2 mutations had severe and fairly consistent early-onset phenotypes. Their heterozygous relatives were asymptomatic but had macular pattern dystrophy or other maculopathy. Wang et al. (2013) attributed the difference to a dose-dependent effect. They noted that individuals heterozygous for the L185P mutation in previously reported digenic RP families had been described as asymptomatic. They suggested that such individuals should be expected to show a subclinical phenotype and might develop asymptomatic macular flecks later in life.

### Population Genetics

Manes et al. (2015) found PRPH2 mutations in 32 of 310 French families with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, a prevalence of 10.3%. This figure was higher than previously reported (0 to 8%) and made PRPH2 the second most frequent autosomal dominant RP gene after RHO in their series.

### Animal Model

Kajiwara et al. (1991) noted that the murine retinal degeneration slow (rds) mutation is semidominant. In this phenotype, rod and cone photoreceptors develop abnormally and then slowly degenerate, which made it a possible model for retinitis pigmentosa.

Wang et al. (2013) noted that the rds/rds mouse, which is homozygous for a null mutation, fails to develop photoreceptor outer segments and has early-onset severe retinal degeneration, whereas the heterozygous rds/+ mouse has milder retinal degeneration. They suggested that patients with homozygous PRPH2 mutations could be modeled by the rds/rds mouse.

Wang et al. (2013) found a homozygous c.554T-C transition, resulting in a leu185-to-pro (L185P) substitution in PRPH2, in a patient with LCA (3256) and in a patient with juvenile retinitis pigmentosa (741). Both parents of patient 741 were heterozygous. The 56-year-old father had macular pattern dystrophy with essentially normal visual acuity. The affected residue is conserved across species.

Using targeted next-generation sequencing, Wang et al. (2013) found a homozygous c.637T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a cys213-to-arg (C213R) substitution, in a patient with LCA (1318). The mutation had previously been associated with autosomal dominant pattern dystrophy. The patient's mother and son were heterozygous carriers. The 57-year-old mother was asymptomatic, with 20/20 visual acuity, but had a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy. The 7-year-old son had a miniature form of foveal butterfly-shaped pattern dystrophy. An unaffected brother was homozygous wildtype. The affected residue is conserved across species.
