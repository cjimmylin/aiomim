### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Sedaghatian type (SMDS), is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the GPX4 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia is a neonatal lethal form of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. It is characterized by severe metaphyseal chondrodysplasia with mild limb shortening, platyspondyly, cardiac conduction defects, and central nervous system abnormalities (Smith et al., 2014).

### Nomenclature

Sedaghatian (1980) first described the disorder under the name congenital lethal metaphyseal chondrodysplasia. Opitz et al. (1987) called it Sedaghatian congenital lethal metaphyseal chondrodysplasia and Sedaghatian syndrome. Peeden et al. (1992) used the name spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Sedaghatian type.

### Clinical Features

Sedaghatian (1980) reported 2 Iranian brothers, born within a year of each other, who had a similar skeletal disorder with severe congenital metaphyseal involvement, mild rhizomelic shortness of the upper limbs, and mild platyspondyly. Both died at 3 days of age of cardiorespiratory insufficiency. Only 1 had laboratory tests before death. These showed low calcium, high phosphorus, and high alkaline phosphatase levels, which were attributed to a renal defect. At autopsy, the same infant had pulmonary, renal, and adrenal hemorrhage, as well as subendocardial myocarditis and myocardial necrosis. The parents were normal and had recently had an affected baby girl. Peeden et al. (1992) noted that 1 of Sedaghatian's patients had cortical necrosis of the kidneys at autopsy and showed 'laciness' of the iliac wings on radiographs. Opitz et al. (1987) noted that 1 of Sedaghatian's patients also had 'microphthalmia, asymmetry of ears, depressed nasal bridge, broad nose, short neck, prominent sternum, and short lower extremities.' ⚠︎

Opitz et al. (1987) described another Iranian infant with the Sedaghatian syndrome. The infant died on day 4, and histologic studies showed severe epimetaphyseal dysplasia. Opitz et al. (1987) suggested that the syndrome may have more complex pleiotropic effects than the radiographs suggest.

Peeden et al. (1992) reported another infant with spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Sedaghatian type, who had a lethal course.

Elcioglu and Hall (1998) presented the radiologic findings in 2 unrelated patients with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. From these patients and a review of the literature, they identified additional anomalies: disproportionately long fibulae, dysharmonious maturation, and turricephaly.

Koutouby et al. (2000) reported an infant born to nonconsanguineous Yemeni parents whose radiologic findings were consistent with Sedaghatian-type lethal metaphyseal chondrodysplasia. All previously reported patients had died within 4 days of life, but this patient survived 161 days. The patient underwent extensive investigation, including serum calcium, magnesium, zinc, ammonia, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, and parathormone levels; liver and renal function tests; TORCH and metabolic screening; skeletal survey; chromosome studies; muscle enzymes; EEG; and CT and MRI of the brain.

English et al. (2006) reported a female infant, born to nonconsanguineous Caucasian parents, who had metaphyseal dysplasia and a neuronal migration abnormality consistent with a diagnosis of Sedaghatian spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. She was hypotonic from birth and had recurrent cyanotic episodes within a few hours of delivery. Brain imaging showed absence of the corpus callosum and marked frontotemporal pachygyria. She developed seizures on day 14 of life and died at 17 days of age. English et al. (2006) suggested that agenesis of the corpus callosum is a novel association of the disorder and that neuronal migration abnormalities can occur in it.

Aygun et al. (2012) reported a patient with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia who had metaphyseal dysplasia, congenital atrioventricular block, a simplified gyral pattern, hypogenesis of the corpus callosum, and severe cerebellar hypoplasia. They emphasized that central nervous system malformations might be a major component of this congenital lethal skeletal dysplasia, whose etiology was unknown at the time.

### Inheritance

Sedaghatian (1980) proposed autosomal recessive inheritance because the parents of the affected brothers were normal and had recently had an affected daughter. Opitz et al. (1987) supported autosomal recessive inheritance, citing affected children of both sexes in 1 sibship and the birth of an affected infant to normal first-cousin parents. ⚠︎

Smith et al. (2014) found that an affected child carried 2 GPX4 mutations, one inherited from her mother and the other de novo. In a second family, both unaffected parents were heterozygous for the same GPX4 mutation.

### Molecular Genetics

Koutouby et al. (2000) submitted genomic DNA from their patient and the parents for analysis, but no abnormal gene was detected.

Smith et al. (2014) studied 2 unrelated families with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia as part of the FORGE Canada Consortium. Whole-exome sequencing of an affected girl and her unaffected parents identified 2 rare variants in the GPX4 gene: c.587+5G-A, inherited from the mother, and c.588-8_588-4del, which was de novo (NM_001039848.1). Both variants were predicted to affect splicing. In vitro studies confirmed that the first mutation caused part of exon 4 to be spliced out and the second caused skipping of exon 5. Both resulted in a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4. In a second family, DNA from the affected child was not available. Sanger sequencing showed that both unaffected parents were heterozygous for the same stop mutation in exon 3 of GPX4 (c.381C-A, Y127X).

### Population Genetics

At the time of their report, the disorder appeared to be confined to Iranians. Opitz et al. (1987) suggested that the gene frequency is high in this population.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPONDYLOMETAPHYSEAL DYSPLASIA, SEDAGHATIAN TYPE**  
GPX4, 587+5G-A [NM_001039848.1:c.587+5G>A]

Smith et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to study a girl with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. They found that she was compound heterozygous for a c.587+5G-A mutation in the GPX4 gene (NM_001039848.1), inherited from her mother, and a de novo 5-bp deletion (c.588-8_588-4del). In vitro studies showed that the c.587+5G-A mutation caused part of exon 4 to be spliced out, resulting in a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4.

**.0002 SPONDYLOMETAPHYSEAL DYSPLASIA, SEDAGHATIAN TYPE**  
GPX4, 5-BP DEL, 588-8 [NM_001039848.1:c.588-8_588-4del]

Smith et al. (2014) found that a girl with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia was compound heterozygous for a de novo 5-bp deletion (c.588-8_588-4del) in the GPX4 gene (NM_001039848.1) and a maternally inherited c.587+5G-A mutation. In vitro studies showed that the deletion caused skipping of exon 5, resulting in a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4.

**.0003 SPONDYLOMETAPHYSEAL DYSPLASIA, SEDAGHATIAN TYPE**  
GPX4, TYR127TER [NM_001039848.1:c.381C>A; p.Tyr127*]

Smith et al. (2014) studied a family with a child who had Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. DNA from the child was not available, but Sanger sequencing showed that both unaffected parents were heterozygous for a c.381C-A transversion in exon 3 of the GPX4 gene (NM_001039848.1), resulting in a tyr127-to-ter (Y127X) substitution.
