### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A (JEB5A) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding integrin beta-4 (ITGB4) on chromosome 17q. ⚠︎

### Description

Intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A (JEB5A) is an autosomal recessive mucocutaneous fragility disorder caused by mutation in the ITGB4 gene. It is a nonlethal form, unlike the usually fatal ITGB4-related epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia. Patients have mild skin blistering. Pyloric atresia may or may not be present, and urologic involvement and nail dystrophy have been reported (Mellerio et al., 1998; Inoue et al., 2000). † ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Has et al. (2020) published a consensus reclassification of inherited epidermolysis bullosa (EB) and other disorders with skin fragility. It classifies EB using both clinical and genetic features.

### Clinical Features

Mellerio et al. (1998) reported 2 unrelated British boys with junctional epidermolysis bullosa associated with pyloric atresia (EB-PA). Both survived into early childhood and had mild cutaneous involvement. The first patient had pyloric atresia. The second patient had mild skin fragility. ✗

Inoue et al. (2000) described a patient with non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia. The patient had chronic skin fragility, nail dystrophy, hair loss, and recurrent urethral stenosis. The authors suggested that pyloric atresia is not necessarily a feature of integrin-associated junctional EB, whereas urologic problems are common. †

Jonkman et al. (2002) reported a 49-year-old woman who had had mild blistering of the hands and feet since birth. She also had nail dystrophy with onychogryposis and enamel hypoplasia. She had no alopecia and no history of pyloric atresia. Electron microscopy and antigen mapping of a skin blister showed an intraepidermal split, low in the basal keratinocytes through the attachment plaque of the hemidesmosome. On immunofluorescence microscopy, monoclonal antibody 450-11A, which recognizes the third fibronectin type III repeat in the intracellular domain of integrin beta-4, did not bind. Binding of monoclonal antibodies against amino- and carboxy-terminal epitopes was reduced. The authors considered the phenotype to be mild, with predominant features of epidermolysis bullosa simplex.

### Inheritance

Mellerio et al. (1998) noted that junctional epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Their patients with the mild form were compound heterozygous for ITGB4 mutations, and the patient reported by Inoue et al. (2000) was homozygous for an ITGB4 mutation. Both findings are consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 unrelated patients with mild junctional epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia, Mellerio et al. (1998) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the ITGB4 gene. The first patient carried a splice site mutation in intron 30 (3793+1G-A) and a nonsense mutation in exon 36 (W1478X). The second patient carried a missense mutation in exon 3 (C38R) and a 1-bp deletion in exon 36 (4776delG). The nonsense and deletion mutations were predicted to markedly reduce beta-4 integrin mRNA levels. The authors suggested that the missense or splice site mutation on the second allele might allow synthesis of some functional, though perturbed, beta-4 polypeptide. ⚠︎

In a patient with non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia, Inoue et al. (2000) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the ITGB4 gene, gly931-to-asp (G931D), in exon 25. The mutation is located in the cytoplasmic tail of beta-4 integrin and disrupted hemidesmosome assembly. †

In a 49-year-old woman with mild blistering and predominant features of epidermolysis bullosa simplex, Jonkman et al. (2002) identified a heterozygous 2-bp deletion in the ITGB4 gene (4733delCT). The deletion caused in-frame skipping of exon 36, predicted to remove 50 amino acids (residues 1450-1499) within the third fibronectin type III repeat of the cytoplasmic domain. Immunoblot analysis showed a beta-4 polypeptide that was 5 kD shorter than normal. No full-size protein was detected in vitro, and the 450-11A epitope was absent in vivo. The authors therefore expected the patient also to be heterozygous for a null allele. They concluded that deletion of the third fibronectin type III repeat, which is thought to interact with BP180/type XVII collagen, is pathogenic.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Pulkkinen et al. (1998) studied the lethal form of epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia. They found that 3 of 4 distinct ITGB4 mutations in their patients were predicted to produce truncated polypeptide chains. In contrast, each of the 2 patients with mild, nonlethal disease reported by Mellerio et al. (1998) carried a missense or splice site mutation on one allele, together with a nonsense or deletion mutation on the other. Mellerio et al. (1998) suggested that the missense or splice site allele may allow synthesis of some functional beta-4 polypeptide.
