### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 (MC1DN7) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NDUFV2 gene.

### Description

Mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 (MC1DN7) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by a defect in NDUFV2, which encodes the 24-kD subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I (NADH dehydrogenase ubiquinone flavoprotein 2). Affected individuals have been reported with early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, truncal hypotonia, and encephalopathy (Benit et al., 2003), and with Leigh syndrome without cardiac involvement (Cameron et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Benit et al. (2003) reported 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family who had complex I deficiency and early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with trunk hypotonia. Benit et al. (2003) noted that whereas mutations in many genes encoding complex I subunits mainly cause neurologic symptoms, the NDUFV2 defect in this family was strikingly associated with cardiomyopathy.

Cameron et al. (2015) reported 2 sibs with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and brain atrophy. Complex I deficiency was diagnosed on the basis of low enzyme activity in muscle and a high lactate/pyruvate ratio in fibroblasts. Cameron et al. (2015) also reported a second, consanguineous family in which 3 of 5 sibs were affected. These patients had Leigh syndrome but no cardiac involvement.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MC1DN7 in the families reported by Benit et al. (2003) and Cameron et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Affected sibs in consanguineous families were homozygous for the mutation, and affected sibs in a third family were compound heterozygous. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Benit et al. (2003) noted that respiratory chain complex I deficiencies are a genetically heterogeneous group of diseases that result from mutations in either mitochondrial or nuclear DNA.

### Molecular Genetics

Benit et al. (2003) studied 3 sibs from a consanguineous family who had complex I deficiency, early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and encephalopathy. Using denaturing high performance liquid chromatography and sequence analysis, they identified a homozygous 4-bp deletion in intron 2 of the NDUFV2 gene (IVS2+5_+8delGTAA). The mutation altered the consensus splice donor site of exon 2 and caused a 70% decrease in NDUFV2 protein, as well as complex I deficiency.

Cameron et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing on fibroblast genomic DNA from 1 of 2 sibs with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, brain atrophy, and complex I deficiency. Both sibs were compound heterozygous for mutations in the NDUFV2 gene. One mutation was an intronic deletion that Cameron et al. (2015) designated c.IVS2+1delGTAA and described as previously reported. The other was a novel insertion (c.669_670insG) predicted to cause a frameshift (Ser224ValfsTer3). Cameron et al. (2015) then screened 10 probands with complex I deficiency from different families. They found homozygosity for the intronic c.IVS2+1delGTAA mutation in a second, consanguineous family, in which 3 of 5 sibs had Leigh syndrome without cardiac involvement.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Cameron et al. (2015) observed that homozygosity for the NDUFV2 intron 2 4-bp deletion caused Leigh syndrome without cardiac involvement in their consanguineous family. In contrast, the same genotype had previously been reported with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, trunk hypotonia, and encephalopathy (Benit et al., 2003). They concluded that the phenotypic spectrum of this mutation includes Leigh syndrome.
