### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome (CLPED1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the PVRL1 gene on chromosome 11q23. Heterozygosity for a PVRL1 mutation has been associated with sporadic nonsyndromic cleft lip/palate (OFC7). †

### Description

Cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its features include cleft lip/palate, ectodermal dysplasia affecting the hair, teeth, and nails, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers and/or toes, and a distinctive facial appearance. Some patients also have mental retardation (Suzuki et al., 1998; Yoshida et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) called the association of autosomal recessive ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, and syndactyly the 'Zlotogora-Ogur syndrome.' Suzuki et al. (1998) used the name Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia (ED4) for the disorder. After they identified the causative gene, Suzuki et al. (2000) renamed the syndrome CLPED1, replacing the earlier designation ED4.

### Clinical Features

Rosselli and Gulienetti (1961) reported 4 patients with hypohidrosis, hypotrichosis, microdontia, dystrophic nails, cleft lip and palate, deformities of the extremities, and malformations of the genitourinary system. The most prominent digital deformity was syndactyly. †

Zlotogora et al. (1987) reported 2 sibs with cleft lip and palate, sparse scalp hair, malformed protruding ears, and partial syndactyly of the fingers and toes. The older child also had mental retardation and pili torti.

Oğur and Yüksel (1988) reported 2 Turkish brothers whose parents were second cousins. Both had tetramelic syndactyly, ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip and palate, renal anomalies, and mental retardation.

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) reported 3 Brazilian brothers born to normal consanguineous parents (F = 1/16). The brothers had ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, syndactyly of fingers 2-3, accessory nipples, and ear anomalies.

Bustos et al. (1991) described 27 affected individuals from 7 families that were very probably related to one another. The families lived in a relatively isolated, inbred population with very small neighboring communities on Margarita Island, in Nueva Esparta State in northeastern Venezuela. Affected individuals had ectodermal dysplasia and other anomalies. Females were affected as severely as males, and expressivity was variable. All parents were normal. The authors could not classify the condition among the known ectodermal dysplasias and considered it a newly recognized dysplasia/malformation syndrome.

Zlotogora (1994) reviewed the association of syndactyly, ectodermal dysplasia, and cleft lip/palate. He noted that affected individuals have distinctive facial features, including an underdeveloped lower jaw. † ⚠︎

Suzuki et al. (1998) described Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia as characterized by unusual facies, dental anomalies, hypotrichosis, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and onychodysplasia, syndactyly, and cleft lip/cleft palate.

Yoshida et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old Japanese boy who was the first child of a consanguineous marriage. He had hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia with sparse, brittle, fine, dry hair and hypodontia. He also had a distinctive facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers, and mild mental retardation. Scanning electron microscopy of the hair showed pili torti and pili trianguli et canaliculi.

### Inheritance

Zlotogora et al. (1987) concluded that the syndrome was most probably inherited as an autosomal recessive disorder, based on its occurrence in 2 sibs. Oğur and Yüksel (1988) and Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) reported affected sibs born to consanguineous parents. Bustos et al. (1991) observed 7 inbred Margarita Island families with normal parents and equal severity in females and males. They concluded that the disorder is an autosomal recessive syndrome. Yoshida et al. (2015) found a homozygous PVRL1 mutation in a child of consanguineous parents, and both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Suzuki et al. (1998) used an affected-only DNA-pooling strategy to map the ED4 gene to chromosome 11q23 by linkage disequilibrium. Haplotype analysis of 4 complex Margarita Island families placed the gene in an interval of approximately 1 to 2 Mb, spanned by just 2 YACs.

### Molecular Genetics

Suzuki et al. (2000) used positional cloning to identify PVRL1 as the gene responsible for CLPED1. PVRL1 encodes nectin-1, an immunoglobulin-related transmembrane cell-cell adhesion molecule that is part of the NAP cell adhesion system. They identified PVRL1 mutations in CLPED families from Margarita Island, Israel, and Brazil. In animal studies, PVRL1 was expressed in the medial edge epithelium of the palatal shelves and in the skin surface epithelium, sites that corresponded to the clinical features of CLPED. †

Sözen et al. (2001) stated that homozygosity for a nonsense mutation in PVRL1, trp185-to-ter (W185X), causes CLPED1 on Margarita Island. They found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for this mutation and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate in northern Venezuela.

Yoshida et al. (2015) studied a Japanese boy with CLPED1 who was the child of consanguineous parents (see Clinical Features). Analysis of exon 2 of PVRL1 showed a novel homozygous nonsense mutation, c.400C-T (R134X). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The authors noted that all 4 PVRL1 mutations reported in CLPED1 up to that time, including this one, produce truncated proteins that lack the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1. These domains are needed to initiate cell-cell adhesion.

### Population Genetics

Bustos et al. (1991) identified the disorder in a relatively isolated and inbred population on Margarita Island, Venezuela. Nectin-1 is also the principal cell surface receptor for alpha-herpesviruses. Suzuki et al. (2000) suggested that the high frequency of CLPED1 on Margarita Island might result from resistance of heterozygotes to infection by these viruses.

They also found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for W185X and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate in northern Venezuela.

**.0002 CLEFT LIP/PALATE-ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA SYNDROME**  
PVRL1, ARG134TER [c.400C>T; p.Arg134*]

Yoshida et al. (2015) studied a 7-year-old Japanese boy with CLPED1 who was born to consanguineous parents. He had hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers, and mild mental retardation. Analysis of exon 2 of PVRL1 showed a homozygous c.400C-T transition, resulting in an arg134-to-ter (R134X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The predicted truncated protein lacks the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1.
