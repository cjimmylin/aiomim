### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding the Na+/glucose cotransporter (SGLT1, or SLC5A1) on chromosome 22q13.1.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disorder that begins in the first weeks of life. The intestine selectively fails to absorb dietary glucose and galactose, which causes severe diarrhea and dehydration. The disorder is usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991; Martin et al., 1996). Intestinal biopsies have shown a specific defect in Na(+)-dependent absorption of glucose in the brush border (Turk et al., 1991).

### Clinical Features

Anderson et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption and her family. Her 5-year-old brother had no symptoms.

Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) described glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family. They found that the severe neonatal diarrhea of the disorder lessened with age. †

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported remission of glucose intolerance in familial glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 Arab children with glucose-galactose malabsorption from 8 different families. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. Their mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. All had had chronic watery diarrhea since birth and failure to thrive. The diagnosis was confirmed by laboratory studies, including small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and a second child was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All affected individuals had classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. Other features at presentation were increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite their illness, irritability, and apathy. When patients were correctly diagnosed and adequately managed, the diarrhea stopped immediately and they were quickly rehydrated, and they then grew and developed normally. When the disease was not recognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) studied glucose transport in jejunal mucosa in vitro and in the kidney in vivo in a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption, her family, and 16 normal volunteers. Biopsy specimens from 6 normal controls and from the girl's asymptomatic 5-year-old brother accumulated glucose to 16 times the concentration in the incubation medium. The proband's mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. Both parents and a half sister had impaired glucose transport, with values between normal and those of the proband. In these 3 heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired but glucose efflux from the mucosa was the same as in 3 normal controls. Kinetic analysis showed reduced capacity (Vmax) but normal affinity (Km) for intestinal glucose transport. All subjects accumulated fructose to a similar degree. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption in the proband. Her brother's titration curve was similar to those of 7 normal volunteers. Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that heterozygotes can be detected and have reduced glucose transport capacity, and that absent intestinal glucose transport is accompanied by partial impairment of renal glucose transport. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded from family studies that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Xin and Wang (2011) found homozygous SLC5A1 mutations in affected members of a large Old Order Amish pedigree, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1991) noted that they had previously shown that the SGLT1 gene lies on the distal q arm of chromosome 22. Using a cosmid probe for fluorescence in situ hybridization, Turk et al. (1993) refined the location of SGLT1 to chromosome 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family with GGM. Sequence analysis identified a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. Xenopus oocytes injected with cRNA carrying the mutation completely lacked Na(+)-dependent glucose transport. Turk et al. (1993) identified this mutation as asp28-to-asn (D28N) in 2 patients and showed in vitro that it abolished SGLT1 transport activity.

Martin et al. (1996) noted that they had previously found a missense mutation in the SGLT1 gene in 2 sisters with GGM. They then screened the gene in 30 new patients and used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype. The title of their report states that defects in both SGLT1 trafficking and SGLT1 function cause GGM.

In 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree, Xin and Wang (2011) sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons of the SLC5A1 gene and their exon-intron boundaries. They found 4 homozygous missense mutations, N51S, A411T, R558H, and H615Q, which cosegregated with the GGM phenotype in all affected individuals.

### Diagnosis

Martin et al. (1996) carried out prenatal screening for GGM in 2 pregnancies in a large consanguineous family in which 2 proband sibs carried the D28N mutation in SGLT1. They PCR-amplified the first exon of the SGLT1 gene from genomic DNA and tested for the D28N mutation by EcoRV restriction digestion. The probands' sib was heterozygous, and a cousin did not carry the mutation. At 2 years of age, both children were healthy and had had no diarrhea.

### Clinical Management

Turk et al. (1991) noted that the severe diarrhea and dehydration of GGM are usually fatal unless glucose and galactose are removed from the diet. In the 8 Arab children reported by Abdullah et al. (1996), all improved clinically on a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up. In Old Order Amish patients, Xin and Wang (2011) found that correct diagnosis and adequate management led to an immediate end to the diarrhea, quick rehydration, and normal growth and development afterward.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) found that SLC5A1 mutations in a large cohort of Old Order Amish patients with GGM indicated a founder effect. They concluded that population-specific genetic testing is needed for early diagnosis, which is critical for a good outcome.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 Arab children with GGM from 8 different families, 6 of whom were Saudi Arabs.
