### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the WASHC4 gene (also known as SWIP or KIAA1033). ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is an intellectual disability disorder caused by defects in WASHC4 (SWIP), a member of the WASH complex. The WASH complex is involved in actin polymerization and endosomal transport. The disorder was first reported as nonsyndromic intellectual disability (Ropers et al., 2011). Later reports described a broader, variable phenotype. Features can include short stature, macrocephaly or microcephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, motor dysfunction, and skeletal muscle involvement (Assoum et al., 2020; Courtland et al., 2021; Gangfuss et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. Assoum et al. (2020) later described this family as having 7 affected individuals with moderate intellectual disability and short stature.

Assoum et al. (2020) reported 3 patients from 2 unrelated families with syndromic intellectual disability. In the first family, 2 sisters aged 4 and 5.5 years had learning disabilities, macrocephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, and subependymal heterotopic nodules. The younger sister also had congenital absence of the right internal carotid artery and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. The third patient was 34 years old and had mild intellectual disability, short stature, and microcephaly. Assoum et al. (2020) concluded that the phenotype associated with KIAA1033 is heterogeneous.

Courtland et al. (2021) retrospectively reviewed clinical findings from 7 children in Oman who were homozygous for the P1019R mutation; these were the patients originally reported by Ropers et al. (2011). Developmental delay or learning difficulties had been the main reason for medical evaluation. However, all patients also had motor symptoms (mean age, 10.4 years). Their movements were described as 'clumsy', with notable fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria on clinical examination. The patients were now an average of 21 years old, and their parents reported no notable worsening of symptoms.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) reported 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype that included dysmorphisms, intellectual disability, profound developmental disorder, and skeletal muscle involvement. In one patient, a muscle biopsy showed dysregulation of proteins relevant for proper muscle function. Histologic and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering microscopy studies suggested altered protein processing and clearance. Gangfuss et al. (2022) stated that this was the first report of skeletal muscle involvement in patients.

### Inheritance

Ropers et al. (2011) established autosomal recessive inheritance in a large consanguineous family. Affected individuals in later reports were homozygous or compound heterozygous for WASHC4 mutations, which is also consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance (Assoum et al., 2020; Courtland et al., 2021; Gangfuss et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability, Ropers et al. (2011) used homozygosity mapping, targeted exon enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing to identify a missense mutation in the SWIP gene. After SNP filtering, only 2 sequence changes remained. The SWIP change was considered causative based on high pathogenicity and conservation scores and on functional considerations. The mutation caused significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex. Courtland et al. (2021) identified this mutation as a homozygous c.3056C-G transversion in WASHC4, resulting in a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution. ⚠︎

Using exome sequencing, Assoum et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous KIAA1033 variants in 3 patients from 2 unrelated families with syndromic intellectual disability. Two sisters carried a stop-gain variant (Q442*) and a missense variant (D1048G), each inherited from one parent. The third patient carried 2 missense variants (K1079R and H503R), one inherited from each parent.

Courtland et al. (2021) studied the P1019R mutation in HEK cells. Compared with wildtype SWIP, the mutant protein co-immunoprecipitated significantly less Strumpellin and WASH1 (54.8% and 41.4% of wildtype, respectively), suggesting that the mutation impairs WASH complex formation.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) identified a homozygous WASHC4 variant in 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype that included skeletal muscle involvement. Proteomic profiling of one patient's fibroblasts showed dysregulation of proteins relevant for maintaining the neuromuscular axis.

### Animal Model

Courtland et al. (2021) used CRISPR to create a mouse model carrying the human SWIP P1019R mutation. In brains of homozygous mutant mice, Strumpellin and WASH1 were markedly decreased (3.8% and 1.1% of wildtype, respectively). Spatial proteomics showed destabilization of the WASH complex, decreased abundance of an endosomal module, and increased abundance of a lysosomal module. Mutant cortical neurons had enlarged EEA1-positive early endosomes, as well as fewer but larger cathepsin D-positive puncta. At 7 months, motor cortex neurons of mutant mice showed lipofuscin accumulation. At 10 months, cleaved caspase-3 staining in the motor cortex was increased. Mutant mice had deficits in cued fear memory recall at both adolescence and adulthood. They also had motor deficits on the rotarod at both ages and gait abnormalities that appeared by 5.5 months, consistent with progressive motor decline.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE 43**  
WASHC4, PRO1019ARG [c.3056C>G; p.Pro1019Arg]

Ropers et al. (2011) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the SWIP (WASHC4) gene in affected members of a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. The mutation caused significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the WASH complex. ⚠︎

Courtland et al. (2021) identified the mutation in the 7 affected children from Oman originally reported by Ropers et al. (2011) as a homozygous c.3056C-G transversion, resulting in a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution. All 7 patients had motor symptoms. In HEK cells, the mutant protein co-immunoprecipitated less Strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype protein. Knockin mice carrying the mutation had markedly reduced WASH complex proteins, endolysosomal abnormalities, cognitive deficits, and progressive motor deficits.
