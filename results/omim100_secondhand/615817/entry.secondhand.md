### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the WASHC4 gene (also known as KIAA1033 or SWIP). ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is a neurodevelopmental disorder marked by mild to moderate impaired intellectual development. It was first described as a nonsyndromic form of intellectual disability, and short stature was also noted in the first family reported. Later patients showed a wider, variable syndromic phenotype that can include macrocephaly or microcephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, motor dysfunction, and skeletal muscle involvement (Ropers et al., 2011; Assoum et al., 2020; Courtland et al., 2021; Gangfuss et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability, which they regarded as nonsyndromic. Assoum et al. (2020) later summarized that this family had 7 affected individuals with moderate intellectual disability and short stature.

Assoum et al. (2020) reported 3 patients from 2 unrelated families with syndromic intellectual disability. Two sisters, aged 4 and 5.5 years, had learning disabilities, macrocephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, and subependymal heterotopic nodules. The younger sister also had congenital absence of the right internal carotid artery and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. The third patient, aged 34 years, had mild intellectual disability, short stature, and microcephaly. Assoum et al. (2020) concluded that the phenotype is heterogeneous and that more descriptions would be needed to refine it.

Courtland et al. (2021) retrospectively reviewed clinical findings in the 7 affected children from the family reported by Ropers et al. (2011), who had been evaluated in Muscat, Oman. Developmental delay or learning difficulties were the main reason for medical evaluation, but all of the patients also had motor symptoms (mean age 10.4 years). Their movements were described as 'clumsy'. On examination they had notable fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. When the parents were contacted again, at which time the patients were an average of 21 years old, they reported no notable worsening of symptoms.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) reported 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype consisting of dysmorphisms, intellectual disability, profound developmental disorder, and skeletal muscle involvement. The authors stated that this was the first report of skeletal muscle involvement in patients. A muscle biopsy from 1 patient showed dysregulation of proteins relevant for proper muscle function.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance of MRT43 was supported by the large consanguineous family reported by Ropers et al. (2011). Later reports described compound heterozygous mutations, with each variant inherited from a different parent (Assoum et al., 2020), and a homozygous mutation in 2 sisters (Gangfuss et al., 2022).

### Molecular Genetics

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability using homozygosity mapping, targeted exon enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing. After SNP filtering, only 2 molecular changes remained. One was a nonsynonymous variant in the SWIP (WASHC4) gene, which encodes a member of the WASH complex. Affected individuals were homozygous for this change, a c.3056C-G transversion causing a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution (Courtland et al., 2021). Ropers et al. (2011) considered the variant causative because of its high pathogenicity and evolutionary conservation scores and on functional grounds. They found that the mutation significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex. Courtland et al. (2021) noted that cell lines derived from these patients had reduced amounts of WASH proteins. ⚠︎

Using exome sequencing, Assoum et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the KIAA1033 (WASHC4) gene in 3 patients from 2 unrelated families with syndromic intellectual disability. Two sisters carried a nonsense variant (Q442X) and a missense variant (D1048G), and each variant had been inherited from one parent. The third patient carried 2 missense variants (K1079R and H503R), each also inherited from one parent.

Courtland et al. (2021) studied the function of the P1019R mutation in HEK cells. Compared with wildtype SWIP, the mutant protein co-immunoprecipitated significantly less strumpellin (54.8% of wildtype) and WASH1 (41.4% of wildtype), suggesting that the mutation hinders formation of the WASH complex.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) identified a homozygous WASHC4 variant in 2 sisters with intellectual disability, dysmorphisms, profound developmental disorder, and skeletal muscle involvement. The abstract does not state which variant was found.

### Pathogenesis

Gangfuss et al. (2022) performed proteomic profiling of fibroblasts from a patient with WASHC4 deficiency and found dysregulation of proteins relevant for maintaining the neuromuscular axis. Immunostaining of a muscle biopsy from the same patient confirmed dysregulation of proteins needed for normal muscle function. Histologic studies and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering microscopy supported a role for WASHC4 in protein processing and clearance in humans.

### Animal Model

Courtland et al. (2021) used CRISPR to introduce the mutation corresponding to human P1019R into exon 29 of mouse Washc4. In brain lysates from adult homozygous mutant mice, strumpellin and WASH1 were markedly reduced, to 3.8% and 1.1% of wildtype, respectively. Spatial proteomics of mutant mouse brain showed that the mutation destabilized the WASH complex. An endosomal module containing WASH, CCC, Retriever, and CORVET/HOPS complex components was reduced, whereas a lysosomal module was increased. ER chaperones were also increased, and a synaptic module was reduced, along with fewer excitatory synapses. Cultured mutant cortical neurons had enlarged EEA1-positive early endosomes. They had fewer cathepsin D-positive lysosomal puncta, but the remaining puncta were larger. At 10 months, mutant mice showed increased cleaved caspase-3 staining in the motor cortex. At 7 months, electron microscopy showed lipofuscin accumulation in motor cortex neurons. Mutant mice had a persistent deficit in cued fear memory at both adolescence and adulthood, but performed normally on novel object recognition and Y-maze tasks. Their rotarod performance was impaired at both ages. Gait abnormalities, including longer, slower strides and altered gait symmetry, appeared in adulthood, indicating progressive motor decline. Dopaminergic cell numbers in the substantia nigra were not significantly different from wildtype.
