### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), also known as tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), or TYK2 deficiency, is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency. Its core feature is susceptibility to intracellular bacteria and/or viruses. The bacteria include mycobacteria (bacille Calmette-Guerin (BCG) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis), Salmonella, and Brucella. The viruses include herpesviruses. Kreins et al. (2015) attributed these infections to impaired cellular responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta. The first patient reported had features of hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES), including atopic dermatitis, high serum IgE, and staphylococcal infections (Minegishi et al., 2006). However, most patients reported later did not have HIES (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015), and Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that HIES does not appear to be an intrinsic feature of TYK2 deficiency. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

TYK2 deficiency was first described in a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome (Minegishi et al., 2006). Wu et al. (2020) noted a proposal that human TYK2 deficiency is a distinct primary immunodeficiency rather than a common cause of autosomal recessive HIES. They also noted that the International Union of Immunological Societies (IUIS) classifies TYK2 deficiency among the disorders of mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD).

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) described a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. He was unusually susceptible to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kreins et al. (2015) gave further clinical details on the patient of Minegishi et al. (2006) (P1), a Japanese man born to consanguineous parents. He had atopic dermatitis from the age of 1 month and recurrent bacterial and viral infections from the age of 1 year. These included BCGitis at age 22 months (6 months after vaccination), repeated Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, pneumonia without pneumatocele formation, otitis, and sinusitis. At age 15 years he was hospitalized with sepsis after severe nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis. He also had parainfluenza virus-3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV infections with tonsillitis, molluscum contagiosum, and mild oral candidiasis. Laboratory studies repeatedly showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE (1,430-2,100 IU/ml). He was diagnosed with autosomal recessive HIES based on an NIH score of 48 points. At age 30 years he had ocular sarcoidosis.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy born to healthy first-cousin parents. He was vaccinated with BCG at age 2 months. Left axillary lymphadenopathy appeared at 8 months, and generalized lymphadenopathy and fatigue followed at 21 months. On admission he had fever and enlarged, fistulous lymph nodes in the cervical, axillary, and inguinal regions, and M. bovis BCG was isolated from the draining cervical sinuses. His BCG disease responded to antimycobacterial treatment. At age 8 years he developed Brucella meningitis, with blood and CSF cultures positive for Brucella. The sequelae were severe hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, and lacunar infarcts on MRI. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the right maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve, which was treated with acyclovir. He had no skeletal or dental features of HIES and no atopy, asthma, candidiasis, boils, folliculitis, or cold abscesses. Serum IgE measured at ages 2, 11, 16, and 18 years was normal or slightly high (80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml, respectively). Kilic et al. (2012) noted that, unlike the Japanese patient, their patient had none of the cardinal features of HIES. They suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in patients with BCG disease or other intramacrophage infections, particularly with unusually severe herpesvirus infections, even when HIES features are absent.

Kreins et al. (2015) reported 7 additional TYK2-deficient patients (P2-P8) from 5 unrelated families from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. P2 was the Turkish patient reported by Kilic et al. (2012). P3 and P4 were Moroccan sibs born to first-cousin parents. P3, a girl, developed disseminated extrapulmonary tuberculosis at age 13 years. She had ascites and a psoas abscess from which M. tuberculosis was isolated, and she died after bacterial meningitis, 15 months after symptom onset. Her brother, P4, had meningitis of unknown etiology at age 8 months, recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels. P5, a boy born to consanguineous Iranian parents, had right axillary lymphadenitis at 2 months and multiple cervical lymphadenitis at 4 months after BCG vaccination. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with pulmonary involvement. His younger sister (P6) had BCG lymphadenopathy after vaccination and, at age 1 year, an unidentified viral skin infection. P7, a 9-year-old Iranian girl born to consanguineous parents, had miliary tuberculosis. P8, an 11-year-old boy from Argentina, had herpes gingivostomatitis, recurrent aseptic (herpes) meningitis, and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions, as well as mild mental disability of uncertain origin. None of P2 through P8 had documented staphylococcal or other pyogenic infections or fungal infections, including mucocutaneous candidiasis, and serum IgE was never high. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infection.

Fuchs et al. (2016) reported a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels above 1,000 U/mL, but no viral or mycobacterial infections. Wu et al. (2020) listed this patient as a 5-year-old Kurdish boy.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy born to young nonconsanguineous parents. After BCG vaccination on day 3 of life, he had repeated abscess and ulceration at the injection site. At 14 months he was hospitalized with enlarged left axillary lymph nodes, which showed acid-fast bacilli, and M. bovis BCG was isolated from the draining sinuses. From age 6 months he had recurrent respiratory tract infections, with pneumonia or upper respiratory tract infection every 1 to 2 months, and diarrhea. Pathogens identified included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and M. bovis BCG, and IgM antibodies to influenza A and B viruses were positive. He had no high serum IgE (27.4 IU/ml), atopy, staphylococcal disease, or lymphopenia, but CD3-CD16/CD56+ natural killer cells were low. He began to say simple words at 3 years 4 months, his motor development was normal, and he had a small head circumference. Wu et al. (2020) reviewed 12 reported TYK2-deficient patients. Of these, 58% had intracellular bacterial infections and 58% had recurrent viral infections. Markedly increased serum IgE was reported in 3 (25%), and EBV-related B-cell lymphoma and T-cell lymphopenia in 2 (17%). None of the 12 had the facial features, abnormal bone development or pathologic fractures, delayed loss of deciduous teeth, or aneurysms seen in STAT3-related HIES.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 additional TYK2-deficient patients with or without hyper-IgE levels and atopy, and with distinct profiles of pathogen infection. Lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted.

Zhang et al. (2022) studied an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. They identified 3 unrelated Turkish children, aged 2, 4, and 9 years and each born to consanguineous parents, with TYK2 deficiency. The 2-year-old girl had moderate COVID-19 pneumonia and did not need oxygen therapy. The 2 boys had critical pneumonia requiring ICU admission and high-flow oxygen. Before COVID-19, the 4-year-old boy had been admitted to the ICU for influenza pneumonia at age 3 years, and the girl had been hospitalized for a viral vaccine infection. The 3-year-old younger sister of the 4-year-old boy had the same genotype. She had a history of critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and of disseminated varicella, but had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 is autosomal recessive. Minegishi et al. (2006) found the disorder in a patient with a homozygous TYK2 mutation. Kreins et al. (2015) found homozygous TYK2 mutations in patients from consanguineous families; in all kindreds, only homozygotes had clinical disease, and all homozygotes did. Wu et al. (2020) found the parents of a homozygous patient to be heterozygous carriers. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis in 3 TYK2-deficient children. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells were defective in signaling through multiple cytokine pathways, including those for type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. Transducing intact TYK2 restored these signals. Kreins et al. (2015) stated that this patient carried a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 (C70HfsX21). Wu et al. (2020) listed this mutation as c.550_553delGCTT.

In a Turkish boy with disseminated BCG infection, neurobrucellosis, and herpes zoster, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene, which they designated c.2302_2310del. The deletion created a premature termination codon at position 767, and no full-length TYK2 protein was detected in the patient's EBV-B cells. Kreins et al. (2015) designated this deletion c.2303_2311del (L767X). They reported that it had been identified by genome-wide linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing.

In 7 patients from 5 unrelated families with mycobacterial and/or viral infections but no HIES, Kreins et al. (2015) identified homozygous mutations in the TYK2 gene. The methods were linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, or targeted next-generation sequencing. Besides the 9-bp deletion in the Turkish patient, they found 4 novel mutations, each predicted to cause a premature stop codon: a 1-bp insertion (c.3318_3319insC; T1106HfsX4), E154X, a 1-bp deletion (c.149delC; S50HfsX1), and R638X. The mutations were absent from public databases. The 130-kD TYK2 protein was undetectable in cells from all patients, indicating complete deficiency. Expression of wildtype TYK2 rescued surface expression of IFN-alpha-R1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1, as well as STAT phosphorylation in response to IL-23, IFN-alpha, and IL-10. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, and high IgE. In a monocyte assay, they showed that the patient's mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response and partly reduced IL-10 signaling, but not IL-6 signaling. Wu et al. (2020) listed this patient's mutation as a homozygous c.647delC (P216RfsX14) in exon 7. ⚠︎

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent respiratory infections, Wu et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2395G-A transition in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous carriers. The patient's cells showed decreased TYK2 mRNA and no detectable TYK2 protein. In HEK293T cells, the mutation decreased both expression and protein levels of tagged TYK2. The variant was present in gnomAD. Wu et al. (2020) also listed 2 patients, reported by Nemoto et al., who were compound heterozygous for TYK2 mutations (c.209_212delGCTT, C70SfsX21, and c.691C-T, R231W).

In 5 TYK2-deficient patients, Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations by high-throughput sequencing and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. Pathogenic effects were confirmed by qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays.

In 3 unrelated Turkish children with moderate or critical COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified homozygosity for the TYK2 P216fs*14 frameshift by exome or genome sequencing, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Autosomal recessive inheritance was confirmed by family segregation. Fibroblasts expressing ACE2 from a patient homozygous for this variant failed to control SARS-CoV-2 replication. These cells also failed to induce interferon-stimulated genes in response to infection or to IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kreins et al. (2015) found that cells from all 8 TYK2-deficient patients had impaired but not abolished responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta, and IL-10. Only cells from P1, the patient with HIES, had an impaired response to IL-6. Expressing wildtype TYK2 in P1 cells did not restore the IL-6 response, and silencing TYK2 did not reproduce the defect. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the IL-6 hyporesponsiveness in P1 was independent of his TYK2 genotype and might underlie his HIES features, at least the staphylococcal disease. Whole-exome sequencing found no mutation in known components of the IL-6 pathway in P1.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) found that cells from a TYK2-deficient patient had defects in signaling for type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. They proposed that TYK2 deficiency accounted for the patient's phenotype of impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation. They concluded that TYK2 has obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals in humans, which differ substantially from its role in mice.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that TYK2-deficient T and NK cells responded to IL-12 with impaired but not abolished IFN-gamma production. They suggested this accounted for the mycobacterial disease and its relatively favorable response to antibiotics. IFN-alpha/beta responses were also impaired but not abolished, consistent with the viral infections and their mild course. Responses to IL-23 were impaired, but proportions of circulating IL-17-positive T cells were normal, possibly explaining the absence of mucocutaneous candidiasis. Residual IL-10 responses might explain the absence of early-onset colitis. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda, and LIF were normal. Numbers of central and effector memory T cells were normal.

Fuchs et al. (2016) found nearly normal type III IFN signaling and only minimal impairment of virus control in a TYK2-deficient human cell line. NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, were normal in their patient. They suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function contribute to antiviral protection in TYK2 deficiency.

Lv et al. (2022) found heterogeneous responses to IFN-alpha/beta/gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 5 TYK2-deficient patients. They proposed that different combinations of cytokine-signaling defects account for the varied clinical manifestations.

### Diagnosis

Kreins et al. (2015) suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in children with severe tuberculosis. Wu et al. (2020) suggested considering TYK2 deficiency in patients with repeated intracellular bacterial infections (including tuberculosis), repeated viral infections, or eczema, especially when there is a family history. Fuchs et al. (2016) established a monocyte assay to show abolished IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral responses.
