### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene.

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), or tyrosine kinase-2 (TYK2) deficiency, is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency. Its core feature is susceptibility to mycobacterial and/or viral infections, which results from impaired cellular responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta. Patients may also have infections with other intracellular bacteria, such as Salmonella and Brucella. The first reported patient also had features of hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES), including atopic dermatitis, high serum IgE, and staphylococcal abscesses. Most patients reported later did not have these features, and impaired IL-6 responses and HIES do not appear to be intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency (Kreins et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

TYK2 deficiency was first described by Minegishi et al. (2006) in a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. After further TYK2-deficient patients without HIES features were identified (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015), Wu et al. (2020) noted that human TYK2 deficiency had been proposed to be a distinct clinical entity rather than a common cause of autosomal recessive HIES. Wu et al. (2020) also noted that the International Union of Immunological Societies had classified TYK2 deficiency as a form of mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD).

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. He had unusual susceptibility to a range of microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE. Kreins et al. (2015) gave further details on this Japanese man (P1), who was born to consanguineous parents. He had had atopic dermatitis since 1 month of age and recurrent bacterial and viral infections since 1 year of age. He developed BCGitis at 22 months, 6 months after vaccination, and had repeated Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, pneumonia without pneumatocele formation, otitis, and sinusitis. At 15 years of age he was admitted with sepsis after severe nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis. His viral infections included parainfluenza virus-3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV infections associated with tonsillitis, and molluscum contagiosum. He also had mild oral candidiasis. Laboratory studies repeatedly showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE (1,430-2,100 IU/ml). He was diagnosed with autosomal recessive HIES on the basis of an NIH score of 48 points. At 30 years of age he had ocular sarcoidosis. He had no bone fractures, and his dentition and facial appearance were normal (Kilic et al., 2012).

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, born to first-cousin parents, who received BCG vaccine at 2 months of age. Left axillary lymphadenopathy appeared at 8 months, and by 21 months he had generalized lymphadenopathy and fatigue. He had fistulous bilateral cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymphadenitis, and M. bovis BCG was isolated from the draining cervical sinuses. At 8 years of age he developed meningitis, with Brucella spp. grown from blood and CSF. Neurobrucellosis left him with severe sensorineural hearing loss and severe cognitive impairment, and MRI showed lacunar infarcts. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve. He had no skeletal or dental features of HIES and no history of atopy, asthma, candidiasis, boils, folliculitis, or cold abscess. Serum IgE measured at 2, 11, 16, and 18 years of age was normal or slightly high (80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml, respectively). Kilic et al. (2012) concluded that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in patients susceptible to herpesviruses and intramacrophage pathogens, even when atopy, high serum IgE, and staphylococcal disease are absent. ⚠︎

Kreins et al. (2015) reported 7 further TYK2-deficient patients (P2-P8) from 5 unrelated families from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. P2 was the patient reported by Kilic et al. (2012). In a Moroccan family with first-cousin parents, a girl (P3) developed disseminated extrapulmonary tuberculosis at 13 years of age and died 15 months after symptom onset. Her brother (P4) had meningitis of unknown etiology at 8 months of age, recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels. An Iranian boy (P5) born to consanguineous parents developed BCG lymphadenitis in infancy and, at 4.5 years of age, paravertebral and psoas abscesses with pulmonary involvement. His younger sister (P6) had BCG lymphadenopathy after vaccination and was hospitalized at 1 year of age for an unknown viral infection of the skin. A 9-year-old Iranian girl (P7) born to consanguineous parents had miliary tuberculosis. An 11-year-old boy from Argentina (P8), adopted at 1 day of age, had recurrent herpes gingivostomatitis, aseptic herpes meningitis with disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions, and mild mental disability. None of P2 to P8 had documented pyogenic infections, including staphylococcal disease, or fungal infections, including mucocutaneous candidiasis, and their serum IgE levels were never high. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infection.

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels above 1,000 U/mL, who had no viral or mycobacterial infections. In the review by Wu et al. (2020), this patient was a 5-year-old Kurdish boy.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy born to young nonconsanguineous parents. He received BCG vaccine on the third day of life and had repeated abscess and ulceration at the injection site. At 14 months of age he was hospitalized with BCG-associated tuberculosis of the left axillary lymph nodes. From 6 months of age he had recurrent respiratory tract infections and diarrhea. Pathogens identified during hospitalizations included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and M. bovis BCG, and IgM antibodies to influenza A and B viruses were positive. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal illness, or lymphopenia. NK cells were low (3.6%) and CD19+ B cells were increased. He began to say simple words at 3 years and 4 months of age, with normal motor development, and had a small head circumference. In their review of 12 reported patients, Wu et al. (2020) found intracellular bacterial infections in 58%, recurrent viral infections in 58%, and markedly increased serum IgE in 25%. None of the 12 had the special facial features, abnormal bone development, pathologic fractures, delayed loss of deciduous teeth, or aneurysms seen in STAT3-related HIES. The review included 2 sibs reported by Nemoto, who were compound heterozygous for TYK2 mutations, had partial TYK2 deficiency with T-cell lymphopenia, and developed EBV-associated B-cell lymphoma.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 further TYK2-deficient patients. Some did and some did not have hyper-IgE levels and atopy, and their profiles of pathogen infection differed. Lymphocyte homeostasis was also disrupted.

In an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated Turkish children with TYK2 deficiency, each born to a different set of consanguineous parents. A 2-year-old girl had moderate COVID-19 pneumonia. She had previously been hospitalized for sepsis in the neonatal period, had been hospitalized for infection after VZV vaccination, and had been diagnosed with Kawasaki disease at 11 months of age. A 4-year-old boy and a 9-year-old boy were both admitted to the ICU with critical COVID-19 pneumonia. The 4-year-old had been admitted to the ICU for influenza pneumonia at 3 years of age, and the 9-year-old had recurrent bronchitis. The 3-year-old sister of the 4-year-old boy had the same genotype and had had critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation, as well as disseminated varicella. She had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2.

### Inheritance

IMD35 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Kreins et al. (2015) found that in all kindreds they studied, clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. In the patient reported by Wu et al. (2020), the homozygous mutation was carried in heterozygous state by both parents. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis in 3 patients.

### Molecular Genetics

Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene in a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient's cells had defects in signaling for several cytokines, including type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. Transduction of the intact TYK2 gene restored these signals. Kreins et al. (2015) reported that this patient carried a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 of TYK2.

In a Turkish boy with disseminated BCG infection, neurobrucellosis, and herpes zoster, Kilic et al. (2012) found a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene, which creates a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein was detected by Western blotting of the patient's EBV-transformed B cells.

Kreins et al. (2015) identified homozygous null mutations in the TYK2 gene in 7 patients from 5 families. The mutations were found by linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, or targeted next-generation sequencing. They were absent from public databases, and all were predicted to result in a premature stop codon. TYK2 protein was undetectable in patient cells. All 8 TYK2-deficient patients, including the patient of Minegishi et al. (2006), had impaired but not abolished cellular responses to IL-12, IFN-alpha/beta, IL-23, and IL-10. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda, and LIF were normal. Expression of wildtype TYK2 rescued surface expression of IFNAR1, IL10R2, and IL12RB1 and restored signaling responses. Cells from the 7 new patients responded normally to IL-6, unlike those of the first patient. In that patient's cells, neither expression of wildtype TYK2 nor silencing of endogenous TYK2 rescued the IL-6 hyporesponsiveness, suggesting that this phenotype was not a consequence of his TYK2 genotype.

Fuchs et al. (2016) used a monocyte assay in a TYK2-deficient patient with features of HIES and showed that the mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response. It also partly reduced IL-10-mediated signaling, with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not affect IL-6-mediated signaling. Type III IFN signaling was almost normal in a TYK2-deficient human cell line, and NK-cell phenotype and function were normal in the patient. Wu et al. (2020) listed the patient's mutation as c.647delC (P216RfsX14) in exon 7. ⚠︎

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent respiratory infections, Wu et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene (c.2395G-A, G799R). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The patient's cells had reduced TYK2 mRNA and no detectable TYK2 protein. Introduction of the mutation into expression constructs in HEK293T cells also reduced TYK2 mRNA and protein expression.

Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations in 5 patients by high-throughput sequencing, with confirmation by Sanger sequencing. The pathogenic effects were confirmed by qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays. Patient PBMCs had heterogeneous responses to IFN-alpha/beta/gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23.

By whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing in a cohort of children with COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated Turkish children who were homozygous for the same TYK2 loss-of-function variant (P216Rfs*14). Fibroblasts from a patient with the same genotype did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally and failed to induce interferon-stimulated genes in response to infection or IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that TYK2 deficiency probably accounted for their patient's complex clinical features, including impaired T helper-1 differentiation and accelerated T helper-2 differentiation. They also concluded that the function of Tyk2 in humans differs substantially from its function in mice.

Kreins et al. (2015) proposed explanations for the main clinical features. They attributed the mycobacterial disease to impaired IL-12-dependent IFN-gamma production by T and NK cells, and the viral infections to impaired responses to IFN-alpha/beta. They suggested that residual responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta explained the favorable outcome of infections in most patients. Residual IL-23 responses and normal proportions of IL-17-positive T cells probably accounted for the lack of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, and residual IL-10 responses for the lack of early-onset colitis. The poor IL-6 responses of the first patient, but not of the other 7, probably underlay at least his staphylococcal disease and perhaps other features of HIES. Kreins et al. (2015) considered this IL-6 defect to be independent of TYK2. Whole-exome sequencing found no mutation in known components of the IL-6 pathway in this patient.

Fuchs et al. (2016) suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may contribute to antiviral protection in TYK2 deficiency and so explain its surprisingly mild phenotype.

### Diagnosis

Kilic et al. (2012) suggested that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in patients with BCG disease, especially if they also have unusually severe herpesvirus infections, even in the absence of the cardinal features of HIES. This may also apply to patients with other mycobacterial infections and with infections by other intramacrophage pathogens such as Salmonella and Brucella. Wu et al. (2020) recommended considering TYK2 deficiency in patients with repeated intracellular bacterial infections, including tuberculosis, repeated viral infections, or eczema. Fuchs et al. (2016) developed a simple diagnostic assay in monocytes based on the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response.

**.0002 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**
