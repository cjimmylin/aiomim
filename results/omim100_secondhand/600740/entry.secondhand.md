### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene on chromosome 19q13.

### Description

Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (FHH3) is an autosomal dominant disorder of extracellular calcium homeostasis that affects the parathyroids, kidneys, and bone. Like the other forms of FHH, it causes lifelong high serum calcium with inappropriately low urinary calcium excretion. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that FHH3 also shows increased serum parathyroid hormone (PTH), hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia. All reported mutations are heterozygous missense changes at arg15 of the sigma-2 subunit of adaptor protein-2 (AP2), which is encoded by AP2S1 (Nesbit et al., 2013).

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) named the form of familial benign hypercalcemia (FBH) seen in a kindred from Oklahoma the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok). Hannan et al. (2010) noted that the 3 genetically distinct types of familial benign hypocalciuric hypercalcemia had been designated FBHH1, FBHH2, and FBHH3, mapping to 3q21.1, 19p, and 19q13, respectively, and that FBHH3 was also called the Oklahoma variant (FBHH-Ok).

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) described a large kindred with familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia. After the proposita was identified, 46 family members spanning 5 generations and aged 1.5 to 91 years were surveyed biochemically and/or by chart review, and 19 were found to be affected. In the 15 hypercalcemic subjects studied biochemically, serum immunoreactive PTH, measured in 3 separate immunoassays, became supranormal by about age 30 years and appeared to rise further thereafter. Serum alkaline phosphatase activity and creatinine were normal, but inorganic phosphate was lower than in unaffected family members. Of 5 affected adults older than 40 years who were studied radiographically, 3 had changes suggestive of osteomalacia. In one of them, a 51-year-old woman, iliac crest biopsy after tetracycline labeling confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. McMurtry et al. (1992) concluded that in this kindred the disorder could be especially hard to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism in adults, and that it might not be entirely benign, because osteomalacia, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia, could develop in adulthood.

Hannan et al. (2010) restudied 40 members of the Oklahoma kindred from 5 generations. Affected status was defined as hypercalcemia with a calcium clearance to creatinine clearance ratio below 0.01. Elevated serum PTH had been observed in some affected individuals older than 30 years, and 3 adults older than 40 years showed evidence of osteomalacia.

Nesbit et al. (2010) studied a second kindred, from Northern Ireland. The proband was a 43-year-old woman who did not have a CASR mutation. Her corrected serum calcium was 2.74 mmol/liter (normal, 2.15-2.55), her serum PTH was 47 pg/ml (normal, 10-65), and her urinary calcium clearance to creatinine clearance ratio was 0.006. Of 24 relatives from 3 generations, 16 were hypercalcemic, with normal or elevated serum PTH and mild hypophosphatemia, features the authors considered consistent with FHH3. ⚠︎

Nesbit et al. (2013) summarized the biochemistry of the 2 FHH3 kindreds. In the Oklahoma kindred (13 affected females and 3 affected males; mean age at diagnosis 36 years), mean serum calcium was 2.88 mmol/l, mean serum phosphate 0.77 mmol/l, mean PTH 65 pg/ml, and mean urinary calcium:creatinine clearance ratio 0.003. In the Northern Ireland kindred (11 affected females and 5 affected males; mean age at diagnosis 32 years), the corresponding means were 2.72 mmol/l, 0.95 mmol/l, 60 pg/ml, and 0.006. In unrelated probands with AP2S1 mutations, ages ranged from 8 days to 78 years.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese girl whose hypercalcemia was found on routine laboratory testing for poor weight gain on breastfeeding. At 49 days of age her serum calcium, adjusted by Payne's formula, was 13.1 mg/dL, intact PTH was 27 pg/mL, and urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg. There was no evidence of hyperparathyroidism, a PTHrP-producing neoplasm, or vitamin D excess. The authors concluded that absence of hypocalciuria does not necessarily argue against an AP2S1 mutation, and suggested that the patient's early infantile age played a role in her hypercalciuria.

Howles et al. (2016) described 3 previously unreported, unrelated probands with FHH3 and symptomatic hypercalcemia. A 34-year-old woman with a 12-year history of hypercalcemia had fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. A 22-year-old man had hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. A 52-year-old woman with about a 20-year history of hypercalcemia had headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy. Howles et al. (2016) described FHH3 as the most clinically severe form of familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia.

### Inheritance

In the Oklahoma kindred, the 5-generation pedigree studied by McMurtry et al. (1992) and Hannan et al. (2010) was consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. Hannan et al. (2010) obtained significant LOD scores under an autosomal dominant model with 90% penetrance. Nesbit et al. (2013) found heterozygous AP2S1 mutations, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, and showed that the mutation cosegregated with disease in 2 multigenerational kindreds. Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a presumably de novo heterozygous AP2S1 mutation. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Trump et al. (1995) studied the 5-generation Oklahoma kindred (19 affected, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected) with 17 polymorphic markers. They excluded linkage to the FBH regions on 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3, the MEN1 region on 11q13, and the PTH region on 11p15 (LOD scores below -2.00 at theta = 0.05-0.25). Analysis of multipoint crossovers and the LINKMAP program confirmed the exclusions.

Lloyd et al. (1999) carried out a genomewide search in the Oklahoma family and found linkage to 8 loci on chromosome 19q13. The highest LOD score, 6.67 at a recombination fraction of 0.00, was obtained with D19S606. Recombinants placed the locus in an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866. The calmodulin III gene lies within this interval, but sequencing its coding region, 5-prime UTR, and part of its promoter in an affected individual found no abnormalities.

Hannan et al. (2010) studied 24 polymorphic loci in the Oklahoma kindred. They obtained a peak LOD score of 6.92 at 0% recombination with D19S606 and narrowed the FBHH3 locus to a 4.1-Mb interval flanked centromerically by D19S112 and telomerically by rs245111. The syntenic region on mouse chromosome 7 contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs genes), but no human homologs were found. Comparative analysis showed that an 11.6-Mb segment of the mouse region containing these genes is absent from the human genome. Sequencing the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries of 12 candidate genes from the interval that are expressed in parathyroid and/or kidney (PTGIR, GNG8, EMP3, SPHK2, TULP2, NUCB1, HRC, CYTH2, PTH2, RCN3, RRAS, and ATF5) found no abnormalities in an affected individual.

In the Northern Ireland kindred, Nesbit et al. (2010) found that microsatellite and SNP loci from chromosome 19q13.3 cosegregated with FHH, with a peak LOD score of 5.98 at 0% recombination with D19S412. Analysis of recombinants placed the locus in a 3.46-Mbp interval flanked centromerically by rs1990932 and telomerically by D19S604.

### Heterogeneity

Trump et al. (1995) excluded linkage of the Oklahoma variant to the loci for FBH, MEN1, and PTH and concluded that a third FBH locus exists. Nesbit et al. (2013) found AP2S1 mutations in more than 20% of FHH patients who lacked CASR mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on 1 affected member of each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, from Oklahoma and from Northern Ireland. Within the 3.46-Mbp FHH3 interval on 19q13.3, they found a single shared variant, a C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene that predicted an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. The change was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Restriction enzyme analysis showed that it cosegregated with disease in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. The authors then sequenced AP2S1 in 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations and found 11 heterozygous missense mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 R15C, 3 arg15-to-his (R15H), and 4 arg15-to-leu (R15L). Arg15 is conserved in all AP2-sigma-2 orthologs and in the human AP sigma paralogs, and the crystal structure of AP2 showed that it contacts the dileucine motifs of cargo proteins. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, all 3 mutants shifted the concentration-response curves to the right, with higher EC50 values, indicating reduced sensitivity to extracellular calcium. The R15C mutant raised the EC50 significantly above that of vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. Wild-type AP2S1 increased calcium-stimulated CaSR internalization, but the R15C mutant did not. In addition, mutating the CaSR C-terminal dileucine motif reduced CaSR signaling.

In a Japanese infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria who had no pathogenic mutation in CASR or GNA11, Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a presumably de novo heterozygous R15L mutation in the AP2S1 gene.

Howles et al. (2016) reported 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic hypercalcemia, each carrying a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation: R15C, R15H, or R15L.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that patients with the R15C, R15H, and R15L mutations had similar rises in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium excretion. They concluded that mutation did not correlate with clinical phenotype.

### Clinical Management

Fujisawa et al. (2013) treated their infant patient with a low-calcium formula containing 2.6 mg/dL calcium. She showed catch-up growth, and both serum calcium and urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio fell. Afterwards, a 2:1 mixture of low-calcium and standard formula kept serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without a marked rise in serum PTH.

Howles et al. (2016) found that 10 nM cinacalcet corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in cells expressing the R15C, R15H, or R15L mutants. They gave oral cinacalcet, 30 to 60 mg daily, to 3 probands for 33 to 45 months. Serum calcium fell by more than 20% and symptoms abated. Serum phosphate rose and serum PTH fell, although both remained within the normal range. None of the patients developed nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15CYS [Arg15Cys; R15C]

Nesbit et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution, in affected members of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, from Oklahoma and from Northern Ireland. The mutation was found by exome sequencing, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and cosegregated with disease in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members. They also found R15C in 4 of 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. In CaSR-expressing HEK293 cells, the mutant raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium above both wild type and vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. Unlike wild-type AP2S1, the mutant did not increase calcium-stimulated CaSR internalization. ⚠︎

Howles et al. (2016) reported a proband with symptomatic hypercalcemia who carried a heterozygous R15C mutation and responded to cinacalcet.

Howles et al. (2016) reported a proband with symptomatic hypercalcemia who carried a heterozygous R15H mutation and responded to cinacalcet.
