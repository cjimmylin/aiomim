### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also called Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene on chromosome 14q24. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is a connective tissue disorder. Its features overlap with Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Marfan syndrome, and Shprintzen-Goldberg syndrome. Craniofacial features include hypertelorism, bifid uvula, and cleft palate. Skeletal features include arachnodactyly, joint hyperextensibility, pectus deformity, scoliosis, and clubfoot. Stature can be short or tall (Rienhoff et al., 2013; Kuechler et al., 2015). Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) showed that cardiovascular involvement varies widely. It includes thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysm and dissection and mitral valve disease, and some mutation carriers have no cardiovascular abnormalities. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Kuechler et al. (2015) referred to the emerging connective tissue disorder caused by TGFB3 mutations as Rienhoff syndrome, a tentative name.

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European American girl born to nonconsanguineous, healthy parents. At birth she had contractures of the right hand, most severe in the third and fourth fingers, and of all toes. She also had a midline facial nevus flammeus and mild hypotonia. Her birth weight was 2.9 kg (5th centile). At 3 months she was evaluated for failure to thrive and delayed gross motor development. At 17 months she weighed 7.5 kg (less than 1st centile) and had the following features: bilateral pes planus, mild pectus excavatum, hyperextensibility of multiple large joints, mild retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, blue sclerae, a small metopic ridge, and a bifid uvula with an intact hard palate. She had decreased bulk in all appendicular and axial muscles, strength of 1/5, low tone, diminished reflexes, and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. At 20 months she stood independently with a positive Gowers sign, and at 24 months she walked with a 'hip waddle.' Neurocognitive development was appropriate for age. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. A quadriceps biopsy at age 7 years showed type 1 fiber predominance with mild, focal type 1 fiber disproportion consistent with disuse, and no dystrophic or inflammatory changes. At age 7 years she weighed 15.5 kg (less than 1st centile) and was 115 cm tall (5th centile). Yearly echocardiograms from 18 months onward showed no cardiac defect. At age 6.5 years the aortic annulus and root dimensions were normal. The authors described the disorder as a syndrome of low muscle mass, growth retardation, and distal arthrogryposis with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, without evidence of vascular disease. ⚠︎

Matyas et al. (2014) described a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) and Kuechler et al. (2015) characterized this patient as a child with tall stature, aged 10.5 years when reported, who had cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with features overlapping Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Seven family members aged 40 to 68 years had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three died from aortic dissection and rupture. Four family members had mitral valve abnormalities ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation that required surgery. Craniofacial abnormalities were subtle: long face, high-arched palate, and retrognathia. Pectus deformity and scoliosis were frequent. Other recurrent findings were velvety skin, varices, and hiatal hernia. Several family members had autoimmune features, including HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease, and celiac disease.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) also described 10 additional families. In total they reported 43 affected individuals from 11 families. Vascular involvement ranged from no cardiovascular abnormalities at age 64 years to aortic dissection. Type A dissection occurred at a median age of 51 years (range, 40 to 80 years) and type B dissection at a median age of 44.5 years (range, 30 to 57 years). One patient died from dissection of a cerebral aneurysm at age 55 years. No early arterial dissection or dissection at small aortic dimension was observed, and there was no striking aortic or arterial tortuosity. Other cardiovascular features were mitral valve disease, persistent foramen ovale, and atrial or ventricular septal defects. Typical Loeys-Dietz features were common, including hypertelorism (14 patients), bifid uvula (11), cleft palate (5), cervical spine instability, and clubfoot. Other recurrent features were dolichocephaly, high-arched palate, retrognathia, tall stature, joint hypermobility, arachnodactyly, pectus deformity, kyphoscoliosis, and inguinal hernia. Early-onset osteoarthritis was reported in only 2 individuals, and no ectopia lentis was found. One patient had short stature and received growth hormone. The authors noted striking intrafamilial and interfamilial variability. Typical Loeys-Dietz features were present in some patients and completely absent in others. Aortic tissue from a dissected aorta showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition.

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a father and his 2 children with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. All 3 had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula. None had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation, or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) reported autosomal dominant inheritance in a large Dutch family. Kuechler et al. (2015) reported transmission of the disorder from an affected father to his 2 children. De novo heterozygous TGFB3 mutations were found in the patient of Rienhoff et al. (2013) and in 1 family studied by Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015).

### Mapping

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed genomewide linkage analysis using SNP genotypes from 6 patients in a large Dutch family. They identified 2 large genomic regions on chromosomes 14 and 15 that were shared by all affected individuals. The chromosome 14 region contained the TGFB3 gene, at 14q24.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, low muscle mass, and growth retardation. They found a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution, and confirmed it by Sanger sequencing. Before exome sequencing, they had found no mutation in TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1, or FBN2. The patient also carried a de novo heterozygous nonsense mutation in the CDH2 gene (c.208C-T; Q70X). N-cadherin levels in her fibroblasts did not differ from controls, and the authors judged the CDH2 variant unlikely to drive the phenotype. The mutant TGFB3 ligand did not activate a TGF-beta reporter in cultured cells. When coexpressed with wildtype TGFB3 in Xenopus embryos, it reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals but did not affect BMP signaling. The authors concluded that the mutation is hypomorphic. ⚠︎

Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. As cited by Kuechler et al. (2015), the mutation was a c.899G-A transition resulting in an arg300-to-gln (R300Q) substitution.

In a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms and dissections, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) first sequenced known thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection genes and found no causal mutation. Linkage analysis followed by sequencing of the candidate gene TGFB3 then identified a heterozygous splice site mutation (c.754+2T-C). The mutation segregated with the phenotype. It caused skipping of exon 4 and an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids (Glu216_Lys251del). They then screened 470 additional probands with thoracic aortic aneurysms by Sanger or exome sequencing. This identified heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 more probands. There were 4 missense mutations: D263H, R300W (in 4 families), I322T, and L401P. There was also 1 nonsense mutation (Y365X), which occurred de novo, and 2 single-base deletions that caused frameshifts (Leu386ArgfsTer21 and Asn235MetfsTer11). None of the mutations was present in control databases. The proband with the de novo Y365X mutation also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance, which he had inherited from his mother. The mother had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement. ⚠︎

Kuechler et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing in 2 of 3 affected members of a family with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. They identified a heterozygous c.898C-G transversion in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and showed that it segregated with the phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that TGFB3 mutations seem to have opposing effects on height. The patients with C409Y and D263H had short stature, whereas other patients, including the one reported by Matyas et al. (2014), had tall stature. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) also found that mutations affecting arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula. Kuechler et al. (2015) proposed that mutations at arg300 lead to increased TGF-beta signaling. They suggested that the short or tall stature seen with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of different mutations on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) concluded that their patient's phenotype resulted from decreased TGF-beta signaling caused by a hypomorphic TGFB3 allele. They proposed that it differed from the excess TGF-beta signaling seen in Marfan syndrome. In contrast, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed immunohistochemistry on aortic wall tissue from a patient with the D263H mutation. They found increased pSMAD2, increased pERK, and elevated TGFB1 mRNA, indicating a paradoxical increase in both canonical and noncanonical TGF-beta signaling. This resembled findings with mutations in TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, and TGFB2. Based on the truncating and splice site mutations, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) hypothesized that most TGFB3 mutations cause loss of function. They suggested that the earlier patients lacked obvious cardiovascular disease because they were young (8 and 10.5 years old).

### Clinical Management

Starting at age 3 years, Rienhoff et al. (2013) treated their patient with losartan at doses of up to 2.0 mg/kg/day for 3 years. Muscle strength and muscle mass did not change.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers. They also recommended at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches. They suggested that brain vessel imaging may be indicated, depending on family history.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.Cys409Tyr]

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, low muscle mass, and growth retardation. In her they identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It affects a cysteine of the cysteine knot that is conserved in all TGF-beta family ligands. The mutant ligand did not activate a TGF-beta reporter. In Xenopus embryos, coexpression of the mutant with wildtype TGFB3 reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signaling.

**.0002 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLN [c.899G>A; p.Arg300Gln]

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a child with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. The child carried a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3: a c.899G-A transition resulting in an arg300-to-gln (R300Q) substitution. ⚠︎

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, IVS4DS, T-C, +2 [c.754+2T>C; p.Glu216_Lys251del]

In a large Dutch family with aortic aneurysms and dissections, mitral valve disease, and features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.754+2T-C mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects the canonical donor splice site of exon 4 and was found by linkage analysis followed by Sanger sequencing. cDNA analysis showed skipping of exon 4, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids (Glu216_Lys251del). The mutation segregated with the phenotype and was absent from variant databases.

**.0004 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ASP263HIS [p.Asp263His]

In a family (family 3) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous asp263-to-his (D263H) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation disrupts the RGD integrin-binding motif of the latency-associated peptide domain. Aortic tissue from a patient carrying this mutation showed elastic fiber fragmentation. It also showed increased pSMAD2, pERK, and TGFB1 mRNA. This patient had short stature and received growth hormone therapy.

**.0005 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300TRP [p.Arg300Trp]

In 4 families (families 2, 4, 5, and 11) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous arg300-to-trp (R300W) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects the last residue of the latency-associated peptide domain, within the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif. It was absent from control databases. Mutations affecting arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

**.0006 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ILE322THR [p.Ile322Thr]

In a family (family 10) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous ile322-to-thr (I322T) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the active cytokine region. Homology modeling predicted that it would alter the positioning of arg325, a residue important for binding to TGFBR2.

**.0007 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, LEU401PRO [p.Leu401Pro]

In a family (family 8) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous leu401-to-pro (L401P) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the active cytokine region and is predicted to change hydrophobic interactions.

**.0008 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, TYR365TER [p.Tyr365*]

In a proband with syndromic aortic aneurysm (family 6), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a de novo heterozygous tyr365-to-ter (Y365X) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T), which had been inherited from his mother. ⚠︎

**.0009 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLY [c.898C>G; p.Arg300Gly]

In a father and his 2 children with tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula, Kuechler et al.
