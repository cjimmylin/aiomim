### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications (SENEBAC) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NRROS gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications (SENEBAC) is a severe autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder. Development is normal or mildly delayed at first. Refractory seizures begin in infancy or the second year of life and are followed by developmental regression. Brain imaging shows intracranial calcification, reduced white matter volume or white matter signal changes, and progressive cerebral atrophy. In some patients the disease has caused death in early childhood (Smith et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2020). Smith et al. (2020) considered the disorder a microgliopathy. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Smith et al. (2020) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Two sisters (P1 and P2) were born to nonconsanguineous parents of Maori descent. The third patient (P3), a boy, was born to first-cousin parents of south Asian origin. All 3 children were born after a normal pregnancy and delivery, and their early development was unremarkable. In the second year of life they developed refractory seizures and neurodegeneration, and they died between the ages of 27 and 36 months. Metabolic testing, including for mitochondrial dysfunction, was noncontributory. Early neuroimaging showed fine calcification at the depths of the cerebral gyri and normal white matter. CT showed widespread spot and linear calcification in the deep and subcortical white matter. Initial MRI showed mild loss of cerebral volume with relatively good myelination. As the disease progressed, repeat imaging showed more calcification, severe generalized atrophy with ventricular dilatation, and diffuse signal changes in the cerebral and cerebellar white matter.

Smith et al. (2020) performed a postmortem examination of P3, which showed abnormalities confined to the central nervous system. There was significant cerebral atrophy, with gray and white matter pathology throughout the cerebrum, cerebellum, and brainstem. Focal calcification was seen in the neuropil, and there was widespread neuronal loss with reactive gliosis throughout the gray matter. The most striking finding was an accumulation of foamy macrophages, mainly around blood vessels. These cells extended through the white matter from the frontal to the occipital lobes and through the cerebellar white matter and the descending corticospinal pathways in the basis pontis. The foamy cells were immunoreactive for CD68, MHC class II, and p22phox. They were not immunoreactive for CD163, IBA1, NRROS, CD3, P2Y12, or TMEM119. Myelin basic protein expression in the white matter was reduced, although the U fibers were preserved. Occasional axonal spheroids were present. Parenchymal microglia were markedly abnormal: the distribution, density, and morphology of IBA1-positive cells were disrupted, P2Y12 staining was lost, and TMEM119 immunoreactivity was weak. There was no sign of immune-mediated inflammation.

Dong et al. (2020) reported 6 individuals from 4 families. All had neurodegenerative disease with refractory epilepsy, developmental regression, and reduced white matter volume with delayed myelination. Development was normal or mildly delayed at first. Seizures began within the first year of life and were followed by developmental regression. Intracranial calcification was found in 3 individuals. All affected children had variable seizure types that evolved into refractory epilepsy. ✗

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SENEBAC in the families reported by Smith et al. (2020) and Dong et al. (2020) is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Smith et al. (2020) found homozygous mutations in affected children, including a child of first-cousin parents. Dong et al. (2020) found biallelic variants. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Smith et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to study the 3 patients from 2 families with SENEBAC. All carried homozygous mutations in the NRROS gene. The Maori family carried c.1777C-T (Q593X), and the south Asian family carried a 1-bp deletion (c.1257del) that caused a frameshift (Gly420AlafsTer14). Both mutations were predicted to produce a truncated protein. The Q593X mutation had not been reported before, and the 1-bp deletion appeared on only 1 of 251,438 alleles in the gnomAD database. No cellular material from the patients was available for functional studies.

Dong et al. (2020) identified biallelic NRROS variants in 6 individuals with SENEBAC from 4 families. The variants affected 2 important functional domains of NRROS and produced abnormal proteins that were less able to anchor latent TGF-beta-1 at the cell surface. In HEK293T cells studied by confocal microscopy, both wildtype and mutant NRROS colocalized with latent TGF-beta-1 inside the cell. Flow cytometry showed that, unlike wildtype NRROS, the mutant proteins failed to anchor latent TGF-beta-1 at the cell surface. Wildtype NRROS corrected the mutants' defect in bringing latent TGF-beta-1 to the cell surface. Dong et al. (2020) concluded that loss of NRROS function causes the disorder.

### Pathogenesis

Using curated transcriptomic data sets, Smith et al. (2020) found that NRROS expression in human brain was enriched more than 50-fold in isolated microglia compared with whole brain. This indicated that microglia are the main cell type expressing NRROS in the brain parenchyma. In mouse brain, Nrros expression was likewise enriched in microglia, and it was even higher in perivascular macrophages. In their autopsied patient, p22phox was markedly upregulated in perivascular macrophages. Smith et al. (2020) suggested that loss of functional NRROS may increase p22phox-NOX2 binding and could increase superoxide formation. They concluded that NRROS is essential for the early development and/or postnatal maintenance of a homeostatic microglial population, and that loss of NRROS function causes a microgliopathy. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Wong et al. (2017) found that Nrros-deficient mice developed spontaneous neurological disorders, with motor defects and death before 6 months of age. The mice showed astrogliosis and lacked normal CD11b(hi)CD45(lo) microglia, but they had no detectable demyelination or neuronal loss. Instead, the central nervous system was populated by myeloid cells resembling perivascular macrophages. Cx3cr1-driven deletion of Nrros showed that NRROS has a critical role in establishing microglia during early embryonic stages. NRROS was required for normal expression of Sall1 and of other genes important for microglial development and function.

Smith et al. (2020) noted that the features in their patients resembled those of Nrros-deficient mice. Neuropathology in the mouse models included neuronal loss, demyelination, axonal pathology, astrogliosis, and increased numbers of foamy macrophages.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SEIZURES, EARLY-ONSET, WITH NEURODEGENERATION AND BRAIN CALCIFICATIONS**  
NRROS, GLN593TER [c.1777C>T; p.Gln593Ter]

Smith et al. (2020) studied 2 sisters of Maori descent with SENEBAC who were born to nonconsanguineous parents (family F1442). Exome sequencing showed that both carried a homozygous c.1777C-T transition in the NRROS gene, resulting in a gln593-to-ter (Q593X) substitution. The mutation was predicted to produce a truncated protein and had not been recorded before. No cellular material was available for functional studies.

**.0002 SEIZURES, EARLY-ONSET, WITH NEURODEGENERATION AND BRAIN CALCIFICATIONS**  
NRROS, 1-BP DEL, NT1257 [c.1257del; p.Gly420AlafsTer14]

Smith et al. (2020) studied a boy with SENEBAC who was born to first-cousin parents of south Asian origin (family F2382). Exome sequencing showed that he carried a homozygous 1-bp deletion (c.1257del) in the NRROS gene, resulting in a frameshift and premature termination (Gly420AlafsTer14). The variant was predicted to produce a truncated protein and appeared on only 1 of 251,438 alleles in gnomAD. The patient died, and a postmortem examination showed cerebral atrophy, widespread neuronal loss with gliosis, focal calcification, perivascular foamy macrophages in the white matter, and abnormal parenchymal microglia.
