### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is caused by homozygous mutation in the KIRREL1 gene on chromosome 1.

### Description

Nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is an autosomal recessive kidney disorder. It presents in childhood as steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) and has no extrarenal features. Renal biopsy shows low-grade mesangial hypercellularity, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), or both, with foot process effacement on electron microscopy. Response to treatment varies, and the disease may progress to chronic kidney disease (Solanki et al., 2019).

### Clinical Features

Solanki et al. (2019) described a boy (A666_21) from a consanguineous family who was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at 5 years of age. He was steroid-resistant from presentation and also resistant to several other immunosuppressive drugs. He never achieved complete remission. His proteinuria fluctuated, with only partial responses to treatment, and ACE inhibitors and ARBs did not help. His renal function stayed normal throughout, and he stopped attending clinic after 12.5 years of follow-up. On light microscopy, renal biopsy showed only minor mesangial hypercellularity, consistent with minimal change disease. His younger sister reportedly had periorbital edema and proteinuria, and in the extended family 3 children had end-stage renal disease and 1 child had nephrotic syndrome. None of these relatives could be genotyped.

Solanki et al. (2019) also described a boy of Italian descent (B742_21) from a second, unrelated consanguineous family. He was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at 14 years of age and was steroid-resistant from presentation. He was started on tacrolimus and weaned off corticosteroids, and he received a calcium channel blocker and an ACE inhibitor for high blood pressure and proteinuria. With this treatment and a gradual increase in the ACE inhibitor dose, he reached complete remission. Over 4 years of follow-up, however, he developed chronic kidney disease, which had progressed to stage 2 at the time of the report. Light microscopy of renal biopsy showed FSGS. Electron microscopy showed extensive foot process effacement, increased mesangial matrix, and mesangial proliferation. Neither of the 2 patients had extrarenal or syndromic features.

### Inheritance

Solanki et al. (2019) found homozygous KIRREL1 mutations in affected children from 2 consanguineous families, consistent with autosomal recessive transmission of NPHS23.

### Molecular Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) performed whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping in 596 individuals from 406 families with SRNS. They identified homozygous missense mutations in the KIRREL1 gene in affected individuals from 2 unrelated consanguineous families: arg440-to-cys (R440C) in patient A666_21 and ser573-to-leu (S573L) in patient B742_21. KIRREL1 mutations therefore accounted for 2 of 406 families (0.4%) in the cohort. Both mutations lay within the maximum homozygosity peak on chromosome 1, and both affected residues are conserved to D. rerio. In gnomAD, R440C had a minor allele frequency of 3.97 x 10(-6) and S573L had a minor allele frequency of 7.87 x 10(-4). S573L had an allele frequency of 0.0011 in Europeans and was present in the homozygous state in 1 control individual. The authors suggested that this variant might show variable expressivity or incomplete penetrance.

In functional studies, Solanki et al. (2019) stably expressed the mutant proteins in cultured human podocytes. Unlike wildtype KIRREL1, both mutants were mislocalized and were present at significantly lower levels in the membrane fraction. Both mutants accumulated in the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes, indicating defective trafficking. Membrane localization of NPHS1 was not affected. In a transepithelial permeability assay, podocyte monolayers expressing the mutants showed significantly increased albumin flux compared with wildtype.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2003) noted that Neph1-deficient mice develop nephrotic syndrome at birth. Using immunogold electron microscopy in rodents, they localized Neph1 to the glomerular slit diaphragm. They showed that Neph1 forms dimers and multimers, interacts with nephrin through its extracellular segment, and binds ZO-1 through its cytoplasmic tail. In rats, injecting combinations of individually subnephritogenic doses of anti-Neph1 and anti-nephrin antibodies disrupted the Neph1-nephrin interaction. This caused complement- and leukocyte-independent proteinuria with preserved foot processes, modestly reduced Neph1 and nephrin protein expression, and markedly reduced ZO-1 protein expression. ⚠︎
