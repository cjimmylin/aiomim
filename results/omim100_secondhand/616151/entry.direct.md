### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that vitelliform macular dystrophy-4 (VMD4) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the IMPG1 gene.

### Description

Vitelliform macular dystrophies (VMD) are inherited retinal dystrophies characterized by yellow, round deposits visible upon fundus examination (Manes et al., 2013). VMD4, caused by mutation in the IMPG1 gene, may be inherited in an autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive manner (Manes et al., 2013). Most patients have a normal or moderately decreased electrooculogram (EOG) Arden ratio (Manes et al., 2013). The vitelliform material lies above a well-preserved retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) on spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) (Meunier et al., 2014; Brandl et al., 2017).

### Clinical Features

Manes et al. (2013) studied a large family with autosomal dominant VMD and 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD and the same phenotype. They also reported 2 individuals from 1 family with autosomal recessive VMD and 2 individuals from another family. Most of the patients had a normal or moderately decreased EOG Arden ratio.

Meunier et al. (2014) characterized the clinical features of 3 families (11 patients) with VMD caused by IMPG1 mutation and 1 family (2 patients) with VMD caused by IMPG2 mutation. Patients with IMPG1 or IMPG2 mutations had late onset and moderate visual impairment (mean visual acuity, 20/40; mean age of onset, 42 years). This was also true of the sporadic case in family 3, which had presumed recessive transmission (age at onset, 38 years; mean visual acuity, 20/50). In 9 of the 13 patients, drusen-like lesions were seen next to the vitelliform deposits. At every stage of the dystrophy, the vitelliform material lay above the RPE. On SD-OCT, the RPE was well preserved and kept its classical reflectivity. EOG results were normal or borderline in 9 cases. Meunier et al. (2014) concluded that the following features suggest IMPG1 or IMPG2 involvement in adult-onset vitelliform dystrophy: moderate visual impairment, drusen-like lesions, normal reflectivity of the RPE line on SD-OCT, and vitelliform deposits located between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines on SD-OCT. They noted that these features are not seen in the classical forms of BEST1 or PRPH2 vitelliform dystrophy.

Brandl et al. (2017) reported 2 simplex VMD cases with IMPG1 mutations and disease onset in early childhood. The first patient was a German boy who had reduced visual acuity at age 13 years (0.2 and 0.1 logMAR in the right and left eyes, respectively). Both fundi showed central RPE abnormalities with foveal detachment. The second patient, a boy from a consanguineous Turkish family, was diagnosed with VMD at age 2 years. Over 8 years of follow-up, his visual acuity fluctuated at about 0.2 logMAR in both eyes. At age 10, multifocal ERG was normal and the EOG Arden ratio was borderline to slightly abnormally low. Each eye had a single vitelliform lesion in the central macula, and the rest of the fundus was normal. The lesion enlarged during follow-up. SD-OCT showed a bilateral, central, dome-shaped foveal detachment of the neurosensory retina. The hyperreflective material beneath it was reabsorbed during follow-up, leaving an optically empty cavity. Photoreceptor outer segments were elongated, and Bruch membrane and the RPE appeared well preserved. Fundus autofluorescence showed circular hyperfluorescence that decreased as the disease progressed. No satellite drusen were seen. Brandl et al. (2017) noted that their patients were much younger than the previously reported patients with satellite drusen-like lesions. They stated that it remained to be seen whether such lesions would develop in adulthood.

Gupta et al. (2021) described a 25-year-old woman with a symptomatic scotoma and reduced vision who had small yellow foveal spots in both eyes. On OCT, the foveal ellipsoid and interdigitation zones were disorganized and had reduced reflectance, more so in the left eye than the right. Fundus autofluorescence findings were minimal, and dye angiography showed nothing abnormal. Multifocal electroretinogram showed slightly reduced retinal sensitivity in the central retina of the left eye.

### Inheritance

Manes et al. (2013) found that IMPG1 mutations cause both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive forms of VMD. Meunier et al. (2014) observed autosomal dominant transmission in 2 families with IMPG1 mutation and presumed recessive transmission in a sporadic case.

### Heterogeneity

Meunier et al. (2014) found a heterozygous c.3230G-T (C1077F) mutation in the IMPG2 gene in a family with autosomal dominant VMD. Brandl et al. (2017) identified heterozygous IMPG2 mutations in 8 patients with VMD, including the C1077F mutation. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Manes et al. (2013) used gene mapping and whole-exome sequencing in a large family with autosomal dominant VMD and identified a heterozygous c.713T-G mutation in the IMPG1 gene, resulting in a leu238-to-arg (L238R) substitution. The same mutation was then found in 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD. Structural modeling indicated that the L238R substitution destabilizes the conserved SEA1 domain of the SPACR protein encoded by IMPG1. Manes et al. (2013) then screened 144 probands with various forms of macular dystrophy and found 3 other IMPG1 mutations. Two individuals from a family with autosomal recessive VMD were homozygous for a c.807+1G-T splice site mutation. Two individuals from another family were compound heterozygous for c.461T-C (L154P) and c.1519C-T (R507X) mutations.

Meunier et al. (2014) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 49 families with VMD who had no identified mutation, small deletion, or large rearrangement in the BEST1 and PRPH2 genes. IMPG1 was the causal gene in 3 families (11 patients). Two of these families had autosomal dominant transmission and carried the L238R mutation. IMPG1 and IMPG2 together accounted for 8% of families negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations.

Brandl et al. (2017) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 106 unrelated patients with Best disease or adult-onset VMD who had tested negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations. They identified 2 novel IMPG1 mutations, each in a simplex case with onset in early childhood. A German boy was heterozygous for a c.713T-C mutation resulting in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution in the SEA I domain; data on his parents were not available. A boy from a consanguineous Turkish family was homozygous for a c.807+5G-A splice site mutation, and segregation analysis showed that both parents were heterozygous. In an in vitro splicing assay, this mutation caused partial skipping of exon 7.

In a 25-year-old woman with early-onset vitelliform dystrophy, Gupta et al. (2021) identified a heterozygous L154P mutation in the IMPG1 gene by genetic testing. They suggested that this mutation may cause symptomatic outer retinal disturbance in the heterozygous state.

Olivier et al. (2021) used exome sequencing in a family with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (RP) and identified a splice variant in IMPG1. The same variant was later found in individuals from 2 families with either RP or VMD. A retrospective study of patients with RP or VMD found 8 more families with various missense or nonsense IMPG1 variants. Morpholino knockdown of Impg1 and its paralog Impg2 in medaka fish reduced the length of rod and cone photoreceptor outer segments.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Brandl et al. (2017) observed that clinical symptoms tended to be more severe in patients with IMPG1 mutations than in those with IMPG2 mutations. They noted that, as reported in the literature, heterozygous carriers of the c.807+1G-T mutation have a slightly decreased EOG Arden ratio and tiny extramacular deposits. In contrast, the heterozygous parents of their patient homozygous for the c.807+5G-A mutation had full visual acuity and normal multimodal retinal imaging. Brandl et al. (2017) suggested that this is because a large fraction of pre-mRNAs carrying the c.807+5G-A mutation are spliced correctly.

### Animal Model

Olivier et al. (2021) used morpholinos to knock down Impg1 and its paralog Impg2 in medaka fish. The resulting phenotype was consistent with that seen in patients and included shorter rod and cone photoreceptor outer segments.

One allele carried a c.461T-C transition resulting in a leu154-to-pro (L154P) substitution, and the other carried a c.1519C-T transition resulting in an arg507-to-ter (R507X) substitution.

**.0005 MACULAR DYSTROPHY, VITELLIFORM, 4**  
IMPG1, LEU238PRO [c.713T>C; p.Leu238Pro]

Brandl et al. (2017) identified a heterozygous c.713T-C transition in exon 7 of the IMPG1 gene in a German boy with VMD4. The mutation results in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution in the SEA I domain. He was the only affected member of his family, and data on his parents were not available. All 3 in silico algorithms used predicted the variant to be pathogenic. At age 13 years, he had reduced visual acuity and central RPE abnormalities with foveal detachment in both eyes.

**.0006 MACULAR DYSTROPHY, VITELLIFORM, 4**  
IMPG1, IVS7DS, G-A, +5 [c.807+5G>A]

Brandl et al. (2017) identified a homozygous c.807+5G-A splice site mutation in the IMPG1 gene in a boy from a consanguineous Turkish family. He had been diagnosed with VMD4 at age 2 years. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation and had no symptoms. In an in vitro minigene splicing assay in HEK293 cells, about half of the transcripts skipped exon 7.
