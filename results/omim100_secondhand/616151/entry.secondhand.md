### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that vitelliform macular dystrophy-4 (VMD4) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the IMPG1 gene.

### Description

Vitelliform macular dystrophies are inherited retinal dystrophies in which round, yellow deposits are seen on fundus examination. They include juvenile Best macular dystrophy and adult-onset vitelliform macular dystrophy (Manes et al., 2013). In IMPG1-related VMD, onset is usually in adulthood and visual impairment is moderate. Drusen-like lesions may lie next to the vitelliform deposits, the vitelliform material sits above a well-preserved retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and the electrooculogram (EOG) is usually normal or only borderline (Manes et al., 2013; Meunier et al., 2014). Inheritance can be autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive (Manes et al., 2013), and onset in early childhood has been reported (Brandl et al., 2017).

### Clinical Features

Manes et al. (2013) studied a large family with autosomal dominant VMD and 2 more families with autosomal dominant VMD and the same phenotype. They also studied 2 affected members of a family with autosomal recessive VMD and 2 affected members of another family. Most patients had a normal or moderately decreased EOG Arden ratio.

Meunier et al. (2014) described 13 patients: 11 patients from 3 families with IMPG1 mutations and 2 patients from 1 family with an IMPG2 mutation. Onset was late and visual impairment was moderate, with a mean visual acuity of 20/40 and a mean age at onset of 42 years. The pattern was similar in the sporadic case in IMPG1 family 3, whose transmission was presumed to be recessive (age at onset, 38 years; mean visual acuity, 20/50). Nine of the 13 patients had drusen-like lesions next to the vitelliform deposits. On spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT), the vitelliform material lay above the RPE at every stage of the dystrophy, and the RPE was well preserved with its classical reflectivity. The vitelliform deposits were located between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines. The EOG was normal or borderline in 9 patients. Meunier et al. (2014) noted that these features are not seen in the classical forms of BEST1- or PRPH2-related vitelliform dystrophy.

Brandl et al. (2017) described 2 sporadic VMD patients with IMPG1 mutations whose disease began in early childhood. The first patient, a boy from a German family, had reduced visual acuity at age 13 (0.2 logMAR in the right eye and 0.1 logMAR in the left eye). Both fundi showed central RPE abnormalities with foveal detachment. He was the only affected member of his family. The second patient was a boy from a consanguineous Turkish family. He was first diagnosed with VMD at age 2, and his visual acuity fluctuated around 0.2 logMAR in both eyes over 8 years of follow-up. At age 10, the multifocal electroretinogram (ERG) was normal and the EOG Arden ratio was borderline to slightly low. Each eye had a single central vitelliform lesion, and the lesion area enlarged during follow-up. SD-OCT showed a bilateral, central, dome-shaped foveal detachment of the neurosensory retina. The hyperreflective material beneath it was reabsorbed, leaving an optically empty cavity, and the photoreceptor outer segments were elongated. Bruch's membrane and the RPE kept their classical reflectivity. Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) showed circular hyperautofluorescence that lessened over time, and there were no satellite drusen. Brandl et al. (2017) commented that clinical symptoms tended to be more severe with IMPG1 mutations than with IMPG2 mutations.

Gupta et al. (2021) reported a 25-year-old woman with a symptomatic scotoma and decreased vision who had small yellow foveal spots in both eyes. Optical coherence tomography showed disorganization and reduced reflectance of the foveal ellipsoid and interdigitation zones, worse in the left eye than the right. FAF findings were minimal and dye angiography was unrevealing. The multifocal ERG showed slightly reduced retinal sensitivity in the central retina of the left eye.

### Inheritance

Manes et al. (2013) found that IMPG1 mutations cause both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive forms of VMD. Meunier et al. (2014) confirmed autosomal dominant transmission in 2 families and presumed recessive transmission in a sporadic case.

### Molecular Genetics

Manes et al. (2013) studied a large family with autosomal dominant VMD using gene mapping and whole-exome sequencing. They identified a heterozygous c.713T-G mutation in the IMPG1 gene, resulting in a leu238-to-arg (L238R) substitution, and then found the same mutation in 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD. Structural modeling predicted that the L238R substitution destabilizes the conserved SEA1 domain of SPACR, the protein encoded by IMPG1. Manes et al. (2013) then screened 144 probands with various forms of macular dystrophy and found 3 more IMPG1 mutations. Two affected members of a family with autosomal recessive VMD were homozygous for a splice site mutation (c.807+1G-T). Two affected members of another family were compound heterozygous for a leu154-to-pro (L154P) mutation and an arg507-to-ter (R507X) mutation.

Meunier et al. (2014) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 49 families with vitelliform macular dystrophy who had no mutation, small deletion, or large rearrangement in BEST1 or PRPH2. They found IMPG1 mutations in 3 families (11 patients) and an IMPG2 mutation in 1 family. Two IMPG1 families had autosomal dominant transmission and carried the L238R mutation. In the third IMPG1 family, a sporadic case, transmission was presumed to be recessive. Overall, IMPG1 and IMPG2 accounted for 8% of the families without BEST1 or PRPH2 mutations.

Brandl et al. (2017) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 106 unrelated patients with VMD who had no mutation in BEST1 or PRPH2. In 2 sporadic patients whose disease began in childhood, they found 2 novel IMPG1 mutations. The first patient was heterozygous for a c.713T-C mutation in exon 7, resulting in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution in the SEA I domain. All 3 in silico tools predicted it to be pathogenic, and his parents could not be tested. The second patient, from a consanguineous Turkish family, was homozygous for a c.807+5G-A mutation in intron 7. Testing of the parents confirmed homozygosity, and both heterozygous parents were clinically unaffected. In an in vitro minigene splicing assay, the mutation caused exon 7 to be skipped in about half of the transcripts. Brandl et al. (2017) also found heterozygous IMPG2 mutations in 8 patients.

In the 25-year-old woman with early-onset outer retinal disruption and small foveal yellow spots, Gupta et al. (2021) found a heterozygous L154P mutation in the IMPG1 gene. They suggested that this mutation may cause symptomatic outer retinal disturbance in the heterozygous state, while noting that further studies were needed.

Olivier et al. (2021) identified an IMPG1 splice variant by exome sequencing in a family with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (RP). They then found the same variant in individuals from 2 families with either RP or VMD. A retrospective study of patients with RP or VMD found 8 more families with different IMPG1 missense or nonsense variants.

### Animal Model

Olivier et al. (2021) knocked down Impg1 and its paralog Impg2 in medaka fish using morpholinos. The fish showed a phenotype consistent with that of the human families, including shortened rod and cone photoreceptor outer segments.
