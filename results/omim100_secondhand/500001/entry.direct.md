### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is caused by mutation in one of several mitochondrial DNA-encoded complex I genes, including MTND6, MTND4, MTND1, and MTND3. ⚠︎

### Description

Leber optic atrophy and dystonia is a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder. Affected people have different combinations of vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia. Some have subacute visual loss like that of Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), others have asymptomatic optic atrophy, and others have dystonia linked to bilateral lesions of the basal ganglia, especially the putamen. These features can occur together or separately within the same family (Marsden et al., 1986; Novotny et al., 1986; Wang et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Miyoshi et al. (1969) reported familial holotopistic striatal necrosis.

Marsden et al. (1986) described 7 members of 2 families with dystonia. In these patients, the dystonia occurred with subacute visual loss or with asymptomatic optic atrophy to varying degrees. CT scans showed striking bilateral symmetrical lucencies, particularly in the putamen. The authors considered that the disorder might be a variant of Leigh disease, but they noted considerable differences between these patients and those with pathologically proven Leigh disease. They suggested that the condition must be excluded in all patients thought to have idiopathic dystonia, subacute visual failure resembling Leber optic neuropathy, or both.

Novotny et al. (1986) studied a kindred in which 8 members had the neuroretinopathy of Leber disease and 14 had a progressive generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration. One member had both disorders. The authors suggested that the disorder might be a mitochondrial disease.

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family. Members had maternally transmitted Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, early-onset dystonia with bilateral basal ganglia lesions, or both.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family with a rare form of LHON associated with hereditary spastic dystonia. Some family members had both the neurologic and ophthalmologic abnormalities, whereas others had only one of them.

Watanabe et al. (2006) described a Japanese family with generalized dystonia beginning in childhood, attributed to striatal degeneration, and with late-onset optic neuropathy.

Spruijt et al. (2007) reported a family in which LHON and spastic dystonia both occurred. They noted that the clinical features of this family were similar to those of previously described families with LHON and dystonia caused by an ND6 mutation.

Wang et al. (2009) described a Chinese family with 18 patients with LDYT. The patients had variable combinations of vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

In a Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia, De Vries et al. (1996) found severe complex I deficiency on biochemical analysis of a muscle biopsy.

Spruijt et al. (2007) found that the 3697G-A mutation in the MTND1 gene appeared to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency.

### Inheritance

Novotny et al. (1986) found that inheritance in their kindred was compatible with maternal transmission. Maternal transmission was also observed in the families reported by Jun et al. (1994) and Wang et al. (2009), consistent with mitochondrial inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-generation Hispanic family with LHON and/or dystonia, Jun et al. (1994) amplified buffy coat mtDNA from a severely affected child by PCR and sequenced more than 90% of it. The mtDNA belonged to Native American haplogroup D and differed from the Cambridge sequence at 40 nucleotide positions. One of these differences was a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 14459 in the MTND6 gene, causing an ala72-to-val (A72V) substitution at a moderately conserved residue. This variant was not found in 38 Native American haplogroup D mtDNAs, 108 Asian mtDNAs, 103 Caucasian mtDNAs, or 99 African mtDNAs. Six maternal relatives in 3 generations carried the mutation. One female with LHON was heteroplasmic for it. Jun et al. (1994) concluded that the mutation was likely the cause of the disease.

In a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia, De Vries et al. (1996) did not find mtDNA mutations previously reported in LHON. Sequencing of the protein-coding mitochondrial genes revealed 2 previously unreported mutations. The first was a heteroplasmic A-to-G transition at nucleotide 11696 in the MTND4 gene, causing a val312-to-ile (V312I) substitution. The second was a homoplasmic T-to-A transition at nucleotide 14596 in the MTND6 gene, causing an ile26-to-met (I26M) substitution. A muscle biopsy showed severe complex I deficiency.

Watanabe et al. (2006) sequenced the entire mtDNA of the proband from a Japanese family with childhood-onset dystonia and late-onset optic neuropathy. Compared with the revised Cambridge reference sequence, the proband's mtDNA had 42 nucleotide changes. Two of these, A3203G and G14459A, were absent from 200 healthy Japanese individuals. The G14459A mutation is in the MTND6 gene. The significance of the A3203G change was unknown.

Spruijt et al. (2007) screened the complete mtDNA of a patient with LHON who did not carry any of the 3 primary LHON mutations. Spastic dystonia was also present in the patient's family. They identified a pathogenic 3697G-A mutation in the MTND1 gene, which appeared to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency. They noted that this mutation can also cause MELAS syndrome and suggested that other genetic factors may contribute to its clinical expression.

In a Chinese family with 18 patients with LDYT, Wang et al. (2009) sequenced the entire mitochondrial genome using a resequencing microarray. They identified a 10197G-A mutation in the MTND3 gene that causes an ala47-to-thr (A47T) substitution at a highly conserved residue, on a haplogroup D4b background. Quantitative analysis showed that the mutation was homoplasmic in the leukocytes of all affected individuals on the maternal side. The authors noted that the same mutation had been described in French and Korean patients with Leigh syndrome.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wang et al. (2009) noted that the 10197G-A mutation in the MTND3 gene had also been reported in patients with Leigh syndrome. They suggested that the clinical presentations associated with this mutation include both LDYT and Leigh syndrome. Similarly, Spruijt et al. (2007) noted that the 3697G-A mutation in the MTND1 gene can cause either MELAS syndrome or the LHON and spastic dystonia phenotype.
