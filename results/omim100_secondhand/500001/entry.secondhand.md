### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is caused by homoplasmic or heteroplasmic mutation in one of several mitochondrial genes encoding subunits of complex I: MTND6, MTND4, MTND1, or MTND3. ⚠︎

### Description

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and dystonia (LDYT) is a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder. Affected people have different combinations of vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia (Wang et al., 2009). The dystonia is linked to bilateral, symmetric lesions of the basal ganglia, especially the putamen (Marsden et al., 1986; Jun et al., 1994). Within one family, some members have only optic neuropathy, some have only dystonia, and some have both (Novotny et al., 1986; De Vries et al., 1996).

### Nomenclature

The alternative designations 'familial dystonia with visual failure and striatal lucencies' and 'Marsden syndrome' come from the report of Marsden et al. (1986), titled 'Familial dystonia and visual failure with striatal CT lucencies.' ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Miyoshi et al. (1969) reported familial holotopistic striatal necrosis.

Marsden et al. (1986) described a disorder in 7 members of 2 families. In these patients, dystonia was associated to a variable degree with subacute visual loss or asymptomatic optic atrophy. CT scans showed striking bilateral symmetric lucencies, especially in the putamen. The authors considered that this might be a variant of Leigh disease, but noted considerable differences between these patients and those with pathologically proven Leigh disease. They stated that this condition must be excluded in all patients thought to have idiopathic dystonia, subacute visual failure similar to Leber optic neuropathy, or a combination of the two.

Novotny et al. (1986) studied a kindred in which 8 members had the neuroretinopathy of Leber disease and 14 had progressive generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration. One member had both disorders. The authors suggested that the condition might be a mitochondrial disease.

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family in which members had maternally transmitted LHON and/or early-onset dystonia with bilateral basal ganglia lesions.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family in which LHON was associated with hereditary spastic dystonia. Some family members had both neuropathy and ophthalmologic lesions, whereas others had only one type of abnormality.

Watanabe et al. (2006) reported a Japanese family in which generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration began in childhood and optic neuropathy began late.

Spruijt et al. (2007) reported a family in which some members had LHON and others had spastic dystonia. The clinical features resembled those of previously described families with LHON and dystonia caused by an MTND6 mutation. ⚠︎

Wang et al. (2009) described a Chinese family with 18 patients with LDYT.

### Biochemical Features

De Vries et al. (1996) found a severe complex I deficiency in a muscle biopsy from a member of a Dutch family with LHON and spastic dystonia.

Spruijt et al. (2007) found that the MTND1 3697G-A mutation appeared to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency.

### Inheritance

Novotny et al. (1986) found that the mode of inheritance in their kindred was compatible with maternal transmission. Jun et al. (1994) confirmed maternal transmission in a 5-generation Hispanic family carrying a mitochondrial DNA mutation.

### Molecular Genetics

Jun et al. (1994) studied a severely affected child from a 5-generation Hispanic family with LHON and/or dystonia. They amplified the child's buffy coat mtDNA by PCR and sequenced more than 90% of it. The mtDNA had a Native American haplogroup D genotype and differed from the Cambridge reference sequence at 40 nucleotide positions. One variant was a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 14459 of the MTND6 gene, which changed a moderately conserved alanine to valine at residue 72 (A72V). The variant was absent from 38 Native American haplogroup D mtDNAs and from 108 Asian, 103 Caucasian, and 99 African mtDNAs. Six maternal relatives across 3 generations carried the mutation. One female relative with LHON was heteroplasmic. The authors concluded that this mutation was likely the cause of the disease.

In a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia, De Vries et al. (1996) found none of the mtDNA mutations previously reported in LHON. They sequenced the protein-coding mitochondrial genes and found 2 previously unreported mutations. The first was a heteroplasmic A-to-G transition at nucleotide 11696 in the MTND4 gene, resulting in a substitution between valine and isoleucine at amino acid 312. The second was a homoplasmic T-to-A change at nucleotide 14596 in the MTND6 gene, which substituted methionine for isoleucine at residue 26. A muscle biopsy showed severe complex I deficiency, which supported a link between these mtDNA mutations and the phenotype.

Watanabe et al. (2006) sequenced the entire mtDNA of the proband from a Japanese family with LDYT. Compared with the Revised Cambridge Reference Sequence, it had 42 nucleotide changes. Two of these, 3203A-G and the MTND6 14459G-A mutation, were absent from 200 healthy Japanese controls. The significance of the 3203A-G change was unknown.

Spruijt et al. (2007) screened the complete mtDNA of a patient with LHON who did not carry any of the 3 primary LHON mutations. Other members of the patient's family had spastic dystonia. They found a pathogenic 3697G-A mutation in the MTND1 gene and determined its heteroplasmy level and biochemical effect; it appeared to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency. The authors noted that this mutation can also cause MELAS syndrome and suggested that other genetic factors may contribute to its clinical expression.

Wang et al. (2009) studied a Chinese family with 18 patients with LDYT. They sequenced the entire mitochondrial genome with a resequencing microarray and found a 10197G-A mutation in the MTND3 gene on a haplogroup D4b background. The mutation substituted threonine for a highly conserved alanine at codon 47 (A47T). Quantitative analysis showed the mutation was homoplasmic in leukocytes of all affected individuals on the maternal side.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wang et al. (2009) noted that the MTND3 10197G-A mutation had also been described in French and Korean patients with Leigh syndrome. They suggested that the clinical spectrum of this mutation is wide and includes both LDYT and Leigh syndrome. Spruijt et al. (2007) likewise noted that the MTND1 3697G-A mutation, which causes LHON and spastic dystonia, can also cause MELAS syndrome.
