### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Knobloch syndrome-2 (KNO2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PAK2 gene.

### Description

Knobloch syndrome-2 (KNO2) is a disorder characterized by high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration with retinal detachment, and occipital encephalocele or scalp defect. Affected individuals have also shown intellectual disability, autistic behavior, and pulmonary abnormalities (Wilson et al., 1998; Antonarakis et al., 2021). Unlike the autosomal recessive form of Knobloch syndrome caused by COL18A1 mutation, KNO2 is not linked to COL18A1 (Menzel et al., 2004) and is caused by a dominant monoallelic variant in PAK2 (Antonarakis et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Menzel et al. (2004) excluded linkage to the COL18A1 locus in a family from New Zealand with Knobloch syndrome and named the second, unmapped Knobloch syndrome locus KNO2.

### Clinical Features

Wilson et al. (1998) reported 2 sibs from New Zealand with high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration, and occipital encephalocele or a scalp lesion. In one sib the eye disease was severe and began very early. In the other sib the scalp defect was very mild. Both sibs appeared to have an unusual pulmonary lymphatic condition, and Wilson et al. (1998) suggested that mesoderm might be involved in the morphogenesis of the disorder. ⚠︎

Antonarakis et al. (2021) restudied the family reported by Wilson et al. (1998) and described the phenotype as Knobloch syndrome type 2 with intellectual disability, autistic behavior, retinal degeneration, and encephalocele. The 2 sibs had varying combinations of severe developmental delay, incomprehensible speech, and autism spectrum disorder. ✗

### Inheritance

Antonarakis et al. (2021) found that KNO2 in the family they studied followed a dominant pattern. The heterozygous PAK2 mutation was apparently de novo in both affected sibs, which suggested germline mosaicism in one of the parents.

### Mapping

Menzel et al. (2004) excluded linkage to the COL18A1 locus on chromosome 21q22.3 in a New Zealand family with Knobloch syndrome. This provided evidence for a second Knobloch syndrome locus, which they called KNO2. Mutation analysis also excluded COL15A1 as the gene responsible for KNO2.

### Heterogeneity

Menzel et al. (2004) confirmed that COL18A1 is involved in Knobloch syndrome by finding COL18A1 mutations in a Hungarian family. They showed genetic heterogeneity by excluding linkage to COL18A1 in a New Zealand family.

### Molecular Genetics

Antonarakis et al. (2021) performed whole-exome sequencing on the 2 affected sibs originally reported by Wilson et al. (1998) and on their unaffected parents. They found a heterozygous glu435-to-lys (E435K) substitution in the PAK2 gene in both sibs. The variant appeared to be de novo in both sibs, suggesting germline mosaicism in one parent, but deep sequencing of parental blood DNA did not detect mosaicism. The variant lies in the kinase domain of PAK2, and structural analysis predicted that it could compromise kinase activity. In transfected HEK293T cells, the E435K variant caused partial loss of kinase activity. Antonarakis et al. (2021) concluded that KNO2 in this family was dominant and caused by the PAK2 variant.
