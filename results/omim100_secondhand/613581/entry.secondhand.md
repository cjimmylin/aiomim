### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-56 (RP56) is caused by homozygous mutation in the IMPG2 gene on chromosome 3.

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-56 is an autosomal recessive retinal disease caused by progressive degeneration of the photoreceptor cells. Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) found that most patients had an early-onset form of RP with progressive loss of visual field and worsening visual acuity.

### Clinical Features

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) studied 2 families that each had 3 affected sibs with retinitis pigmentosa. One family was of Iraqi Jewish ancestry and the other was Dutch. They also studied 10 more index cases of Dutch, Israeli, Italian, and Pakistani origin. Most patients with IMPG2 mutations had early-onset RP with progressive visual-field loss and deterioration of visual acuity. One patient, who carried a missense mutation, was instead diagnosed with maculopathy.

### Inheritance

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) found homozygous IMPG2 mutations that cosegregated with disease in families with affected sibs, which established autosomal recessive inheritance of RP56.

### Mapping

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) used autozygosity mapping to identify 2 families, each with 3 affected sibs. The affected sibs shared large, overlapping homozygous regions on chromosome 3 that contained the IMPG2 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) sequenced IMPG2 in the index cases of 2 families with autosomal recessive RP. They found homozygous mutations that cosegregated with the disease in each family. The 3 affected sibs of Iraqi Jewish ancestry carried a nonsense mutation. The Dutch family carried a 1.8-kb genomic deletion that removes exon 9, which results in the loss of 7 amino acids from a conserved SEA domain of the IMPG2 protein. In transiently transfected COS-1 cells, a construct expressing the wildtype SEA domain was properly targeted to the plasma membrane. The mutant lacking the 7 amino acids appeared to be retained in the endoplasmic reticulum. The authors then analyzed IMPG2 in 10 additional index cases of Dutch, Israeli, Italian, and Pakistani origin who had homozygous regions encompassing the gene. They found 5 more mutations: 4 nonsense mutations and 1 missense mutation that affected a highly conserved phenylalanine residue. The patient with the missense mutation had maculopathy.
