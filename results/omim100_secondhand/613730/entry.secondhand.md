### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Affected individuals have congenital cataracts and severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain with subependymal calcification (Mochida et al., 2010). Akawi et al. (2013) described the neonatal course as catastrophic, and most affected children died in the first weeks of life.

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) studied 8 affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. All affected individuals had hemorrhagic destruction of the brain parenchyma, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. Mochida et al. (2010) noted that the clinical picture overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with patients who have mutations in occludin, another tight-junction component. However, the massive intracranial hemorrhage set these patients apart. In a later summary of this family, Akawi et al. (2013) stated that 3 of the 8 affected individuals died early in infancy. They also stated that brain imaging showed multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage with liquefaction and massive cystic degeneration, resulting in large ventricles. ✗

Akawi et al. (2013) reported affected children from 3 families of different ethnic origin. In family 1, the parents were Turkish second cousins. Their first child was a preterm girl who had intracranial bleeding and died in the neonatal period. The second child was a girl born at term with a birth weight of 2480 gm (less than 10th centile). On day 5 she was admitted with tonic seizures that did not respond to treatment. Examination showed a head circumference of 30 cm, a sloping forehead, a prominent, bulging anterior fontanel, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Brain CT showed calcification of the subependymal region and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Tests for toxoplasmosis, rubella, and cytomegalovirus were negative, and the platelet count was normal. She developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus and died at 2 months of age.

In family 2 of Akawi et al. (2013), the parents were nonconsanguineous and of Afghani origin. The first affected boy had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth. At 2 weeks of age he developed vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. Brain MRI at 15 days showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities, and multiple subependymal cysts. Renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys. He died at 2 weeks of age, and extensive metabolic, mitochondrial, cytogenetic, and coagulation studies were normal. His affected brother had bilateral cataracts at birth. Cranial ultrasound at first showed periventricular echogenicity without bleeding. On day 5 his hemoglobin fell from 180 to 96 g/L, and repeat ultrasound showed extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage. EEG had shown bilateral epileptiform activity, and abdominal ultrasound showed right pelvicalyceal dilatation. He died on the fifth day of life.

In family 3 of Akawi et al. (2013), the parents were Moroccan first cousins. Earlier, a male child had died at 10 days of age with cerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus, and a stillborn child had hydrocephalus with necrotic degeneration of the cerebral tissue. The index patient was a girl with central and nuclear bilateral cataracts at birth. On the second day of life she had irritability and a bulging anterior fontanel. Head CT showed multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, and diffusely hypodense brain parenchyma. MRI showed intraventricular and intraparenchymal hemorrhage, edema, cystic changes, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. The circulation in the circle of Willis was normal. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus, but she had no clinical seizures. She also had severe anemia. Metabolic screening and platelet levels were normal. By day 18 her head circumference was 41 cm (+4 SD), and she died at 39 days of age.

Akawi et al. (2013) found that the phenotype in their patients was very similar to that in the original family. All of their patients had bilateral cataracts and intraparenchymal hemorrhage with brain destruction and calcification. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement varied more between patients. The authors reported that the disorder was lethal in 7 of 10 cases, with death in the first few weeks of life. The 3 survivors had severe mental retardation, severe spasticity, and seizures.

### Inheritance

In a large consanguineous family, Mochida et al. (2010) found that the disorder was transmitted in an autosomal recessive pattern. Akawi et al. (2013) supported this finding: in 3 further families, the parents were heterozygous carriers of the mutations.

### Mapping

Mochida et al. (2010) performed homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family and mapped the disease locus to chromosome 11q25, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochida et al. (2010) studied affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. They sequenced the 9 protein-coding genes in the candidate interval on 11q25 and found a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene (c.747+1G-T). The change was predicted to abolish the normal splice donor site. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblastoid cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which led to a frameshift with early termination. ⚠︎

Akawi et al. (2013) sequenced the coding exons of JAM3 in affected children from 3 families of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin. They found 3 different homozygous mutations: C219Y, E116K, and M1R. The mutations segregated with the disease. Parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs available for testing were either homozygous for the wildtype allele or heterozygous. None of the mutations was present in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. In transfected HeLa cells, the C219Y mutant protein was mostly retained in the endoplasmic reticulum. In contrast, the E116K mutant reached the plasma membrane normally, like wildtype JAM3.

### Diagnosis

Akawi et al. (2013) discussed the differential diagnosis. The syndrome of band-like intracranial calcification with simplified gyration and polymicrogyria, caused by OCLN mutations, lacks intracranial hemorrhage and congenital cataract, and their patients had no polymicrogyria. Disorders caused by COL4A1 mutations can include antenatal intracranial hemorrhage and porencephaly, but they are inherited as autosomal dominant traits and present differently. Proliferative vasculopathy and hydranencephaly-hydrocephaly (Fowler syndrome), caused by FLVCR2 mutations, presents with fetal akinesia deformation sequence, which differs from HDBSCC.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, IVS5DS, G-T, +1 [c.747+1G>T; c.612+1G>T; p.V250LfsX26; p.V205Lfs]

Mochida et al. (2010) studied affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts. In these patients they found a homozygous G-to-T transversion at the first base of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene (c.747+1G-T). RT-PCR of patient lymphoblastoid cells confirmed abnormal splicing, causing a frameshift with early termination. Akawi et al. (2013) described this mutation as c.612+1G-T (p.V205Lfs) using reference sequence NM_032801.4. ⚠︎

**.0002 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, MET1ARG [c.2T>G; p.M1R]

In a girl with hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.2T-G transversion in the JAM3 gene, resulting in a met1-to-arg (M1R) substitution at the initiator codon. The patient was the daughter of Moroccan first cousins (family 3). The mutation segregated with the disease in the family and was not found in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. It was predicted to impair protein synthesis.

**.0003 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, GLU116LYS [c.346G>A; p.E116K]

In 2 affected brothers with hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.346G-A transition in exon 4 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution in the Ig-like V-type domain. The brothers were born to nonconsanguineous parents of Afghani origin (family 2). The mutation segregated with the disease and was not found in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. In transfected HeLa cells, the mutant protein reached the plasma membrane normally. The authors suggested that the mutation may interfere with dimerization or with binding to interacting partners.

**.0004 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, CYS219TYR [c.656G>A; p.C219Y]

In a girl with hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.656G-A transition in exon 6 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution in the Ig-like C2-type domain. The patient was born to Turkish parents who were second cousins (family 1). The mutation segregated with the disease and was not found in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. It was predicted to disrupt a disulfide bond. In transfected HeLa and COS-7 cells, the mutant protein was mostly retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and did not reach the plasma membrane, indicating a trafficking defect.
