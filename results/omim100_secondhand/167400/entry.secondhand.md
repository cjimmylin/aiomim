### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN9A gene, which encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.7.

### Description

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEPD), previously known as familial rectal pain, is an inherited condition with paroxysms of rectal, ocular, or submandibular pain with flushing (Fertleman et al., 2006). Onset is in the neonatal period or infancy and the disorder persists throughout life. Autonomic manifestations, including skin flushing, harlequin color change, tonic attacks, and syncope with bradycardia, predominate at first. Later, attacks of excruciating deep burning pain occur, triggered by factors such as defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion (Fertleman et al., 2007).

### Nomenclature

Hayden and Grossman (1959) described the disorder as rectal, ocular, and submaxillary pain, a familial autonomic disorder related to proctalgia fugax. Dugan (1972) used the name familial rectal pain, and Mann and Cree (1972) also published on familial rectal pain. Fertleman and Ferrie (2006) proposed that familial rectal pain syndrome be renamed paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.

### Clinical Features

Hayden and Grossman (1959) reported a family with a familial disorder of very brief episodes of excruciating rectal pain with flushing of the buttocks and legs, ocular pain with flushing of the eyelids and periorbital skin, and submaxillary pain. †

Fertleman et al. (2007) described the clinical phenotype in 77 individuals from 15 families identified by an international consortium; 14 affected individuals completed a validated pain questionnaire. The disorder began in the neonatal period or infancy and persisted throughout life. At first, autonomic manifestations predominated: all had skin flushing, and most had harlequin color change and tonic attacks. Dramatic syncopes with bradycardia and sometimes asystole were common. Later, the disorder was characterized by attacks of excruciating deep burning pain, often in the rectal, ocular, or jaw areas but also diffuse. Attacks were triggered by factors such as defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion.

Meglič et al. (2014) reported a 3-year-old girl who had had extremely painful voiding since birth. Her father, who carried the same SCN9A mutation, had occasionally had jaw pain while yawning since childhood.

### Inheritance

Fertleman et al. (2007) stated that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder is an autosomal dominant condition. In the family reported by Meglič et al. (2014), the girl inherited a heterozygous SCN9A mutation from her father, who had milder symptoms.

### Mapping

Fertleman et al. (2006) carried out a genome-wide linkage search in families with PEPD and then analyzed the candidate gene SCN9A for mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

In 11 families and 2 sporadic cases with PEPD, Fertleman et al. (2006) identified 8 different missense mutations in the SCN9A gene. Functional analysis in vitro of 3 of the mutant Nav1.7 channels showed reduced fast inactivation, leading to persistent sodium current. By contrast, other SCN9A mutations that cause primary erythermalgia are associated with more negative activation thresholds. Carbamazepine, which is effective in PEPD but not in primary erythermalgia, selectively blocked the persistent current of PEPD mutants but did not affect the negative activation threshold of a primary erythermalgia mutant. Fertleman et al. (2006) concluded that PEPD and primary erythermalgia are allelic disorders with distinct underlying biophysical mechanisms.

In a 3-year-old girl with PEPD who had painful micturition, Meglič et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.554G-A mutation in the SCN9A gene, resulting in an arg185-to-his (R185H) substitution. She inherited the mutation from her father, who had occasional jaw pain. The authors noted that the same mutation had been found in patients with small fiber sensory neuropathy. They suggested that intra- and interfamilial phenotypic variability associated with gain-of-function Nav1.7 variants might explain this.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fertleman et al. (2006) found that the PEPD mutations they studied reduced fast inactivation of Nav1.7, whereas the SCN9A mutations that cause primary erythermalgia shift activation thresholds to more negative potentials. They concluded that the 2 disorders are allelic variants with distinct channel defects and phenotypes.

### Diagnosis

Fertleman et al. (2007) noted that PEPD is most likely to be misdiagnosed as epilepsy and, particularly in infancy, as hyperekplexia or reflex anoxic seizures.

### Clinical Management

Fertleman et al. (2007) found that carbamazepine was effective in almost all patients who tried it, although the response was often incomplete. Fertleman et al. (2006) showed that in vitro, carbamazepine selectively blocked the persistent current associated with PEPD mutant channels. In the girl reported by Meglič et al. (2014), carbamazepine significantly reduced the pain.
