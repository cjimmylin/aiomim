### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN9A gene, which encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel Na(v)1.7.

### Description

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEPD) is an inherited condition characterized by paroxysms of rectal, ocular, or submandibular pain with flushing (Fertleman et al., 2006). Onset is in the neonatal period or infancy and the disorder persists throughout life. Autonomic manifestations, including skin flushing, harlequin color change, tonic attacks, and syncope with bradycardia, predominate early, and attacks of excruciating deep burning pain occur later (Fertleman et al., 2007). The disorder is a sodium channelopathy and is allelic to primary erythermalgia, which has a different biophysical mechanism (Fertleman et al., 2006). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

The disorder was previously known as familial rectal pain (FRP). Fertleman and Ferrie (2006) and Fertleman et al. (2006) proposed the name paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEPD). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hayden and Grossman (1959) reported a family with rectal, ocular, and submaxillary pain. They regarded the condition as a familial autonomic disorder related to proctalgia fugax.

Dugan (1972) and Mann and Cree (1972) published reports on familial rectal pain.

Fertleman et al. (2007) described the clinical phenotype in 77 individuals from 15 families identified by an international consortium. Fourteen affected individuals completed a validated pain questionnaire. Onset was in the neonatal period or infancy, and the disorder persisted throughout life. Autonomic manifestations predominated at first: all patients had skin flushing, and most had harlequin color change and tonic attacks. Dramatic syncopes with bradycardia, sometimes with asystole, were common. Later, the disorder was characterized by attacks of excruciating deep burning pain, often in the rectal, ocular, or jaw areas, but sometimes diffuse. Triggers included defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion. Fertleman et al. (2007) noted that the disorder is most likely to be misdiagnosed as epilepsy and, particularly in infancy, as hyperekplexia or reflex anoxic seizures.

Meglič et al. (2014) described a 3-year-old girl with an unusual presentation of PEPD: extremely painful voiding had been present since birth. Her father, who carried the same SCN9A mutation, had occasional jaw pain when yawning since childhood.

### Inheritance

Fertleman et al. (2007) described paroxysmal extreme pain disorder as autosomal dominant. In the family reported by Meglič et al. (2014), a heterozygous SCN9A mutation was inherited from the father.

### Mapping

Fertleman et al. (2006) performed a genome-wide linkage search in families with PEPD and then carried out mutation analysis of SCN9A as a candidate gene.

### Molecular Genetics

After a genome-wide linkage search, Fertleman et al. (2006) analyzed the candidate gene SCN9A and identified 8 missense mutations in 11 families and 2 sporadic cases with PEPD. In vitro functional analysis of 3 of the mutant Na(v)1.7 channels showed reduced fast inactivation, which led to a persistent sodium current. Other SCN9A mutations, which shift the activation threshold to more negative values, cause primary erythermalgia. Carbamazepine, which is effective in PEPD but not in primary erythermalgia, selectively blocked the persistent current of the PEPD mutants. It did not affect the negative activation threshold of a primary erythermalgia mutant. Fertleman et al. (2006) concluded that PEPD and primary erythermalgia are allelic disorders with distinct biophysical mechanisms.

In a 3-year-old girl with PEPD who had had painful micturition since birth, Meglič et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.554G-A mutation in the SCN9A gene, resulting in an arg185-to-his (R185H) substitution. The mutation was inherited from her father, who had mild symptoms. The authors noted that the same mutation had been found in patients with small fiber sensory neuropathy. They suggested that the intra- and interfamilial phenotypic variability reported for gain-of-function Na(v)1.7 variants could explain this discrepancy.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fertleman et al. (2006) found that PEPD mutations in SCN9A reduced fast inactivation and caused a persistent sodium current, whereas SCN9A mutations causing primary erythermalgia shifted activation thresholds to more negative values. This difference matched the different responses of the 2 disorders to carbamazepine. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Fertleman et al. (2007) found carbamazepine effective in almost all patients who tried it, although the response was often incomplete. Fertleman et al. (2006) showed that carbamazepine selectively blocked the persistent current produced by PEPD mutant channels. In the child reported by Meglič et al. (2014), carbamazepine substantially reduced the pain.
