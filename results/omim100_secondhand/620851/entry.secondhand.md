### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene, which encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA of the major spliceosome, on chromosome 12.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder. Its main features are moderate to severe global developmental delay, intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, hypotonia, microcephaly, short stature, seizures, abnormalities on brain MRI, and distinctive facial features. Many organ systems can be involved, including the visual, gastrointestinal, and skeletal systems (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). The disorder is caused by heterozygous variants, usually de novo, in a short, highly constrained central region of the noncoding RNU4-2 gene. Most patients carry the same recurrent single-base insertion, n.64_65insT (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) studied participants in the UK 100,000 Genomes Project who had rare variants in 2 regions of RNU4-2. They analyzed the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms for 46 cases annotated with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Of these 46 cases, 91% had intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay. Compared with other cases with neurodevelopmental abnormality, several terms were significantly overrepresented. These were microcephaly in 57% (versus 18%), drooling in 13% (versus 1%), proportionate short stature in 28% (versus 7%), generalized hypotonia in 39% (versus 13%), seizures in 52% (versus 27%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion in 13% (versus 2%). No terms were significantly underrepresented. The 34 cases with intellectual disability that carried 1 of the 3 most strongly associated variants were more phenotypically homogeneous than expected by chance.

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures, whom they followed for 5 years. Photographs showed facial features that the authors considered recognizable for this disorder.

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed phenotypic information on 49 individuals (21 females and 28 males) with RNU4-2 variants. Their median age at last evaluation was 10 years (range, 2 to 38 years). All had global developmental delay, which was severe in 34 and moderate in 10, and all 45 assessed had intellectual disability. Of the individuals older than 5 years, 83% were ambulatory, but walking was delayed (average age 3.4 years, range 17 months to 7.5 years), and some had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Only 3 individuals had fluent speech; 35 were nonverbal and 10 had a few words. Other features were short stature (76%), microcephaly (77%, mostly congenital), hypotonia (87%), seizures (77%), behavioral issues (67%), autism spectrum disorder (48%), sleep issues (47%), and intrauterine growth restriction (18%).

In the cohort of Chen et al. (2024), seizure onset varied, with a median of 3 years and a range from the first year of life to 10 years. Seizure types included infantile spasms, focal seizures, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. Brain MRI was abnormal in 91% of individuals. The most frequent findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, delayed myelination, and other nonspecific white matter abnormalities.

Chen et al. (2024) also reported involvement of several organ systems in all individuals. Visual problems occurred in 79%, including optic nerve hypoplasia, cortical blindness, strabismus (51%), and nystagmus (45%). Gastrointestinal problems included constipation (66%), gastroesophageal reflux (49%), feeding difficulties (76%), gastrostomy tube placement (32%), and poor growth. Bone and/or skeletal abnormalities occurred in 64% and included osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, and hip dysplasia. Less often, individuals had hearing loss (22%), endocrine abnormalities (44%; hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, pituitary abnormalities), limb abnormalities, and genitourinary, dental, cardiac, or cutaneous problems.

In the same cohort, 45 of 48 individuals had dysmorphic facial features (Chen et al., 2024). These varied, but included a myopathic face, deep-set eyes (some widely spaced and some narrowly spaced), epicanthus, a wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, large cupped ears (some posteriorly rotated), full cheeks, and a distinctive mouth with full lips and downturned corners. Patients also had a high-arched palate and a large or protruding tongue. Presentation did not differ significantly between males and females.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous RNU4-2 mutations that cause ReNU syndrome are mostly de novo, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Greene et al. (2024) identified 1 case who had inherited the n.76C-T variant from an affected mother, and 1 parent who was mosaic for the n.65A-G variant. Chen et al. (2024) phased the variant in 54 individuals, and in all 54 it was on the maternal allele. This contrasts with the paternal bias usually seen for de novo small mutations. In the cohort of Chen et al. (2024), 1 individual had a maternally inherited n.76C-T variant, 1 mother appeared to be mosaic for n.65A-G, and the n.77_78insT variant was found in 2 affected sibs.

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) used whole-genome sequencing data from the UK 100,000 Genomes Project to compare the burden of rare variants in 41,132 noncoding genes between 5,529 unrelated cases with intellectual disability and 46,401 unrelated controls. RNU4-2 showed an extremely strong dominant association (posterior probability of association approximately 1), much stronger than any other noncoding gene. Three variants, found in 34 cases with intellectual disability, had a posterior probability of pathogenicity greater than 0.5. None of these 34 cases had previously been explained by a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant. Five more nearby variants were found only in affected participants. Together, the variants defined 2 contiguous regions, n.62-70 and n.73-79, that lay within a locus markedly depleted of variation in gnomAD. One region maps to a quasi-pseudoknot interaction between U4 and U6 snRNAs, and the other maps to stem III. Of 47 cases with rare alleles in these regions, 37 variants were de novo and 10 had unknown inheritance. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, occurred de novo in 33 families. The authors replicated the finding in 3 further collections (the NIHR BioResource, the 100,000 Genomes Project Pilot, and the UK Genomic Medicine Service), bringing the total to 73 unrelated cases. In the replication cohorts, 19 of 26 affected probands had de novo variants, 6 had variants of unknown inheritance, and 1 had inherited n.76C-T from an affected mother.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 164 trios with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. They identified the recurrent de novo RNU4-2 n.64_65insT variant in a 5-year-old girl. Extensive previous genetic investigations had not found another cause for her phenotype.

Chen et al. (2024) used genome sequencing of 8,841 probands with genetically undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders in the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project, together with additional cohorts. They identified heterozygous variants in an 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 (n.62-79) in 115 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. This region was depleted of variation in population datasets. Most of the 115 individuals (89; 77.4%) carried the recurrent single-base insertion n.64_65insT. Five of the 11 other variants were also single-base insertions, including n.77_78insT in 6 individuals, and 15 individuals had SNVs. In 86 of the 115 individuals, sequencing of both parents confirmed that the variant had arisen de novo. In all 54 individuals in whom it could be determined, the variant was on the maternal allele. Single-base insertions in this region were strongly enriched in undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorder probands compared with UK Biobank participants (odds ratio = 1,531). SNVs were only modestly enriched. SNVs in the central part of the region, particularly at nucleotides 71 to 74, were also seen in non-NDD individuals and population controls. RNA sequencing of blood from 5 affected individuals showed a systematic increase in the use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites compared with controls.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) observed n.76C-T as a de novo mutation in 4 families and as an inherited variant from an affected mother in 1 family. It was the only variant in their pedigrees that was present in gnomAD (allele count of 1 in 152,108). They suggested that it may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases than other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. They also observed n.76C-G in 1 participant without neurodevelopmental abnormality, suggesting that some mutations at position 76 may have a limited or benign effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that children with SNVs had fewer reports of severe global developmental delay than those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 versus 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV, and individuals with SNVs had fewer organ systems reported as involved. The authors noted that this suggestion of a milder phenotype with SNVs was based on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) noted that the variants lie in the central region of the U4/U6 snRNA duplex. They predicted that the variants disrupt Watson-Crick interactions within U4 stem III (n.76C-T and n.77_78insT) or disrupt the configuration of the U4 quasi-pseudoknot (for example, n.64_65insT). Either effect would destabilize the interactions of U4 snRNA with U6 snRNA and with RNA-binding proteins. Because n.64_65insT could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the quasi-pseudoknot, the authors proposed that delayed U4-U6 unwinding might be the underlying mechanism. They compared this with the phenotypically similar disorder caused by WBP4 deficiency.

Chen et al. (2024) showed that the critical region maps to the T-loop (quasi-pseudoknot) and stem III of U4. These structural elements are thought to stabilize U4/U6 pairing and position the U6 ACAGAGA sequence to receive the 5-prime splice site, and the region is loaded into the BRR2 helicase. RNA sequencing of blood from 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants showed more abnormal splicing events at unannotated 5-prime splice sites than in controls (mean 8.8 versus 0.7). The splice sites with increased use had more T at position +3 and more C at positions +4 and +5. These positions pair directly with the U6 ACAGAGA region. Twelve abnormal splicing events were shared by 2 or more affected individuals, and none of these were seen in 378 controls. Chen et al. (2024) also found that RNU4-2 is expressed at consistently higher levels than its homolog RNU4-1 in the developing human prefrontal cortex and has much greater chromatin accessibility there. They concluded that RNU4-2 is the main U4 transcript in the brain.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 is not captured by commercial clinical exome sequencing. In the Deciphering Developmental Disorders exome data, 22 individuals who carried n.64_65insT by genome sequencing had either no reads at the RNU4-2 locus or a single reference read. The authors concluded that the sensitivity of exome sequencing for these variants is very low. Schot et al. (2024) showed that reanalysis of existing whole-genome sequencing data can identify RNU4-2 variants in patients who had previously received a negative result.

### Population Genetics

In the 100,000 Genomes Project, Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 cases in 46 of 9,112 (0.50%) cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. This made RNU4-2 the most prevalent cause of neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 variants were found in 21 of 5,527 (0.38%) such cases, making it the second most prevalent cause after MECP2 (24 cases).

Chen et al. (2024) found that the n.64_65insT variant was absent from 76,215 genome-sequenced individuals in gnomAD v4.0 and from 245,400 individuals in the All of Us dataset. It was present in 1 individual in the UK Biobank. The authors estimated that variants in the critical region of RNU4-2 explain about 0.4% of all individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. The rate was much lower in cohorts recruited mainly for autism spectrum disorder (3 of 7,149; 0.042%).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 64T [n.64_65insT; NR_003137.2(RNU4-2):n.64_65insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291839:T:TA]

Greene et al. (2024) found an n.64_65insT variant in the RNU4-2 gene as a de novo mutation in 33 different families in the 100,000 Genomes Project with intellectual disability or neurodevelopmental abnormality. It was the most common variant they identified. They suggested that the insertion could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the U4/U6 quasi-pseudoknot.

Schot et al. (2024) identified a de novo heterozygous n.64_65insT variant in RNU4-2 by reanalysis of whole-genome sequencing trio data. The patient was a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) found the heterozygous n.64_65insT variant in 89 of 115 individuals (77.4%) with neurodevelopmental disorders who had variants in the critical region of RNU4-2. The variant arose de novo in 38 Genomics England probands sequenced with their unaffected parents, and inheritance was consistent with de novo occurrence in a further 8. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in 8 individuals, and the variant was confirmed de novo by both short- and long-read trio sequencing in 3 families. In all 46 individuals in whom the parental origin could be resolved, the variant was on the maternal allele. The variant was absent from gnomAD v4.0 and All of Us, and was seen in 1 UK Biobank individual. RNA sequencing of blood from affected individuals showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.

**.0002 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 76C-T [n.76C>T]

Greene et al. (2024) found an n.76C-T variant in the RNU4-2 gene as a de novo mutation in 4 families. In a fifth family, the proband had inherited it from an affected mother. The variant lies in U4 stem III and was predicted to disrupt Watson-Crick pairing. It was present once in 152,108 alleles in gnomAD, and the authors suggested that it may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases.

Chen et al. (2024) found a maternally inherited n.76C-T SNV in RNU4-2 in 1 individual with a neurodevelopmental disorder.

**.0003 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 77T [n.77_78insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291826:T:TA]

Greene et al. (2024) identified an n.77_78insT variant in the RNU4-2 gene in affected individuals. They predicted that it disrupts Watson-Crick interactions within U4 stem III.

Chen et al. (2024) found the n.77_78insT variant in RNU4-2 in 6 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders, 2 of whom were affected sibs. In 1 individual with this variant, RNA sequencing of blood contributed to the finding of increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.
