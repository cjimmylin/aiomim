### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MEHMO syndrome (MEHMO) is caused by hemizygous mutation in the EIF2S3 gene on chromosome Xp22.11.

### Description

MEHMO syndrome is a rare X-linked intellectual disability syndrome. Its clinical hallmarks are mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity (Steinmuller et al., 1998; Skopkova et al., 2017). Life expectancy of the patients in the original family was less than 2 years (Steinmuller et al., 1998). Severity varies: patients with a C-terminal frameshift mutation in EIF2S3 had the full syndrome, whereas patients with missense mutations had milder disease with only some of the features (Skopkova et al., 2017; Gregory et al., 2019).

### Nomenclature

Steinmuller et al. (1998) named the condition MEHMO, an acronym for its major clinical features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity.

Skopkova et al. (2017) pointed out that the family with EIF2S3 mutation and X-linked intellectual disability reported by Borck et al. (2012), and the 2 families reported by Moortgat et al. (2016), had features that clearly overlapped with MEHMO syndrome. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) described 2 brothers with a severe syndrome of postnatal growth retardation and mental retardation. Features included extreme microcephaly, obesity beginning in infancy, microgonadism, and a characteristic amphora-shaped face. Neurologic examination was very abnormal, with hypertonia, hyperreflexia, nystagmus, and extremely irritable and agitated behavior. The first child died at 4.5 years of age; he also had neonatal hypoglycemia and chronic constipation. The authors noted that the patients most closely resembled individuals with Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome. However, the brothers had much more severe dwarfism and mental retardation than any reported patient with that syndrome, and their neurologic and dysmorphic features differed significantly. The authors considered that the disorder might be a new recessive condition, either autosomal or X-linked.

Steinmuller et al. (1998) described a large 3-generation family with a previously unrecognized X-chromosomal mental retardation syndrome. Its clinical hallmarks were mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Life expectancy of affected patients was less than 2 years.

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) described a child with MEHMO and lactic acidosis. Muscle biopsy showed markedly reduced activities of complexes I, III, and IV of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Histologic staining showed mitochondrial proliferation and lipid storage. On electron microscopy, mitochondria were abnormal and enlarged, with concentric cristae and electron-dense bodies. The authors proposed that MEHMO was an X-linked mitochondrial disorder. Skopkova et al. (2017) later reported that this patient carried a deleterious tyr430-to-ter (Y430X) mutation in the SERAC1 gene (see HETEROGENEITY).

Borck et al. (2012) studied a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry in the maternal line in which 3 males had an intellectual disability syndrome with apparent X-linked inheritance. The maternal grandparents were first cousins. Clinical features included moderate to severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and dysmorphic facial features. Some features were seen in only 1 patient each: cleft lip and palate and behavioral impairment in 1, generalized seizures in another, and postpubertal microgenitalism and obesity in the third. Brain MRI showed a thin corpus callosum and enlarged lateral ventricles. Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that 1 of these patients lived into adulthood, that the patients walked with an ataxic gait, and that 1 could speak in short sentences and feed himself. ⚠︎

Moortgat et al. (2016) reported 2 unrelated families with suspected X-linked syndromic intellectual disability. The affected males had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy.

Skopkova et al. (2017) reported 4 families with MEHMO syndrome, including the family described by Steinmuller et al. (1998). The proband from family 1, a Slovak boy born to nonconsanguineous parents, was found to have microcephaly prenatally at 28 weeks' gestation. At birth his occipitofrontal circumference was 32 cm (-2.6 SD), and he had micropenis, cryptorchidism, and peripheral hypertonia. Partial complex seizures resistant to anticonvulsants were diagnosed at 4 months of age. He had severe mental and motor delay and central obesity, with a BMI of +3.7 SD at 2 years. Dysmorphic features included full cheeks, large ears, downturned corners of the mouth, epiblepharon, long eyelashes, thick eyebrows, tapered fingers, and talipes. Non-autoimmune diabetes mellitus was diagnosed at 10 months, and insulin treatment was started. Brain MRI showed delayed myelination and, at 19 months, acute demyelinating changes and cerebral atrophy. Muscle biopsy showed abnormal muscle and mitochondria, with decreased complex II activity and normal complex IV activity. Repeated respiratory infections worsened his diabetes control and his seizures. At 5 years his head circumference was 39 cm (-8.4 SD), and he reacted only to strong tactile stimuli. The proband from family 2, also Slovak, had a birth OFC of 29 cm (-5.1 SD), therapy-resistant seizures from 6 months, hypogenitalism, severe delay, central obesity, insulin-treated non-autoimmune diabetes from 10 months, and delayed myelination. The index patient from family 3, the family of Steinmuller et al. (1998), was diagnosed prenatally with microcephaly. He had therapy-resistant seizures from 7 weeks of age, hypogenitalism, severe delay, central obesity, facial telangiectasias, edematous hands and feet, and bilateral talipes. Diabetes was diagnosed at 6 months during a ketoacidotic crisis, and he died at 2 years of age. The proband from family 4, a boy of Northern European origin, had microcephaly, hypospadias, cryptorchidism, developmental delay, and obesity, but no seizures. He also had chronic lung disease, dysphagia, delayed gastric emptying, and grade 5 vesicoureteral reflux. The 3 patients with the frameshift mutation never achieved head control, made no voluntary movements, and had no social interaction. ⚠︎

Gregory et al. (2019) reported 3 males from a nonconsanguineous white European family: monozygotic twin brothers and their maternal cousin. They had severe recurrent hypoglycemia, short stature with GH and TSH deficiencies, and an unusual form of glucose dysregulation that fluctuated between hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and postprandial hyperglycemia. The twins developed central hypothyroidism at 2 years of age and had microphallus. At 10 years of age they had impaired glucose tolerance with elevated HbA1c. Brain MRI showed variable white matter loss and a small anterior pituitary. The cousin also had hypoplasia of the corpus callosum and ventricular asymmetry. None of the boys had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, or obesity; they had mild learning difficulties and attended mainstream school. The mother of the twins had secondary amenorrhea. The authors concluded that this phenotype was distinct from the classic spectrum of MEHMO syndrome. They suggested that untreated hypoglycemia in earlier patients may have contributed to their more severe neurologic impairment and seizures.

### Inheritance

Steinmuller et al. (1998) described MEHMO as an X-chromosomal syndrome. Hemizygous EIF2S3 mutations were found in affected males and in the heterozygous state in obligate carrier females (Borck et al., 2012; Moortgat et al., 2016). Skopkova et al. (2017) reported that female carriers of the mutation in the family of Steinmuller et al. (1998) were unaffected, and they listed the inheritance as X-linked recessive. Gregory et al. (2019) found that the P432S variant segregated in an X-linked recessive manner through 3 generations.

### Mapping

Using haplotype and 2-point linkage analysis in a large 3-generation family, Steinmuller et al. (1998) mapped the MEHMO locus to Xp21.1-p22.13, in a region flanked by CYBB and DXS365.

In a Moroccan Jewish family with X-linked intellectual disability and microcephaly, Borck et al. (2012) used targeted linkage analysis to identify a 10.7-Mb candidate region at Xp22.11-p21.1 containing 35 annotated genes.

### Heterogeneity

Skopkova et al. (2017) found no variants in the coding and adjacent intronic regions of EIF2S3 in the patients reported by Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) and Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002). The patient reported by Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) carried a deleterious Y430X mutation in the SERAC1 gene. In the family of Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989), the authors suggested that the genetic defect might lie outside the EIF2S3 region examined by exome sequencing, or that a mutation in another gene mimicking MEHMO syndrome might cause the disease.

### Molecular Genetics

Borck et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in a Moroccan Jewish family with X-linked intellectual disability and microcephaly. In the 3 affected males they identified a hemizygous missense mutation in the EIF2S3 gene (c.665T-C; I222T), which cosegregated with the disease in the family. The mutation was not found in 188 X chromosomes from Moroccan Jewish controls or in public databases. Ile222 lies in the GTP-binding domain of eIF2-gamma. In the archaeal structure, the corresponding residue lies in a cleft that binds helix alpha-1 of the beta subunit. When expressed in HeLa cells, the I222T mutant bound substantially less eIF2-beta than wildtype, but eIF2-alpha binding was not impaired. In yeast, mutation of the corresponding residue (V281T or V281K) also impaired eIF2-beta binding. These mutations derepressed GCN4 expression and increased initiation at UUG codons, and the defects were suppressed by overexpressing eIF2-beta. Borck et al. (2012) found no additional mutations in 60 patients from families with nonspecific, potentially X-linked intellectual disability. They also noted a V151L variant in EIF2S3 that an earlier resequencing study had reported in a family with X-linked intellectual disability, without phenotypic or segregation data. ⚠︎

Moortgat et al. (2016) performed X-chromosome exome sequencing in 2 unrelated families with syndromic intellectual disability. They identified 2 mutations in the EIF2S3 gene: a missense mutation (c.777T-G; I259M) and a frameshift mutation (c.1394_1397del; Ile465SerfsTer4). The mutations were hemizygous in affected males and heterozygous in obligate carrier females. Morpholino knockdown of the zebrafish ortholog reproduced the microcephaly and short stature.

Skopkova et al. (2017) studied 4 families with MEHMO syndrome using whole-exome or X-chromosome exome sequencing. In 3 families, including the family of Steinmuller et al. (1998), they identified the same hemizygous 4-bp deletion in the last exon of EIF2S3 (c.1394_1397delTCAA). The deletion is predicted to cause a frameshift (Ile465SerfsTer4) that replaces the last 8 amino acids with 3 different ones, and it cosegregated with the disease. In the fourth family, the proband had a milder phenotype and carried a maternally inherited missense variant (c.324T-A; S108R). Neither variant was present in public databases. In yeast, a human/yeast chimeric eIF2-gamma carrying the frameshift caused slow growth, about a 3-fold derepression of GCN4 expression, and about a 2-fold increase in the UUG/AUG initiation ratio. In contrast, the yeast equivalent of S108R (D167R) had no detectable effect, and the authors classified S108R as a variant of unknown significance. Patient fibroblasts expressed the truncated protein and showed a 2.5-fold increase in DDIT3 transcript levels and elevated CHOP protein, consistent with activation of the integrated stress response. ⚠︎

Gregory et al. (2019) performed X-chromosome exome sequencing in a family with hypopituitarism and glucose dysregulation. In 3 affected males they identified a hemizygous missense variant in the EIF2S3 gene (c.1294C-T; P432S), inherited from their heterozygous mothers. The variant was absent from ExAC and gnomAD. In yeast, the corresponding mutation, made in a locally humanized eIF2-gamma, did not affect growth. However, it modestly but significantly increased GCN4-lacZ expression (about 1.7-fold) and increased UUG initiation from about 1.6% to 3% of AUG initiation. Screening of 103 patients with congenital hypopituitarism found no further EIF2S3 variants. Knockdown of EIF2S3 in a human pancreatic beta-cell line increased caspase activity.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that all 4 index patients with the C-terminal frameshift mutation (Ile465SerfsTer4) had the full MEHMO phenotype, and 3 of them developed insulin-dependent diabetes in the first year of life. Patients with missense mutations were less severely affected and had only some of the symptoms, which could differ even within a family. In yeast, the frameshift mutation impaired eIF2-gamma function to a greater extent than the missense mutations tested. The authors proposed that more severe EIF2S3 mutations cause the full MEHMO phenotype, whereas less deleterious mutations cause a milder form with only some of the symptoms.

Gregory et al. (2019) found that the P432S mutation, which modestly impaired eIF2 function in yeast, was associated with a milder phenotype than earlier EIF2S3 mutations. This phenotype consisted of hypopituitarism, glucose dysregulation, and mild learning difficulties, without microcephaly, epilepsy, or obesity.

### Pathogenesis

Skopkova et al. (2017) proposed that MEHMO syndrome belongs to the group of disorders caused by dysregulation of translation or the unfolded-protein response. They noted that patient 1 in their study, like patients with vanishing white matter disease, worsened markedly during periods of illness.

### Population Genetics

The same EIF2S3 4-bp deletion was found in 3 MEHMO families with no known common ancestors (Skopkova et al., 2017). Identity-by-descent analysis excluded relationships closer than about fourth-degree cousins between 2 of the probands. Comparison of sequencing data suggested a founder effect in the 3 families, but with distant relatedness.

### Animal Model

Moortgat et al. (2016) found that morpholino knockdown of the zebrafish EIF2S3 ortholog, eif2s3, reproduced the microcephaly and short stature seen in patients.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE222THR [c.665T>C; p.Ile222Thr]

Borck et al. (2012) studied 3 affected males from a Moroccan Jewish family with intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. They identified a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution in the GTP-binding domain. The mutation was found by exome sequencing, cosegregated with the disease, and was absent from 188 control X chromosomes and from public databases. Expressed in HeLa cells, the mutant protein showed impaired binding of eIF2-beta. In yeast, mutation of the corresponding residue disrupted eIF2-beta binding, derepressed GCN4 expression, and reduced the fidelity of start codon selection.

**.0002 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE259MET [c.777T>G; p.Ile259Met]

Moortgat et al. (2016) identified a c.777T-G transversion in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile259-to-met (I259M) substitution, in a family with syndromic intellectual disability with severe microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy. The mutation was hemizygous in affected males and heterozygous in obligate carrier females, and was found by X-chromosome exome sequencing.

Gregory et al. (2019) found that the yeast equivalent of this mutation (I318M) increased GCN4-lacZ expression nearly 11-fold.

**.0003 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, 4-BP DEL, 1394TCAA [c.1394_1397delTCAA; p.Ile465Serfs*4]

Moortgat et al. (2016) identified a 4-bp deletion in the EIF2S3 gene (c.1394_1397del), resulting in a frameshift (Ile465SerfsTer4). It was found in the hemizygous state in affected males of a family with syndromic intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy, and in the heterozygous state in obligate carrier females.

Skopkova et al. (2017) identified the same hemizygous c.1394_1397delTCAA deletion in the last exon of EIF2S3 in 3 families with the full MEHMO syndrome: 2 from Slovakia and the German family reported by Steinmuller et al. (1998). The deletion cosegregated with the disease and replaces the last 8 amino acids of eIF2-gamma with 3 different ones. The truncated protein was present in patient fibroblasts, which showed elevated DDIT3 transcript and CHOP protein levels. In yeast, a chimeric eIF2-gamma carrying the mutation caused slow growth, derepressed GCN4 expression, and increased initiation at UUG codons. Haplotype analysis suggested distant relatedness of the 3 families. ⚠︎

**.0004 MEHMO SYNDROME, MILD VARIANT WITH HYPOPITUITARISM AND GLUCOSE DYSREGULATION**  
EIF2S3, PRO432SER [c.1294C>T; p.Pro432Ser]

Gregory et al. (2019) found a hemizygous c.1294C-T transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in a pro432-to-ser (P432S) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the C-terminal domain. It was present in 3 related males with hypopituitarism (GH and TSH deficiencies), hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia followed by hyperglycemia, and mild learning difficulties, without microcephaly or epilepsy. The variant was found by X-chromosome exome sequencing, was inherited from heterozygous mothers, and was absent from ExAC and gnomAD. In yeast, the equivalent mutation modestly increased GCN4-lacZ expression and about doubled initiation at UUG codons.
