### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MEHMO syndrome (MEHMO) is caused by hemizygous mutation in the EIF2S3 gene on chromosome Xp22.11.

### Description

MEHMO syndrome is a rare X-linked intellectual disability syndrome. Its name is an acronym of its main features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity (Steinmuller et al., 1998; Skopkova et al., 2017). In severely affected males, early-onset insulin-dependent diabetes, profound developmental delay, and death in early childhood have been reported. Skopkova et al. (2017) proposed that more severe EIF2S3 mutations cause the full MEHMO phenotype, while less deleterious mutations cause a milder form with only some of the features. Gregory et al. (2019) described a milder phenotype with hypopituitarism and glucose dysregulation caused by an EIF2S3 missense mutation.

### Nomenclature

Steinmuller et al. (1998) named the condition using the acronym MEHMO, after its major clinical features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Borck et al. (2012) described an X-linked intellectual disability syndrome caused by an EIF2S3 missense mutation but did not call it MEHMO syndrome. Skopkova et al. (2017) later noted that this phenotype overlapped with MEHMO syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) described 2 brothers with severe postnatal growth retardation and severe mental retardation. Other features were extreme microcephaly, obesity that developed during infancy, microgonadism, and a characteristic amphora-shaped face. The neurologic examination was highly abnormal, showing hypertonia, hyperreflexia, nystagmus, and extremely irritable and agitated behavior. The first child died at 4.5 years; he also had neonatal hypoglycemia and chronic constipation. The authors noted a resemblance to Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome. However, the dwarfism and mental retardation in their patients were much more severe, and the neurologic and dysmorphic features differed. They suggested a new disorder with recessive inheritance, either autosomal or X-linked. Skopkova et al. (2017) did not find EIF2S3 variants in the index patient of this family (see HETEROGENEITY).

Steinmuller et al. (1998) described a previously unrecognized X-linked mental retardation syndrome in a large 3-generation family. The main features were mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Life expectancy of affected individuals was less than 2 years.

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) reported a child with MEHMO and lactic acidosis. A muscle biopsy showed markedly reduced activities of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes 1, 3, and 4. Histologic staining showed mitochondrial proliferation and lipid storage. Electron microscopy showed abnormal, enlarged mitochondria with concentric cristae and electron-dense bodies. The authors proposed that MEHMO is an X-linked mitochondrial disorder. Skopkova et al. (2017) later found that this patient, who was of Ethiopian Jewish origin, carried a SERAC1 mutation and no EIF2S3 variant (see HETEROGENEITY).

Borck et al. (2012) studied 3 affected males from a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry in the maternal line; the grandparents were first cousins. Features included moderate-to-severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. Each affected individual also had features of his own: cleft lip and palate and behavioral impairment in one, generalized seizures in another, and postpubertal microgenitalism and obesity in the third. Brain MRI showed a thin corpus callosum and enlarged lateral ventricles. Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that one of these patients could speak in short sentences and feed himself, and that the patients were able to walk, with an ataxic gait.

Moortgat et al. (2016) reported affected males from 2 unrelated families with suspected X-linked inheritance. They had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy. In one family, 2 male sibs were affected. Neonatal or early-onset hypoglycemia was reported. The family carrying the frameshift mutation was of Spanish origin. Its patient died at 12 months of age, and Skopkova et al. (2017) suggested that this early death was probably why the phenotype had not been described as MEHMO syndrome. In the family carrying the missense mutation, Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that only 1 of the 2 affected males had epilepsy. Both of these males could make movements and interact socially, and one spoke several words. †

Skopkova et al. (2017) reported 4 families with MEHMO syndrome; family 3 was the family originally reported by Steinmuller et al. (1998). The proband of family 1 was born to nonconsanguineous Slovak parents. Microcephaly was diagnosed prenatally at 28 weeks' gestation. At birth his length was 47 cm (-2.0 SD) and his occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) was 32 cm (-2.6 SD), and he had micropenis, cryptorchidism, and peripheral hypertonia. Partial complex seizures began at 4 months and were resistant to anticonvulsants. He had severe mental and motor delay and central obesity, with a BMI of +3.7 SD at 2 years. Dysmorphic features were full cheeks, large ears, downturned corners of the mouth, epiblepharon, long eyelashes, thick eyebrows, tapered fingers, and talipes. Brain MRI showed delayed myelination, and at 19 months it also showed acute demyelinating changes and cerebral atrophy. Non-autoimmune diabetes mellitus was diagnosed at 10 months and treated with insulin. During repeated respiratory infections his seizures worsened and his diabetes control deteriorated. Muscle biopsy at 17 months showed abnormal muscle and mitochondria, and complex II activity was decreased while complex IV activity was normal. At 5 years his height was 88 cm (-5 SD) and his head circumference 39 cm (-8.4 SD). He reacted only to strong tactile stimuli. Neonatal hypoglycemia and panhypopituitarism were also recorded.

The proband of family 2 in the report by Skopkova et al. (2017) was from a nonconsanguineous Slovak family. His OFC at birth was 29 cm (-5.1 SD). Seizures were diagnosed at 6 months and were resistant to treatment. He also had hypogenitalism, severe mental and motor delay, central obesity, and dysmorphic features very similar to those of the family 1 proband. Non-autoimmune diabetes was treated with insulin from 10 months, and MRI at 10 months showed delayed myelination. At 3 years his height was 78 cm (-4.7 SD). He was hypotonic, had no voluntary movements, and showed no social interaction. Partial hypopituitarism was recorded.

In family 3 of Skopkova et al. (2017), the index patient was diagnosed with microcephaly prenatally and was born at 32 weeks. His seizures began at 7 weeks and were resistant to therapy. He also had hypogenitalism, severe developmental delay, and central obesity. Dysmorphic features included a narrow forehead, full cheeks, facial telangiectasias, downturned corners of the mouth, large ears, edematous hands and feet, tapered fingers, and bilateral talipes. Diabetes was diagnosed at 6 months, when he presented in ketoacidotic crisis. At 2 years his head circumference was 37.7 cm (-8.2 SD). He died at 2 years with permanent seizures that did not respond to treatment. Female carriers in this family were unaffected.

The proband of family 4 in the report by Skopkova et al. (2017) was born to nonconsanguineous parents of Northern European origin living in the US. He had microcephaly, hypospadias, cryptorchidism, and developmental delay. He developed obesity after a period of failure to thrive. He did not have seizures, and his EEG was normal. At 4 years and 7 months his height was 93 cm (-3.2 SD) and his head circumference 46.5 cm (-3.4 SD). He had central hypotonia and peripheral hypertonia with spasticity. Other problems were chronic lung disease, dysphagia, delayed gastric emptying, and grade 5 vesicoureteral reflux. He did not have diabetes.

Gregory et al. (2019) studied 3 affected males from a nonconsanguineous white European family: monozygotic twin brothers and their maternal cousin. They had severe recurrent hypoglycemia and short stature with growth hormone and TSH deficiencies. Their glucose levels fluctuated between hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and postprandial hyperglycemia. Later, the twins had elevated HbA1c and impaired glucose tolerance. The twins had a microphallus, hypoglycemic seizures at 2 years of age, and central hypothyroidism from 2 years. They nevertheless progressed through puberty spontaneously. Brain MRI showed variable white matter loss and a small anterior pituitary in all 3 boys. The cousin also had hypoplasia of the corpus callosum and asymmetric ventricles. None of the boys had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, or obesity. They had mild learning difficulties and attended mainstream school. Gregory et al. (2019) considered this phenotype distinct from classic MEHMO syndrome.

### Inheritance

Steinmuller et al. (1998) described MEHMO as an X-linked syndrome. Skopkova et al. (2017) classified it as X-linked recessive: affected males were hemizygous for EIF2S3 mutations, and female carriers in the Steinmuller et al. (1998) family were unaffected. Moortgat et al. (2016) found EIF2S3 mutations in the heterozygous state in obligate female carriers. In the family reported by Gregory et al. (2019), the EIF2S3 mutation segregated in an X-linked recessive manner over 3 generations.

### Mapping

Steinmuller et al. (1998) performed haplotype and 2-point linkage analyses in a large 3-generation family and assigned the disease locus to Xp21.1-p22.13, in a region flanked by CYBB and DXS365.

In a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry with X-linked intellectual disability, Borck et al. (2012) used targeted linkage analysis to identify a 10.7-Mb candidate region on Xp22.11-p21.1. This region contained 35 annotated genes.

### Heterogeneity

Skopkova et al. (2017) found no variants in the coding and adjacent intronic regions of EIF2S3 in the index patients reported by Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) and Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002). The patient reported by Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) carried a deleterious tyr430-to-ter (Y430X) mutation in the SERAC1 gene. In the family reported by Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989), Skopkova et al. (2017) suggested that the defect might lie outside the region of EIF2S3 examined by exome sequencing, or in another gene that mimics MEHMO syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 affected males from a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry with X-linked intellectual disability, Borck et al. (2012) identified a hemizygous missense mutation in the EIF2S3 gene (I222T). The mutation was found by linkage analysis followed by exome sequencing and was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It segregated with the disorder in the family and was not found in 188 X chromosomes from Moroccan Jewish controls or in public databases. Borck et al. (2012) modeled the mutation in yeast eIF2-gamma and expressed the human mutant in HeLa cells. In both systems the mutation impaired binding of eIF2-beta to eIF2-gamma. In yeast it also impaired translation start codon selection and eIF2 function. Sequencing all 12 EIF2S3 exons in 60 individuals from families with nonspecific, possibly X-linked intellectual disability identified no further mutations.

In affected males from 2 unrelated families with syndromic intellectual disability and suspected X-linked inheritance, Moortgat et al. (2016) used X-chromosome exome sequencing to identify hemizygous mutations in the EIF2S3 gene. One family carried a missense mutation (c.777T-G, I259M) and the other a frameshift mutation (c.1394_1397del, I465SfsTer4). The mutations were present in the heterozygous state in obligate female carriers. Morpholino knockdown of the zebrafish eif2s3 ortholog reproduced the microcephaly and short stature seen in the patients. ⚠︎

Skopkova et al. (2017) studied 4 families with MEHMO syndrome using whole-exome or X-chromosome exome sequencing. In affected males from 3 of the families, including the family originally reported by Steinmuller et al. (1998), they identified a hemizygous 4-bp deletion in the EIF2S3 gene (c.1394_1397delTCAA; I465SfsTer4) that segregated with the disorder. Identity-by-descent and haplotype analyses suggested a founder effect, with the 3 families related only distantly. In the fourth family, which had a milder phenotype, the proband carried a maternally inherited missense variant (c.324T-A, S108R). In yeast, the corresponding change had no effect on growth, GCN4 expression, or start site selection. Skopkova et al. (2017) therefore classified S108R as a variant of unknown significance.

In 3 related males with hypopituitarism, glucose dysregulation, and mild learning difficulties, Gregory et al. (2019) used X-chromosome exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous missense mutation in the EIF2S3 gene (P432S). Their heterozygous mothers also carried the mutation. In yeast, the mutation modestly impaired eIF2 function. Sequencing of EIF2S3 in 103 patients with various forms of congenital hypopituitarism identified no further variants.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that all 4 index patients with the C-terminal frameshift mutation (I465SfsTer4) had the full MEHMO phenotype. Three of them developed insulin-dependent diabetes in the first year of life, and none achieved head control, made voluntary movements, or interacted socially. Patients with missense mutations were less severely affected and had only some of the features, which could differ even between family members with the same mutation. In yeast assays, the frameshift mutation impaired cell growth, whereas the missense changes corresponding to I222T and S108R did not. Skopkova et al. (2017) proposed that more deleterious EIF2S3 mutations cause the full MEHMO phenotype and less deleterious mutations a milder form.

Gregory et al. (2019) found that the P432S mutation impaired eIF2 function only modestly in yeast. The equivalent of I259M, which is associated with a more severe phenotype, caused a much larger effect. They concluded that milder loss of function caused a phenotype distinct from classic MEHMO syndrome. They also suggested that untreated hypoglycemia in earlier patients may have contributed to their more severe neurodevelopmental impairment and seizures.

### Pathogenesis

Skopkova et al. (2017) found that the truncated eIF2-gamma protein was present in fibroblasts from patients with the I465SfsTer4 mutation. In fibroblasts from one patient, DDIT3 transcript levels were increased 2.5-fold and CHOP protein levels were elevated, consistent with activation of the integrated stress response. They placed MEHMO syndrome among disorders caused by dysregulation of translation or the unfolded protein response.

Gregory et al. (2019) detected EIF2S3 expression in the developing human hypothalamus, Rathke pouch, pituitary, and fetal pancreatic islets of Langerhans. Knockdown of EIF2S3 in a human pancreatic beta-cell line increased caspase activity and cell death.

### Animal Model

Moortgat et al. (2016) found that morpholino-based knockdown of eif2s3, the zebrafish ortholog of EIF2S3, reproduced the microcephaly and short stature seen in patients.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE222THR [c.665T>C; p.Ile222Thr]

In 3 affected males from a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry with X-linked intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism, Borck et al. (2012) identified a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in exon 7 of the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution in the GTP-binding domain of eIF2-gamma. The mutation was found by exome sequencing within a linked region on Xp22.11-p21.1. It segregated with the disorder in the family and was absent from 188 X chromosomes from Moroccan Jewish controls and from public databases. In yeast, the corresponding V281T mutation substantially impaired eIF2-beta binding to eIF2-gamma. It also increased GCN4-lacZ expression 2.5-fold and increased the UUG/AUG initiation ratio. When expressed in HeLa cells, human eIF2-gamma carrying I222T bound substantially less eIF2-beta, while eIF2-alpha binding was unaffected.

**.0002 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE259MET [c.777T>G; p.Ile259Met]

In affected males from a family with severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy, Moortgat et al. (2016) used X-chromosome exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous c.777T-G transversion in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile259-to-met (I259M) substitution. The mutation was present in the heterozygous state in female obligate carriers. Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that only 1 of the 2 affected males in this family had epilepsy. Gregory et al. (2019) found that the corresponding I318M mutation in yeast eIF2-gamma increased GCN4-lacZ expression nearly 11-fold.

**.0003 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, 4-BP DEL, 1394TCAA [c.1394_1397delTCAA; p.Ile465Serfs*4]

In affected males from a family with severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy, Moortgat et al. (2016) used X-chromosome exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous 4-bp deletion in the EIF2S3 gene (c.1394_1397del), resulting in a frameshift and premature termination (Ile465SerfsTer4). Female obligate carriers were heterozygous for the deletion. The family was of Spanish origin, and the affected individual died at 12 months of age. †

Skopkova et al. (2017) identified the same hemizygous 4-bp deletion (c.1394_1397delTCAA; I465SfsTer4) in affected males from 3 families with the full MEHMO phenotype, 2 from Slovakia and 1 from Germany. The German family was the one originally reported by Steinmuller et al. (1998). The deletion segregated with the disorder in each family and was not present in dbSNP or ExAC. Analyses suggested a founder effect with distant relatedness. The frameshift replaces the last 8 amino acids of eIF2-gamma with 3 different amino acids. Patient fibroblasts produced the truncated protein. In a yeast/human chimeric eIF2-gamma, the mutation caused slow growth that was worse at 37 degrees C, derepressed GCN4 expression nearly 3-fold, and increased the UUG/AUG initiation ratio about 2-fold. Patient fibroblasts also showed increased DDIT3 mRNA and CHOP protein levels. ⚠︎

**.0004 MEHMO SYNDROME, MILD FORM**  
EIF2S3, PRO432SER [c.1294C>T; p.Pro432Ser]

In 3 related males with hypopituitarism (growth hormone and TSH deficiencies), glucose dysregulation, and mild learning difficulties, Gregory et al. (2019) identified a hemizygous c.1294C-T transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in a pro432-to-ser (P432S) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the C-terminal domain of eIF2-gamma. The patients were monozygotic twin brothers and their maternal cousin from a nonconsanguineous white European family. The mutation was found by X-chromosome exome sequencing. It was inherited from the heterozygous mothers and was absent from the ExAC and gnomAD databases. In yeast, the corresponding mutation increased GCN4-lacZ expression about 1.7-fold. It also raised UUG-initiated reporter expression from about 1.6% to 3% of AUG-initiated expression, indicating a modest impairment of eIF2 function.
