### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene on chromosome 6q21-q22.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are neonatal diabetes, a hypoplastic or annular pancreas, duodenal and/or jejunal atresia with or without intestinal malrotation, and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder (Mitchell et al., 2004; Smith et al., 2010). Affected infants have low birth weight and may have intrahepatic cholestasis and intractable diarrhea that does not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement. Many die in the first year of life (Smith et al., 2010; Sansbury et al., 2015). In some patients with biallelic RFX6 mutations, diabetes did not appear until childhood (Sansbury et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Chappell et al. (2008) proposed that cases with neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis, and cases with overlapping patterns such as those described by Martínez-Frías et al. (1992), may be variations of the same syndrome. Smith et al. (2010) acknowledged that their patients with RFX6 mutations were similar to some other reported cases, especially those with Martinez-Frias syndrome. However, they considered their cases distinct from that syndrome for three reasons: the original Martinez-Frias syndrome patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias, they did not have diabetes, and Smith et al. (2010) found no RFX6 mutation in the parents of 1 case of Martinez-Frias syndrome. Smith et al. (2010) proposed the name Mitchell-Riley syndrome, after the 2 clinicians who first described the disorder.

### Clinical Features

Martínez-Frías et al. (1992) studied 2 sibs born to consanguineous parents. Both had a multiple congenital anomaly pattern of low birth weight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias, which the authors regarded as a previously unreported autosomal recessive syndrome. A search of the Spanish Collaborative Study of Congenital Malformations (ECEMC) data files found 3 other unrelated infants with intestinal atresias, hypospadias, and low birth weight. The authors suggested these infants might have a milder form of the same syndrome.

Annerén et al. (1998) reported a lethal autosomal recessive syndrome of intrauterine growth retardation, intra- and extrahepatic biliary atresia, and esophageal and duodenal atresia.

Gentile and Fiorente (1999) reported esophageal, duodenal, rectoanal, and biliary atresia, intestinal malrotation, a malformed/hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. They regarded this as further evidence for a new distinct syndrome. ⚠︎

Mitchell et al. (2004) described 5 infants: 2 sib pairs from 2 families and 1 isolated case. All presented with neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder. One sib pair was born to consanguineous parents. One patient with a milder form survived free of insulin. The other 4 children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. Pancreatic immunohistochemistry in 3 infants showed a few scattered chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but no insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. Exocrine histology varied. The authors concluded that this combination of anomalies probably represented a new autosomal recessive syndrome caused by a genetic abnormality that interferes with normal islet development. ⚠︎

Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) described a female infant born to consanguineous parents. She had low birth weight, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, a hypoplastic pancreas, and intestinal malrotation, and she died 60 days after birth. Chromosomes at the 550-600 band level were normal (46,XX). The authors considered that this patient confirmed the autosomal recessive disorder described by Martínez-Frías et al. (1992). They suggested the disorder was most probably of blastogenetic origin, mainly affecting the midline development of the duodenal-biliary-pancreatic junction. ⚠︎

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient born to consanguineous parents who had neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis. This was the first such case with neonatal diabetes but no demonstrable structural abnormality of the pancreas, which the authors took as evidence that impaired pancreatic function is involved. In a later review, Sansbury et al. (2015) summarized this patient as a Pakistani girl born at 35 weeks' gestation weighing 1,700 g. She had duodenal atresia, malrotation, gallbladder agenesis, undetectable fecal elastase, intrahepatic cholestasis, and an anteriorly placed anus. Diabetes began on day 8.

Martinovici et al. (2010) reported a newborn of consanguineous parents who had multiple congenital anomalies and neonatal hemochromatosis. The anomalies were intrauterine growth retardation, intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus. Because these fitted a diagnosis of Martinez-Frias syndrome, the authors suggested that neonatal hemochromatosis may be part of that syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) noted that the reported patients with neonatal diabetes and small bowel obstruction from bowel atresia had somewhat small pancreases but were not deficient in exocrine pancreatic enzymes. Autopsies of 2 cases showed normal-appearing exocrine pancreas with clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells, but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. The syndrome also included a hypoplastic gallbladder and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 Turkish double first cousins. There was no stated parental consanguinity, but the mothers were sisters and the fathers were brothers. The first patient, a girl born at 32 weeks' gestation weighing 1.65 kg, had duodenal atresia, a jejunal web, and a Meckel diverticulum. Hyperglycemia (10.6 mmol/l) was noted at 2 years of age, and at 3 years she presented with diabetic ketoacidosis. At that time insulin was less than 2 microIU/ml, C-peptide was 0.023 ng/ml, and anti-GAD, IAA, and ICA antibodies were negative. She was admitted many times for gastrointestinal bleeding. A small intestinal resection at age 5 years showed patches of gastric heterotopia in the small bowel, after which the bleeding resolved. Her gallbladder was absent, but the rest of the biliary tract and her liver function tests were normal. Her identical twin had duodenal atresia and died at 1 month of age from complications of prematurity and surgery. The second patient, a boy born at 34 weeks' gestation weighing 1.70 kg, had duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation. He had asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia (7.2 mmol/l) and was diagnosed with diabetes at age 6 years after presenting with hyperglycemia (16.0 mmol/l). He also had chronic iron deficiency anemia of unknown cause. No structural hepatobiliary abnormality was found. Both cousins were alive at 9 years of age. Sansbury et al. (2015) also reviewed the 8 previously reported cases with molecular confirmation. Duodenal atresia was present in all of them, with or without jejunal atresia or midgut malrotation. Gallbladder agenesis was present in 7 of 8, intrahepatic cholestasis in 7 of 8, and ascites in 1 further case. Five of the 8 children had died, all within the first year of life. In all of these earlier cases, diabetes had been diagnosed within the first 2 weeks of life.

### Inheritance

Mitchell et al. (2004) proposed autosomal recessive inheritance on the basis of affected sib pairs, one of them born to consanguineous parents. Smith et al. (2010) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance: affected individuals carried homozygous or compound heterozygous RFX6 mutations inherited from carrier parents. Sansbury et al. (2015) studied heterozygous carriers in their family, some of whom had adult-onset diabetes. They found no support for the idea that heterozygosity for either RFX6 mutation was a risk factor for abnormal HbA1c or C-peptide levels or for diabetes. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) performed high-resolution homozygosity mapping in 2 probands, the offspring of first cousins and of second cousins, respectively. Only 3 homozygosity-by-descent regions, totaling 24 Mb and containing 194 RefSeq genes, were shared by both probands. These were on chromosomes 2p15-p16, 6q21-q22, and 6q23. Of these genes, only RFX6, in the largest segment at 6q21-q22, showed pancreas-enriched expression in the TiGER database (Liu et al., 2008).

### Molecular Genetics

Mitchell et al. (2004) studied 1 infant from the consanguineous family. They found no duplication or uniparental isodisomy of PLAGL1 at 6q24, no contiguous gene deletion involving the glucokinase gene, and no mutation in the coding sequences or splice sites of PDX1. Chappell et al. (2008) sequenced KCNJ11, ABCC8, GCK, IPF1, HNF1B, NEUROD1, TCF7L2, and HNF6 in their patient and found no mutations.

Smith et al. (2010) used homozygosity mapping followed by deep sequencing of all exons in the shared homozygous regions. The only nonsynonymous variant found in proband 2 was a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) substitution in the RFX6 gene. Direct sequencing of RFX6 found mutations in 5 of 6 available probands, all inherited from carrier parents. Three were homozygous, respectively, for a splice donor site mutation in intron 2 (IVS2+2T-C), an out-of-frame deletion in exon 7, and an arg181-to-gln (R181Q) missense mutation. Proband 3 was compound heterozygous for splice site mutations in intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G) and intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G), and proband 2 was homozygous for S217P. In pancreas from proband 1 obtained at autopsy, the properly spliced RFX6 transcript was not detected. In DNA-binding assays, R181Q completely abolished DNA binding, whereas S217P only modestly reduced it. No RFX6 mutation was found in proband 6, whose DNA was unavailable; the authors considered this case most likely a phenocopy. They also found no RFX6 mutations in a case of Martinez-Frias syndrome.

In 2 Turkish double first cousins with intestinal atresias and childhood-onset diabetes, Sansbury et al. (2015) used targeted next-generation sequencing of neonatal diabetes genes and found compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene: R726X and R866X, in exons 17 and 18, respectively. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X and the mothers for R866X. Both mutations had been inherited from the grandmothers.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) reported a genotype-phenotype correlation. Probands homozygous for the IVS2+2T-C splice mutation, the exon 7 frameshift deletion, or the R181Q mutation all died in the first few months of life. In contrast, the compound heterozygous proband (IVS6+2T-G/IVS1-12A-G) was alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin, and the proband homozygous for S217P was alive at 4.5 years.

Sansbury et al. (2015) noted that the mutations in the earlier probands affected splicing or the reading frame in exons 2 to 8, or were missense mutations within or close to the DNA-binding domain. In contrast, their patients carried truncating mutations in exons 17 and 18. The R866X mutation was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay and to produce a partially truncated protein with a fully preserved DNA-binding domain. The authors suggested that this partial inactivation of RFX6 could explain the later onset of diabetes and the milder phenotype.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) found that Rfx6 is expressed broadly in the definitive endoderm and later becomes restricted to the gut, the pancreatic bud, and finally the pancreatic islets. Mice lacking Rfx6 were born at the expected mendelian ratio. They failed to feed normally, developed gross bowel distension from small bowel obstruction, and died within 2 days after birth; some had a reduced pancreas size. Their pancreases contained many chromogranin A-positive endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin; only pancreatic polypeptide-producing cells were present. Rfx6 acted downstream of Neurog3. Rfx6 and Rfx3 formed a heterodimeric DNA-binding complex and together activated an X box-containing promoter.

### History

Smith et al. (2010) noted that, at the time of their report, OMIM listed their cases under Martinez-Frias syndrome.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2 [c.380+2T>C; IVS2+2T>C]

In proband 1, a child of first-cousin parents with Mitchell-Riley syndrome who died in the first months of life, Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous T-to-C transition at the splice donor site of intron 2 (IVS2+2T-C) in the RFX6 gene. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. RT-PCR of pancreatic RNA from the patient's autopsy did not detect the properly spliced RFX6 transcript or any RNA from exons 1 and 2. Smith et al. (2010) attributed this probably to nonsense-mediated decay. Sansbury et al. (2015) designated this mutation c.380+2T-C and identified the patient and an affected sib as cases reported by Mitchell et al. (2004). Both were of Pakistani origin, and they died at 158 and 194 days of age.

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, 13-BP DEL, NT776 [c.776_780+8del13]

In proband 4 with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, who died in the first months of life, Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7 of the RFX6 gene, inherited from carrier parents. Sansbury et al. (2015) designated this mutation c.776_780+8del13 and listed the patient as a French boy from a nonconsanguineous family. He had duodenal atresia and diabetes from day 2 and died at 2.5 months of age.

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181GLN [c.542G>A; p.Arg181Gln; R181Q]

In proband 5 with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, who died in the first months of life, Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous arg181-to-gln (R181Q) mutation in the RFX6 gene, inherited from carrier parents. The mutation affects a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain and completely abolished DNA binding in vitro. Sansbury et al. (2015) gave the nucleotide change as c.542G-A, although their table listed the protein change as p.Arg181Gly. They identified this patient as the infant with neonatal hemochromatosis reported by Martinovici et al. (2010).

**.0004 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2 [c.672+2T>G; IVS6+2T>G]

In proband 3 with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, Smith et al. (2010) found compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the RFX6 gene, inherited from carrier parents. One was a T-to-G transversion at the splice donor site of intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G); the other was an A-to-G transition disrupting the splice acceptor site of intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G). The patient was alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin. Sansbury et al. (2015) designated the mutations c.672+2T-G and c.224-12A-G and identified the patient as a case reported by Mitchell et al. (2004).

**.0005 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS1AS, A-G, -12 [c.224-12A>G; IVS1-12A>G]

For discussion of the IVS1-12A-G mutation in the RFX6 gene (c.224-12A-G), which Smith et al. (2010) found in compound heterozygous state in a patient with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, see RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2. The second-cited paper, Sansbury et al. (2015), supplied the c. nomenclature.

**.0006 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [c.649T>C; p.Ser217Pro; S217P]

In proband 2 with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, a child of second-cousin parents, Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) mutation in the RFX6 gene. It was identified by unbiased deep sequencing of exons in shared homozygous regions and confirmed by direct sequencing. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. It lies between the DNA-binding and dimerization domains; it only modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation. The patient was alive at age 4.5 years. Sansbury et al. (2015) designated the mutation c.649T-C and identified the patient as the case reported by Chappell et al. (2008).

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.Arg726X]

In 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes, Sansbury et al. (2015) found compound heterozygosity for 2 nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene. One was a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution; the other was a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution. The mutations were found by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X and the mothers for R866X, and both mutations had been inherited from the grandmothers. R726X was predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay. R866X, which lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary, was predicted to escape it.

**.0008 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG866TER [c.2596C>T; p.Arg866X]

For discussion of the c.2596C-T transition in the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution, which Sansbury et al. (2015) found in compound heterozygous state in 2 double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, see RFX6, ARG726TER. Sansbury et al. (2015) predicted that the R866X transcript would escape nonsense-mediated decay and produce a partially truncated protein with a preserved DNA-binding domain that might retain some function.
