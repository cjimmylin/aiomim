### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene on chromosome 6q21-q22.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, duodenal and/or jejunal atresia or intestinal malrotation, and gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia (Mitchell et al., 2004; Smith et al., 2010). Affected infants often have low birth weight, intrahepatic cholestasis, and malabsorption that does not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement, and many die within the first year of life (Mitchell et al., 2004; Smith et al., 2010; Sansbury et al., 2015). Sansbury et al. (2015) reported patients with the typical intestinal atresias whose diabetes did not begin until childhood. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Martínez-Frías et al. (1992) described 2 sibs, born to consanguineous parents, with low birth weight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. They suggested this was a previously unreported autosomal recessive syndrome. A search of the Spanish Collaborative Study of Congenital Malformations (ECEMC) found 3 other unrelated infants with intestinal atresias, hypospadias, and low birth weight, who might represent a milder form of the same syndrome. Related reports followed. Annerén et al. (1998) reported a lethal autosomal recessive syndrome of intrauterine growth retardation, intra- and extrahepatic biliary atresia, and esophageal and duodenal atresia. Gentile and Fiorente (1999) reported esophageal, duodenal, rectoanal, and biliary atresia, intestinal malrotation, malformed/hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) described a patient born to consanguineous parents with low birth weight, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and intestinal malrotation, and considered her a further example of the Martínez-Frías syndrome. ⚠︎

Chappell et al. (2008) proposed that cases with overlapping features, including those described by Martínez-Frías, might be variants of the same syndrome. Martinovici et al. (2010) diagnosed Martínez-Frías syndrome in a newborn with intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus. Smith et al. (2010), however, noted that the original Martínez-Frías patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias and did not have diabetes. They also found no RFX6 mutation in the parents of one case of Martínez-Frías syndrome. On this basis, they concluded that their RFX6-mutated cases were distinct from Martínez-Frías syndrome. They proposed the name Mitchell-Riley syndrome, after the 2 clinicians who first described the condition.

### Clinical Features

Mitchell et al. (2004) described 5 infants with neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia. The infants came from 2 sib pairs in 2 families plus 1 isolated case, and one sib pair was born to consanguineous parents. One patient had a milder form and was surviving without insulin. The other 4 children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. Pancreatic immunohistochemistry in 3 infants showed a few scattered chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but no insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. Exocrine histology varied. Mitchell et al. (2004) concluded that this combination probably represented a new autosomal recessive syndrome.

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient, born to consanguineous parents, with neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis. This was the first such case with neonatal diabetes but no demonstrable structural abnormality of the pancreas. In a later review, Sansbury et al. (2015) identified this patient as a Pakistani girl born at 35 weeks' gestation weighing 1,700 g. She had onset of diabetes on day 8, duodenal atresia, malrotation, undetectable fecal elastase, intrahepatic cholestasis, and an anteriorly placed anus, and was alive at 1 year 9 months.

Martinovici et al. (2010) reported a newborn of consanguineous parents with intrauterine growth retardation, intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, diabetes mellitus, and neonatal hemochromatosis. They suggested that neonatal hemochromatosis might be part of the syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) described patients with neonatal diabetes and small bowel obstruction due to bowel atresia. Their pancreata were somewhat small, but the patients were not deficient in exocrine pancreatic enzymes. Autopsies of 2 cases showed normal-appearing exocrine pancreata containing clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells, with no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. The syndrome also included a hypoplastic gallbladder and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 Turkish double first cousins with intestinal atresias at birth whose diabetes did not present until ages 3 and 6 years. There was no history of parental consanguinity, but the mothers of the 2 patients were sisters and the fathers were brothers. The first patient was a girl born at 32 weeks' gestation weighing 1.65 kg, with duodenal atresia, a jejunal web, and Meckel diverticulum. Hyperglycemia (10.6 mmol/l) was noted at age 2 years, and she presented with diabetic ketoacidosis at age 3 years. At that time, insulin was less than 2 microIU/ml, C-peptide was 0.023 ng/ml, and anti-GAD, IAA, and ICA antibodies were negative. She had repeated gastrointestinal bleeding, and a small intestinal resection at age 5 years showed patches of gastric heterotopia. She also had an absent gallbladder, with an otherwise normal biliary tract and normal liver function. Her identical twin had duodenal atresia and died at 1 month of age from complications of prematurity and surgery.

The second patient described by Sansbury et al. (2015) was a boy born at 34 weeks' gestation weighing 1.70 kg, with duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation. He was diagnosed with diabetes at age 6 years, when hyperglycemia (16.0 mmol/l) was found. Asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia had been noted 1 year earlier. Autoantibodies were negative, and he required about 0.7 units/kg of insulin per day. He also had chronic iron deficiency anemia of unknown cause. No structural hepatobiliary abnormality was found, and his liver function was normal. Sansbury et al. (2015) compared these 2 patients with 8 previously reported molecularly confirmed cases. All 8 earlier cases had duodenal atresia, and 7 of 8 had gallbladder agenesis. All had liver involvement, with intrahepatic cholestasis in 7 and ascites in 1, and 5 had died within the first year of life. Apart from the later onset of diabetes, the 2 new patients differed from earlier cases in having no liver pathology, and they survived longer.

### Inheritance

Mitchell et al. (2004) proposed autosomal recessive inheritance for this syndrome, based on affected sib pairs and parental consanguinity in one family. Smith et al. (2010) confirmed it by finding homozygous or compound heterozygous RFX6 mutations in affected patients, all inherited from carrier parents. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) performed high-resolution homozygosity mapping in 2 probands whose parents were first cousins and second cousins, respectively. Only 3 homozygosity-by-descent regions were shared by both probands, at 2p15-16, 6q21-22, and 6q23, totaling 24 Mb and containing 194 RefSeq genes. Using the TiGER database (Liu et al., 2008), Smith et al. (2010) found that RFX6, located in the largest segment at 6q21-22, was the only one of these genes with pancreas-enriched expression.

### Molecular Genetics

Smith et al. (2010) studied patients with this syndrome, including those reported by Mitchell et al. (2004), Chappell et al. (2008), and Martinovici et al. (2010). They used 2 independent approaches. First, they captured and deep-sequenced the exons in the shared homozygous regions in 1 proband. This identified 30 novel variants, of which the only nonsynonymous change was a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) mutation in the RFX6 gene. Second, direct sequencing of RFX6 found missense, splice site, or frameshift mutations in 5 of 6 probands. Four probands were homozygous for these mutations and 1 was compound heterozygous, and all mutations were inherited from carrier parents. In 1 proband homozygous for a splice site mutation, RT-PCR of autopsy pancreas detected no properly spliced RFX6 transcript. In an electrophoretic mobility shift assay, the R181Q mutation completely abolished DNA binding, whereas the S217P mutation reduced it only modestly. No RFX6 mutation was found in 1 proband, whose DNA was unavailable and who was considered most likely a phenocopy. Smith et al. (2010) also found no RFX6 mutations in a case of Martínez-Frías syndrome.

Sansbury et al. (2015) used targeted next-generation sequencing of 29 monogenic diabetes genes in the Turkish double first cousins with childhood-onset diabetes. Both patients were compound heterozygous for 2 nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene, R726X and R866X, located in exons 17 and 18. The fathers carried the R726X mutation and the mothers carried the R866X mutation, and both mutations had been inherited from the grandmothers. Several heterozygous relatives had adult-onset diabetes. However, C-peptide and HbA1c measurements did not support heterozygosity for either mutation as a risk factor for diabetes.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) found that the 3 probands who died in the first few months of life were homozygous for, respectively, a splice donor mutation in intron 2, an out-of-frame deletion in exon 7, and the R181Q missense mutation, which abolished DNA binding. A proband still alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin was compound heterozygous for 2 splice site mutations. Another proband, alive at 4.5 years, was homozygous for the S217P mutation, which only modestly reduced DNA binding.

Sansbury et al. (2015) noted that in 8 earlier molecularly confirmed cases, the mutations were splice site or frameshift mutations in exons 2 to 8 or missense mutations within or near the DNA-binding domain. In contrast, their 2 patients with childhood-onset diabetes and a milder phenotype carried truncating mutations in exons 17 and 18 of the 19-exon gene. The R866X mutation was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay and to produce a truncated protein that keeps the DNA-binding domain. Sansbury et al. (2015) suggested that the later onset of diabetes might be due to incomplete inactivation of RFX6.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) generated Rfx6-null mice by replacing the first 5 exons, which include the DNA-binding domain, with an eGFP-cre cassette. Homozygous mutant mice were born at the expected mendelian ratio. They fed poorly, had gross bowel distension due to small bowel obstruction, and died within 2 days of birth. Some had a reduced pancreas size. Mutant pancreata still contained many chromogranin A-positive and synaptophysin-positive endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin. Pancreatic polypeptide-expressing cells were increased in number. Neurog3 expression was unaffected, whereas expression of Arx, Irx2, Pax6, Mafa, Neurod1, and Pdx1 was reduced. Smith et al. (2010) concluded that Rfx6 acts downstream of Neurog3 to direct islet cell differentiation.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2 [IVS2+2T>C; c.380+2T>C]

Smith et al. (2010) found this mutation in homozygous state in a proband with Mitchell-Riley syndrome who died in the first few months of life. The mutation is a T-to-C change at the intron 2 splice donor site and abolishes it. RT-PCR of autopsy pancreas detected no properly spliced RFX6 transcript, and no RNA from exons 1 and 2 was detected, probably because of nonsense-mediated decay. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this mutation as c.380+2T-C, homozygous in 2 Pakistani sibs, both of whom died before 7 months of age.

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, 13-BP DEL, NT776 [c.776_780+8del13]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7 of the RFX6 gene in a proband with Mitchell-Riley syndrome who died in the first few months of life. Sansbury et al. (2015) gave the deletion as c.776_780+8del13 and described the patient as a French boy from a nonconsanguineous family. He had duodenal atresia and diabetes from day 2 and died at 2.5 months of age.

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181GLN [c.542G>A; p.Arg181Gln]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous arg181-to-gln (R181Q) mutation in the RFX6 gene in a proband with Mitchell-Riley syndrome who died in the first few months of life. The mutation affects a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain, and in an electrophoretic mobility shift assay it completely abolished DNA binding. Sansbury et al. (2015) identified this patient as the one reported by Martinovici et al. (2010) and listed the nucleotide change as c.542G-A. However, their table gave the protein change as arg181-to-gly.

**.0004 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2 [IVS6+2T>G; c.672+2T>G]

Smith et al. (2010) found this mutation in compound heterozygous state in a patient with Mitchell-Riley syndrome. The mutation is a T-to-G change that abolishes the intron 6 splice donor site, and the second mutation was IVS1-12A-G. The patient was alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this mutation as c.672+2T-G in the patient reported by Mitchell et al. (2004).

**.0005 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS1AS, A-G, -12 [IVS1-12A>G; c.224-12A>G]

Smith et al. (2010) found this mutation, an A-to-G change that disrupts the intron 1 acceptor site, in compound heterozygous state with the IVS6+2T-G mutation in a patient with Mitchell-Riley syndrome. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed it as c.224-12A-G.

**.0006 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [c.649T>C; p.Ser217Pro]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) mutation in the RFX6 gene in a proband with Mitchell-Riley syndrome whose parents were second cousins. The mutation was detected by unbiased sequencing of captured exons within the shared homozygous regions, where it was the only nonsynonymous novel variant, and was confirmed by direct sequencing. It lies between the DNA-binding and dimerization domains. In vitro, it modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation. The patient was alive at age 4.5 years. Sansbury et al. (2015) gave the nucleotide change as c.649T-C and identified the patient as the one reported by Chappell et al. (2008).

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.Arg726X]

Sansbury et al. (2015) found a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution. It was present in compound heterozygous state with R866X in 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome whose diabetes began at ages 3 and 6 years. The mutation was found by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The fathers were heterozygous for it, and it was predicted to be subject to nonsense-mediated decay.

**.0008 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG866TER [c.2596C>T; p.Arg866X]

Sansbury et al. (2015) found a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution. It was present in compound heterozygous state with R726X in 2 double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes. The mothers were heterozygous for it. Because the mutation lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary, it was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay and to produce a truncated protein that keeps the DNA-binding domain.
