### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that oblique facial clefting-1 (OBLFC1) is caused by mutation in the SPECC1L gene.

### Description

Oblique facial clefts are rare facial malformations. Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) noted that they include types 2 through 6 of the classification of facial clefts proposed by Tessier (1976). Saadi et al. (2011) described oblique facial clefts as a unique class of facial malformations.

### Nomenclature

Tessier (1976) proposed an anatomic classification of facial, craniofacial, and laterofacial clefts based on personal observations. The orbit served as the primary structure of reference, and 15 cleft locations could be distinguished. Tessier (1976) stated that the classification removed the older, confusing terminology and made it easier to record malformations. The classification noted a relationship between the soft tissue and the underlying bone. Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) stated that oblique facial clefts include Tessier types 2 to 6. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (1988) reported an 8-month-old girl who was born with symmetric bilateral oblique facial clefts and a calcaneovarus foot deformity. CT scan of the head showed severe bilateral ocular hypoplasia and normal brain parenchyma. Dasouki et al. (1988) suggested that the pattern of anomalies would help to distinguish oblique facial clefts from amniotic band disruption.

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) reported 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts and a strikingly similar facial appearance. Three of the patients had consanguineous parents. The authors proposed that the pattern of facial involvement and the overall clinical picture pointed to a unique dysmorphogenetic process. They suggested that in some instances this process could represent an oculomaxillofacial dysostosis.

### Inheritance

In the patient reported by Dasouki et al. (1988), the translocation disrupting SPECC1L had occurred de novo. Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) observed parental consanguinity in 3 of 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts and proposed genetic heterogeneity. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

In the girl with bilateral oblique facial clefts, Dasouki et al. (1988) found a de novo balanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 22 on peripheral blood karyotype. The authors could not exclude a submicroscopic imbalance resulting from the translocation. They recommended chromosome studies in children with such rare malformations.

Saadi et al. (2011) restudied the patient reported by Dasouki et al. (1988). They mapped the chromosome 22 breakpoint of the translocation to intron 14 of the SPECC1L gene, which disrupted the gene. †

### Heterogeneity

From their study of 4 Brazilian patients, Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) concluded that oblique facial clefts show clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in the SPECC1L gene cause oblique facial clefting. In the patient originally reported by Dasouki et al. (1988), the de novo t(1; 22) translocation disrupted SPECC1L, with the breakpoint located in intron 14 of the gene. During mouse embryogenesis, Specc1l was expressed in the cell populations of the developing facial primordia, which proliferate and fuse to form the face. At embryonic day 10.5, SPECC1L was present in both the epithelium and the underlying mesenchyme of the first pharyngeal arch. In mammalian cells, SPECC1L colocalized with tubulin and with actin. Saadi et al. (2011) established a stable SPECC1L-knockdown U2OS osteosarcoma cell line with a 60 to 70% reduction in SPECC1L transcript and protein. These cells showed defective reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and abnormal cell adhesion and migration. The authors concluded that SPECC1L functions in actin cytoskeleton reorganization and is required for normal facial morphogenesis. †

### Animal Model

Saadi et al. (2011) found that knockdown of a SPECC1L homolog in zebrafish produced a faceless phenotype, with loss of the jaw and facial structures. In Drosophila, knockdown of the homolog phenocopied mutants in the integrin signaling pathway, which show defects in cell migration and adhesion.
