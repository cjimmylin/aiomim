### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 is a RASopathy. Its facial features resemble those of Noonan syndrome, and it also has distinctive ectodermal abnormalities. Cordeddu et al. (2009) described the main features as reduced growth, often with proven growth hormone (GH) deficiency, cognitive deficits, distinctive hyperactive behavior, and hair anomalies. The hair is easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow growing, and loose anagen hair can be confirmed on microscopic examination. Most patients also have darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis, cardiac anomalies (especially mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects), and a hypernasal voice (Cordeddu et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed the name 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair' for the disorder. Motta et al. (2019) referred to the disorder caused by the recurrent SHOC2 ser2-to-gly mutation as Mazzanti syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. Her hair was short, blond, and easily pluckable, and it had never been cut. On trichogram, almost all of the anagen bulbs were abnormally shaped and lacked inner and outer root sheaths. A scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reviewed 19 patients with loose anagen hair, aged 8 months to 47 years. One of them was a 5-year-old boy with Noonan syndrome. In all cases, trichograms showed that 80% to 100% of hairs were anagen hairs devoid of sheaths. Apart from a high incidence of inner root sheath fragmentation, pathologic study of hair from 5 patients showed no morphologic abnormalities of the hair follicles.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) reported 3 children who had short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, and severe GH deficiency. They also had mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities, including darkly pigmented, hairless skin and loose anagen hair. Two of the children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. Their appearance was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, and Mazzanti et al. (2003) suggested that the children represented a distinct, previously unreported syndrome.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) reviewed 25 patients with a SHOC2 mutation and found a fairly consistent phenotype. Facial features seemed typical of Noonan syndrome and included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, low-set and posteriorly rotated ears, a short webbed neck, and pectus anomalies. Patients had reduced growth, often with proven GH deficiency, as well as cognitive deficits. Most had distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age. Their hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow growing. In 12 patients, loose anagen hair was confirmed by microscopic examination of pulled hairs. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Most also had cardiac anomalies; mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were significantly more common than in the general Noonan syndrome population. The voice was characteristically hypernasal. Referring pediatricians had thought that several of the patients had features suggestive of Costello syndrome or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome as newborns or young infants. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 patients. All of them had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) concluded that easily pluckable or loose anagen hair is distinctive in patients with a SHOC2 mutation.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with the S2G mutation who presented with fetal distress, which required premature delivery at 32 weeks. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed rapidly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. Hoban et al. (2012) noted that these cardiac findings were atypical for patients with this mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 unrelated males with the S2G mutation. Four of them had initially been suspected of having Costello syndrome. All 5 had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay. All 4 who were assessed had a structural cardiac anomaly. Four of the 5 had hypotonia and macrocephaly. Three had polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, or needed a feeding tube. None had been formally diagnosed with loose anagen hair. However, all had sparse, very slow-growing hair of unruly texture and unusually light color, together with unusually long eyelashes. On brain imaging, Gripp et al. (2013) found relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus. They also found a relatively small posterior fossa, shown by a vertical tentorium, and cerebellar tonsillar ectopia in 3 of 4 patients. One patient had periventricular nodular heterotopia, and a Chiari I malformation developed after birth. Another patient developed myelofibrosis at age 2 years without the JAK2 val617-to-phe mutation; he died at age 2.5 years after splenectomy in preparation for bone marrow transplant. This was the first hematologic neoplasm reported in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation. Individual patients also had pyloric stenosis, chronic lung disease, rectal prolapse, or severe behavior problems that required psychotropic medication. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that all cases were sporadic. In each family in which parental DNA was available, the SHOC2 mutation had arisen de novo. Hoban et al. (2012) and Gripp et al. (2013) also reported de novo occurrence of the mutation. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with a pro49-to-arg (P49R) mutation in the PPP1CB gene. His hair was sparse, thin, and slow growing, similar to that seen in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. Bertola et al. (2017) noted that this was the fourth reported individual with this PPP1CB variant and concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair is very similar to the SHOC2-related disorder. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) used in silico protein network analysis to rank candidate genes. They then sequenced SHOC2 in 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who had no mutation in known disease genes. In 4 unrelated sporadic patients, they found a heterozygous c.4A-G transition predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. In 3 of these patients the mutation was absent from the parents and paternity was confirmed, showing that it had arisen de novo. In a further cohort of 410 mutation-negative patients with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, they found the same mutation in 21 patients and showed that it was de novo in 12 families. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. The mutation introduces an N-myristoylation site. Unlike the wildtype protein, the mutant protein incorporated myristic acid, was targeted to the plasma membrane, and did not move into the nucleus after growth factor stimulation. In Neuro2A cells, expression of the mutant protein increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation. When expressed in C. elegans, the mutant caused a neomorphic protruding vulva phenotype. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome or related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They found the S2G mutation in 8 patients and developed a rapid detection system for it based on melting curve analysis. They found no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

Hoban et al. (2012) used clinical testing of genes known to cause Noonan syndrome and related disorders. In a premature infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, they found a de novo S2G mutation in SHOC2.

Gripp et al. (2013) found the S2G mutation in 5 patients. In 2 of them it was shown to have arisen de novo.

Hannig et al. (2014) studied a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry whose parents were nonconsanguineous. Using a RASopathy gene panel, they found a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in SHOC2, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. The girl had inherited it from her father. He was tall and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. The proband had macrocephaly, ptosis, nystagmus, right optic nerve hypoplasia, and sparse, slow-growing hair that was not loose. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, or loose anagen hair. In SHOC2-depleted cells, the mutant protein restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 activity to only about 10% above basal levels. It bound PP1c poorly and did not efficiently promote phosphorylation of RAF1 at ser338. Hannig et al. (2014) concluded that this was a loss-of-function variant and that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT (gln269_his270delinsHisTyr), in association with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy that began before birth. In functional studies, the mutant protein bound MRAS and PPP1CB more strongly than wildtype and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade. Unlike the S2G mutant, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) found that the 2 individuals with the SHOC2 M173I variant had phenotypes clearly different from those of patients with the S2G mutation. The proband had craniofacial features suggestive of Noonan syndrome and sparse, slow-growing hair more typical of cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair, and her developmental delay was relatively mild.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that the S2G mutation makes SHOC2 a substrate for N-myristoylation. As a result, the protein is constantly targeted to the cell membrane, which increases MAPK activation in a cell context-specific way. The mutant did not form heterodimers with wildtype SHOC2, and in C. elegans it had no dominant-negative effect. Cordeddu et al. (2009) proposed that a SHOC2 protein constantly located at the membrane would prolong PP1C-mediated dephosphorylation of RAF1 at ser259 and so sustain RAF1-stimulated MAPK activation.

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS, SHOC2, and PP1 form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site on RAF kinases. Noonan syndrome-associated mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB all promoted formation of this complex. Young et al. (2018) suggested that enhanced formation of the ternary complex likely drives the disease at the biochemical level.

### Diagnosis

Tosti et al. (1997) concluded that the pathologic findings of loose anagen hair are not specific and are of little diagnostic value. They also noted that adult-onset loose anagen hair can easily be misdiagnosed as telogen hair loss. Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve assay to screen for the recurrent SHOC2 S2G mutation. Gripp et al. (2013) noted that several patients with the SHOC2 mutation had been suspected of having Costello syndrome, and they supported a gene panel approach to molecular testing in patients with a RASopathy phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Gripp et al. (2013) combined the series of Cordeddu et al. (2009) and Komatsuzaki et al. (2010), assuming that they did not overlap. They estimated that a SHOC2 mutation is present in about 6% (12 of 188) of patients with Noonan syndrome or a Noonan-like condition who lack a mutation in a known gene. They calculated that this corresponds to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype human SHOC2 could replace the function of SUR-8 in C. elegans, but SHOC2 with the S2G mutation could not. When the S2G mutant was expressed in wildtype worms at the early L3 stage, it caused protruding vulva, reduced egg-laying efficiency, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. The same phenotypes were seen with wildtype SHOC2 tagged with an N-myristoylation sequence. These defects arose from abnormal vulval morphogenesis rather than from altered specification of vulval precursor cells.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.Ser2Gly; p.S2G]

In 25 patients with Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, Cordeddu et al. (2009) found a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. In 15 families with available parental DNA, the mutation had arisen de novo. The substitution creates an N-myristoylation site. As a result, the mutant protein was myristoylated, targeted to the plasma membrane, and failed to move into the nucleus after growth factor stimulation. In Neuro2A cells it increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome or related disorders who had no mutations in other known genes.

Hoban et al. (2012) found a de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) found the S2G mutation in 5 patients. One of them developed myelofibrosis, and others had structural brain anomalies.

SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.Met173Ile; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old girl and her father, Hannig et al. (2014) found a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution.
