### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is a disorder of the neuro-cardio-facial-cutaneous (RASopathy) family. Its features include facial features resembling Noonan syndrome, reduced growth often with proven growth hormone (GH) deficiency, cognitive deficits, and distinctive hyperactive behavior that improves with age in most patients. Cardiac anomalies, notably mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects, are common, and the voice is hypernasal. The distinctive ectodermal features are easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair (loose anagen hair) and darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis (Mazzanti et al., 2003; Cordeddu et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed that the disorder in their patients was a distinct, previously unreported syndrome and named it 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.' Motta et al. (2019) called the disorder Mazzanti syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. Her hair was short, blond, and easily pluckable, and it had never been cut. On trichogram, almost all hairs had abnormally shaped anagen bulbs lacking inner and outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts. ⚠︎

Tosti et al. (1997) reviewed 19 patients (14 children and 5 adults) diagnosed with loose anagen hair. One of them was a 5-year-old boy with Noonan syndrome. In all patients, the trichogram showed a predominance (80 to 100%) of anagen hairs devoid of sheaths.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) reported 3 children who had the same facial phenotype, short stature, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, and mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Two of them had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. They also had a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented, hairless skin and hair with the features of loose anagen hair syndrome. Their appearance was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome. Gripp et al. (2013) noted that 1 patient reported by Mazzanti et al. (2003) had ventriculomegaly, required a ventriculoperitoneal shunt at age 2 years, and had a Chiari malformation at age 4 years.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) reviewed 25 patients with the SHOC2 mutation and found a consistent phenotype. Facial features appeared typical for Noonan syndrome and included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, low-set and posteriorly rotated ears, a short webbed neck, and pectus anomalies. Patients had reduced growth, frequently with proven GH deficiency, as well as cognitive deficits and distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most of them. Hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow growing, and in 12 patients microscopy of pulled hairs confirmed loose anagen hair. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. The majority had cardiac anomalies, with mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects significantly overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population. The voice was characteristically hypernasal. Referring pediatricians had thought that several of the patients had features suggestive of Costello syndrome or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome as newborns or young infants.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All of them had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. Clinical features in these patients partly overlapped those of typical Noonan syndrome or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, and easily pluckable or loose anagen hair was distinctive.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with the SHOC2 S2G mutation who was delivered prematurely at 32 weeks because of fetal distress. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed rapidly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors considered his cardiac findings and other features atypical for patients previously reported with this mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 boys with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. Four of them had initially been suspected of having Costello syndrome. All 5 had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse, slow-growing, light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles on the palms, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay. They also had unusually long eyelashes, and 4 of 4 assessed patients had a structural cardiac anomaly. Hypotonia and macrocephaly were each present in 4 of 5 patients. Three of 5 had polyhydramnios and increased birth weight, and 3 needed a feeding tube. Brain imaging showed relative megalencephaly and enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, together with a relatively small posterior fossa, as indicated by a vertical tentorium. Cerebellar tonsillar ectopia was present in 3 of 4 patients. One patient had periventricular nodular heterotopia, a thick and dysplastic corpus callosum, and a Chiari I malformation that developed after birth. Another patient (patient 1) had myelofibrosis at age 2 years without the JAK2 V617F mutation and died at age 2.5 years. This was the first hematologic neoplasm reported in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation. None of the patients had a formal microscopic diagnosis of loose anagen hair.

Hannig et al. (2014) reported a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry, born to nonconsanguineous parents, who carried a heterozygous SHOC2 M173I mutation. She had right optic nerve hypoplasia, bilateral nystagmus and ptosis, speech delay, hyperactive behavior, macrocephaly (98th percentile), and average stature. Her hair was sparse and slow growing but not loose, and she also had widely spaced nipples and clubbed nails. MRI showed multiple irregularities of unknown significance in the midbrain, pons, and medulla. Her father, who carried the same variant, was tall (6 feet 3 inches) and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. Neither of them had short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair. Their features partly overlapped those of Noonan syndrome, cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, and Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. ⚠︎

Motta et al. (2019) reported a SHOC2 mutation (Q269_H270delinsHY) associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that the SHOC2 mutation had arisen de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available. Hannig et al. (2014) found a heterozygous SHOC2 mutation in a girl and her affected father, consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent P49R mutation in the PPP1CB gene. His hair was sparse, thin, and slow growing, like the typical ectodermal finding in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair closely resembles the SHOC2-related disorder. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified SHOC2 as a candidate gene using an in silico analysis of a protein interaction network. They sequenced SHOC2 in 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who were negative for mutations in known disease genes. Four unrelated patients with sporadic disease carried the same heterozygous c.4A-G transition, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. In 3 of these patients, parental DNA was available, the parents did not carry the change, and paternity was confirmed, indicating de novo occurrence. DNA from several tissues of these patients carried the mutation, supporting germline origin. Among 410 further mutation-negative patients with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, 21 carried the same mutation, and it was shown to be de novo in all 12 families in which parental DNA was available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. All 25 mutation-positive patients had the phenotype previously termed Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. In functional studies, the mutation created an N-myristoylation site. The mutant protein incorporated myristic acid, was targeted to the plasma membrane, failed to translocate to the nucleus after growth factor stimulation, and enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. Eight of them carried the S2G mutation. The authors developed a melting curve analysis to detect this mutation rapidly. They found no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a premature infant with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) used clinical testing of genes for Noonan syndrome and related disorders to identify the de novo SHOC2 S2G mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the SHOC2 S2G mutation in 5 patients. In 2 of them, the mutation was shown to be de novo. In patient 1, the mutation was found postmortem in stored buccal cell DNA and was absent from both parents.

In a girl and her father who had overlapping features of Noonan syndrome, cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, and Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The mutation was found by sequencing of a RASopathy gene panel and was not present in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. In SHOC2-depleted Cos-1 cells, the mutant protein restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation only to about 10% above basal levels. It bound M-RAS and RAF-1 normally but showed impaired interaction with PP1c and reduced RAF-1 ser338 phosphorylation. The authors concluded that the mutation causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway.

Motta et al. (2019) identified a SHOC2 mutation (c.807_808delinsTT; Q269_H270delinsHY) associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. In functional studies, the mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade. Unlike SHOC2 S2G, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the 2 patients with the SHOC2 M173I mutation had features that differed significantly from those of patients with the S2G mutation. The patients with M173I did not have short stature, cardiac anomalies, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair, and their developmental delay was relatively mild. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that the S2G mutation in SHOC2 introduces an N-myristoylation site. As a result, the protein is constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane, and MAPK activation increases in a cell type-specific fashion. The mutant did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2, which ruled out a dominant-negative effect through sequestration of the wildtype protein. The authors proposed that membrane-targeted SHOC2 promotes prolonged PP1C-mediated dephosphorylation of RAF1 at ser259, leading to sustained MAPK activation. They also suggested that the loose anagen hair reflects disturbed proliferation, survival, or differentiation of cells derived from epithelial stem cells in hair follicles.

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS, SHOC2, and PP1 form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory 'S259' site on RAF kinases. Noonan syndrome-associated mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB consistently promoted complex formation among these proteins. The authors proposed that, at the biochemical level, disease in individuals with these mutations is likely driven by enhanced formation of this ternary complex.

Motta et al. (2019) found that the SHOC2 Q269_H270delinsHY mutant upregulates MAPK signaling through enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB rather than through membrane targeting. They concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 dysregulation converge on upregulation of MAPK signaling. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Tosti et al. (1997) concluded that the pathologic findings of loose anagen hair are not specific and are of little value in diagnosing the condition.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that 4 of their 5 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation had been suspected of having Costello syndrome, and that none had a formal microscopic diagnosis of loose anagen hair. They recommended gene panel testing for patients with a RASopathy phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Gripp et al. (2013) combined the case series of Cordeddu et al. (2009) and Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) and estimated that the SHOC2 mutation occurs in about 6% (12 of 188) of patients with Noonan syndrome or a Noonan-like condition who lack a mutation in other known genes. They estimated that this corresponds to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) expressed human SHOC2 in C. elegans. Wildtype SHOC2 could functionally replace the worm ortholog SUR-8, but SHOC2 S2G could not. In a wildtype background, expression of SHOC2 S2G at the early L3 stage caused protruding vulva, reduced egg laying, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. These neomorphic phenotypes also occurred with wildtype SHOC2 carrying an N-myristoylation sequence, but not with untagged wildtype SHOC2. The defect involved abnormal morphogenesis of the descendants of vulval precursor cells rather than changes in how vulval cell fate was specified.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.Ser2Gly]

Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 25 patients with Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. The patients were found among 96 and 410 patients with Noonan syndrome or related phenotypes who were negative for mutations in other genes. The mutation was de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available, and no other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. The mutation created an N-myristoylation site. The mutant protein was myristoylated, targeted to the plasma membrane, and failed to translocate to the nucleus after EGF stimulation, and it enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells. In C. elegans, expression of the mutant caused a neomorphic protruding vulva phenotype. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) identified the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutations in other known genes. All had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant who had fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, along with edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, and thrombocytopenia.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the S2G mutation in 5 boys, and the mutation was de novo in 2 of them. One patient developed myelofibrosis without the JAK2 V617F mutation.

**.0002 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.Met173Ile]

Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution, in a 5-year-old girl and her father. The girl had macrocephaly, ptosis, nystagmus, optic nerve hypoplasia, speech delay, hyperactivity, and sparse, slow-growing hair. Her father had intellectual disability, tall stature, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. The variant was found by RASopathy gene panel sequencing and was absent from 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. The mutant protein had impaired binding to PP1c. In SHOC2-depleted cells, it only partially restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation and RAF-1 ser338 phosphorylation, indicating loss of function.

**.0003 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, GLN269_HIS270DELINSHISTYR [c.807_808delinsTT; p.Gln269_His270delinsHisTyr]

Motta et al. (2019) reported a c.807_808delinsTT mutation in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a Q269_H270delinsHY change, in association with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Structural analyses suggested that the mutation could affect the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains of SHOC2. The mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased MAPK signaling, but it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.
