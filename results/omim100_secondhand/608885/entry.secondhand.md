### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes the glucose transporter GLUT1.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects is a rare form of hereditary stomatocytosis. Red cells leak sodium and potassium, and the leak becomes much greater in the cold. The red cell membrane is deficient in stomatin. Hematologic features include hemolytic anemia, pseudohyperkalemia, and hepatosplenomegaly. Unlike other forms of stomatocytosis, the disorder also involves cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder (Fricke et al., 2004; Flatt et al., 2011; Bawazir et al., 2012).

### Nomenclature

Flatt et al. (2011) described stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis as a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome. Bawazir et al. (2012) called the disorder cation-leaky GLUT1-deficiency syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied the red cell membrane proteins of 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased membrane permeability to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis. In both patients, the 28,000-dalton component of the band 7 proteins was decreased. Two-dimensional electrophoresis showed that 1 of the 2 proteins making up this component, designated 22 x 8, was completely absent, whereas the other protein, 60 x 8, was present. The band 7 proteins, including 22 x 8, were present in all normal subjects and reticulocytosis controls tested. Lande et al. (1982) raised the possibility that loss of this protein caused the increased Na and K permeability.

Fricke et al. (2004) described 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree with congenitally Na(+)-K(+)-leaky red cells of the hereditary stomatocytosis class. They also gave further details on 1 previously reported case. Western blotting showed a deficiency of the 32-kD membrane protein stomatin in all cases. All showed marked cation leaks at 37 degrees C, with very abnormal intracellular Na(+) and K(+) concentrations. Immunocytochemistry showed that the stomatin deficiency was incomplete: some red cells stained for stomatin, whereas most did not, and in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils stained for stomatin. In 3 cases, the passive K(+) leak rose steadily with temperature. In 2 cases, the leak was lowest at about 20 to 25 degrees C, and the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, the cryohydrocytosis phenotype. These were the only 2 known cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Both had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts, and massive hepatosplenomegaly, which Fricke et al. (2004) suggested was a new hemato-neurologic syndrome. One of these patients was later found to carry the G286D mutation (Flatt et al., 2011). This patient's features included seizures, intellectual disability, hepatomegaly, cataracts, and splenomegaly. ⚠︎

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that the 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis had a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, and hepatosplenomegaly. They also had cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder. One patient, an adult female carrying an SLC2A1 deletion, had seizures, intellectual disability, spastic quadriplegia, hepatomegaly, cataracts, and splenomegaly. †

Bawazir et al. (2012) reported a female infant with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. She presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, and microcephaly. Seizures developed later, and her developmental milestones were delayed. MRI and CT scans of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. She had visual impairment caused by both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. Her red cells were extremely leaky, which caused the hemolysis. They also lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, so the hyperkalemia was pseudohyperkalemia. Bawazir et al. (2012) stated that this was the fourth pedigree described with the syndrome. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that the hereditary stomatocytoses are dominantly inherited hemolytic anemias.

### Molecular Genetics

Flatt et al. (2011) found mutations in the SLC2A1 gene in the 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. A patient in the Montpellier pedigree had a gly286-to-asp substitution (G286D). A patient in the San Francisco pedigree had a single-amino-acid deletion of ile435 or ile436. In Xenopus oocytes, both mutants lost glucose transport and caused a cation leak. Using a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, Flatt et al. (2011) proposed mechanisms for the phenotypes caused by the 2 mutations. †

In a female infant with cation-leaky GLUT1-deficiency syndrome, Bawazir et al. (2012) found a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene. The deletion removed isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Flatt et al. (2011) studied how stomatin is lost during erythropoiesis in stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. They found that the loss occurred during reticulocyte maturation and involved endocytosis. They proposed that the G286D mutation might reduce the conformational mobility of GLUT1 by forming a new salt bridge between asp286 and leu38 in the N terminus. †

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the disease affects many systems because of 2 combined effects. One is glucose transport deficiency at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The other is the damaging osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.
