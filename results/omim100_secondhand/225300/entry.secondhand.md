### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene on chromosome 12q13.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation (SHFM) is a complex limb malformation that affects the central rays of the autopod (Ugur and Tolun, 2008). It is characterized by absence of the central digital rays, a deep median cleft, and syndactyly of the remaining digits (Gul and Oktenli, 2002). Most familial cases show autosomal dominant inheritance (Gul and Oktenli, 2002). SHFM6 is a rare autosomal recessive form (Ugur and Tolun, 2008; Blattner et al., 2010).

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split-foot malformation in 2 sibs and interpreted it as autosomal recessive ectrodactyly. †

Freire-Maia (1971) reported a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it meant for genetic counseling.

Verma et al. (1976) described an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. Both sibships were born of first-cousin marriages.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split-hand/foot malformation.

Gul and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 individuals with SHFM in an inbred kindred. The malformation pattern varied within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous Turkish kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. Twelve affected members had central reduction defects of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands. One further affected member had the mildest phenotype and atypical SHFM. ⚠︎

Blattner et al. (2010) reported a Swiss woman with sporadic SHFM. ⚠︎

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. The patient and all members of her family had normal bone density and were not obese. Kantaputra et al. (2018) suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated features of SHFM6.

### Inheritance

Several reports of affected sibs, some born to consanguineous parents, suggested that split-hand/foot malformation can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait (Ray, 1970; Freire-Maia, 1971; Verma et al., 1976; Mufti and Wood, 1987).

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) discussed whether their family represented a rare autosomal recessive form of SHFM. As an alternative, they proposed a 2-locus model in which a dominant SHFM mutation is controlled by a gene at another locus, and a dominant mutation at that controlling locus causes the SHFM mutation to be nonpenetrant. Gul and Oktenli (2002) discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a 2-locus model for their inbred kindred.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) found that the homozygous WNT10B mutation in their family was absent in 1 atypically affected member and present in 1 asymptomatic female. They proposed either that a second locus contributes to the SHFM phenotype or that a suppressor locus prevented the trait from appearing in the nonpenetrant female.

Blattner et al. (2010) noted that sporadic SHFM is generally thought to result from new dominant mutations. They showed that sporadic SHFM can instead be recessive. In that case, the recurrence risk is 25% for sibs rather than very low, and the risk for offspring of patients is very low unless there is consanguinity, rather than an estimated 30 to 50%. They recommended that patients with sporadic SHFM be tested for mutations in WNT10B to determine the recurrence risk.

### Mapping

By homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous Turkish kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, Ugur and Tolun (2008) identified an SHFM locus at chromosome 12q13.11-q13, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47. They excluded linkage to 4 of the 5 previously known SHFM loci. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Ugur and Tolun (2008) used a candidate gene approach within the 12q13 locus in a large consanguineous Turkish kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. They identified a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene (R332W). It was present in all affected individuals except the one with atypical SHFM, and also in 1 asymptomatic female. In the TP63 gene, they found a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) in most affected individuals. This variant lies at the alternate promoter used for transcription of the N-terminally truncated p63 isoform. ⚠︎

Blattner et al. (2010) analyzed WNT10B in a Swiss woman with sporadic SHFM. They identified a homozygous 4-bp duplication (c.458_461dupAGCA) that results in a premature termination codon. Nine heterozygous relatives showed no sign of SHFM. ⚠︎

In an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows, Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in the WNT10B gene (c.695_697delACA). The deletion is predicted to remove a single residue in frame (Asn232del). Protein modeling suggested that losing this residue at a critical position would severely disrupt protein structure and function. They suggested it would likely shorten the protein's lifetime and prevent binding to its receptors and other ligands.

**.0001 SPLIT-HAND/FOOT MALFORMATION 6**  
WNT10B, ARG332TRP [p.R332W; p.Arg332Trp]

In affected members of a large consanguineous Turkish kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM6, Ugur and Tolun (2008) identified a homozygous arg332-to-trp (R332W) mutation in the WNT10B gene. They found it by homozygosity mapping followed by a candidate gene approach. The mutation was present in all affected individuals except 1 with an atypical, mild phenotype, and also in 1 asymptomatic female. The authors proposed that either a second locus contributes to the phenotype or a suppressor locus prevented the trait from appearing in the nonpenetrant female. ⚠︎
