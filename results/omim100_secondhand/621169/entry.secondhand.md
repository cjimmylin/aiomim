### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that monilethrix-2 (MNLIX2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the type II hair cortex keratin gene KRT81 (hHb1), which lies in the type II keratin gene cluster on chromosome 12q13. ⚠︎

### Description

Monilethrix is a rare autosomal dominant hair disorder. Its hallmark is beaded, or moniliform, hair caused by periodic thinning of the hair shaft, which is prone to excess weathering and breakage (Winter et al., 1997). The phenotype varies widely, even within a family. Some affected people have dystrophic alopecia and follicular keratosis, and others have no clinical signs (Winter et al., 1998).

### Clinical Features

Winter et al. (1997) studied a family with monilethrix in which the affected members carried a mutation in the hHb1 (KRT81) gene. The abstract does not describe the clinical features of this family beyond the beaded or moniliform hair that defines monilethrix, which results from periodic thinning of the hair shaft and is prone to excess weathering and fracturing.

Winter et al. (1998) reported a French family with monilethrix in which the phenotype was particularly variable. One infant had pronounced dystrophic alopecia, follicular keratosis, and clear-cut moniliform hair. A second infant had no hair loss at all, and the moniliform hair could be detected only by electron microscopy. An adult family member had no symptoms detectable clinically or by electron microscopy but had clear historical proof of the disease.

### Inheritance

Winter et al. (1998) described monilethrix as having autosomal dominant transmission. In the families reported by Winter et al. (1997), the KRT81 mutations were heterozygous. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Winter et al. (1997) noted that several cases of monilethrix had been linked to the type II keratin gene cluster on chromosome 12q13. This cluster includes the hair keratin genes hHb1 and hHb6. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Winter et al. (1997) studied 2 unrelated families with monilethrix and a single sporadic case. In one family and in the sporadic patient, they found the common glu410-to-lys mutation in hHb6. In the affected members of the second family, they found a glu-to-lys substitution in the type II hair cortex keratin hHb1 (KRT81). They numbered the residue Glu403 and stated that it corresponds to Glu410 of hHb6 in the helix termination motif (see Glu403Lys). The authors suggested that this glutamic acid residue is a mutational hotspot in these closely related type II hair keratins. Because hHb1 and hHb6 are largely coexpressed in cortical trichocytes of the hair shaft, they concluded that monilethrix is a disease of the hair cortex.

In a French family with a variable monilethrix phenotype, Winter et al. (1998) identified a glu402-to-lys (E402K) mutation in the EIATYRRLLEGEE helix termination motif of hHb1 (KRT81). The affected residue is a highly conserved glutamic acid (Glu106 of the 2B subdomain). The authors noted that before this report, pathogenic mutations in hHb6 and hHb1 had been limited to a single highly conserved glutamic acid in this motif. They numbered that residue Glu413 (Glu117 of the 2B subdomain) and noted that it is replaced either by lysine or, less often, by aspartic acid. This numbering differs from the one used by Winter et al. (1997). ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Winter et al. (1998) found marked phenotypic variability among members of a single family who carried the same KRT81 E402K mutation. One infant had pronounced dystrophic alopecia, and another had moniliform hair detectable only by electron microscopy. An adult carrier had no detectable signs but had clear historical proof of the disease.
