### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the variant type of xeroderma pigmentosum (XPV) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding DNA polymerase eta (POLH; hRAD30). ⚠︎

### Description

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant (XPV) is an inherited disorder associated with clinical photosensitivity and a higher rate of sunlight-induced skin cancers (Masutani et al., 1999; Itoh et al., 2000). Cells from most patients with xeroderma pigmentosum cannot excise ultraviolet (UV)-induced pyrimidine dimers from their DNA. XPV cells are different: they carry out nucleotide excision repair normally but cannot replicate UV-damaged DNA properly (Lehmann et al., 1975; Masutani et al., 1999). The disorder is caused by defects in DNA polymerase eta, which copies DNA past UV photoproducts (translesion synthesis) (Masutani et al., 1999; Johnson et al., 1999; Despras et al., 2010).

### Nomenclature

The term 'pigmented xerodermoid' was first used for a condition with mild clinical symptoms that seemed similar to, but separate from, xeroderma pigmentosum. Cleaver et al. (1980) compared UV-irradiated cell cultures and found that a pigmented xerodermoid culture could not be told apart from the XP variant in tests of unscheduled DNA synthesis, strand breakage during repair, chain growth during semiconservative DNA replication with or without caffeine, and recovery of DNA replication. They concluded that the term 'pigmented xerodermoid' was redundant.

### Clinical Features

Wang et al. (1991) noted that patients with XP variant have the clinical features of xeroderma pigmentosum, including more frequent skin cancers. However, their cells repair UV-induced DNA damage by nucleotide excision at a normal or nearly normal rate and are only slightly more sensitive than normal cells to being killed by UV.

Fujiwara et al. (1981) studied an 8-year-old boy (UV81KO) whose only symptom was acute recurrent sunburn, with no other physical or neuromental retardation. His skin had lowered minimal erythema doses between 280 and 300 nm, and erythema did not peak late. His cultured skin fibroblasts were 5-fold more sensitive to killing by 254-nm UV than normal cells. They were also more sensitive to fluorescent sunlamp (UV-B) radiation, 4-nitroquinoline-1-oxide, and N-hydroxy-acetyl aminofluorene, but not to mitomycin C or alkylating agents. Cells from obligate heterozygotes responded normally. No defect was found in nucleotide excision repair or in replicative bypass, including the increase in molecular weight of pulse-chased DNA. However, the cells did not recover DNA synthesis normally after 254-nm UV irradiation. The baseline sister chromatid exchange rate was raised (10 to 12 per cell compared with 5 per cell in normal cells). Fujiwara et al. (1981) proposed that the UV81KO disorder was a new photodermatosis, separate from xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, and Bloom syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

Cleaver (1972) described variants of xeroderma pigmentosum that had normal DNA repair and normal sensitivity to ultraviolet light.

Lehmann et al. (1975) found that XP cells with normal excision repair synthesized DNA abnormally after UV irradiation. In irradiated cells, newly made DNA starts out at low molecular weight and is later converted to high molecular weight. This conversion took much longer in the variant cells than in normal cells. Caffeine strongly inhibited the slow conversion in variant cells but had no effect in normal cells. Two excision repair-deficient XP cell lines showed intermediate effects on both measures.

Cleaver et al. (1980) proposed that the defect shared by XP variant and pigmented xerodermoid cells was the loss of a gene product that lets normal cells replicate DNA without stopping at UV-induced pyrimidine dimers. Without it, replication forks in XP variant cells were blocked more often and at lower UV doses.

Wang et al. (1991) introduced a UV-irradiated shuttle vector carrying the supF target gene into an XP variant cell line. The mutation frequency rose linearly with dose, with a slope 5 times steeper than in normal cells. Of 53 independent mutants, 52 had base substitutions, and 62 of 64 substitutions involved a dipyrimidine. In XP variant cells, 28% of mutations involved A:T base pairs, compared with 11% in normal cells; most of these were at position 136. Wang et al. (1991) suggested that XP variant cells are less likely than normal cells to insert dAMP opposite photoproducts, which could explain why UV causes more mutations in these cells.

Itoh et al. (2000) reported that cells from heterozygous parents of XPV patients had normal unscheduled DNA synthesis and normal recovery of RNA and replicative DNA synthesis. In the presence of 1 mM caffeine, however, their recovery of replicative DNA synthesis was reduced to 53 +/- 8% of the normal control. In colony-forming assays, heterozygous cells survived UV normally without caffeine but survived slightly less well with 1 mM caffeine. The authors suggested that XPV heterozygotes could be identified by their UV sensitivity in the presence of nontoxic levels of caffeine.

### Inheritance

Johnson et al. (1999) described xeroderma pigmentosum as an autosomal recessive disease. Itoh et al. (2000) showed in 1 XPV family that the patient had inherited 2 heterozygous missense mutations in the POLH gene, and that the heterozygous parents were clinically unaffected but showed a cellular phenotype. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Masutani et al. (1999) used a cell-free assay for translesion DNA synthesis to purify a DNA polymerase from HeLa cells that could continue replication past UV-induced thymine dimers in XPV cell extracts. They identified it as the human homolog of yeast Rad30, DNA polymerase eta (POLH). All XPV cells they examined carried mutations in the POLH gene. Recombinant human polymerase eta restored the ability of XPV cell extracts to replicate past thymine dimers on damaged DNA.

Johnson et al. (1999) found nonsense or frameshift mutations in hRAD30 (POLH), the human counterpart of yeast RAD30, in XPV cell lines. Seven of the 8 mutations they identified were predicted to produce a severely truncated protein.

Itoh et al. (2000) studied 2 unrelated families, each with a photosensitive patient identified as having XPV. In 1 family, they confirmed that the patient had inherited 2 heterozygous missense mutations in the POLH gene: a 1840A-G transition causing a lys535-to-glu (K535E) substitution, and a 2003A-C transversion causing a missense change at lys589. Neither mutation was found in 52 unrelated Japanese individuals. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Despras et al. (2010) used DNA combing to show that, in UV-irradiated human cells lacking polymerase eta, far fewer replication forks were active and many forks were stalled. Large regions of single-stranded DNA formed during replication in irradiated XPV cells, which overactivated the ATR/CHK1 pathway after low UVC doses. A low concentration of caffeine given after irradiation reduced CHK1 activation and stopped DNA synthesis in XPV cells. Blocking CHK1 with UCN-01 made fork stalling worse in XPV cells and made them more sensitive to UVC, as caffeine does. The authors concluded that polymerase eta allows stalled forks to restart, and that without it CHK1 becomes essential for restarting replication by other pathways that stabilize the forks.

### Diagnosis

XPV had been diagnosed from clinical findings together with a postreplication repair assay of the patient's cells. Itoh et al. (1996) described a simpler autoradiographic method that measures 3 markers after UV irradiation: unscheduled DNA synthesis, recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. Each was compared with normal control cells. Among the hereditary photosensitive disorders, including other forms of xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome, only XPV cells had normal unscheduled DNA synthesis and normal recovery of RNA synthesis but reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis (about 50% of control). Caffeine made this reduction greater. Because each disorder gave a different combination of the 3 markers, the method could also be used to tell these diseases apart.

Using this method, Itoh et al. (2000) found that recovery of replicative DNA synthesis in XPV cells was 51 +/- 6% of normal controls, falling to 36 +/- 5% in the presence of a nontoxic level of caffeine.
