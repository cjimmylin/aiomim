### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene on chromosome 10.

### Description

Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is an autosomal recessive disorder of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) biosynthesis caused by mutation in PDSS1, which encodes prenyldiphosphate synthase subunit 1 (Mollet et al., 2007). The phenotype varies. Some patients have sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, with mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and cardiac valvulopathy (Nardecchia et al., 2021). Others have a severe, early-onset multisystem disorder with encephalopathy, nephrotic syndrome or pulmonary hypertension, and death in early childhood (Nardecchia et al., 2021; Bellusci et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Nardecchia et al. (2021) summarized the 2 sibs reported by Mollet et al. (2007), a 22-year-old male and a 14-year-old female. Both had neurosensorial deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, lactic acidosis, macrocephaly, peripheral neuropathy, mild to moderate cardiac valvulopathy, and obesity/livedo reticularis. Nardecchia et al. (2021) noted that in these sibs, neurosensorial hearing loss preceded optic atrophy. ✗

Vasta et al. (2012) identified PDSS1 variants in a patient from a cohort of 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders. Nardecchia et al. (2021) noted that this patient had leukoencephalopathy, with a lactic acid peak on spectroscopy. ⚠︎

Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported 2 sisters born to consanguineous Egyptian parents. The older sister walked independently at 15 months but had severe language delay. At 4 years, profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss was found and treated with a prosthesis. She was later diagnosed with mild intellectual disability. Her visual acuity was reported as normal at 11 years. At 14 years she was referred for progressive visual loss, and her visual acuity was 1/10 bilaterally, with red-green dyschromatopsia. Fundus examination showed optic atrophy that was most marked in the inferior temporal sector. Visual-evoked potentials showed increased latency and reduced amplitude of the P100 waveform in both eyes. Optical coherence tomography showed bilateral reduction in retinal nerve fiber layer thickness. She also had diffuse mild muscle weakness and a mild postural tremor of the upper limbs. Brain MRI was normal. Echocardiography showed a moderately dysplastic mitral valve with moderate insufficiency, medium-grade tricuspid insufficiency with an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 40 mmHg, and a moderately dilated left atrium. Her 6-year-old sister had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss and mild intellectual disability. At 6 years she developed decreased visual acuity, with optic atrophy and divergent strabismus. In both sisters, sensorineural deafness preceded optic atrophy.

Bellusci et al. (2021) reported a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency due to biallelic PDSS1 mutations. He had been born prematurely after an in vitro fertilization pregnancy complicated by twin-twin transfusion. At 5 months he presented with failure to thrive, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, bilateral hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, and distal phalangeal erythema. Laboratory studies showed anemia, lactic acidosis, 3-methylglutaconic and tricarboxylic aciduria, and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes. He developed a progressive multisystem disorder with severe developmental delay, pyramidal dysfunction, seizures, tremor, optic atrophy, nystagmus, severe visual and hearing impairment, and chronic heart failure. Brain MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy at 12 months. At 24 months it showed cystic change in the white matter surrounding the lateral ventricles and in the commissural fibers, with small necrotic or porencephalic cysts. He died at 3 years of age. The authors suggested that the combination of phalangeal erythema, livedo reticularis, and pulmonary hypertension pointed to microvascular dysfunction.

### Biochemical Features

In a family with PDSS1 mutation, Mollet et al. (2007) found that quinone-dependent oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activities were in the range of the lowest control values, while OXPHOS enzyme activities were normal. Adding quinone restored normal OXPHOS activities, which confirmed CoQ10 deficiency.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance of COQ10D2 was supported by the finding of a homozygous PDSS1 mutation in sibs by Mollet et al. (2007). Nardecchia et al. (2021) found a homozygous PDSS1 mutation in sisters born to consanguineous parents, and the mutation segregated with the disorder in the family. ⚠︎

### Mapping

In family 1, Mollet et al. (2007) performed a genome-wide search for homozygosity. This identified a region of chromosome 10 that contains the PDSS1 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a family with CoQ10 deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) identified a homozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene (D308E). The mutation altered a conserved amino acid and was found by homozygosity mapping followed by sequencing. Mutant yeast COQ1 was introduced into a yeast strain lacking coq1, and the transformed yeast grew poorly on respiratory medium, indicating that the mutation caused the OXPHOS deficiency. Nardecchia et al. (2021) gave the nucleotide change of this mutation as c.924T-G.

Vasta et al. (2012) performed targeted next-generation sequencing of the exons of 908 known and candidate nuclear genes in 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders, and found variants in PDSS1 among other genes. Nardecchia et al. (2021) stated that the patient with PDSS1 variants was compound heterozygous for a missense variant and a truncating variant.

In 2 sisters with COQ10D2, Nardecchia et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution at a highly conserved position. The variant was not present in the gnomAD database. It lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity. Both parents were heterozygous for the variant. Of the unaffected sibs, 1 was homozygous wildtype and 1 was heterozygous.

In a boy with COQ10D2, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified biallelic mutations in the PDSS1 gene: c.716T-G (V239G) and c.1183C-T (R395X).

### Clinical Management

Bellusci et al. (2021) started their patient on oral ubidecarenone at 15 mg/kg/day at age 5 months and raised the dose to 30 mg/kg/day 3 months later. This high-dose CoQ10 supplementation did not control disease progression.

Mutant yeast COQ1 was introduced into a yeast strain lacking coq1, and the transformed yeast grew poorly on respiratory medium. Nardecchia et al. (2021) gave the nucleotide change as c.924T-G.

**.0002 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**

In 2 sisters, born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, with sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, Nardecchia et al. The variant was absent from gnomAD and lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity. Both parents were heterozygous for the variant. Of the unaffected sibs, 1 was homozygous wildtype and 1 was heterozygous.

**.0003 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, VAL239GLY [NM_014317.3:c.716T>G; p.Val239Gly]

In a boy with severe COQ10D2, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified biallelic mutations in the PDSS1 gene: a c.716T-G transversion resulting in a val239-to-gly (V239G) substitution, and a c.1183C-T transition resulting in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution. The boy had mitochondrial encephalopathy, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, distal phalangeal erythema, 3-methylglutaconic aciduria, and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes. He died at age 3 years.
