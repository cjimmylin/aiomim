A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene (606272) on chromosome 17p13.2. ✗

### Description

Late-onset, or intermediate, cystinosis is a rarer form of cystinosis. Attard et al. (1999) defined it by onset of symptoms between 12 and 15 years of age. Goldman et al. (1971) compared adolescent cystinosis with the infantile and adult forms of the disorder.

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of cystinosis.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis (multinucleated cells) of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis. They also reported an unusual clinical variant of the disorder.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with the intermediate form of cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) described late-onset cystinosis as a rarer form of the disorder, with onset of symptoms between 12 and 15 years of age. They studied 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis and a milder phenotype. They also studied 3 patients whose cystinosis began before 7 years of age but took a milder course than the infantile nephropathic form.

### Inheritance

Late-onset cystinosis is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Langman et al. (1985) reported the disorder in a sibship. Thoene et al. (1999) found that patients with intermediate cystinosis were either homozygous for a mild CTNS mutation or carried a mild mutation together with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) studied 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis and identified mutations in the CTNS gene. The disease was caused either by one mild mutation combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or by homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation. Two of the patients had new mutations that had not been described before. Thoene et al. (1999) transfected cystinotic fibroblasts with either normal CTNS cDNA or a cDNA carrying a mutation that causes intermediate cystinosis. Both constructs partially corrected the phenotype of the cells.

Attard et al. (1999) found 23 different CTNS mutations in patients with cystinosis, 14 of them novel. In 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis, the mutations affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin. Three patients had onset before 7 years of age and a milder course than the infantile nephropathic form, and they carried missense mutations.

Macias-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 patients with cystinosis from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) studied 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Twelve had 2 severely truncating CTNS mutations. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains of cystinosin. In contrast, the mutations in 2 late-onset patients affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin, which the authors stated accounted for the milder phenotype. Three patients had onset before 7 years of age but a milder course than the infantile nephropathic form. The authors suggested that the missense mutations found in these patients allow production of functional protein.
