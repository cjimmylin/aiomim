### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene.

### Description

Late-onset cystinosis is rarer than infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Attard et al. (1999) noted that its symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age. Renal failure has been reported in affected sibs (Langman et al., 1985). The disorder is caused by mutations in the CTNS gene, which encodes cystinosin. These mutations are thought to be milder than those that cause the infantile nephropathic form (Thoene et al., 1999; Attard et al., 1999).

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of the disorder.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis (multinucleated cells) of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis. They also described an unusual clinical variant of the disorder.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) noted that late-onset cystinosis is rarer than infantile nephropathic cystinosis and that its symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age.

### Inheritance

Thoene et al. (1999) and Attard et al. (1999) showed that intermediate or late-onset cystinosis is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Patients carried either 2 mutant CTNS alleles or a homozygous CTNS mutation. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Attard et al. (1999) described 3 patients whose cystinosis began before 7 years of age but whose disease course was milder than the infantile nephropathic form. The authors suggested that the missense mutations found in these patients allow production of functional protein. They also suggested that these mutations may mark regions of cystinosin that are not functionally important.

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with the intermediate form of cystinosis who had mutations in the CTNS gene, 2 of them new. In some patients, a mild mutation was combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis. In others, a predicted mild mutation was homozygous. Thoene et al. (1999) transfected cystinotic fibroblasts with either normal CTNS cDNA or a cDNA carrying a mutation that causes intermediate cystinosis. Both partly corrected the cystinotic phenotype. ⚠︎

Attard et al. (1999) screened the CTNS gene in patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, patients with late-onset cystinosis, and patients whose phenotype did not fit the classical definitions. They found 23 different CTNS mutations, 14 of them novel. In 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis, the mutations affected regions of cystinosin that are not functionally important. The authors said this accounts for the milder phenotype.

Macías-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 patients with cystinosis from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Thoene et al. (1999) proposed that intermediate cystinosis arises in 2 ways. In one, a mild CTNS mutation is paired with a severe mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis. In the other, a predicted mild mutation is homozygous.

Attard et al. (1999) studied 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Twelve had 2 severely truncating CTNS mutations, consistent with loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions that were predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains of cystinosin and cause loss of protein function. In contrast, the mutations in patients with late-onset disease affected regions of the protein that are not functionally important.
