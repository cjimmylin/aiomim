### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis, also called intermediate cystinosis, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Late-onset cystinosis is a rarer form of cystinosis than infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Attard et al. (1999) noted that it is characterized by onset of symptoms between 12 and 15 years of age. Thoene et al. (1999) called it the intermediate form of cystinosis. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of cystinosis.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis and also described an unusual clinical variant of the disorder.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) noted that late-onset cystinosis, a rarer form of the disorder than infantile nephropathic cystinosis, is characterized by onset of symptoms between 12 and 15 years of age.

### Inheritance

Thoene et al. (1999) found that intermediate cystinosis results either from one mild CTNS mutation combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or from homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation. This is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Attard et al. (1999) described infantile nephropathic cystinosis, which is allelic with the late-onset form, as an autosomal recessive disease. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Attard et al. (1999) described 3 patients whose cystinosis began before 7 years of age but whose disease course was milder than the infantile nephropathic form. The authors suggested that the missense mutations found in these patients allow production of functional protein and may indicate regions of cystinosin that are not functionally important.

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis who had mutations in the CTNS gene. Two of the mutations had not been described before. The disease resulted either from one mild mutation combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or from homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation. Transfecting cystinotic fibroblasts with either normal cDNA or a cDNA derived from a mutation causing intermediate cystinosis partly corrected their phenotype. ⚠︎

Attard et al. (1999) screened the CTNS gene in patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, patients with late-onset cystinosis, and patients whose phenotype did not fit the classical definitions. They found 23 different mutations, 14 of them novel. The mutations found in 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin, which the authors said accounts for the milder phenotype.

Macías-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 patients with cystinosis from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) studied 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Twelve had 2 severely truncating CTNS mutations, consistent with loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions that were predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains and cause loss of protein function. In contrast, the mutations in 2 late-onset patients affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin.

Thoene et al. (1999) concluded that intermediate cystinosis occurs when a mild CTNS mutation is combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or when a predicted mild mutation is homozygous.
