### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis, also called intermediate cystinosis, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene (606272) on chromosome 17p13.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Late-onset cystinosis is a rarer form of cystinosis than infantile nephropathic cystinosis, in which cystine transport across the lysosomal membrane is defective. Symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age (Attard et al., 1999). Renal failure has been reported in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis (Langman et al., 1985).

### Nomenclature

Goldman et al. (1971) used the term 'adolescent cystinosis' for this form. Thoene et al. (1999) called it 'intermediate cystinosis,' and Attard et al. (1999) called it 'late-onset cystinosis.'

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of cystinosis.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis. They also described an unusual clinical variant of the disorder.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with the intermediate form of cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) characterized late-onset cystinosis by onset of symptoms between 12 and 15 years of age. In infantile nephropathic cystinosis, by contrast, renal proximal tubular dysfunction begins early. Attard et al. (1999) studied 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis. They also studied 3 patients whose phenotype did not fit the classical definitions: cystinosis began before 7 years of age, but the disease course was milder than in the infantile nephropathic form.

### Inheritance

Langman et al. (1985) reported late-onset cystinosis in a sibship. Thoene et al. (1999) found that intermediate cystinosis was caused by either homozygosity or compound heterozygosity for mutations in the CTNS gene, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) studied 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis. The disease was caused either by one mild CTNS mutation combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or by homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation. Two of the patients had new mutations that had not been described before. Transfecting cystinotic fibroblasts with either normal cDNA or a cDNA carrying a mutation that causes intermediate cystinosis partially corrected their phenotype.

Attard et al. (1999) screened the CTNS gene in patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, patients with late-onset cystinosis, and patients whose phenotype did not fit the classical definitions. In 2 patients with late-onset disease, the mutations affected regions of cystinosin that are not functionally important. The authors stated that this accounted for the milder phenotype. In 3 patients with onset before 7 years of age and a milder course, they found missense mutations. They suggested that these mutations allow production of functional protein.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) found 23 different CTNS mutations, 14 of which were novel. Of 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, 12 had 2 severely truncating mutations, consistent with loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains and abolish protein function. In contrast, the mutations in 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin, which the authors stated accounted for the milder phenotype.

Thoene et al. (1999) found that intermediate cystinosis resulted either from a mild mutation combined with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or from homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation.
