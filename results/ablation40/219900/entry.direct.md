### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene.

### Description

Late-onset cystinosis is a rarer form of cystinosis, a disorder caused by a defect in the transport of cystine across the lysosomal membrane. It is milder than infantile nephropathic cystinosis, and symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age (Attard et al., 1999).

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of the disorder.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis and reported an unusual clinical variant of the disorder.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with the intermediate form of cystinosis.

### Inheritance

Langman et al. (1985) reported late-onset cystinosis in a sibship. Thoene et al. (1999) found that intermediate cystinosis resulted from either compound heterozygosity for a mild mutation and a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation in the CTNS gene. This is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance, which Attard et al. (1999) stated for the infantile nephropathic form. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis, Thoene et al. (1999) identified mutations in the CTNS gene. Two of the mutations had not been reported before. The patients had either a mild mutation together with a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis (compound heterozygosity) or 2 copies of a predicted mild mutation (homozygosity). Transfecting cystinotic fibroblasts with normal CTNS cDNA, or with a cDNA carrying a mutation that causes intermediate cystinosis, partly corrected the cellular phenotype.

Attard et al. (1999) screened the CTNS gene in patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, patients with late-onset cystinosis, and patients whose phenotype did not fit the classic definitions. They found 23 different CTNS mutations, 14 of which were novel. In 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis, the mutations affected regions of the predicted cystinosin protein that were not functionally important, which the authors suggested explained the milder phenotype.

Macías-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 patients with cystinosis from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) found that, of 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, 12 had 2 severely truncating CTNS mutations, consistent with loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions predicted to disrupt the transmembrane domains and abolish protein function. In contrast, the mutations in 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin. Three patients had onset of cystinosis before 7 years of age but a milder course than the infantile nephropathic form. The authors suggested that the missense mutations in these patients allowed production of functional protein and might mark regions of cystinosin that are not functionally important.
