### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that orofaciodigital syndrome XVIII (OFD18) is caused by homozygous mutation in the IFT57 gene (606621) on chromosome 3q13.

### Description

Orofaciodigital syndrome XVIII (OFD18) is a type of oral-facial-digital syndrome with short stature and skeletal dysplasia. Phadke et al. (1999) and Thevenon et al. (2016) described oral anomalies, including supernumerary frenulae and missing incisors, and a medial cleft of the upper lip. They also described pre- and postaxial polydactyly, brachydactyly with shortening of the middle phalanges, short stature, short femoral necks, and genu valgum. Intelligence was normal. The features overlapped with those of Ellis-van Creveld syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Phadke et al. (1999) reported a consanguineous family with what they considered a new variety of oral-facial-digital syndrome (OFDS). The patients had the oral, facial, and digital malformations typical of OFDS, together with acromelic short stature and genu valgum, and these features overlapped with Ellis-van Creveld syndrome (EVCS). One of the sibs had urinary incontinence and growth hormone deficiency. The authors stated that these features had not previously been reported in any type of OFDS. ⚠︎

Thevenon et al. (2016) reassessed the family reported by Phadke et al. (1999), which had 3 affected sibs born to healthy consanguineous parents. The family had been followed for 15 years. One sib (II-3) died at 17 years of age from a complicated pulmonary infection. Subject II-1 was a man examined at 25 years of age who had normal intelligence. His final height was 155 cm (-3 SD), his weight was 45 kg (less than -3 SD), and his head circumference was 50 cm (mean). He had a square face with a short forehead, upslanting palpebral fissures, a high nasal bridge, a pyriform nose, a short philtrum, and a medial cleft of the upper lip. Oral anomalies were supernumerary frenulae and missing incisors. He had pronounced brachydactyly and postaxial polydactyly of the upper limbs. There was spacing between the first and second rays of the feet. He had no thoracic deformity. X-rays showed severe mesophalangeal shortening and duplication of the first metatarsal, which was interpreted as preaxial polydactyly. He also had bilateral short femoral necks with varus femurs and symmetric genu valgum, but no vertebral anomalies. Heart, renal, and abdominal ultrasound examinations were normal.

Subject II-2 was a woman examined at 22 years of age by Thevenon et al. (2016). She had normal intelligence and normal timing of puberty. Her final height was 122 cm (-5 SD), her weight was 37 kg (-2 SD), and her head circumference was 51 cm (mean). She had a square face with a narrow forehead, enophthalmia, upslanting palpebral fissures, and a broad nasal bridge. Her lip findings were bilateral hypopigmented pseudoclefts with medial labial clefting, and her oral findings were missing incisors and supernumerary frenulae. She had marked brachydactyly and postaxial polydactyly of all 4 limbs, which had been removed surgically in infancy. Hand X-rays showed mesophalangeal shortening and residual postaxial polydactyly of the right hand, with fused proximal phalanges and bifid distal phalanges. Foot X-rays showed duplication of the first metatarsal, suggesting preaxial polydactyly, and absent middle phalanges. She also had vertebral bodies with medial notches but no platyspondyly, bilateral cervical ribs without a narrow thorax, gracile long bones, and short femoral necks. Brain MRI and abdominal and heart ultrasounds were normal.

Thevenon et al. (2016) concluded that the family had an unclassified OFDS type with skeletal dysplasia rather than EVCS. Their reasons were that the patients had no rib shortening and no narrow or deformed thorax, but did have short stature, brachymesophalangia, and stocky femoral necks. They noted that OFD type II might be suspected because of hallux valgus and normal intelligence, but that short stature was atypical for it. The patients had no visual complaints, but the authors could not exclude ophthalmologic involvement such as retinopathy.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of OFD18 in the family reported by Phadke et al. (1999) and Thevenon et al. (2016) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The parents were consanguineous and unaffected, and the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for the IFT57 mutation.

### Mapping

Thevenon et al. (2016) performed homozygosity mapping in 2 affected sibs from the consanguineous family first reported by Phadke et al. (1999). The sibs shared runs of homozygosity totaling 11 Mb, on chromosome 3 (chr3:104822019-111671862) and chromosome 10 (chr10:1372501-6029726, hg19). Neither interval contained genes previously associated with a recessive ciliopathy. The chromosome 10 interval was excluded because a missense variant in it was heterozygous in the third affected sib.

### Molecular Genetics

Thevenon et al. (2016) studied the 3 affected sibs of a consanguineous Indian family with OFD18, first reported by Phadke et al. (1999). Using homozygosity mapping followed by exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous synonymous c.777G-A transition (lys259-to-lys; K259K) in the IFT57 gene, which affects the last base of exon 6. Sanger sequencing confirmed that all 3 affected sibs were homozygous for the variant. It was absent from the ExAC database and from 50 healthy individuals from northern India. In patient fibroblasts, the variant produced the normal transcript along with 3 abnormal transcripts: in-frame skipping of exon 6, in-frame skipping of exons 5 and 6, and out-of-frame skipping of exons 4 to 6. The abnormal transcripts were detected only after inhibition of nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. Total IFT57 mRNA was significantly reduced in patient fibroblasts compared with controls. The authors concluded that the mutation was hypomorphic. They suggested that it reduced but did not completely block the production of IFT57 message, which could explain the relatively mild phenotype. They also noted that biallelic truncating mutations had never been observed in genes encoding ciliary transporters.

Thevenon et al. (2016) found no causative IFT57 mutation by Sanger sequencing of the gene in 13 patients with OFDS (selected for polydactyly with cleft lip/palate) or in 12 patients with EVCS who had tested negative for EVC1 and EVC2 mutations. Exome or ciliome data from more than 172 patients with various ciliopathy phenotypes showed no biallelic causative IFT57 mutations.

### Pathogenesis

Thevenon et al. (2016) found that primary cilia in fibroblasts from an OFD18 patient were similar in number and length to those in controls, but intraflagellar transport was impaired. IFT57 staining of the ciliary tip was significantly decreased (90% vs 35%; p less than 0.001) and staining of the basal body was significantly increased. Stimulation with Smoothened agonist poorly induced GLI1 expression in patient fibroblasts. siRNA knockdown of IFT57 in control fibroblasts abolished the induction of GLI1, pointing to defective sonic hedgehog signaling. The authors suggested that defective hedgehog signaling underlies many of the patients' features.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

IFT57, 777G-A [c.777G>A; p.K259K; NM_018010.3:c.777G>A; p.Lys259Lys; chr3:g.107910368C>T]

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Indian family with OFD18, first reported by Phadke et al. (1999), Thevenon et al. (2016) identified a homozygous c.777G-A transition in the IFT57 gene. The change is synonymous (lys259-to-lys; K259K) and affects the last base of exon 6. It was found by homozygosity mapping and exome sequencing, and segregation in the 3 affected sibs was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The variant was absent from ExAC and from 50 healthy individuals from northern India. In patient fibroblasts, it caused abnormal splicing, with in-frame skipping of exon 6, in-frame skipping of exons 5 and 6, and out-of-frame skipping of exons 4 to 6, alongside the normal transcript. Total IFT57 mRNA was reduced. Patient fibroblasts also showed reduced IFT57 staining at the ciliary tip and poor induction of GLI1 on sonic hedgehog pathway stimulation.
