### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.33. ⚠︎

### Description

Rodan et al. (2021) described a CACNA1C-related neurological disorder that variably included developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, epilepsy, hypotonia, and ataxia. In all but one of their 25 individuals, significant cardiac abnormalities and the classic features of Timothy syndrome were absent. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She did not have QT prolongation.

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (patient 1) of English (maternal) and Ukrainian (paternal) ancestry with unaffected, nonconsanguineous parents. She had neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. Within a few hours of birth she had numerous subtle tonic seizures each day, as well as prolonged tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures. Later she had epileptic tonic spasms and focal seizures with eye blinking and facial twitching, and her epilepsy was drug-resistant. EEG showed diffuse excessive fast activity. Other findings included profound axial and appendicular hypotonia and no developmental progression; at 18 months she could not visually track or fixate. She also had short length at birth, neonatal hypoglycemia, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubfeet, bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, camptodactyly, micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, very scant hair, inverted nipples, and mild optic nerve hypoplasia. She had severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, anal stenosis, and severe dysphagia requiring gastrostomy, as well as sleep-wake cycle disturbance and central and obstructive sleep apnea. Brain MRI, echocardiogram, and Holter monitoring were normal. The authors noted that she shared many hallmark features of Timothy syndrome, including cutaneous syndactyly, dysmorphic features, and sleep disturbances, but had no cardiac abnormalities. She differed from previously reported individuals in having severe refractory neonatal-onset epilepsy, no neurodevelopmental progression, and a recognizable EEG pattern of diffuse fast activity. ⚠︎

In a second, nonconsanguineous family of Irish and German ancestry, Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (patient 2) who was found to have tetralogy of Fallot after neonatal discharge; it was surgically repaired at 2 months of age. She also had left ventricular noncompaction and an episode of acute severe biventricular failure. Her dysmorphic features included a flat nasal bridge, low-set ears, cupping of the superior helices, deep-set eyes with shallow orbits, a thin vermilion border of the upper lip, a round face, and mild bilateral 2-3-4 toe syndactyly. At 8 months she had mild developmental delays. By 12 months she spoke 10 words and first walked, and examination showed mild diffuse hypotonia. Her father (patient 3) had a congenital ventricular septal defect and aortic valve stenosis, repaired at age 2.5 months. He also had learning disabilities requiring an individualized education program at school and attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and he developed focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures by 18 years of age. His craniofacial features were similar to his daughter's: low-set ears, a thin vermilion border of the upper lip, premaxillary underdevelopment, and a round face. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants. All 14 individuals with de novo nontruncating variants had developmental delay or intellectual disability. Of these 14, 9 were nonverbal, 12 had hypotonia, 10 had epilepsy with onset between 2 months and 8 years of age, and 12 had orthopedic abnormalities, including clubfoot, hip dysplasia, syndactyly, and joint hypermobility. Seizure types included infantile spasms (patients P6 and P13; in P13 they evolved to Lennox-Gastaut syndrome), myoclonic seizures (P7 and P12), focal seizures (P2 and P3), absence and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (P1), and generalized epilepsy (P8 and P14). Seizures were fever-sensitive in P2 and P8, and P11 had febrile seizures. P4, P5, and P10 had no seizures. Several individuals never walked, and P7 walked at 3 years. Other neurologic findings included abnormal balance or coordination, an action tremor in the arms that later resolved (P4), intention tremor in the arms with head tremor (P5), leg spasticity (P10), and autism (P8). Skeletal findings included bilateral clubfoot (P7 and P10), adducted thumbs (P7, P10, and P14), camptodactyly of the fingers (P10), congenital hip dysplasia (P2 and P14), hip dislocation and subluxation (P4), 2-3 toe and 3-4 finger syndactyly (P11), 2-3 toe syndactyly (P14), pes planovalgus (P1 and P5), kyphosis (P8), kyphoscoliosis and coxa valga (P12), and congenital torticollis (P11). Ocular findings included strabismus, hyperopia, myopia, esotropia, and cortical visual impairment (P13). P13 was the only individual in the cohort known to have cardiac disease (prolonged QT). In addition, P12 had a possible ventricular conduction delay, and P4 had a small interatrial communication with a left-to-right shunt.

Rodan et al. (2021) also reported 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating CACNA1C variants, whose deficits were milder and more selective. In family 16, a 6-year-old boy (P16a) had motor delay, hypotonia, abnormal balance and coordination, high-frequency tremor, autism, mild to moderate intellectual disability, and large ears, and he spoke only 2 words. His 33-year-old father (P16b) had language delay and learning disability with normal IQ, without seizures. In family 22, two brothers aged 2 years (P22a) and 3 years (P22b) had hypotonia, autism, dysmorphic features, and language delay, speaking only a few words. P22a had metatarsus adductus, and P22b had abnormal balance and coordination and intention tremor. Their 32-year-old father (P22c) had a learning disability and a history of seizures. He was the only one of the 11 individuals with truncating variants to have seizures, and he was seizure-free without treatment. The other individuals with truncating variants were as follows: a 15-year-old girl (P15) with language delay, autism, ADHD, Chiari I malformation, scoliosis, and IQ in the low-normal range; a 14.5-year-old girl (P17) with language delay, borderline intellectual disability, and dysmorphic features; a 16-year-old boy (P18) with language delay, abnormal balance and coordination, ADHD, moderate intellectual disability, pes planus, genu valgum, mildly broad thumbs and halluces, and hypoplastic fifth fingernails and toes; a 6-year-old boy (P19) with motor and language delay, abnormal coordination, ADHD, and dysmorphic features; a 4-year-old girl (P20) with language delay, autism, and moderate global developmental delay; and a 40-year-old woman (P21) who walked at 26 months and had language delay, moderate intellectual disability, and strabismus.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous CACNA1C mutations in the patients reported by Kosaki et al. (2018), Bozarth et al. (2018), and Rodan et al. (2021) were mostly de novo. In the families of Rodan et al. (2021), truncating variants were transmitted from father to son or daughter and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Rodan et al. (2021) noted that penetrance and expressivity appeared to vary among family members.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension without QT prolongation, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a previously undescribed de novo heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (R1024G).

In a girl with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy and syndactyly (patient 1), Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous missense variant in the CACNA1C gene (c.4087G-A, V1363M). She also carried a de novo heterozygous terminal deletion of the MSH2 gene (exons 11-16), which was not considered causal. On a predicted 3D structure of Cav1.2, V1363M lies in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV, faces the pore-forming sixth helix, and is in direct contact with A1473G, a Timothy syndrome variant. The authors suggested that the larger methionine residue likely disrupts the sixth helix and affects channel gating or calcium transmission. ⚠︎

In a girl with tetralogy of Fallot, left ventricular noncompaction, dysmorphic features, mild developmental delay, and hypotonia (patient 2), Bozarth et al. (2018) identified a heterozygous splice site variant in the CACNA1C gene (c.3717+1_3717+2insA). It was inherited from her affected father (patient 3). She also carried a heterozygous splice site variant in the ALPK3 gene (c.5105+2dupT), which was not considered causal, and a heterozygous I130T variant in the TPM1 gene (191010), which was absent in her father. The significance of the CACNA1C and TPM1 variants was considered uncertain. ⚠︎

In 25 individuals from 22 families with neurodevelopmental disorders, Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous variants in the CACNA1C gene: 13 de novo missense variants, 1 de novo in-frame deletion (I743del), and truncating variants in 11 individuals from 8 families. None of the variants were present in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or the Exome Variant Server. Truncating variants arose de novo in P15, P19, P20, and P21, and in P16b, who transmitted his variant (R161X) to his son. The variant in P17 (W528X) was paternally inherited. Two brothers carried R1989X, which their father also carried; the father's inheritance was unknown. For the splice site variant in P18 (c.2663+1G-C), paternal inheritance was excluded and a maternal sample was unavailable. The 3 inherited truncating variants segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families. One truncating variant was located in exon 45 of 45 and could potentially escape nonsense-mediated decay. In patch-clamp studies in HEK293 cells, L1408V reduced current density to about 30% of wildtype with unchanged protein expression, and L657F increased both current and protein level about 2-fold. L614P produced a modest, nonsignificant increase in current, and L614R had no significant effect.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kosaki et al. (2018) noted that their patient was the first to have syndactyly and a CACNA1C mutation without QT prolongation, a feature long considered obligatory for Timothy syndrome.

Rodan et al. (2021) observed that individuals with truncating CACNA1C variants had milder, more selective deficits than those with nontruncating variants. Ten of the 11 with truncating variants had delayed expressive language, early motor development was essentially normal in all but 1, 5 had autism, and only 1 had seizures. In contrast, all 14 individuals with de novo nontruncating variants had developmental delay or intellectual disability, and 10 had epilepsy.

### Pathogenesis

Rodan et al. (2021) suggested that CACNA1C may be haploinsufficient (gnomAD pLI of 1.0). They proposed that if patients with missense loss-of-function variants had a more severe phenotype than patients with whole-gene or exonic deletions, this would better support a dominant-negative effect. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

In the patient with the V1363M variant, Bozarth et al. (2018) found that seizures did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs. Topiramate and valproic acid produced a small reduction. Clobazam reduced seizures from numerous per day to fewer than 10 per day and improved sleep.
