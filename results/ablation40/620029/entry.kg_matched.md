### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.33.

### Description

NEDHLSS is a mainly neurological disorder. Features vary and include developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, epilepsy and ataxia. Patients do not have significant cardiac manifestations or the other features of Timothy syndrome (Rodan et al., 2021). Orthopedic abnormalities are common. They include clubfoot, adducted thumbs, hip dysplasia, kyphosis or scoliosis, syndactyly and joint hypermobility. Rodan et al. (2021) found that individuals with non-truncating variants tended to have more severe developmental and motor impairment and frequent epilepsy. Those with truncating variants tended to have more selective expressive language deficits, and autism was more common in this group.

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay and pulmonary hypertension. Unlike patients with Timothy syndrome, she did not have prolongation of the QT interval.

Bozarth et al. (2018) described a girl (Patient 1) born at 38 weeks to nonconsanguineous parents of English and Ukrainian ancestry. She had short birth length (z-score -3.92) and neonatal hypoglycemia. Other features were profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubfeet, 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, camptodactyly, mild optic nerve hypoplasia and anal stenosis. She had severe dysphagia that required a gastrostomy tube. Her dysmorphic features were micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, very scant hair and inverted nipples. She had a neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. Seizures began within hours of birth, with numerous tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures, and epileptic tonic spasms were diagnosed at 3 months. The seizures did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs, although clobazam reduced the seizure burden. Her first EEG, at 5 days of life, showed distinctive diffuse excessive fast activity, and this persisted beyond the neonatal period. At 18 months she had made no developmental progress, could not visually track, and had a severe sleep-wake cycle disturbance. Brain MRI at 7 days and at 12 months was normal. MR spectroscopy, echocardiogram, 24-hour Holter monitoring and biochemical workup were also normal. Bozarth et al. (2018) noted that the patient had many hallmark features of Timothy syndrome, including cutaneous syndactyly, dysmorphic features and sleep disturbance, but no cardiac abnormalities. She differed from previously reported patients in having severe refractory neonatal-onset epilepsy, no neurodevelopmental progress, and a recognizable EEG pattern of diffuse fast activity.

Bozarth et al. (2018) also described a girl of Irish and German descent (Patient 2). She had tetralogy of Fallot, left ventricular noncompaction with acute biventricular failure at 4 months, mild dysmorphic features, mild 2-3-4 toe syndactyly, mild developmental delay and mild diffuse hypotonia. Her father (Patient 3) had a congenital ventricular septal defect, aortic valve stenosis, learning disabilities and ADHD. He developed focal-to-generalized tonic-clonic seizures at 18 years, and his craniofacial features were similar to his daughter's. The authors considered this family's phenotype most consistent with Timothy syndrome with variable expressivity.

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals (13 male, 12 female) from 22 families who carried heterozygous CACNA1C variants and had a mainly neurological phenotype. The patients were ascertained through GeneMatcher/Matchmaker Exchange and ClinVar. Age at last follow-up ranged from 10 months to 40 years (median 6.8 years). Features varied and included developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, epilepsy and ataxia. At most recent follow-up, all but one of the patients lacked significant cardiac abnormalities. The exception had prolonged QT.

Fourteen of the patients reported by Rodan et al. (2021) were unrelated and carried de novo non-truncating CACNA1C variants. All 14 had developmental delay or intellectual disability. At last follow-up, 9 were nonverbal and 6 were nonambulatory. Twelve were hypotonic and 4 had ataxia. One had a formal diagnosis of autism, and 6 others had autistic features. The course was consistent with a static neurodevelopmental disorder; 1 individual regressed during the onset of infantile spasms. Prenatal findings were reduced fetal movement (3 of 14), bilateral clubfoot (2 of 14) and oligohydramnios (2 of 14). Ten of the 14 had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years. Both generalized and focal epilepsies occurred, including West syndrome/infantile spasms and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. Seizures were fever-sensitive in 3 individuals and medically refractory in 4. EEG findings varied between patients. Brain MRI was normal except for an incidental arachnoid cyst in one individual and prominent cerebellar folia in another. Twelve of the 14 had orthopedic abnormalities. These were bilateral clubfoot with congenital finger contractures (2), persistently adducted thumbs (2 additional), kyphosis or scoliosis (2), congenital hip dysplasia (3), syndactyly (2) and joint hypermobility (3). Other findings were tremor (2 of 14), hyperopia (4 of 14), strabismus (4 of 14) and leg spasticity (1 of 14). Two individuals had microcephaly. Three had short stature, including the 2 with clubfoot. Facial dysmorphisms were minor and inconsistent. Few cardiac abnormalities were found in this group. Eleven individuals had ECGs. One patient (P13) had a prolonged QTc with repolarization instability and macroscopic T-wave alternans on Holter monitoring. Another had a possible ventricular conduction delay, with only premature ventricular contractions on Holter monitoring. Of 9 echocardiograms, 7 were normal; 1 showed trace valvular regurgitation and 1 a small interatrial communication. None of the individuals had physical features of Timothy syndrome.

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating CACNA1C variants. Most had essentially normal early motor development and walked independently. Ten of the 11 had delayed expressive language. Five had autism, and 3 of these were nonverbal. Only 1 had seizures, and 3 had hypotonia. Four had poor balance or coordination, 2 had tremor and 1 had paroxysmal dyskinesia. Four of the 11 had recurrent infections, and 1 of these had hypogammaglobulinemia. ECGs in 5 individuals were normal.

### Inheritance

NEDHLSS shows autosomal dominant inheritance. Most mutations have occurred de novo (Kosaki et al., 2018; Bozarth et al., 2018; Rodan et al., 2021). In the series of Rodan et al. (2021), all non-truncating variants and all but 3 truncating variants arose de novo. The 3 inherited truncating variants were paternally transmitted and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities, with variable penetrance and expressivity among family members.

### Molecular Genetics

Kosaki et al. (2018) studied a 5-year-old girl with syndactyly, developmental delay and pulmonary hypertension but no QT prolongation. Exome analysis identified a previously undescribed de novo heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene, R1024G. The authors stated that this was the first patient with syndactyly and a CACNA1C mutation who did not have QT prolongation, which had long been considered an obligatory feature of Timothy syndrome.

In Patient 1, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous missense variant in exon 33 of the CACNA1C gene, c.4087G-A (V1363M). The residue is highly conserved, and the variant was absent from the gnomAD, ExAC and 1000 Genomes databases. In silico tools predicted it to be damaging (GERP 4.6, SIFT 0, MutationTaster disease causing), and the authors interpreted it as likely pathogenic. Structural modeling placed V1363M in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV. There it faces the pore-forming sixth helix and directly contacts the residue altered by the Timothy syndrome-associated variant A1473G. The authors suggested that replacing valine with the larger methionine probably disrupts the sixth transmembrane helix and affects channel gating or calcium transmission. Exome sequencing also found a de novo heterozygous terminal deletion of MSH2 exons 11-16, which had previously been reported as pathogenic for Lynch syndrome. Karyotype, chromosomal microarray and mtDNA analysis were normal.

In Patient 2, Bozarth et al. (2018) used a 120-gene cardiomyopathy panel to identify a heterozygous splice site variant in CACNA1C, c.3717+1_3717+2insA. She had inherited it from her affected father (Patient 3). The variant was absent from population databases and was predicted to destroy the canonical splice donor site of intron 28. In the absence of functional mRNA studies it was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). The authors suggested that haploinsufficiency could account for the phenotype. The panel also found a heterozygous ALPK3 splice variant and a heterozygous TPM1 variant of uncertain significance (c.389T-C, I130T); the father did not carry the TPM1 variant.

Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous CACNA1C variants in 25 individuals from 22 families by exome sequencing or an intellectual disability gene panel. Thirteen unrelated individuals had missense variants and 1 had an in-frame deletion. Eleven individuals from 8 families had predicted truncating variants, one of which was a canonical splice variant. All non-truncating variants and all but 3 truncating variants arose de novo. The 3 inherited truncating variants segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities. None of the variants was present in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes or the Exome Variant Server. All missense variants affected highly conserved positions and were predicted to be damaging. One truncating variant, located in exon 45 of 45, might escape nonsense-mediated decay; all the others were predicted to undergo it. Rodan et al. (2021) studied several variants by whole-cell patch clamp in HEK293 cells, with differing results. L657F caused a statistically significant increase in calcium current, with a hyperpolarizing shift in activation, and raised protein levels about 2-fold. L1408V reduced current density to about 30% of control without changing activation kinetics or protein expression, indicating loss of function. L614P produced modest current increases that were not statistically significant, with a hyperpolarizing shift in activation. L614R caused no statistically significant change in current or voltage-dependent gating.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bozarth et al. (2018) suggested that the variable phenotypes associated with CACNA1C may reflect alternative splicing, tissue-specific expression and where the variant lies within the channel.

Rodan et al. (2021) found phenotypic differences between the two variant groups. Individuals with non-truncating variants, all de novo, had more severe global developmental delay or intellectual disability and motor impairment; about half were nonverbal and nonambulatory, and more than two-thirds had epilepsy. Individuals with truncating variants had more selective expressive language deficits with mostly normal motor development. Autism was more common and seizures were rare in this group. The missense variants clustered in linker segments of domain II (exon 13) and domain IV (exon 35).

### Pathogenesis

Rodan et al. (2021) concluded that both gain-of-function and loss-of-function CACNA1C variants can cause a neurological phenotype. Patients with missense loss-of-function variants had a more severe phenotype than patients with whole-gene or exonic deletions, and the authors suggested that this would better support a dominant-negative effect. They noted that their functional studies used only the canonical transcript and different beta subunits (CACNB3 vs CACNB2), which might explain the apparently neutral results for some variants.
