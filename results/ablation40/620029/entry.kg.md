### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.33.

### Description

NEDHLSS is a mainly neurological disorder. Features vary and may include developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, epilepsy, and ataxia. Affected individuals do not have significant cardiac manifestations or the other features of Timothy syndrome. Rodan et al. (2021) noted broad phenotypic differences between individuals with truncating and nontruncating CACNA1C variants.

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She did not have prolongation of the QT interval. The authors described her condition as Timothy syndrome-like.

Bozarth et al. (2018) described a girl (patient 1) of English/Ukrainian ancestry, born to nonconsanguineous parents, who had neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. Tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures began within hours of birth and evolved into epileptic tonic spasms at 3 months of age. Multiple antiepileptic drugs failed to control the seizures, although clobazam reduced them. She had profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, no developmental progress at 18 months, inability to fixate or track visually, and severe sleep-wake disturbance. Other features were laryngomalacia, bilateral clubfeet, 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, camptodactyly, micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, scant hair, inverted nipples, mild optic nerve hypoplasia, anal stenosis, and dysphagia requiring a gastrostomy tube. EEG showed distinctive diffuse excessive fast activity. Brain MRI, echocardiogram, and Holter monitoring were normal.

Bozarth et al. (2018) also described a family of Irish and German descent. The proband (patient 2), a girl, had tetralogy of Fallot, which was repaired at 2 months of age. At 4 months she developed severe biventricular failure with left ventricular noncompaction. She had mild dysmorphic features, including flat nasal bridge, low-set cupped ears, deep-set eyes, thin upper lip, and round face. She also had mild 2-3-4 toe syndactyly, mild developmental delay, and mild hypotonia. Her father (patient 3) had a congenital ventricular septal defect, aortic valve stenosis, learning disabilities, and ADHD. He developed focal-to-generalized tonic-clonic seizures at age 18 years, and his craniofacial features were similar to his daughter's. Bozarth et al. (2018) noted that seizures had rarely been reported in CACNA1C-related disorders, with only 3 individuals reported before their series. They concluded that their findings extended the phenotype to neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy and late-onset epilepsy, and that this family showed variable expressivity. They suggested including CACNA1C on epilepsy gene panels.

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants. Fourteen individuals had de novo nontruncating variants, and all of them had developmental delay or intellectual disability. At last follow-up, 9 were nonverbal and 6 were nonambulatory. One had a formal diagnosis of autism and 6 others had autistic features. Twelve were hypotonic and 4 had ataxia. Prenatal findings included reduced fetal movement (3 of 14), bilateral clubfoot (2 of 14), and oligohydramnios (2 of 14). The course was consistent with a static neurodevelopmental disorder. Brain MRI was normal except for an incidental arachnoid cyst in 1 individual and prominent cerebellar folia in another. Ten of the 14 had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years. Both generalized and focal epilepsies occurred, including West syndrome/infantile spasms, myoclonic seizures, and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. Seizures were fever-sensitive in 3 individuals and medically refractory in 4, and EEG findings were not consistent across the group. Twelve of the 14 had orthopedic abnormalities. Two had bilateral clubfoot with congenital finger contractures and 2 others had persistently adducted thumbs. Other orthopedic findings were kyphosis or scoliosis (2 of 14), congenital hip dysplasia (3 of 14), syndactyly (2 of 14), and joint hypermobility (3 of 14). Nine of 11 individuals with nontruncating variants who had ECGs had normal results. One individual (P13) had prolonged QT with T-wave alternans on Holter monitoring, and another had a possible ventricular conduction delay. Echocardiograms showed only minor findings, and no individual had the physical features of Timothy syndrome.

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating CACNA1C variants. Most had essentially normal early motor development and walked independently. Ten of the 11 had delayed expressive language. Five had autism, and 3 of these were nonverbal. Only 1 had seizures and only 3 had hypotonia. Four had poor balance or coordination and 2 had tremor. Four individuals had recurrent infections. All 5 ECGs performed were normal.

### Inheritance

The evidence supports autosomal dominant transmission of NEDHLSS. Most reported heterozygous CACNA1C mutations occurred de novo (Kosaki et al., 2018; Bozarth et al., 2018; Rodan et al., 2021). Bozarth et al. (2018) reported transmission of a CACNA1C splice site variant from an affected father to his affected daughter. Rodan et al. (2021) found that 3 truncating variants were paternally inherited and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families. Penetrance and expressivity appeared variable among family members with truncating variants. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-year-old girl with syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension but no QT prolongation, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene, arg1024-to-gly (R1024G). This variant had not been described before.

In a girl with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy (patient 1), Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the CACNA1C gene (V1363M). The variant was absent from the gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes databases. It affected a highly conserved residue in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV, and structural modeling suggested that this residue faces the pore-forming sixth helix. The authors concluded that the substitution likely disrupts channel gating. The patient also carried a de novo deletion of MSH2 exons 11-16. ⚠︎

Bozarth et al. (2018) used a 120-gene cardiomyopathy panel to identify a heterozygous splice site variant in the CACNA1C gene (c.3717+1_3717+2insA) in a girl with tetralogy of Fallot and left ventricular noncompaction (patient 2). The variant was inherited from her affected father (patient 3) and was absent from population databases. It was predicted to destroy the canonical splice donor site of intron 28. Because no mRNA studies were done, it was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, although the authors proposed that haploinsufficiency could explain the phenotype. The proband also carried a heterozygous ALPK3 splice variant and a TPM1 I130T variant; her father did not carry the TPM1 variant. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous CACNA1C variants in 25 individuals from 22 families who were ascertained through GeneMatcher and ClinVar. The variants were found by exome sequencing or an intellectual disability gene panel. Thirteen unrelated individuals had missense variants, 1 had an in-frame deletion, and 11 individuals from 8 families had predicted truncating variants. All nontruncating variants occurred de novo, as did all but 3 of the truncating variants. None of the variants were present in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or the Exome Variant Server. All missense variants affected highly conserved positions and were predicted to be damaging. The missense variants clustered in a linker segment of domain II (exon 13) and in domain IV (exon 35). The de novo nontruncating variants were F166L, K177R, R324W, V403M, L601R, M611T, L614P, L614R, L657F, I743del, V1187A, L1408V, and V1411L; V1411L was found in 2 unrelated patients. The de novo truncating variants were Q80fs, V996fs, R1377fs, and L1518fs. The paternally inherited truncating variants were R161X, W528X, and R1989X. A splice variant, c.2663+1G-C, was not inherited from the father. All truncating variants except 1, which lies in the last exon (exon 45), were predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay.

Rodan et al. (2021) studied 4 nontruncating variants with patch clamp studies, and the effects differed. With L1408V, current density in HEK293 cells fell to about 30% of control while protein expression was unchanged, consistent with loss of function. L657F significantly increased calcium current, with a hyperpolarizing shift in activation and about 2-fold higher protein levels, consistent with gain of function. L614P produced modest current increases with a hyperpolarizing shift in activation, but the increase was not statistically significant. The authors called this variant gain of function in the Results and neutral in the Discussion. L614R, studied with CACNB2, showed no statistically significant change in current or gating.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Rodan et al. (2021) observed that individuals with nontruncating CACNA1C variants had more severe global developmental delay or intellectual disability and more motor impairment. Epilepsy was also much more common in this group, affecting more than two-thirds. Individuals with truncating variants had more selective expressive language deficits, and autism was more common among them.

### Pathogenesis

Rodan et al. (2021) concluded that both gain and loss of CACNA1C function can cause a neurological phenotype. They noted that gnomAD constraint metrics are consistent with haploinsufficiency (pLI 1.0). Phenotypes were more severe with loss-of-function missense variants than with whole-gene or exonic deletions, and the authors suggested that this favors a dominant-negative effect. They cautioned that their functional studies used only the canonical transcript and that the choice of accessory subunit may have affected the results.
