### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.33.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures is a CACNA1C-related disorder with mainly neurological features. These vary between patients and include developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, ataxia, and epilepsy. The classic features of Timothy syndrome and long QT syndrome are usually absent, and significant cardiac abnormalities are rare (Rodan et al., 2021).

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She did not have QT prolongation.

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (patient 1) born to nonconsanguineous parents of English (maternal) and Ukrainian (paternal) descent. Her seizures began within a few hours of birth, with many subtle tonic seizures each day as well as prolonged tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures. She later had epileptic tonic spasms and focal seizures with eye blinking and facial twitching. Her epilepsy was drug-resistant and was classified as an epileptic encephalopathy. EEG showed diffuse excessive fast activity and high-voltage occipital sharp wave discharges. She had profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, made no developmental progress, and could not visually track or fixate at 18 months. Other findings included the following: neonatal hypoglycemia; short birth length; laryngomalacia with stridor; severe sleep-wake cycle disturbance with central and obstructive sleep apnea; bilateral clubfeet; bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly; camptodactyly; micrognathia; downturned corners of the mouth; frontal bossing; very scant hair; flared nares; bilaterally inverted nipples; and mild optic nerve hypoplasia. She also had severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, anal stenosis, and severe dysphagia requiring gastrostomy. Brain MRI, echocardiogram, and Holter monitoring were normal. Bozarth et al. (2018) noted that she shared several features with Timothy syndrome, including cutaneous syndactyly, dysmorphic features, and sleep disturbance, but had no cardiac abnormalities. She differed from previously reported patients in having severe refractory neonatal-onset epilepsy, no neurodevelopmental progress, and a recognizable EEG pattern of diffuse fast activity.

Bozarth et al. (2018) also reported a family of Irish and German descent in which a CACNA1C splice site variant of uncertain significance was found. The proband (patient 2) was a girl with tetralogy of Fallot, which was surgically repaired at 2 months of age. She also had acute severe biventricular failure and left ventricular noncompaction. Her dysmorphic features included a flat nasal bridge, low-set ears, cupping of the superior helices, deep-set eyes with shallow orbits, a thin upper lip vermilion, and a round face. She also had mild bilateral 2-3-4 toe syndactyly. At 8 months she had mild developmental delays. By 12 months she spoke 10 words and first walked, and she had mild diffuse hypotonia. Her father (patient 3) had a congenital ventricular septal defect and aortic valve stenosis, which were repaired at 2.5 months of age. He also had learning disabilities requiring an individualized education program and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder. By 18 years he had developed seizures with left-sided numbness that evolved to generalized tonic-clonic seizures. His craniofacial features resembled his daughter's: low-set ears, a thin upper lip vermilion, premaxillary underdevelopment, and a round face. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants and mainly neurological manifestations. Their ages at last report ranged from 10 months to 40 years. Features varied and included developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, ataxia, and epilepsy. Classic features of Timothy syndrome and long QT syndrome were absent. Only 1 individual (P13) was known to have cardiac disease: a prolonged QT interval with T-wave alternans. Fourteen individuals carried de novo nontruncating variants, and their developmental delay and motor impairment were more severe than in those with truncating variants. About half of the nontruncating group were nonverbal and nonambulatory. In this group, 12 of 14 were hypotonic, 10 of 14 had epilepsy, and 12 of 14 had orthopedic abnormalities.

In the nontruncating group reported by Rodan et al. (2021), seizure types varied: - absence and generalized tonic-clonic seizures with onset at 6 years (P1) - fever-sensitive focal motor and absence seizures beginning at 11 months (P2) - focal seizures from 8 years (P3) - West syndrome with infantile spasms from 7 months (P6) - myoclonic and generalized tonic-clonic seizures from 10 months (P7) - fever-sensitive generalized epilepsy from 1 year (P8) - behavioral arrest spells with or without head or limb jerking from 6 years (P9) - febrile seizures from 1 year (P11) - myoclonic seizures evolving to generalized epilepsy from 6 months (P12) - infantile spasms evolving to Lennox-Gastaut syndrome from 11 months (P13) - generalized epilepsy from 2 months (P14) P4, P5, and P10 had no seizures. Intellectual disability was severe in P1, P12, and P13, moderate to severe in P9, and mild in P5. P2 and P3 had learning disability, which in P3 included dyslexia. Abnormal balance or coordination was noted in P2, P5, P6, and P7. Tremor occurred as a resolved action tremor of the arms in P4 and as an intention tremor of the arms with head tremor in P5. P10 had leg spasticity, and P8 had autism. Orthopedic findings were: - bilateral clubfoot (P7, P10) - adducted thumbs (P7, P10, P14) - congenital hip dysplasia (P2, P14) - left hip dislocation with right hip subluxation (P4) - pes planovalgus (P1, P5) - joint hypermobility (P3, P4, P5) - kyphosis (P8) - kyphoscoliosis and coxa valga (P12) - camptodactyly of the fingers with prominent heels in small-appearing feet (P10) - tapering fingers (P9, P10) - cutaneous 2-3 toe syndactyly with mild 3-4 finger syndactyly and congenital torticollis (P11) - bilateral 2-3 toe syndactyly (P14) Eye findings included strabismus (P3, P4, P7, P12), esotropia (P11), hyperopia (P3, P8, P10, P11), myopia (P5), and cortical visual impairment (P13). Apart from P13's QT findings, P4 had a small interatrial communication with left-to-right shunt. P12 had a possible ventricular conduction delay and trace aortic insufficiency.

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating variants. Of these, 10 had delayed expressive language with largely normal early motor development, 5 had autism, and only 1 had seizures. Individual findings were as follows: - P15, a 15-year-old girl, had language delay, autism, attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), an IQ in the low-normal range, Chiari I malformation, and scoliosis, without motor delay or seizures. - P16a, a 6-year-old boy, had motor delay, hypotonia, abnormal balance, high-frequency tremor, speech limited to 2 words, autism, mild to moderate intellectual disability, and large ears. - P16b, a 33-year-old man from the same family, had language delay and learning disability with normal IQ. - P17, a 14.5-year-old girl, had language delay and borderline intellectual disability. - P18, a 16-year-old boy, had abnormal balance, language delay, ADHD, moderate intellectual disability, pes planus, genu valgum, mildly broad thumbs, hypoplastic fifth fingernails, broad halluces, and hypoplastic fifth toes. - P19, a 6-year-old boy, walked at 18 months and had abnormal balance, language delay, and ADHD with normal cognition. - P20, a 4-year-old girl, had language delay, autism, and moderate global developmental delay without motor delay. - P21, a 40-year-old woman, walked at 26 months and had language delay, moderate intellectual disability, and strabismus. - In family P22, 2 boys aged 2 and 3 years had hypotonia, autism, and speech limited to a few words. The younger also had metatarsus adductus, and the older had abnormal balance and intention tremor. A 32-year-old man in the same family had learning disability and was the only individual in the truncating group with a history of seizures. He was seizure-free without treatment at the time of the report.

### Inheritance

This disorder shows autosomal dominant inheritance. In the patients reported by Kosaki et al. (2018) and Bozarth et al. (2018), the heterozygous CACNA1C mutations occurred de novo. In the cohort of Rodan et al. (2021), all nontruncating variants and all but 3 truncating variants occurred de novo. The 3 inherited truncating variants segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in their families, with variable penetrance and expressivity. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis in a 5-year-old girl with NEDHLSS. They identified a de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the CACNA1C gene, arg1024-to-gly (R1024G), which had not been described before.

Bozarth et al. (2018) performed trio exome sequencing in a girl with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, hypotonia, and syndactyly. They identified a de novo heterozygous c.4087G-A transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a val1363-to-met (V1363M) substitution. The variant was absent from gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes, affected a highly conserved residue, and was predicted to be damaging by in silico tools. The authors classified it as likely pathogenic. As an additional finding, the patient also carried a de novo heterozygous terminal deletion of MSH2 exons 11 to 16, which was considered pathogenic for Lynch syndrome.

In the family with cardiac and neurodevelopmental features reported by Bozarth et al. (2018), the proband (patient 2) carried a heterozygous CACNA1C splice site variant (c.3717+1_3717+2insA) inherited from her affected father (patient 3). She also carried a heterozygous splice site variant in the ALPK3 gene (c.5105+2dupT) and a heterozygous missense variant in the TPM1 gene (I130T). The TPM1 variant was absent in her father. The significance of these variants was considered uncertain. Bozarth et al. (2018) proposed that splice site variants and haploinsufficiency might account for a Timothy syndrome phenotype, pending mRNA studies. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous CACNA1C variants in 25 individuals from 22 families with mainly neurological manifestations. There were 13 missense variants and 1 in-frame deletion (see, e.g., I743del), plus truncating variants in 11 individuals from 8 families. All nontruncating variants and all but 3 truncating variants occurred de novo. The 3 inherited truncating variants segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families, with variable penetrance and expressivity. In 1 individual (P18), a splice site variant was shown not to be paternal, but the maternal sample was unavailable for testing. The missense variants were spread throughout the protein but clustered in a linker segment of domain II (exon 13) and of domain IV (exon 35). None of the variants was present in public databases, including gnomAD. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) performed patch-clamp studies of selected variants in HEK293 cells. The L1408V variant reduced current density to about 30% of wildtype without changing protein expression, indicating loss of function. The L657F variant increased current density and protein level about 2-fold, indicating gain of function. L614P caused a modest current increase that was not statistically significant, and L614R had no significant effect.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kosaki et al. (2018) stated that, to their knowledge, their patient was the first with syndactyly and a CACNA1C mutation who did not have QT prolongation. QT prolongation had long been considered an obligatory feature of Timothy syndrome.

Rodan et al. (2021) found that the 14 individuals with de novo nontruncating CACNA1C variants had more severe developmental delay and motor impairment than the 11 individuals with truncating variants. About half of the nontruncating group were nonverbal and nonambulatory, 12 of 14 were hypotonic, 10 of 14 had epilepsy, and 12 of 14 had orthopedic abnormalities. Among those with truncating variants, 10 had delayed expressive language with largely normal early motor development, 5 had autism, and only 1 had seizures.

### Pathogenesis

Bozarth et al. (2018) used a predicted 3D structure of Cav1.2 to place V1363 in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV. In this model, the residue faces the pore-forming sixth helix and is in direct contact with the residue mutated in A1473G. They suggested that substituting the larger methionine likely disrupts the sixth helix and affects channel gating or calcium transmission. They also suggested that Cav1.2 expression in the thalamus, hippocampus, and suprachiasmatic nucleus may underlie the seizures and disturbed circadian rhythm seen in some patients with CACNA1C-related disorders. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) suggested that both gain and loss of CACNA1C function can cause a neurological phenotype. They noted that CACNA1C is constrained against loss-of-function variants (pLI 1.0). They proposed that the phenotype is more severe with loss-of-function missense variants than with whole-gene or exonic deletions, and that this would better support a dominant-negative effect.

### Diagnosis

Bozarth et al. (2018) proposed that CACNA1C be included on early-onset epilepsy gene panels.

### Clinical Management

The patient with the V1363M mutation reported by Bozarth et al. (2018) did not respond to zonisamide or to multiple other antiepileptic drugs, including vigabatrin, levetiracetam, and lacosamide. Clobazam significantly reduced her seizure burden.
