### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.33.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is a mainly neurological disorder. Features vary and include developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, ataxia, and epilepsy. Significant cardiac manifestations and the other classic features of Timothy syndrome are absent. Orthopedic abnormalities are common among individuals with nontruncating variants and include clubfoot, finger contractures, adducted thumbs, hip dysplasia, kyphoscoliosis, and joint hypermobility. Individuals with truncating variants tend to have a milder phenotype that mainly affects expressive language (Rodan et al., 2021).

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. Unlike patients with Timothy syndrome, she did not have prolongation of the QT interval.

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (Patient 1) born at 38 weeks' gestation to nonconsanguineous parents of English (maternal) and Ukrainian (paternal) ancestry. Her birth length was 40.6 cm (z-score -3.92). After birth she had hypoglycemia, profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubfeet, bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, and camptodactyly. Dysmorphic features were micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, very scant hair, and bilaterally inverted nipples. She also had mild optic nerve hypoplasia, severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, and anal stenosis. Severe dysphagia required a gastrostomy tube at 8 months. At 18 months she had made no developmental progress, had profound hypotonia, could not visually track or fixate, and had severe disturbances of the sleep-wake cycle. Her seizures began within a few hours of birth, with numerous subtle tonic seizures as well as prolonged tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures each day. Epileptic tonic spasms were diagnosed at 3 months. The seizures did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs, but clobazam substantially reduced the seizure burden. Her first EEG, at 5 days of life, showed distinctive diffuse excessive fast activity, and this pattern persisted beyond the neonatal period. Brain MRI at 7 days and 12 months, MR spectroscopy, echocardiogram, 24-hour Holter monitoring, and an extensive biochemical workup were normal. Polysomnography at 5 months showed severe central and obstructive sleep apnea. The authors noted that she had many hallmark features of Timothy syndrome, including cutaneous syndactyly, dysmorphic features, and sleep disturbances, but no cardiac abnormalities.

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals (13 male and 12 female) from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants and a mainly neurological phenotype. Ages at the most recent follow-up ranged from 10 months to 40 years. The phenotype variably included developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, epilepsy, and ataxia. Significant cardiac manifestations and the other features of Timothy syndrome were absent. Fourteen unrelated individuals had de novo nontruncating variants. All 14 had developmental delay or intellectual disability. At the last follow-up, 9 were nonverbal and 6 were nonambulatory. Twelve were hypotonic and 4 had abnormal balance and coordination (ataxia). One had a formal diagnosis of autism and 6 others had autistic features. The developmental course was consistent with a static neurodevelopmental disorder; 1 individual regressed in the context of infantile spasms. Prenatal findings included reduced fetal movement (3 of 14), bilateral clubfoot (2 of 14), and oligohydramnios (2 of 14).

Ten of the 14 individuals with nontruncating variants had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years of age. Both generalized and focal epilepsies occurred, including West syndrome/infantile spasms that evolved into Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. Seizures were fever-sensitive in 3 individuals and medically refractory in 4. EEG findings were not consistent across the cohort; 1 individual had excessive diffuse fast (beta) activity. Brain MRI was normal apart from an incidental arachnoid cyst in 1 individual and prominent cerebellar folia in another. Orthopedic abnormalities were present in 12 of 14 individuals. Two had bilateral clubfoot and congenital finger contractures, and 2 others had persistently adducted thumbs. Other orthopedic findings were kyphosis or scoliosis (2 of 14), congenital hip dysplasia (3 of 14), syndactyly (2 of 14), and joint hypermobility (3 of 14). Additional variable features were leg spasticity (1 of 14), tremor (2 of 14), hyperopia (4 of 14), and strabismus (4 of 14). Two individuals were microcephalic, and 3 had short stature, including the 2 with clubfoot. Facial dysmorphisms were minor and not consistent. One individual (P13) had prolonged QT. Another had a possible ventricular conduction delay, and Holter monitoring in that individual showed only premature ventricular contractions. The other 9 individuals who had ECGs had normal results.

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating CACNA1C variants. Most had essentially normal early motor development and walked independently. Ten of 11 had delayed expressive language. Five of 11 had autism, and 3 of these were nonverbal. Only 1 had a history of seizures, and 3 had hypotonia. Four had poor balance or coordination, 2 had tremor, and 1 had paroxysmal dyskinesia; another had a paroxysmal movement disorder in infancy that resolved. Four of 11 had recurrent infections, including 1 with hypogammaglobulinemia. ECGs, performed in 5 individuals, were normal.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous CACNA1C mutations identified in patients with NEDHLSS by Kosaki et al. (2018), Bozarth et al. (2018), and Rodan et al. (2021) occurred de novo in most patients. In the cohort of Rodan et al. (2021), 3 truncating variants were inherited and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families, which is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Penetrance and expressivity varied among family members. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-year-old girl with syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension but no QT prolongation, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene, arg1024-to-gly (R1024G).

In a girl (Patient 1) with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, syndactyly, and other congenital anomalies, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.4087G-A transition in exon 33 of the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a val1363-to-met (V1363M) substitution. The variant affected a highly conserved residue, was absent from gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes, and was predicted by in silico tools to be damaging. Structural modeling placed V1363 in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV, facing the pore-forming sixth helix. Exome sequencing in this patient also identified a de novo heterozygous terminal deletion of MSH2 exons 11 through 16, which had previously been reported as pathogenic for Lynch syndrome. Bozarth et al. (2018) also reported a family (Patients 2 and 3) with a heterozygous CACNA1C splice site variant (c.3717+1_3717+2insA), found on a cardiomyopathy panel and inherited from the affected father. The daughter had tetralogy of Fallot and left ventricular noncompaction, and the father had congenital heart defects, learning disabilities, and late-onset epilepsy. The authors considered this family's phenotype most consistent with Timothy syndrome. Because functional mRNA studies had not been done, they classified the splice variant as a variant of uncertain significance.

In 25 individuals from 22 families with a mainly neurological phenotype, Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous variants in the CACNA1C gene by exome sequencing or an intellectual disability gene panel. Thirteen unrelated individuals had missense variants and 1 had an in-frame deletion; all 14 of these nontruncating variants occurred de novo. Eleven individuals from 8 families had predicted truncating variants, including 1 canonical splice site variant. All but 3 of the truncating variants occurred de novo, and the 3 inherited variants segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families. None of the variants was found in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or the Exome Variant Server. All missense variants affected highly conserved positions and were predicted to be damaging. One truncating variant was in exon 45 of 45 and might escape nonsense-mediated decay; all other truncating variants were predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay. Four missense variants were studied by whole-cell patch clamp in HEK293 cells, and they had differing effects on channel function. L1408V caused loss of function. L657F caused gain of function, with increased protein expression. L614P produced modest current increases that were not statistically significant. L614R had no statistically significant effect.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Rodan et al. (2021) observed phenotypic differences between individuals with nontruncating and truncating CACNA1C variants. As a group, individuals with nontruncating (missense or in-frame deletion) variants, all of which were de novo, had more severe global developmental delay or intellectual disability and more severe motor impairment. About half were nonverbal and nonambulatory, and epilepsy was present in more than two-thirds. Individuals with truncating variants had more selective developmental abnormalities involving expressive language, and early motor development was essentially normal in all but 1. Autism was more common in this group (5 of 11) and seizures were rare. Missense variants clustered in linker segments of domain II (exon 13) and domain IV (exon 35).

### Pathogenesis

Rodan et al. (2021) concluded that both gain-of-function and loss-of-function CACNA1C variants can result in a neurological phenotype. For missense variants with loss of function on patch clamp studies, it was not known whether the mechanism was haploinsufficiency or a dominant-negative effect. The authors noted that the more severe neurodevelopmental and epilepsy phenotype in patients with missense, predicted loss-of-function variants, compared with patients with whole-gene or exonic deletions, would better support a dominant-negative effect. They also noted that the functional studies used only the canonical transcript and different beta subunits (CACNB3 vs CACNB2), which could have influenced the results, including the apparently neutral effect of some variants.
