### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene (601906) on chromosome 12q13.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation (SHFM) is a limb malformation with central reductions of the hands and/or feet. Elliott and Evans (2006) noted that it is genetically heterogeneous, shows variable expressivity and reduced penetrance, and can occur in isolation, in syndromes, or with other distal limb anomalies such as polydactyly and/or syndactyly. Most familial cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner (Gül and Oktenli, 2002). SHFM6 is a form of the malformation that shows autosomal recessive inheritance (Ugur and Tolun, 2008; Blattner et al., 2010).

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split foot in 2 sibs.

Verma et al. (1976) described a consanguineous, intermarried family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. The parents of each sibship were first cousins.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 females, sisters and half sisters, had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Hennekam and Lommen (1988) wrote about ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split hand/foot malformation.

Gül and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 individuals with split hand/split foot malformation in an inbred kindred. The malformation pattern varied in expression within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. Twelve affected members had central reductions of the feet with or without hand involvement. Another affected member had the mildest phenotype and atypical SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) reported a patient with sporadic SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with split-hand/foot malformation, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. The patient and all her family members had normal bone density and were not obese, unlike some patients with WNT10B variants. The authors suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated findings of SHFM6. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Freire-Maia (1971) described a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it means for genetic counseling. Verma et al. (1976) found affected individuals in 2 sibships of a consanguineous family, each born to first-cousin parents. They concluded that their report, together with earlier reports including that of Freire-Maia (1971), further showed that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait.

Mufti and Wood (1987) concluded that autosomal recessive inheritance seemed likely in their extended family with affected sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) discussed the possibility of a rare autosomal recessive form of split hand/foot malformation. As an alternative, they proposed a 2-locus model. In this model, a gene at another locus controls the dominant mutation that causes split hand/foot malformation, and a dominant mutation at the controlling locus causes nonpenetrance of the split hand/foot mutation.

Gül and Oktenli (2002) noted that most familial cases of SHFM are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. They discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a 2-locus model to explain the malformation in their inbred kindred with 9 affected members.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) and Blattner et al. (2010) found homozygous WNT10B mutations in patients with SHFM. In the family reported by Blattner et al. (2010), 9 heterozygous relatives were unaffected. Blattner et al. (2010) concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait and may be recessively inherited. In that case the recurrence risk is 25% for sibs and very low for offspring of patients, unless there is consanguinity. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Ugur and Tolun (2008) used homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM and identified a novel SHFM locus at 12q13.11-q13, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47.

### Heterogeneity

Ugur and Tolun (2008) tested the kindred for linkage to the 5 known SHFM loci and excluded 4 of them. At the remaining locus, TP63 (SHFM4), most affected subjects carried a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) at the alternate promoter used to transcribe the N-terminally truncated p63 isoform.

### Molecular Genetics

Ugur and Tolun (2008) used a candidate gene approach in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM and found a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene (R332W). The mutation was present in all affected members except the one with an atypical phenotype, and it was also present in 1 asymptomatic female. The authors proposed 2 explanations: either a second locus contributes to the SHFM phenotype, or a suppressor locus prevented the phenotype in the nonpenetrant female.

In a patient with sporadic SHFM, Blattner et al. (2010) found a homozygous 4-bp duplication in the WNT10B gene that causes a premature termination codon. Nine heterozygous relatives showed no sign of SHFM. The authors recommended WNT10B mutation testing in patients with sporadic SHFM to determine the recurrence risk.

In an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows, Kantaputra et al. (2018) found a homozygous 3-bp deletion in the WNT10B gene (c.695_697delACA). The deletion was predicted to remove asn232 (N232del) without shifting the reading frame. Protein modeling suggested that removing this critical residue would severely affect protein structure and function, likely shortening the protein's lifetime and preventing it from binding its receptors and other ligands.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPLIT-HAND/FOOT MALFORMATION 6**  
WNT10B, ARG332TRP [p.R332W]

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM6 and found a homozygous arg332-to-trp (R332W) mutation in the WNT10B gene. Twelve affected members, who had central foot reductions with or without hand involvement, carried the mutation. One affected member with the mildest, atypical phenotype did not carry it, and 1 asymptomatic female was homozygous for it. The authors proposed either a contributing second locus or a suppressor locus in the nonpenetrant female.
