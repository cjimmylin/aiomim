### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene (601906) on chromosome 12q13.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation (SHFM) is a limb malformation that affects the central rays of the hands and/or feet (Blattner et al., 2010). Gül and Oktenli (2002) described it as absence of the central digital rays, a deep median cleft, and syndactyly of the remaining digits, and noted that most familial cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. SHFM6 is a form of the malformation with autosomal recessive inheritance (Ugur and Tolun, 2008; Blattner et al., 2010).

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split foot in 2 sibs.

Verma et al. (1976) described an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. The parents of each sibship were first cousins.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Hennekam and Lommen (1988) also reported ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split hand/foot malformation.

Gül and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 individuals with SHFM in an inbred kindred. Expression of the malformation varied within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. Twelve affected members had central reductions of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands. One other affected member had the mildest phenotype and atypical SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) reported a patient with sporadic SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. The patient and all her family members had normal bone density and were not obese. The authors suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be features associated with SHFM6.

### Inheritance

Freire-Maia (1971) described a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it means for genetic counseling. Verma et al. (1976) reported affected sibships born to first-cousin parents. They concluded that their report, together with earlier reports, further showed that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait.

In their family of sisters and half sisters with ectrodactyly, Mufti and Wood (1987) concluded that an autosomal recessive gene was the likely mode of inheritance.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) considered that a rare autosomal recessive form of SHFM might exist. As an alternative, they proposed a 2-locus model. In this model, the dominant mutation causing split hand/foot malformation is controlled by a gene at another locus, and a dominant mutation at the controlling locus leads to nonpenetrance of the split hand/foot mutation. Gül and Oktenli (2002) discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a 2-locus model for their inbred kindred.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) found a homozygous WNT10B mutation in a consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. One asymptomatic female also carried the mutation, and one mildly affected member did not. The authors proposed that either a second locus contributes to manifestation of the phenotype or a suppressor locus prevented manifestation in the nonpenetrant female.

Blattner et al. (2010) found a homozygous WNT10B mutation in a patient with sporadic SHFM. Nine heterozygous relatives had no sign of the malformation. The authors concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait. In recessive cases the recurrence risk is 25% for sibs and very low for offspring of patients, unless there is consanguinity. They recommended testing patients with sporadic SHFM for WNT10B mutations to determine the recurrence risk.

### Mapping

Ugur and Tolun (2008) performed homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. They identified a novel SHFM locus at 12q13.11-q13, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47.

### Heterogeneity

Ugur and Tolun (2008) tested the 5 known SHFM loci for linkage in their kindred and excluded 4 of them. In the TP63 gene (SHFM4), they found a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) in most affected subjects. The variant lies in the alternate promoter used to transcribe the N-terminally truncated p63 isoform.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, Ugur and Tolun (2008) used a candidate gene approach after homozygosity mapping. They found a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene (R332W). All affected members carried the mutation except one with an atypical, mild phenotype. One asymptomatic female was also homozygous for it. The authors stated that this was the first WNT10B mutation reported in limb development and the first recessive mutation reported in SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) examined WNT10B in a patient with sporadic SHFM. They found a homozygous 4-bp duplication that results in a premature termination codon. Nine heterozygous relatives showed no sign of SHFM.

In an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows, Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in the WNT10B gene (c.695_697delACA). The deletion was predicted to remove asn232 in frame (Asn232del). Protein modeling suggested that losing this residue at a critical position would severely affect protein structure and function. The deletion was likely to shorten the protein's lifetime and prevent it from binding its receptors and other ligands.
