### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene (601906) on chromosome 12q13.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation (SHFM) is a limb malformation that affects the central rays of the autopod. It is characterized by absence of the central digital rays, a deep median cleft, and syndactyly of the remaining digits (Gul and Oktenli, 2002; Ugur and Tolun, 2008). Most familial cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner (Gul and Oktenli, 2002). SHFM6 is an autosomal recessive form of the malformation (Ugur and Tolun, 2008; Blattner et al., 2010).

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split-foot malformation in 2 sibs.

Freire-Maia (1971) reported a recessive form of ectrodactyly.

Verma et al. (1976) described an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. Both sibships were the offspring of first-cousin marriages.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Hennekam and Lommen (1988) also reported on ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split hand/foot malformation.

Gul and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 cases of split hand/split foot malformation in an inbred kindred. The malformation pattern varied within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. Twelve affected members had central reductions of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands. One further affected member had the mildest phenotype and atypical SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) reported a patient with sporadic SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. The patient and all of her family members had normal bone density and were not obese, unlike some patients with WNT10B variants. The authors suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated findings of SHFM6.

### Inheritance

Freire-Maia (1971) reported a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it implies for genetic counseling. Verma et al. (1976) concluded that their consanguineous family, in which the affected sibships were born to first-cousin parents, further showed that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. They noted that this was also supported by the earlier reports of Ray and of Freire-Maia (1971).

Mufti and Wood (1987) concluded that an autosomal recessive gene was the likely mode of inheritance in the extended family they studied, in which ectrodactyly occurred in sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) discussed whether a rare autosomal recessive form of split hand/foot malformation exists. They also proposed an alternative 2-locus model, in which the dominant split hand/foot mutation is controlled by a gene at a second locus. In this model, a dominant mutation at the controlling locus would make the split hand/foot mutation nonpenetrant. Gul and Oktenli (2002) discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a 2-locus model for the inbred kindred they studied.

In the consanguineous kindred they studied, Ugur and Tolun (2008) found an asymptomatic female who was homozygous for the WNT10B mutation. They proposed two explanations: either a second locus contributes to manifestation of the SHFM phenotype, or a suppressor locus prevented the trait from manifesting in the nonpenetrant female.

Blattner et al. (2010) concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait and may instead show recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Ugur and Tolun (2008) used homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM and identified a novel SHFM locus at 12q13.11-q13. The maximum multipoint lod score was 5.47.

### Heterogeneity

Elliott and Evans (2006) noted that SHFM is genetically heterogeneous, with at least 5 loci identified. They also noted that variable expressivity, reduced penetrance, and accompanying distal limb anomalies make its phenotypic classification difficult. Using descriptive epidemiology, chi-square analysis, and discriminant function analysis, they identified phenotypic patterns associated with the mapped SHFM loci.

In their consanguineous kindred with SHFM6, Ugur and Tolun (2008) tested linkage to the 5 known SHFM loci and excluded 4 of them. At TP63 (SHFM4), most affected subjects carried a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) in the alternate promoter used to transcribe the N-terminally truncated p63 isotype.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM mapped to 12q13, Ugur and Tolun (2008) took a candidate gene approach and identified a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene (R332W). The mutation was present in all affected individuals except the one with atypical SHFM, and it was also present in an asymptomatic female. The authors stated that this was the first reported WNT10B mutation involved in the pathogenesis of limb development and the first reported recessive mutation in SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) examined WNT10B in a patient with sporadic SHFM and identified a homozygous 4-bp duplication that results in a premature termination codon. Nine relatives who were heterozygous for the duplication showed no sign of SHFM.

In an Indian girl with SHFM6, Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in the WNT10B gene (c.695_697delACA), predicted to cause an in-frame deletion of asparagine-232 (Asn232del). Based on protein modeling, they predicted that the deletion would severely affect protein structure and function and would likely shorten the protein's lifetime and prevent it from binding its receptors and other ligands.

### Diagnosis

Blattner et al. (2010) recommended that patients with sporadic SHFM be tested for WNT10B mutations to determine the recurrence risk.

### Clinical Management

Blattner et al. (2010) noted that sporadic SHFM may show recessive rather than dominant inheritance. In that case, the recurrence risk for sibs would be 25% rather than very low. The risk for offspring of patients would be very low unless there is consanguinity, rather than the generally estimated 30 to 50%.
