### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene on chromosome 12q13. ⚠︎

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation (SHFM) is a complex limb malformation that affects the central rays of the autopod (Ugur and Tolun, 2008). It is characterized by absence of the central digital rays, a deep median cleft, and syndactyly of the remaining digits (Gul and Oktenli, 2002). Most familial cases of SHFM are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner (Gul and Oktenli, 2002). SHFM6 is the autosomal recessive form. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split foot in 2 sibs.

Freire-Maia (1971) reported a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it meant for genetic counseling.

Verma et al. (1976) described an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. Both sibships were born to first-cousin marriages.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split-hand/foot malformation.

Gul and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 cases of SHFM in an inbred kindred. Expression of the malformation pattern varied within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. Twelve affected members had central reductions of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands. One further affected member had the mildest phenotype, which was atypical SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) reported a patient with sporadic SHFM. Nine relatives who were heterozygous for the familial mutation showed no sign of SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl who had SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. The patient and all of her family members had normal bone density and were not obese. The authors suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated findings of SHFM6.

### Inheritance

Verma et al. (1976) concluded that their consanguineous family, together with the reports of Ray (1970) and Freire-Maia (1971), showed that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Mufti and Wood (1987) also considered autosomal recessive inheritance likely in their family. ⚠︎

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) raised the possibility of a rare autosomal recessive form of SHFM. They also discussed an alternative 2-locus model, in which a dominant SHFM mutation is controlled by a gene at another locus and a dominant mutation at that controlling locus causes nonpenetrance. Gul and Oktenli (2002) discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a 2-locus model for their inbred kindred.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) found homozygous WNT10B mutation in a consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, but inheritance in this family appeared complex (see Molecular Genetics). Blattner et al. (2010) found a homozygous WNT10B mutation in a patient with sporadic SHFM whose heterozygous relatives were unaffected. They concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait and may show recessive inheritance. In that case the recurrence risk is 25% for sibs and very low for the offspring of patients, unless there is consanguinity.

### Mapping

By homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, Ugur and Tolun (2008) identified a novel SHFM locus at 12q13.11-q13, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47. Linkage analysis excluded 4 of the 5 known SHFM loci. At the TP63 locus (SHFM4), most affected individuals carried a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) in the alternate promoter used to transcribe the N-terminally truncated p63 isotype.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, Ugur and Tolun (2008) used a candidate gene approach within the 12q13 linkage region and identified a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene (R332W). The mutation was present in all affected individuals except the 1 with atypical SHFM. It was also present in 1 asymptomatic female. The authors proposed either that a second locus contributes to the SHFM phenotype or that a suppressor locus prevented the trait from appearing in the nonpenetrant female. They described this as the first reported WNT10B mutation in the pathogenesis of limb development and the first recessive mutation in SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) examined the WNT10B gene in a patient with sporadic SHFM. They identified a homozygous 4-bp duplication that was predicted to result in a premature termination codon. Nine heterozygous relatives had no sign of SHFM. The authors recommended that patients with sporadic SHFM be tested for WNT10B mutations to determine recurrence risk.

In an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows, Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in the WNT10B gene (c.695_697delACA), predicted to cause an in-frame deletion of asn232 (N232del). Protein modeling suggested that deleting this single amino acid at a critical position would severely affect protein structure and function. The authors suggested that the deletion would likely shorten the protein's lifetime and prevent it from binding its receptors and other ligands.

**.0001 SPLIT-HAND/FOOT MALFORMATION 6**  
WNT10B, ARG332TRP [p.R332W]

In a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, Ugur and Tolun (2008) identified a homozygous arg332-to-trp (R332W) mutation in the WNT10B gene. The mutation was found by candidate gene analysis within a locus mapped by homozygosity mapping to 12q13.11-q13 (maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47). It was present in all affected individuals except 1 with an atypical, mild phenotype. It was also present in 1 asymptomatic female, which the authors explained by a possible second contributing locus or a suppressor locus.
