### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene (601906) on chromosome 12q13.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation-6 is an autosomal recessive form of split-hand/foot malformation (ectrodactyly). Affected individuals have central reductions of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands, and the malformation pattern can vary within a family (Ugur and Tolun, 2008; Gul and Oktenli, 2002). Split-hand/foot malformation in general is genetically heterogeneous. Variable expressivity, reduced penetrance, and accompanying distal limb anomalies make phenotypic classification difficult (Elliott and Evans, 2006).

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split-foot malformation in 2 sibs.

Verma et al. (1976) described an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. Both sibships came from first-cousin marriages.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Hennekam and Lommen (1988) reported ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split hand/foot malformation.

Gul and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 cases of split hand/split foot malformation in an inbred kindred. The malformation pattern varied within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. Twelve affected members had central reductions of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands. One other affected member had the mildest phenotype and atypical SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. Unlike some patients with WNT10B variants, the patient and all of her family members had normal bone density and were not obese. The authors suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated findings of SHFM6. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Freire-Maia (1971) reported a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it means for genetic counseling. Verma et al. (1976) described affected sibships born to first-cousin parents. They concluded that their family, together with earlier reports, further showed that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Mufti and Wood (1987) observed ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters of an extended family and concluded that an autosomal recessive gene was the likely mode of inheritance.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) questioned whether a rare autosomal recessive form of split hand/foot malformation exists. As an alternative, they proposed a 2-locus model. In this model, a dominant split hand/foot mutation is controlled by a gene at a second locus, and a dominant mutation at the controlling locus makes the split hand/foot mutation nonpenetrant. Gul and Oktenli (2002) studied an inbred kindred with 9 affected members and discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a 2-locus model. They noted that most familial cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) found an asymptomatic female in their kindred who was homozygous for the WNT10B mutation, indicating incomplete penetrance. They proposed two explanations: either a second locus contributes to manifestation of the SHFM phenotype, or a suppressor locus prevented the trait from manifesting in the nonpenetrant female.

Blattner et al. (2010) concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait and may instead be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. In that case the recurrence risk for sibs is 25%, and the risk for offspring of patients is very low unless there is consanguinity.

### Mapping

Ugur and Tolun (2008) performed homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM and identified a novel SHFM locus at 12q13.11-q13. The maximum multipoint lod score was 5.47.

### Heterogeneity

Ugur and Tolun (2008) tested their kindred for linkage to the 5 known SHFM loci and excluded 4 of them. At TP63 (SHFM4), most affected subjects carried a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) in the alternate promoter used to transcribe the N-terminally truncated p63 isotype.

### Molecular Genetics

Ugur and Tolun (2008) used a candidate gene approach in a consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM mapped to 12q13. They identified a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene (R332W). The mutation was present in all affected individuals except the one with atypical SHFM, and it was also present in an asymptomatic female. The authors described this as the first reported WNT10B mutation involved in the pathogenesis of limb development and the first recessive mutation in SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) screened WNT10B in a patient with sporadic SHFM and found a homozygous 4-bp duplication that results in a premature termination codon. Nine heterozygous relatives had no sign of SHFM.

In an Indian girl with SHFM6, Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a novel homozygous 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delACA) in the WNT10B gene. The deletion was predicted to remove asparagine-232 in frame (Asn232del). Protein modeling suggested that deleting this single amino acid at a critical position likely severely affects protein structure and function, probably shortening the lifetime of the protein and preventing it from binding its receptors and other ligands.

### Diagnosis

Blattner et al. (2010) recommended testing patients with sporadic SHFM for WNT10B mutations to determine the recurrence risk.
