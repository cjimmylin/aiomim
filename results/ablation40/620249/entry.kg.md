### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myopathy-10B, mild variant (CMYO10B), is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene (612453) on chromosome 5q23.

### Description

Takayama et al. (2016) noted that mutations in the MEGF10 gene cause early-onset myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD). They also cause a milder phenotype of minicore myopathy. Features of the milder form include weakness beginning in infancy or childhood that is mainly axial and proximal, facial weakness, contractures, scoliosis, hypernasal speech, palatal abnormalities, and respiratory involvement. Muscle biopsy shows minicores and dystrophic features (Boyden et al., 2012).

### Nomenclature

Croci et al. (2022) used the term mvEMARDD for the milder, later-onset form of MEGF10-related myopathy. This form has been linked to missense variants affecting cysteines in the EGF-like domains of MEGF10.

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) described a non-consanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an autosomal recessive myopathy that resembled multiminicore disease in some respects. Weakness began by 12 months of age, first in the neck. It was mainly axial and proximal, included facial weakness, and stabilized in adolescence. The patients had neck contractures in infancy and elbow, knee, and heel cord contractures by adulthood. Serum CK was mildly elevated. All 3 had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion, and respiratory problems from age 10 to 11 years that were treated with nocturnal BiPAP. All 3 had hypernasal speech; 1 had a cleft palate and another had a midline palatal ridge. Muscle biopsies showed dystrophic features: myofibers replaced by fat, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers. They also showed moth-eaten fibers and minicores, which suggested a congenital myopathy, and a slight predominance of type I fibers. Rimmed vacuoles, inclusions, central cores, nemaline rods, and ragged red fibers were absent.

Boyden et al. (2012) also described a girl (subject 105-1), a Caucasian with distant Portuguese ancestry, who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease and had no affected relatives. At several weeks of age she had hypotonia with a weak neck, a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive from poor feeding, blue sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. In childhood she had frequent pulmonary infections but no chronic breathing problems. Serum CK was 302 U/L at age 10, and her scoliosis had progressed to 20 degrees by age 12. Muscle biopsy showed minicores, which were confirmed by electron microscopy.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, together with facial weakness and dysphagia. In his 50s, while still ambulant, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to healthy consanguineous parents. He was born at 33 weeks' gestation, needed resuscitation for asphyxia at birth, and had a weak suck. Elbow contractures and scoliosis were present from the first months of life. He had no motor developmental delay or intellectual disability. He later developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement, and he needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years. On examination he had axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy and weakness, preserved facial expression, cleft palate, global areflexia, bilateral elbow and ankle contractures, equinovarus foot stance, and hyperlordosis. Serum CK, brain and spine MRI, ECG, and echocardiogram were normal. From age 17 he had swallowing difficulties and recurrent aspiration pneumonia. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: variation in fiber size, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later biopsy showed myofibrillar changes, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores. Electrophysiology showed normal conduction apart from reduced amplitudes of muscle evoked potentials. The authors proposed that the minicores seen in the late-onset group may result from a long-standing myofibrillar process that begins in infancy.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CMYO10B is autosomal recessive. Boyden et al. (2012) showed that compound heterozygous MEGF10 mutations were in trans and segregated with the disease in an affected sibship. Croci et al. (2022) found a homozygous MEGF10 mutation in a patient whose healthy consanguineous parents were both heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) performed a genomewide linkage scan in family 1030 and found positive LOD scores at 16 loci. The MEGF10 gene lay in a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible LOD score of 1.329. SEPN1, RYR1, ACTA1, and TPM3 were excluded.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 3 affected sibs of family 1030 with a myopathy resembling multiminicore disease, Boyden et al. (2012) used linkage-filtered whole genome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene. The mutations were C326R and C774R. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans and segregated with the disease. Both mutations were absent from public databases and from genotyped controls. Boyden et al. (2012) also sequenced MEGF10 in 190 patients with unexplained myopathies. One patient (subject 105-1) was heterozygous for an R71W mutation inherited from an unaffected parent. No second mutation was found by cDNA sequencing, and the patient had no SEPN1 or RYR1 mutations. The authors suggested that she may carry a second MEGF10 mutation they were unable to detect. In zebrafish, knockdown of megf10 produced a myopathic phenotype, which the authors took as support for the pathogenicity of the human mutations.

In a Japanese patient with the milder MEGF10 myopathy phenotype, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G) and a novel missense mutation (C810Y).

In a 66-year-old man with adult-onset myopathy and respiratory failure, Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One was a novel essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and the other was a novel missense variant (C118R). ⚠︎

In an Italian man with a progressive form of mild-variant EMARDD, Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation in the MEGF10 gene (C699S). The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both healthy consanguineous parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was classified as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. No functional studies were done.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that patients with MEGF10 missense mutations had a phenotype similar to, but milder than, that of patients with null mutations who have a severe congenital myopathy. They attributed this to possible residual MEGF10 function. Minicores were a prominent feature in their patients with missense mutations but had not been noted in families with null mutations.

Takayama et al. (2016) described 2 Japanese patients with MEGF10 myopathy. One had the severe EMARDD phenotype and a homozygous frameshift mutation (c.131_132del). The other had the milder phenotype and compound heterozygous mutations, including a cysteine missense mutation (C810Y). The authors noted that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain may be responsible for the milder phenotype, and they concluded that their findings indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed all 20 reported MEGF10 cases and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. In patients with at least 1 null mutation, respiratory dysfunction began in the first year of life. In patients with 2 missense mutations, it began at age 10 years or much later. The authors stated that their findings expanded the MEGF10 phenotype to include onset in the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that missense variants affecting cysteines in the EGF-like domains of MEGF10 had previously been linked to the milder, later-onset form, and null variants to severe EMARDD. Their patient, however, carried a homozygous missense variant yet had an early-onset, progressive myopathy. The authors proposed that MEGF10-related phenotypes form a continuum rather than 2 separate forms.

### Pathogenesis

Holterman et al. (2007) identified mouse Megf10 as a marker of quiescent satellite cells. Megf10 was upregulated more than 100-fold when satellite cells were activated. siRNA knockdown of Megf10 in activated satellite cells on isolated mouse muscle fibers caused precocious differentiation and a 62% loss of self-renewing Pax7+/MyoD- cells. The authors concluded that Megf10 acts on Notch signaling to balance proliferation and differentiation of satellite cells.

Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that loss-of-function MEGF10 missense mutations could cause excessive differentiation of satellite cells. This would deplete the population of self-renewing cells that regenerate muscle and could explain the dystrophic features. They suggested that satellite cell dysfunction is a pathogenic mechanism shared with SEPN1-related multiminicore disease.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish using 4 splice-blocking morpholinos. The morphants had curved tails, unhatched eggs, impaired motility, reduced birefringence, and disorganized somites with gaps between myofibers. Electron microscopy showed myofibrillar disorganization. The authors concluded that these findings supported the pathogenicity of the human mutations and pointed to a myopathic process.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS326ARG [c.976T>C; p.C326R]

In 3 affected sibs of a non-consanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) with a myopathy resembling multiminicore disease, Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One was a c.976T-C transition in exon 10, resulting in a cys326-to-arg (C326R) substitution at the fourth cysteine of EGF-like domain 6; this residue is conserved in 43 of 43 species. The other was C774R. The mutations were found by linkage-filtered whole genome sequencing. Sanger sequencing confirmed that they were in trans and segregated with the disease. The C326R mutation was absent from dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, the Exome Sequencing Project, and 715 genotyped controls.

**.0002 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS774ARG [c.2320T>C; p.C774R]

In 3 affected sibs of family 1030 with a myopathy resembling multiminicore disease, Boyden et al. (2012) found that the second MEGF10 mutation was a c.2320T-C transition in exon 19. It results in a cys774-to-arg (C774R) substitution at the sixth cysteine of EGF-like domain 16 and was in compound heterozygous state with C326R. It was absent from public databases and from 645 genotyped controls. The authors noted that the same mutation had been reported in compound heterozygous state with a frameshift mutation in a family with the more severe congenital myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia. ⚠︎

**.0003 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, ARG71TRP [c.211C>T; p.R71W]

Boyden et al. (2012) sequenced MEGF10 in 190 patients with unexplained myopathies. In a Caucasian girl of distant Portuguese ancestry (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease, they found a heterozygous c.211C-T transition in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in an arg71-to-trp (R71W) substitution in the emilin domain. The mutation was inherited from an unaffected parent and was absent from 699 controls. No second mutation was found by cDNA sequencing, and the patient had no SEPN1 or RYR1 mutations. The authors suggested that she may carry a second MEGF10 mutation that they were unable to detect.

**.0008 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS699SER [c.2096G>C; p.C699S]

In a 22-year-old Italian man, born to consanguineous parents, with a progressive form of mild-variant EMARDD, Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion (NM_001256545.2) in the MEGF10 gene, predicted to result in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. It was classified as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. No functional studies were done.
