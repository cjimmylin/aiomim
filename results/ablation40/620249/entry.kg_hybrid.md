### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10 (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene (612453) on chromosome 5q23. ⚠︎

### Description

Takayama et al. (2016) noted that MEGF10 mutations cause early-onset myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD), and also a milder phenotype, minicore myopathy. Croci et al. (2022) described the MEGF10-related phenotypic spectrum as variable. At the severe end are infantile forms in which patients are ventilator-dependent and die in childhood. At the milder end are chronic disorders with a more favorable course and later onset, classified as the mild variant (mvEMARDD). Patients with the mild variant may have only very mild muscle weakness and mild respiratory dysfunction, and some, but not all, have minicores on muscle biopsy.

### Nomenclature

Croci et al. (2022) referred to the milder chronic MEGF10-related disorders as the mild variant of EMARDD, or mvEMARDD. Boyden et al. (2012) had described the disorder in their patients as a recessive congenital myopathy with minicores that resembled multiminicore disease in some respects.

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) reported a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an autosomal recessive myopathy. Weakness appeared by 12 months of age, first in the neck. It was present throughout the body but was mainly axial and proximal, and it stabilized in adolescence. Facial weakness was also present. Neck contractures were seen in infancy, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords had developed by adulthood. Serum creatine kinase measured before age 10 years was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion. Beginning at age 10 to 11 years, all developed respiratory problems treated with nighttime bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation. One patient was born with a cleft palate that was surgically repaired. A second had a midline ridge on her palate in adulthood and tended to reflux liquids into her nose. All 3 had hypernasal speech. Muscle biopsies showed dystrophic features: myofibers replaced by fatty tissue, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers. They also showed moth-eaten fibers and minicores, which suggested a congenital myopathy. Rimmed vacuoles, inclusions, central cores, nemaline rods, and ragged red fibers were absent. Type I fibers slightly predominated and varied more in size than type II fibers. Before genetic testing, the diagnosis had been given as limb-girdle muscular dystrophy or spinal muscular atrophy.

Boyden et al. (2012) also described a Caucasian girl with distant Portuguese ancestry (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease; no other family member was affected. At several weeks of age she had hypotonia, first seen as a weak neck and floppy head. Other early findings were a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. As a child she had frequent acute pulmonary infections but no chronic breathing problems, and she breathed unassisted at night. Serum creatine kinase at age 10 years was mildly elevated at 302 U/L. Mild scoliosis noted at that age had progressed to 20 degrees by age 12 years. Throughout childhood she had mild, nonprogressive, mainly proximal weakness, including facial weakness. Muscle biopsy showed marked variation in fiber size, internalized nuclei, slightly increased endomysial connective tissue, and patches of decreased NADH activity. Electron microscopy confirmed minicores.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. In his fifties, while still ambulant, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to healthy consanguineous parents. He was born at 33 weeks' gestation, needed resuscitation for asphyxia, and had a weak suck. From the first months of life he had a congenital myopathic phenotype with elbow contractures and scoliosis. He had no motor developmental delay or intellectual disability. He developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement, and needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years. Examination showed axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy and weakness, preserved facial expression, cleft palate, global areflexia, and bilateral contractures of the elbows and ankles. He walked without support with an equinovarus-supinated foot stance, hyperlordosis, and pelvic tilt, but needed a manual wheelchair for longer trips. He underwent bilateral Achilles tendon tenotomy and vertebral arthrodesis. From age 17 years he had swallowing difficulties and recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration of food. Brain and spine MRI, ECG, echocardiogram, and serum creatine kinase were normal. Motor and sensory conduction was normal apart from reduced amplitude of the muscle evoked potential, and sensation was not impaired. A first muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: variation in fiber size, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later biopsy showed myofibrillar alterations, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores.

### Inheritance

Boyden et al. (2012) found autosomal recessive inheritance in family 1030: affected sibs were compound heterozygous for MEGF10 mutations that segregated with the disease. Takayama et al. (2016) also found compound heterozygous mutations in a patient with the milder phenotype. Croci et al. (2022) found a homozygous mutation in a patient born to consanguineous parents, both of whom were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) performed a genomewide SNP linkage scan in family 1030 and found positive lod scores at 16 loci. MEGF10 lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible lod score of 1.329. The SEPN1, RYR1, ACTA1, and TPM3 genes were excluded.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 3 affected sibs of family 1030 with a congenital myopathy with minicores, Boyden et al. (2012) combined linkage data with whole genome sequencing. They identified compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: C326R and C774R. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans and segregated with the disorder in all 6 family members. Both mutations were absent from dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, and the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project data, and from 715 and 645 genotyped controls, respectively. Each changes a cysteine in an atypical EGF-like domain that is conserved in 43 species, and the authors expected the changes to disrupt disulfide bonds. Morpholino knockdown of megf10 in zebrafish caused myopathic phenotypes, which the authors took as support for the pathogenicity of the human mutations.

Boyden et al. (2012) sequenced the full MEGF10 open reading frame in 190 patients with unexplained myopathies. One patient (subject 105-1), whose clinical and histologic features were similar to those of family 1030, carried a single heterozygous c.211C-T transition in exon 4, resulting in an arg71-to-trp (R71W) substitution in the emilin domain. Her unaffected parent also carried the mutation. It was absent from public databases and from 699 controls. Both alleles were expressed in muscle cDNA, and no second mutation or structural change was found. The authors suggested that she may carry a second MEGF10 mutation that they could not detect.

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a c.2981-2A-G splice site mutation and a novel missense mutation, cys810-to-tyr (C810Y).

In a 66-year-old man with adult-onset myopathy and respiratory failure, Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify 2 mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One was a novel essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T); the other was a novel missense mutation, c.352T-C, predicting a cys118-to-arg (C118R) substitution.

In an Italian man with a progressive form of mild-variant EMARDD, born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution at a conserved cysteine in an EGF-like domain. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the patient was homozygous and that both parents were carriers. The variant was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD and was predicted to be pathogenic by several in silico tools. The authors classified it as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria and noted that functional studies were needed.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that nonsense and frameshift mutations in MEGF10 had been reported to cause a severe congenital myopathy with diaphragmatic weakness, areflexia, and dysphagia. They described the phenotypes in their patients with missense mutations as generally similar but less severe. In the earlier families, MEGF10 expression was totally lost, and the authors suggested that the missense mutations in family 1030 may leave a small amount of residual MEGF10 function. Minicores were prominent in their patients with missense mutations but had not been noted in the families with null mutations. The authors also noted that one previously reported family had been compound heterozygous for C774R and a frameshift mutation.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported 2 Japanese patients with MEGF10 myopathy. One had the EMARDD phenotype and a homozygous frameshift mutation (c.131_132del). The other had the milder phenotype and was compound heterozygous for a splice site mutation and the C810Y missense mutation. The authors noted that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain may be responsible for the milder phenotype, and they concluded that their findings indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed all 20 reported MEGF10 cases and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. In patients with at least 1 null mutation, respiratory dysfunction began in the first year of life. In patients with 2 missense mutations, it began at age 10 years or much later. Their own patient extended the phenotype to include onset in the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that missense variants have been linked to later onset and a benign course. Their patient, however, was homozygous for a missense variant and had early-onset, progressive myopathy. A nonspecific first biopsy was followed by a later biopsy showing minicores. Another reported late-onset patient had nonspecific findings on the first and second biopsies and cores only on the third. On this basis, the authors proposed that the minicores marking the late-onset group may result from a long-standing myofibrillar process that begins in infancy. They also proposed a continuum of MEGF10-related phenotypes rather than a strict division between classic EMARDD and mvEMARDD.

### Pathogenesis

Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that the C326R and C774R mutations cause loss of MEGF10 function. They proposed that excessive differentiation of satellite cells could then deplete the self-renewing cells available to regenerate damaged muscle fibers, which might explain the dystrophic features in their patients' biopsies. They suggested that satellite cell dysfunction may be a pathogenic mechanism shared with SEPN1-related multiminicore disease.

### Diagnosis

Croci et al. (2022) suggested that myofibrillar alterations are a common finding in MEGF10 myopathy. They recommended testing the MEGF10 gene in patients with suspected myofibrillar myopathy who have childhood-onset progressive myopathy, scoliosis, contractures, and respiratory involvement.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish with 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. At 4 days postfertilization, morphants had curled or bent tails, impaired swimming, and reduced motility in response to touch; some eggs failed to hatch, and the highest dose of one morpholino was lethal. Morphants also had disrupted somite boundaries, reduced birefringence, and gaps between myofibers. On electron microscopy, their muscle showed widespread myofibrillar disorganization. The authors concluded that the motor impairment was due at least in part to a myopathic process.

MEGF10, CYS326ARG [c.976T>C; p.C326R]

In 3 affected sibs of a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family with a congenital myopathy with minicores (family 1030), Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One was a c.976T-C transition in exon 10, resulting in a cys326-to-arg (C326R) substitution in EGF-like domain 6. The other was C774R. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the 2 mutations were in trans and segregated with the disorder in all 6 family members. C326R was absent from dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, and Exome Sequencing Project data and from 715 controls. The altered cysteine is conserved in 43 species and is expected to form a disulfide bond.

**.0002 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**

In the 3 affected sibs of family 1030 with a congenital myopathy with minicores, Boyden et al. It was in trans with C326R and segregated with the disorder. The mutation was absent from public databases and from 645 controls, and the residue is conserved in 43 species.
