### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10 (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene (612453) on chromosome 5q23.

### Description

The mild variant of congenital myopathy-10 (CMYO10B) is an autosomal recessive myopathy. Affected individuals have axial, proximal and facial weakness, scoliosis, joint contractures, and respiratory involvement that begins in childhood or later. Muscle biopsy shows myopathic and dystrophic changes, often with minicores (Boyden et al., 2012; Harris et al., 2018). Mutations in the MEGF10 gene also cause a more severe early-onset myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD). Takayama et al. (2016) and Harris et al. (2018) described the minicore myopathy as the milder end of the MEGF10-related spectrum.

### Nomenclature

Croci et al. (2022) noted that the milder, chronic MEGF10-related disorders, which have a more favorable course and later onset, had been classified as the mild variant of EMARDD (mvEMARDD).

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) reported a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an unclassified autosomal recessive myopathy that resembled multiminicore disease in some respects. Weakness appeared by 12 months of age, first in the neck. It affected the whole body but was mainly axial and proximal, and it stabilized in adolescence. Facial weakness was also present. Neck contractures were seen in infancy, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords had developed by adulthood. Serum creatine kinase measured before age 10 years was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion. From age 10 to 11 years, all developed respiratory problems treated with nocturnal bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation. One patient had a cleft palate at birth, a second had a midline palatal ridge in adulthood, and all 3 had hypernasal speech. Muscle biopsies showed fatty replacement of myofibers, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers, which are dystrophic features. Biopsies also showed moth-eaten fibers and minicores, and there was a slight predominance of type I fibers. Rimmed vacuoles, inclusions, central cores, nemaline rods, and ragged red fibers were absent.

Boyden et al. (2012) also described a Caucasian female of distant Portuguese ancestry (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease and had a similar clinical and histologic presentation. She had hypotonia from several weeks of age, with a weak neck and floppy head. She also had a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. She had frequent pulmonary infections in childhood but no chronic breathing problems. Serum creatine kinase was 302 U/L at age 10 years. Mild scoliosis had progressed to 20 degrees by age 12. She had mild, nonprogressive, mainly proximal weakness that included facial weakness. Muscle biopsy showed minicores, and electron microscopy confirmed them as focal areas of Z-band disarray without mitochondria.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. In his fifties, while still ambulant, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins. The authors concluded that the phenotype of MEGF10 mutations extended to onset in the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents. He was born at 33 weeks' gestation and needed resuscitation for asphyxia. He had a weak suck, and elbow contractures and scoliosis were present from the first months of life. Motor development and intellect were normal. He developed slowly progressive weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, and dysphagia. Respiratory involvement required nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years, and from age 17 he had swallowing difficulties and recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration. Serum creatine kinase, brain and spine MRI, ECG, and echocardiogram were normal. Electrophysiologic studies were normal apart from reduced amplitude of the muscle evoked potential. On examination he had axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy and weakness, preserved facial expression, cleft palate, global areflexia, contractures of the elbows and ankles, and equinovarus foot posture with hyperlordosis. He had undergone Achilles tendon tenotomy and vertebral arthrodesis. A first muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: fiber size variation, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later biopsy showed myofibrillar changes, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores.

### Inheritance

Boyden et al. (2012) found autosomal recessive inheritance in family 1030, in which affected sibs were compound heterozygous for MEGF10 mutations and their parents were unaffected. Croci et al. (2022) reported a homozygous MEGF10 mutation in a patient whose consanguineous parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) performed genomewide linkage analysis in family 1030 using a recessive model and found 16 loci with positive LOD scores. MEGF10 lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible LOD score of 1.329.

### Molecular Genetics

Boyden et al. (2012) performed whole-genome sequencing in 1 affected member of family 1030 and filtered the variants using the linkage data. They identified 2 missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene, C326R and C774R, as compound heterozygous changes in the affected sibs. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans and segregated with the disorder in all 6 family members. Both mutations changed cysteines that were conserved in all 43 species examined. These cysteines lie in atypical EGF-like domains and are predicted to form disulfide bonds. The mutations were absent from public databases and from 715 and 645 genotyped controls, respectively. The authors screened the MEGF10 open reading frame in 190 further patients with unexplained myopathies and found only 1 heterozygous mutation, R71W, in subject 105-1. This mutation was inherited from an unaffected parent, and no second mutation was found, including by sequencing of muscle cDNA. In zebrafish, knockdown of megf10 caused a myopathic phenotype.

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G) and a novel missense mutation (C810Y).

By exome sequencing in a 66-year-old man with adult-onset myopathy and respiratory failure, Harris et al. (2018) identified 2 novel mutations in the MEGF10 gene: an essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a missense mutation (c.352T-C; C118R).

By exome sequencing in an Italian man with progressive congenital myopathy born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the MEGF10 gene (c.2096G-C; C699S). Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, and both parents were carriers. The variant was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD and affected a conserved cysteine in an EGF-like domain. In silico tools predicted it to be damaging, and the authors classified it as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that nonsense and frameshift mutations in MEGF10 had been reported in patients with a severe congenital myopathy with diaphragmatic weakness, areflexia, and dysphagia. One such family was compound heterozygous for the C774R mutation and a frameshift mutation. The phenotypes in those families were similar to, but more severe than, those in families 1030 and 105. The authors suggested that this difference fit with total loss of MEGF10 expression in the severe families compared with possible residual function of the misfolded missense proteins. Minicores were prominent in their patients with missense mutations but had not been noted in biopsies from families with null mutations.

Takayama et al. (2016) noted that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain of MEGF10 might be responsible for the milder phenotype, although the relationship was not conclusive. They described 2 Japanese patients. One had a homozygous frameshift mutation (c.131_132del) and the EMARDD phenotype. The other had the milder phenotype and was compound heterozygous for a splice site mutation and a cysteine missense mutation. The authors concluded that these patients indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed all 20 reported MEGF10 cases and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. Patients with at least 1 null mutation had onset of respiratory dysfunction in the first year of life. In patients with 2 missense mutations, respiratory dysfunction began at 10 years of age or much later.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that their patient carried a missense mutation yet had early-onset, progressive myopathy. A first biopsy was nonspecific, and a later biopsy showed minicores and myofibrillar changes. They proposed that minicores, which are typical of the later-onset group, may result from a long-standing myofibrillar process that begins in infancy. They also suggested that MEGF10-related phenotypes form a continuum rather than a strict split between classic EMARDD and mvEMARDD.

### Pathogenesis

Holterman et al. (2007) found that mouse Megf10 was expressed in quiescent and activated satellite cells. Silencing Megf10 on isolated myofibers reduced the number of activated satellite cells and caused them to differentiate too early. Knocking down Megf10 in MyoD-null myoblasts lowered expression of Notch receptors and of Hes1.

Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that loss of MEGF10 function leads to excessive differentiation of satellite cells, which would deplete the self-renewing cells needed to regenerate damaged muscle fibers. They suggested this could explain the dystrophic features in their patients' biopsies.

### Diagnosis

Croci et al. (2022) suggested testing MEGF10 in patients suspected of myofibrillar myopathy who have childhood-onset progressive myopathy, scoliosis, contractures, and respiratory involvement.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish with 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. The fish had curled or bent tails, impaired swimming, unhatched eggs, reduced birefringence, disrupted somite boundaries, gaps between myofibers, and myofibrillar disorganization, consistent with a myopathic process.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS326ARG [c.976T>C; p.C326R]

In 3 affected sibs from a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family (family 1030) with congenital myopathy with minicores (CMYO10B), Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One was a c.976T-C transition in exon 10, resulting in a cys326-to-arg (C326R) substitution in EGF-like domain 6. The other was C774R. The mutations were in trans and segregated with the disorder. C326R was absent from 715 genotyped controls, and the cysteine it changes is conserved in all 43 species examined.

**.0002 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS774ARG [c.2320T>C; p.C774R]

In affected members of family 1030 with CMYO10B, Boyden et al. (2012) found a c.2320T-C transition in exon 19 of the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys774-to-arg (C774R) substitution in EGF-like domain 16. It was compound heterozygous with C326R. The mutation was absent from 645 genotyped controls. Boyden et al. (2012) noted that C774R had also been found, in compound heterozygosity with a frameshift mutation, in a family with a more severe phenotype.

**.0003 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy (CMYO10B), Takayama et al. (2016) found a splice site mutation in the MEGF10 gene (c.2981-2A-G). It was compound heterozygous with a novel missense mutation, C810Y.

**.0006 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**

For discussion of the c.352T-C transition in the MEGF10 gene, which results in a cys118-to-arg (C118R) substitution, see Harris et al. (2018). They found it together with c.1426+1G-T in a 66-year-old man with CMYO10B.

**.0007 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS699SER [c.2096G>C; p.C699S; p.Cys699Ser]

In a 22-year-old Italian man with progressive congenital myopathy (CMYO10B), born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) identified a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion in the MEGF10 gene (NM_001256545.2), resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. It was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD, affected a conserved residue, and was predicted to be damaging by in silico tools.
