### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10B (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene (612453) on chromosome 5q23.2.

### Description

Takayama et al. (2016) stated that mutations in the MEGF10 gene cause early-onset myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD), as well as a milder phenotype of minicore myopathy. Before their report, 6 families with EMARDD and 1 family with the milder disorder had been described. The mild variant is a congenital myopathy that resembles multiminicore disease. Weakness begins in infancy or childhood and is mainly axial and proximal, and patients have facial weakness, contractures, scoliosis, hypernasal speech or palatal abnormalities, and respiratory involvement starting later than in the severe form. Muscle biopsies show minicores and dystrophic or myofibrillar changes (Boyden et al., 2012; Croci et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Croci et al. (2022) referred to the milder disorder as mvEMARDD. They proposed that MEGF10-related phenotypes form a continuum rather than splitting strictly into classic EMARDD and mvEMARDD.

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) reported a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an autosomal recessive myopathy that resembled multiminicore disease. Weakness began by 12 months of age, starting in the neck. It was mainly axial and proximal, included facial weakness, and stabilized in adolescence. Neck contractures appeared in infancy, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords had developed by adulthood. Serum creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion. From age 10 to 11 years, all 3 developed respiratory problems treated with nighttime bilevel positive airway pressure. All had hypernasal speech; 1 was born with a cleft palate, and another had a midline palatal ridge. Muscle biopsies had dystrophic features: myofibers replaced by fatty tissue, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers. The biopsies also showed moth-eaten fibers and minicores, suggesting a congenital myopathy. Rimmed vacuoles, inclusions, central cores, nemaline rods, and ragged red fibers were absent. Type I fibers slightly predominated, and fiber size varied markedly.

Boyden et al. (2012) also described a girl (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease. She was a sporadic case of Caucasian background with distant Portuguese ancestry. She developed hypotonia with a weak neck at several weeks of age. She had a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, hyperflexible joints, and frequent pulmonary infections, but no chronic breathing problems. Serum CK was mildly elevated at age 10 years. Mild scoliosis had progressed to 20 degrees by age 12. She had mild nonprogressive proximal and facial weakness. Muscle biopsy showed fiber size variation, internal nuclei, and minicores, and electron microscopy confirmed the minicores.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man who had had progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia for 20 years. In his fifties, while he could still walk, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man, born to healthy consanguineous parents, who had a congenital myopathy with elbow contractures, scoliosis, and weak suck from the first months of life. He had no motor developmental delay or intellectual disability. He developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement, and needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years. Examination showed axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy and weakness, preserved facial expression, cleft palate, global areflexia, and contractures of the elbows and ankles. Serum CK, brain and spine MRI, and cardiac studies were normal. He walked without support but with an equinovarus foot stance, hyperlordosis, and pelvic tilt, and needed a manual wheelchair for longer trips. He underwent bilateral Achilles tendon tenotomy and vertebral arthrodesis. From age 17 he had swallowing difficulties and recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration. Electrophysiologic studies showed normal motor and sensory conduction except for reduced compound muscle action potential amplitude. Sensation was not impaired. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific myopathic changes: variation in fiber size, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later second biopsy showed myofibrillar alterations, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores. The authors suggested that this pattern pointed to a myofibrillar myopathy and showed that the disease had progressed.

### Other Features

Boyden et al. (2012) pointed out palatal abnormalities in their patients and in previously reported cases. In family 1030, 1 patient had a cleft palate, 1 had a midline palatal ridge with nasal reflux of liquids, and all 3 had hypernasal speech, which can indicate submucous cleft palate. Subject 105-1 had a high-arched palate. The authors suggested that MEGF10 may influence palate formation or facial midline determination.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of the mild variant of CMYO10B is autosomal recessive. In the family reported by Boyden et al. (2012), the affected sibs were compound heterozygous for MEGF10 mutations in trans that segregated with the disease. In the patient reported by Croci et al. (2022), born to consanguineous parents, the mutation was homozygous, and both healthy parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) ran a genomewide SNP linkage scan in family 1030 and found positive LOD scores at 16 loci. The MEGF10 gene lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible LOD score of 1.329. The SEPN1, RYR1, ACTA1, and TPM3 genes were excluded.

### Molecular Genetics

Boyden et al. (2012) combined linkage data with whole-genome sequencing in family 1030. The affected sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: C326R in EGF-like domain 6 and C774R in EGF-like domain 16. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans and segregated with the disease in all 6 family members. Neither mutation was found in public databases or in 645 to 715 controls, and both affected cysteine residues are conserved in 43 species. Knockdown of megf10 in zebrafish caused a myopathic phenotype, which the authors took as support for the pathogenicity of the human mutations.

Boyden et al. (2012) also sequenced the MEGF10 open reading frame in 190 patients with unexplained myopathies. In 1 patient (subject 105-1) they found a single heterozygous R71W mutation, which was inherited from an unaffected parent and was absent from public databases and 699 controls. Both alleles were expressed in muscle cDNA, and no second mutation was found. Because her clinical and histologic features resembled those of family 1030, the authors suggested that she may carry a second, undetected MEGF10 mutation.

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G) and a novel missense mutation (C810Y).

In a 66-year-old man with adult-onset myopathy and respiratory failure, Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a novel essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a novel missense mutation (C118R). ⚠︎

In an Italian man with a progressive form of mild-variant EMARDD, born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation (C699S) in an EGF-like domain of the MEGF10 gene. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the patient was homozygous, and both healthy parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was classified as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. No functional studies were done.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that the phenotypes in their patients with missense mutations were similar to, but less severe than, those of previously reported patients with MEGF10 nonsense and frameshift mutations. Those earlier patients had lost all detectable MEGF10 expression, and the authors suggested that the missense mutations may leave some residual MEGF10 function. Minicores were prominent in their patients with missense mutations but had not been noted in the reported families with null mutations.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported 2 Japanese patients with MEGF10 myopathy. One had the severe EMARDD phenotype and a homozygous frameshift mutation (c.131_132del). The other had the milder phenotype and compound heterozygous mutations that included the cysteine missense mutation C810Y. The authors noted that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain may be responsible for the milder phenotype, and they concluded that their findings indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed all 20 reported MEGF10 cases and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. In patients with at least 1 null mutation, respiratory dysfunction began in the first year of life. In patients with 2 missense mutations, it began at age 10 years or much later. The authors stated that their findings extend the MEGF10 phenotype to include onset in the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that missense MEGF10 variants have been linked to later onset and a milder course, yet their patient, who had a homozygous missense variant, still had early-onset, progressive myopathy. In another reported late-onset patient, cores were seen only on a third biopsy. On this basis, the authors proposed that the minicores typical of the late-onset group may result from a long-standing myofibrillar process that begins in infancy. They also proposed a continuum of MEGF10-related phenotypes rather than a strict split between classic EMARDD and mvEMARDD.

### Pathogenesis

Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that the C326R and C774R mutations cause loss of MEGF10 function in satellite cells. In their model, excessive differentiation of satellite cells would deplete the self-renewing population needed to regenerate damaged muscle, which could explain the dystrophic features. They suggested that satellite cell dysfunction may be a pathogenic mechanism shared with SEPN1-related multiminicore disease.

### Diagnosis

Croci et al. (2022) suggested that myofibrillar alterations are a common finding in MEGF10 myopathy. They recommended testing the MEGF10 gene in patients with suspected myofibrillar myopathy who have childhood-onset progressive myopathy, scoliosis, contractures, and respiratory involvement.

### Population Genetics

Croci et al. (2022) stated that their patient was the first reported Italian patient with a MEGF10 variant and that MEGF10 variants had not been reported before in people of Italian ancestry.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish with 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. Knockdown produced curved or bent tails, impaired swimming and reduced motility, unhatched eggs, and lethality at high doses. Morphants had disrupted somite boundaries, reduced birefringence, and gaps between myofibers, and electron microscopy showed widespread myofibrillar disorganization. The authors took these results as support for the pathogenicity of the human mutations and suggested a myopathic process.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS326ARG [c.976T>C; p.C326R]

In 3 affected sibs from a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) with a mild congenital myopathy resembling multiminicore disease, Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One was a c.976T-C transition in exon 10, resulting in a cys326-to-arg (C326R) substitution in EGF-like domain 6. The other was C774R. The mutations were found by linkage analysis combined with whole-genome sequencing. Sanger sequencing confirmed that they were in trans and segregated with the disease in all 6 family members. C326R was absent from dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, the ESP database, and 715 controls. It changes a cysteine that is conserved in 43 species and is predicted to form a disulfide bond. ⚠︎

**.0002 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS774ARG [c.2320T>C; p.C774R]

In the affected sibs of family 1030 with mild congenital myopathy, Boyden et al. (2012) identified a c.2320T-C transition in exon 19 of the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys774-to-arg (C774R) substitution in EGF-like domain 16. The mutation was in trans with C326R and segregated with the disease. It was absent from public databases and 645 controls, and the residue is conserved in 43 species. The authors noted that a previously reported family carried C774R in compound heterozygosity with a frameshift mutation. ⚠︎

**.0003 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, ARG71TRP [c.211C>T; p.R71W]

In a girl (subject 105-1) of Caucasian background with distant Portuguese ancestry, diagnosed with multiminicore disease, Boyden et al. (2012) identified a single heterozygous c.211C-T transition in exon 4 of the MEGF10 gene, resulting in an arg71-to-trp (R71W) substitution in the emilin domain. The mutation was inherited from an unaffected parent and was absent from public databases and 699 controls. Both alleles were expressed in muscle cDNA, and no second mutation was found. Because her features resembled those of family 1030, the authors suggested that she may carry a second, undetected MEGF10 mutation.
