### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myopathy-10B, mild variant (CMYO10B), is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene (612453) on chromosome 5q23.2.

### Description

Takayama et al. (2016) stated that MEGF10 mutations cause early-onset myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD) as well as a milder phenotype of minicore myopathy. Congenital myopathy-10B is the milder form. It is an autosomal recessive myopathy with onset in infancy, childhood, or as late as the fifth decade. Features include axial, neck, and proximal muscle weakness, facial weakness, scoliosis, joint contractures, hypernasal speech, dysphagia, and respiratory insufficiency. Muscle biopsies show myopathic or dystrophic changes and often minicores (Boyden et al., 2012; Harris et al., 2018; Croci et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) reported a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an undiagnosed autosomal recessive myopathy that resembled multiminicore disease in some respects. Onset was in infancy, with weakness and contractures beginning in the neck. The patients had axial and proximal muscle weakness, facial weakness, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords, and some had neck muscle atrophy. Serum creatine kinase was mildly elevated. All 3 had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion surgery. Beginning at age 10 to 11 years, all 3 developed respiratory problems that were treated with bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation at night. All 3 had hypernasal speech. One had a cleft palate and another had a midline palatal ridge. Muscle biopsies showed fatty replacement of muscle tissue, increased endomysial and perimysial connective tissue, regenerating and moth-eaten fibers, variation in fiber size, increased internalized nuclei, type I fiber predominance, and minicores. There were no rimmed vacuoles, central cores, nemaline rods, or ragged red fibers. Boyden et al. (2012) noted palatal abnormalities in their patients and suggested that MEGF10 may influence palate formation or determination of the facial midline. However, they noted that these features are nonspecific in patients with myopathic facies.

Boyden et al. (2012) also described a Caucasian girl of distant Portuguese ancestry (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease. No other family member was affected. She presented at several weeks of age with hypotonia, which first appeared as a weak neck and floppy head. She had a markedly high-arched palate, poor feeding with failure to thrive, blue-tinged sclerae, hyperflexible joints, and frequent pulmonary infections in childhood, but she did not have chronic breathing problems. She had mild nonprogressive proximal weakness, facial weakness, deficits in motor performance, and mild scoliosis that progressed to 20 degrees. Serum CK was mildly elevated (302 U/L). Muscle biopsy showed marked variation in fiber size, internalized nuclei, and slightly increased endomysial connective tissue. Electron microscopy confirmed minicores and showed Z-band disarray. Only a single heterozygous MEGF10 variant was found in this patient (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

Takayama et al. (2016) reported 2 Japanese patients with MEGF10 mutations. One had the EMARDD phenotype and the other had a milder phenotype.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. In his fifties he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation while he was still ambulant. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins. Harris et al. (2018) concluded that their findings expanded the phenotype of MEGF10 mutations to include onset in the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to healthy consanguineous parents. There was no family history of neuromuscular disease. He was born preterm at 33 weeks with birth asphyxia, and he had a weak suck. He had no motor developmental delay or intellectual disability. However, he developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement. He required nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years and had recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration. Examination showed axial hypotonia, generalized muscular hypotrophy, global areflexia, cleft palate, contractures of the elbows and ankles, equinovarus foot stance, hyperlordosis, and scoliosis. Facial expression was preserved and sensation was not impaired. Serum creatine kinase, brain and spine MRI, echocardiogram, and ECG were normal. Electrophysiologic studies showed reduced amplitude of muscle evoked potentials. Muscle biopsy showed variation in fiber size, internal nuclei, fibrosis, whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores. An earlier muscle biopsy had shown only nonspecific changes, and a later one showed myofibrillar changes with minicores.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CMYO10B is autosomal recessive. In the family reported by Boyden et al. (2012), 3 sibs born to unaffected nonconsanguineous parents were affected and carried compound heterozygous MEGF10 mutations that segregated with the disorder. In the patient reported by Croci et al. (2022), born to consanguineous parents, the mutation was homozygous and both healthy parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) performed genomewide linkage analysis in family 1030 under a recessive model and found 16 positive loci. MEGF10 lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible lod score of 1.329.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 affected sibs from a nonconsanguineous family (family 1030), Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: C326R and C774R. The mutations were found by whole-genome sequencing of 1 affected member, filtered using the linkage data. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans in affected individuals and segregated with the myopathy in all 6 family members. Both mutations substituted conserved cysteines in atypical EGF-like domains that were predicted to form disulfide bonds. Neither was present in dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, or the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project, nor in 715 and 645 genotyped unaffected controls, respectively. Boyden et al. (2012) then screened the MEGF10 open reading frame in 190 additional patients with unexplained myopathies. They found only 1 patient (subject 105-1) with a single heterozygous mutation (R71W). This patient's unaffected parent was also heterozygous for R71W, so it was not a de novo dominant mutation. The authors suggested that the patient may carry a second MEGF10 mutation that they could not detect.

In a Japanese patient with a milder MEGF10 myopathy phenotype, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G) and a novel missense mutation (C810Y).

In a 66-year-old man with adult-onset myopathy and prominent respiratory failure, Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify 2 novel mutations in the MEGF10 gene: an essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a missense mutation (c.352T-C, C118R).

In a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents who had a progressive congenital myopathy, Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion (NM_001256545.2) in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed homozygosity in the patient and showed that both parents were carriers. The variant was not present in ClinVar or gnomAD. It affected a conserved cysteine in an EGF-like domain, and the authors classified it as likely pathogenic by ACMG criteria.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that patients with MEGF10 nonsense and frameshift mutations had a more severe congenital myopathy than their patients with missense mutations. They considered this consistent with total loss of MEGF10 function in the former and possible residual function in the latter. Minicores were prominent in their patients with missense mutations but had not been noted in biopsies from families with null mutations. ⚠︎

Takayama et al. (2016) noted that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain of MEGF10 may be responsible for the milder phenotype. However, they considered the relationship inconclusive because few cases had been reported. They concluded that their 2 Japanese patients, who had 2 different phenotypes, indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed all 20 reported cases with MEGF10 mutations and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. Patients with at least 1 null mutation had onset of respiratory dysfunction in the first year of life, whereas patients with 2 missense mutations developed respiratory dysfunction at 10 years of age or much later.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that their patient had early-onset progressive myopathy even though he carried a missense mutation. His first muscle biopsy showed nonspecific changes, and a later biopsy showed myofibrillar changes with minicores. They proposed that minicores, which are typical of the later-onset group, may result from a long-standing myofibrillar process beginning in infancy. They also proposed a continuum of phenotypes rather than a strict division between classic EMARDD and the mild variant.

### Pathogenesis

Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that loss of MEGF10 function causes excessive differentiation of satellite cells. This would deplete the self-renewing population needed to regenerate damaged muscle fibers and could explain the dystrophic features in patient biopsies. They suggested that MEGF10-related myopathy and SEPN1-related multiminicore disease may share a common mechanism of satellite cell dysfunction. In support of a role in satellite cells, Holterman et al. (2007) had found that siRNA silencing of Megf10 on isolated mouse myofibers reduced the number of activated satellite cells by 30% and the number of self-renewing Pax7+/MyoD- cells by 62%. It also increased myogenin-expressing cells 3.15-fold, indicating precocious differentiation.

### Diagnosis

Croci et al. (2022) suggested that MEGF10 be tested in patients with suspected myofibrillar myopathy who have childhood-onset progressive myopathy, scoliosis, contractures, and respiratory involvement.

### Population Genetics

Croci et al. (2022) stated that MEGF10 variants had not previously been reported in individuals of Italian ancestry and that their patient was the first Italian patient reported.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish using 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. The morphants had curled or bent tails, impaired swimming, unhatched eggs, reduced birefringence, disrupted somite boundaries, gaps between myofibers, and myofibrillar disorganization, consistent with a myopathic process.
