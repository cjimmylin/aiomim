### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10B (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene on chromosome 5. ⚠︎

### Description

The mild variant of congenital myopathy-10B (CMYO10B) is an autosomal recessive myopathy. Its course is milder and its onset later than the severe early-onset form of MEGF10-related myopathy, known as early-onset myopathy, areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD). Affected individuals have predominantly axial and proximal muscle weakness, facial weakness, joint contractures, scoliosis, and respiratory involvement starting in childhood or later. Muscle biopsy can show both dystrophic and myopathic features, and minicores are found in some patients (Boyden et al., 2012; Croci et al., 2022).

### Nomenclature

Takayama et al. (2016) called this disorder a milder phenotype of minicore myopathy, as distinct from EMARDD. Croci et al. (2022) called the milder chronic forms of MEGF10 myopathy, which have a more favorable course, the mild variant of EMARDD (mvEMARDD).

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) studied a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an undiagnosed autosomal recessive myopathy. The affected sibs had weakness by 12 months of age, starting in the neck. The weakness affected the whole body but was mainly axial and proximal, and it stabilized in adolescence. Facial weakness was also present. Neck contractures were seen in infancy, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords had developed by adulthood. Serum creatine kinase measured before age 10 years was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion, and all developed respiratory problems from age 10 to 11 years, treated with nighttime bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation. One patient was born with a cleft palate, a second had a midline ridge on her palate in adulthood, and all 3 had hypernasal speech. Muscle biopsy showed fatty replacement of myofibers, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers, which are dystrophic features. Moth-eaten fibers and minicores were also present, which suggested a congenital myopathy. There was a slight predominance of type I fibers. Before genetic testing, the diagnosis had been given as limb-girdle muscular dystrophy or spinal muscular atrophy.

Boyden et al. (2012) also reported a Caucasian woman of distant Portuguese ancestry (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease. At several weeks of age she had hypotonia, with a weak neck and floppy head. She also had a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. As a child she had frequent acute pulmonary infections but no chronic breathing problems. At age 10 years, serum creatine kinase was 302 U/L and she had mild scoliosis, which had progressed to 20 degrees by age 12 years. She had mild, nonprogressive, mainly proximal weakness, including facial weakness. Muscle biopsy showed marked variation in fiber size, internalized nuclei, slightly increased endomysial connective tissue, and patches of decreased NADH activity. Electron microscopy confirmed minicores.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. In his fifties, while still able to walk, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins. Harris et al. (2018) concluded that the MEGF10 phenotype can begin as late as the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents. He was born at 33 weeks' gestation, needed resuscitation for asphyxia, and had a weak suck. From the first months of life he had a congenital myopathic phenotype with elbow contractures and scoliosis. He had no motor developmental delay or intellectual disability. He developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement, and he needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years. Serum creatine kinase, brain and spine MRI, ECG, and echocardiogram were normal. Electrophysiologic studies were normal apart from reduced amplitude of the muscle evoked potential. On examination he had axial hypotonia, generalized muscular hypotrophy and weakness, preserved facial expression, cleft palate, global areflexia, and contractures of the elbows and ankles. He walked unsupported with an equinovarus supinated foot stance and hyperlordosis, and he later needed a manual wheelchair for longer trips. He underwent bilateral Achilles tenotomy and vertebral arthrodesis. From age 17 he had swallowing difficulties and recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration. A muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: variation in fiber size, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later biopsy showed myofibrillar alterations, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores.

### Inheritance

Boyden et al. (2012) established autosomal recessive inheritance in a family with 3 affected sibs born to unaffected nonconsanguineous parents. The sibs were compound heterozygous for MEGF10 mutations. Croci et al. (2022) found a homozygous MEGF10 mutation in a patient born to consanguineous parents, both of whom were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) carried out a genomewide linkage scan in family 1030 under a recessive model. They found positive lod scores at 16 loci. MEGF10 lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible lod score of 1.329.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 affected sibs from family 1030, Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: C326R and C774R. They found the mutations by combining linkage analysis with whole-genome sequencing. The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing to be in trans and segregated with the disorder in all 6 family members. Neither mutation was found in the dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, or Exome Sequencing Project databases, or in 715 and 645 genotyped controls, respectively. Both affected cysteine residues are completely conserved in 43 species. Each mutation changes 1 of the 8 cysteines in an atypical EGF-like domain (domains 6 and 16), and Boyden et al. (2012) predicted that this would disrupt disulfide bonds. Knockdown of megf10 in zebrafish caused a myopathic phenotype.

Boyden et al. (2012) sequenced the MEGF10 coding region in 190 additional patients with unexplained myopathies. They found 1 patient (subject 105-1) with a similar clinical and histologic presentation who carried only a single heterozygous mutation, R71W, in the emilin domain. Her unaffected parent also carried this mutation. Sequencing of muscle cDNA did not find a second mutation, but both alleles were expressed. Boyden et al. (2012) suggested that the patient might carry a second MEGF10 mutation that they could not detect. ⚠︎

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G) and a novel missense mutation (C810Y).

In a 66-year-old man with adult-onset myopathy and prominent respiratory failure, Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify 2 novel mutations in the MEGF10 gene: an essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a missense mutation (c.352T-C, C118R).

In a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was not present in ClinVar or gnomAD, affected a conserved residue, and was predicted to be pathogenic by in silico tools.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that nonsense and frameshift mutations in MEGF10 had been reported to cause a more severe congenital myopathy with diaphragmatic weakness, areflexia, and dysphagia. One of those families was compound heterozygous for the C774R mutation and a frameshift mutation. Boyden et al. (2012) proposed that the milder disease in their patients with missense mutations reflected residual MEGF10 function. In contrast, MEGF10 expression was totally lost in the families with null mutations. They also noted that minicores were prominent in their patients with missense mutations but had not been reported in the families with null mutations.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported 2 Japanese patients. One patient had the EMARDD phenotype and a homozygous frameshift mutation. The other had the milder phenotype and was compound heterozygous for a splice site mutation and a cysteine missense mutation. Takayama et al. (2016) concluded that these cases supported a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed 20 reported cases of MEGF10 myopathy and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. Patients with at least 1 null mutation had onset of respiratory dysfunction in the first year of life. Patients with 2 missense mutations had onset of respiratory dysfunction at age 10 years or much later.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a patient with a homozygous missense mutation who nevertheless had early-onset, progressive myopathy. They proposed that MEGF10-related phenotypes form a continuum rather than 2 separate forms. Because minicores appeared only on his later biopsy, they also proposed that minicores may result from a long-standing myofibrillar pathologic process.

### Pathogenesis

In mouse, Holterman et al. (2007) found that Megf10 was expressed in quiescent and activated satellite cells. Knocking down Megf10 caused activated satellite cells to differentiate prematurely and depleted the self-renewing Pax7+/MyoD- population. Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that loss of MEGF10 function from the C326R and C774R mutations might cause excessive differentiation of satellite cells. This, in turn, could deplete the self-renewing cells available to regenerate damaged muscle, which would explain the dystrophic features in their patients' biopsies.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish using 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. The fish showed unhatched eggs, curved tails, impaired motility, reduced birefringence, and disorganized muscle tissue with gaps between myofibers. Electron microscopy showed myofibrillar disorganization. Boyden et al. (2012) concluded that these findings supported the pathogenicity of the human mutations.
