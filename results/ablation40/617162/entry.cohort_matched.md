### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-46 (DEE46) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GRIN2D gene (602717) on chromosome 19q13.33.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-46 (DEE46) is a severe neurologic disorder. Patients have epilepsy that does not respond to treatment and have developmental delay or impairment. Status epilepticus has been reported. Known disease-causing variants are heterozygous missense changes in GRIN2D, which encodes the GluN2D subunit of the NMDA receptor (NMDAR).

### Clinical Features

Li et al. (2016) reported 2 unrelated children with epileptic encephalopathy and developmental impairment. In both children, seizures had proven refractory to conventional antiepileptic medications. The older proband later developed refractory status epilepticus, which improved dramatically with ketamine and magnesium. ⚠︎

Tsuchida et al. (2018) reported 3 unrelated patients with severe developmental delay and intractable epilepsy. They compared these 3 patients with the 2 previously reported patients carrying a recurrent de novo GRIN2D missense variant and found that all 5 had similar clinical features.

### Inheritance

Li et al. (2016) found that the heterozygous GRIN2D mutation had arisen de novo in both of their patients, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Tsuchida et al. (2018) also found heterozygous GRIN2D missense variants in their patients.

### Molecular Genetics

Using exome and candidate panel sequencing, Li et al. (2016) identified the same de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the GRIN2D gene, c.1999G-A, in 2 unrelated children with epileptic encephalopathy. The mutation causes a val667-to-ile (V667I) substitution. It lies in the M3 transmembrane domain, which is involved in channel gating. In functional studies, the mutation acted as a gain of function. It increased glutamate and glycine potency 2-fold and increased channel open probability 6-fold. It also reduced sensitivity to negative modulators such as extracellular protons and prolonged the deactivation time course after glutamate was removed. When cultured neurons were transfected with human GRIN2D cDNA carrying the mutation, the neurons showed dendritic swelling and cell death. The authors suggested that this reflected excitotoxicity caused by NMDAR overactivation.

Using whole-exome sequencing, Tsuchida et al. (2018) identified novel heterozygous missense variants in the GRIN2D gene in 3 unrelated patients with severe developmental delay and intractable epilepsy. The altered residues were highly conserved across vertebrates and among the 4 GluN2 subunits. Based on structural considerations, all 3 variants were probably able to impair GluN2D function, either by affecting interaction between subunits or by altering channel gating. The authors concluded that their study provided further evidence that GRIN2D variants cause epilepsy.

### Clinical Management

Li et al. (2016) tested the mutant receptors in vitro for sensitivity to FDA-approved NMDAR antagonists. Based on the results, both of their patients were given oral memantine, which led to mild to moderate improvement in seizure burden and development. The older proband later developed refractory status epilepticus and showed dramatic electroclinical improvement with ketamine and magnesium. The authors suggested that NMDAR antagonists may be useful as adjuvant therapy in individuals with GRIN2D gain-of-function mutations.

Tsuchida et al. (2018) suggested that a genetic diagnosis of GluN2-related disorders may be clinically useful when considering drug therapy targeted at NMDA receptors.
