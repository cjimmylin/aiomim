### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-46 (DEE46) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GRIN2D gene (602717) on chromosome 19q13.33.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-46 is a severe neurologic disorder. It is characterized by epilepsy that does not respond to conventional antiepileptic medications and by severe developmental delay (Li et al., 2016; Tsuchida et al., 2018).

### Clinical Features

Li et al. (2016) reported 2 unrelated children with epileptic encephalopathy. In both, the seizures did not respond to conventional antiepileptic medications. The older proband later developed refractory status epilepticus.

Tsuchida et al. (2018) reported 3 unrelated patients with severe developmental delay and intractable epilepsy. When they compared their 3 patients with the 2 previously reported patients, all 5 had similar clinical features.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous GRIN2D mutation in the patients reported by Li et al. (2016) occurred de novo, which is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Li et al. (2016) used exome and candidate panel sequencing to study 2 unrelated children with epileptic encephalopathy. Both carried the same de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the GRIN2D gene, c.1999G-A (V667I). The V667I substitution lies in the M3 transmembrane domain of GluN2D, a region involved in channel gating. Functional studies showed that it was a gain-of-function mutation. It increased glutamate and glycine potency 2-fold and channel open probability 6-fold, reduced receptor sensitivity to endogenous negative modulators such as extracellular protons, and prolonged deactivation after glutamate removal.

Tsuchida et al. (2018) used whole-exome sequencing to study 3 unrelated patients with severe developmental delay and intractable epilepsy. Each carried a novel heterozygous missense variant in the GRIN2D gene. The altered residues were highly conserved across vertebrates and among the 4 GluN2 subunits. Structural consideration indicated that all 3 variants probably impair GluN2D function, either by affecting intersubunit interaction or by altering channel gating activity.

### Pathogenesis

Li et al. (2016) transfected cultured neurons with human GRIN2D cDNA carrying the c.1999G-A mutation, which caused dendritic swelling and neuronal cell death. The authors suggested that this reflected excitotoxicity mediated by NMDA receptor overactivation.

### Clinical Management

Li et al. (2016) tested the mutant receptors for sensitivity to FDA-approved NMDA receptor antagonists. Based on these results, both children were given oral memantine, and seizure burden and development improved mildly to moderately. The older proband later developed refractory status epilepticus, which improved dramatically with ketamine and magnesium. The authors suggested that NMDA receptor antagonists may be useful as adjuvant epilepsy therapy in individuals with GRIN2D gain-of-function mutations.

Tsuchida et al. (2018) suggested that a genetic diagnosis of GluN2-related disorders may be clinically useful when considering drug therapy targeting NMDA receptors.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

GRIN2D, VAL667ILE [c.1999G>A; p.V667I; p.Val667Ile]

Li et al. (2016) studied 2 unrelated children with epileptic encephalopathy and refractory seizures. Using exome and candidate panel sequencing, they found the same de novo heterozygous c.1999G-A transition in the GRIN2D gene in both children, resulting in a val667-to-ile (V667I) substitution in the M3 transmembrane domain. The mutation caused a gain of function. It increased glutamate and glycine potency 2-fold and channel open probability 6-fold, reduced sensitivity to extracellular protons, and prolonged deactivation. In transfected cultured neurons, the mutant caused dendritic swelling and neuronal cell death. Treatment with oral memantine led to mild to moderate improvement in both children.
