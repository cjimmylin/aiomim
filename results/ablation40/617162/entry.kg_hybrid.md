### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-46 (DEE46) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GRIN2D gene (602717) on chromosome 19q13.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-46 is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder with severe developmental delay and intractable epilepsy that does not respond to conventional antiepileptic medications (Li et al., 2016; Tsuchida et al., 2018).

### Clinical Features

Li et al. (2016) reported 2 unrelated children with epileptic encephalopathy. In both, the seizures were refractory to conventional antiepileptic medications. The older proband later developed refractory status epilepticus.

Tsuchida et al. (2018) reported 3 unrelated patients with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, severe developmental delay, and intractable epilepsy. When they compared these patients with the 2 previously reported patients with a GRIN2D variant, they found that all 5 had similar clinical features.

### Inheritance

In the 2 patients reported by Li et al. (2016), the heterozygous GRIN2D mutation occurred de novo.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 unrelated children with epileptic encephalopathy, Li et al. (2016) used exome and candidate panel sequencing to identify the same de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the GRIN2D gene, c.1999G-A (V667I). The mutation lies in the M3 transmembrane domain, which is involved in channel gating. Functional studies showed that it was a gain-of-function mutation. It increased glutamate and glycine potency 2-fold, increased channel open probability 6-fold, and reduced receptor sensitivity to endogenous negative modulators such as extracellular protons. It also prolonged the deactivation time course after glutamate removal.

In 3 unrelated patients with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, Tsuchida et al. (2018) used whole exome sequencing to identify novel heterozygous missense variants in the GRIN2D gene. All of the altered residues were highly conserved across vertebrates and among the 4 GluN2 subunits. Structural analysis indicated that all 3 variants probably impair GluN2D function, either by affecting intersubunit interaction or by altering channel gating activity.

### Pathogenesis

Li et al. (2016) found that transfecting cultured neurons with human GRIN2D cDNA carrying the c.1999G-A mutation caused dendritic swelling and neuronal cell death. They suggested that this reflected excitotoxicity mediated by NMDAR over-activation.

### Clinical Management

Li et al. (2016) tested whether the mutant NMDARs were sensitive to FDA-approved NMDAR antagonists and then gave oral memantine to both of their patients. Seizure burden and development improved mildly to moderately. The older proband later developed refractory status epilepticus, which showed dramatic electroclinical improvement with ketamine and magnesium. The authors suggested that NMDAR antagonists may be useful as adjuvant epilepsy therapy in individuals with GRIN2D gain-of-function mutations.

Tsuchida et al. (2018) suggested that a genetic diagnosis of GluN2-related disorders may be clinically useful when considering drug therapy that targets NMDA receptors.
