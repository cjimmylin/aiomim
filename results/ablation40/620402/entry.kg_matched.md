### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is caused by homozygous mutation in the COQ7 gene (601683) on chromosome 16p12.3.

### Description

Autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is a progressive, length-dependent motor neuropathy. Distal weakness and atrophy begin in the lower limbs in late childhood or early adolescence and later involve the hands. Pes cavus is common, sensation is preserved, and some patients have pyramidal signs (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023). The disorder results from loss of COQ7 function and is associated with reduced coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) and accumulation of the COQ7 substrate 6-demethoxycoenzyme Q10 (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported 3 Portuguese sibs with distal hereditary motor neuropathy (dHMN): 2 brothers aged 36 and 42 years and a sister aged 25 years. Their parents were healthy and not consanguineous. Early development was normal, and walking difficulties began between ages 9 and 12 years. Distal weakness started in the lower limbs, mainly as loss of foot dorsiflexion, and progressed to involve the hands between ages 13 and 32 years. All 3 sibs had pes cavus and amyotrophy or distal deficit in the lower limbs and hands. Their sensory examination and cranial nerves were normal, and none had a cognitive disorder. Two sibs had pyramidal signs: one had brisk reflexes with a Hoffman sign, and the other had a left Babinski sign. The authors suggested that dHMN with pyramidal features indicated upper motor neuron involvement. The 42-year-old eldest brother developed foot drop at age 10 years and needed ankle-foot orthoses from about age 20. He had hand weakness from age 22. At age 42, he had complete loss of foot plantar flexion and dorsiflexion, with distal amyotrophy. The 25-year-old sister reported reduced perception of cold and warm on her feet, but her sensory examination was normal. All 3 sibs remained ambulant. In 2 of the sibs, electroneuromyography showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy that mainly affected the distal lower limbs, and needle examination showed fibrillations and reduced motor unit recruitment. The index patient had normal brain and spinal cord MRI, echocardiography, ECG and audiometry. Creatine kinase was mildly elevated at 215 U/l.

Smith et al. (2023) reported 3 sibs born to Syrian first-cousin parents: a 24-year-old woman, a 23-year-old man and a 12-year-old boy. They had a progressive length-dependent motor neuropathy. Distal weakness began at 10 to 14 years of age, and distal muscle atrophy affected the lower limbs more than the upper limbs. Sensation in all modalities and deep tendon reflexes were preserved. Electrodiagnostic testing showed reduced distal motor amplitudes with preserved sensory responses. Needle EMG showed distal chronic reinnervation without active denervation. Gene panels for Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, spinal muscular atrophy, distal hereditary motor neuropathy and mtDNA were negative. Whole-body muscle MRI was performed in 2 of the sibs. It showed normal proximal musculature and significant distal muscle wasting. Moderate to severe fat infiltration began at the distal third of the thigh and was most pronounced in both calves, especially the soleus and gastrocnemius. A vastus lateralis biopsy from 1 sib showed neurogenic atrophy, with angular atrophic fibers (sometimes in groups), pyknotic nuclear clumps, group atrophy and fiber type grouping. There were no ragged red fibers and no cytochrome oxidase-negative fibers.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) identified 5 new patients with COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency, adding to 9 previously reported patients from 7 families. They described 2 clinical presentations. The first had neonatal onset, with severe neuromuscular, cardiorespiratory and renal involvement. The second had late onset, with progressive neuropathy, lower-extremity weakness, abnormal gait and variable developmental delay.

### Other Features

In the Syrian family reported by Smith et al. (2023), congenital deafness did not segregate with the motor neuropathy. Two of the 3 affected sibs and their unaffected brother were deaf, while 1 affected sib was not. The deafness was explained by a homozygous c.35delG (p.Gly12ValfsTer2) variant in the GJB2 gene, for which both parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Biochemical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) used HPLC-MS/MS to study fibroblasts from the index patient of their Portuguese family. CoQ10 was markedly reduced (12.5 pmol/mg protein, compared with 71.5 pmol/mg in controls), and the fibroblasts contained the COQ7 substrate 6-demethoxycoenzyme Q10 (6-DMQ). 6-DMQ was also detected in the patient's plasma. After 14 days of supplementation with the bypass precursor 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid, CoQ10 rose slightly (to 18.8 pmol/mg) and 6-DMQ normalized, which confirmed a loss of COQ7 activity.

Smith et al. (2023) found that cultured fibroblasts from the 3 affected Syrian sibs had on average 87% less CoQ10 than fibroblasts from the carrier father and an unaffected sib. The affected fibroblasts also accumulated the COQ7 substrate DMQ10, which was undetectable in carriers. COQ7 protein in affected fibroblasts was 85% lower than in carriers, while PDSS2 levels were unchanged. In muscle from 1 affected sib, COQ7 protein was undetectable and CoQ10 was 67% lower than in a control (63 vs 192.7 ng/mg protein), but no DMQ10 was detected. In permeabilized muscle from this sib, FCCP-induced maximal respiration and complex IV activity were reduced, H2O2 emission was increased, and there were fewer supercomplexes containing complexes I and IV. Fibroblasts from the affected sibs had preserved basal respiration but reduced maximal and spare respiratory capacity. They also had higher basal extracellular acidification, a bioenergetic profile favoring glycolysis, and impaired switching between ATP production pathways. Complex I activity, citrate synthase activity and viability in galactose medium were normal. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HMNR9 is autosomal recessive. In the families reported by Jacquier et al. (2023) and Smith et al. (2023), affected sibs were homozygous for COQ7 mutations and their unaffected parents were heterozygous. Wongkittichote et al. (2023) noted that biallelic pathogenic COQ7 variants cause primary CoQ10 deficiency, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Jacquier et al. (2023) performed whole-exome sequencing in both parents and all 3 affected sibs of a Portuguese family with HMNR9. They identified a homozygous c.3G-T variant (p.Met1?) in the COQ7 gene, which changes the canonical ATG start codon of isoform 1 (NM_016138.5) to ATT. All 3 patients were homozygous and the unaffected parents were heterozygous, and Sanger sequencing confirmed these genotypes. The variant was absent from ExAC and was found once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD (allele frequency 0.000004065). The alternative start codons used by isoforms 2 and 3 were predicted to be unaffected. In fibroblasts from the index patient, COQ7 mRNA was decreased only modestly (by 21% and 17% with 2 primer pairs), but COQ7 protein was barely detectable. Mitochondrial content was similar to controls. In SH-EP neuroblastoma cells transfected with FLAG-tagged constructs, the mutant protein localized like wildtype but was reduced by 85%, with unchanged mRNA levels. The authors interpreted this as a translational defect.

Liu et al. (2023) reported biallelic variants in the COQ7 gene as a cause of distal hereditary motor neuropathy in 2 Chinese families.

Smith et al. (2023) used duo-exome sequencing in 3 sibs with HMNR9 from a consanguineous Syrian family. They identified a homozygous c.1A-G variant in the COQ7 gene (NM_016138.5), which is predicted to abolish the initiating methionine. cDNA sequencing of muscle confirmed the variant. The parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from gnomAD and was predicted to be pathogenic in silico. Fibroblasts from the affected sibs had reduced COQ7 protein and CoQ10 and accumulated DMQ10. ⚠︎

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) studied COQ7 variants in a yeast model of COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency. A cat5-null yeast strain carrying the CAT5 equivalents of p.Arg54Gln, p.Arg107Trp, p.Ile66Asn or the complex allele p.[Thr103Met;Leu111Pro] only partially rescued the oxidative growth defect. The authors concluded that these were hypomorphic alleles.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jacquier et al. (2023) noted that previously reported COQ7 mutations all lie in the catalytic domain and therefore affect every isoform. Those mutations were associated with severe early-onset multisystem primary CoQ10 deficiency with developmental delay. In contrast, the start-codon variant in their family affects only isoform 1. The authors speculated that the alternative isoforms maintain basal mitochondrial COQ7, which would explain the milder, pure dHMN phenotype. In support, they showed that a FLAG-tagged isoform 2, translated from a downstream in-frame ATG, was expressed and colocalized with mitochondria.

Smith et al. (2023) noted that earlier patients with COQ7-related CoQ10 deficiency had demyelinating and axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, developmental delay and upper motor neuron features. In contrast, their sibs had a pure length-dependent motor neuropathy without sensory involvement, demyelination, cognitive impairment or upper motor neuron signs. The authors suggested that this extends the phenotypic spectrum of COQ7 disease, but cautioned that other features might develop with age.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) defined 2 distinct presentations of COQ7-related disorder: a severe neonatal-onset form and a late-onset form with neuropathy. They reported an emerging genotype-phenotype correlation involving hypomorphic variants that retain partial function. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Jacquier et al. (2023) performed Seahorse assays on fibroblasts from their index patient. The patient's cells produced less of their ATP through mitochondria (42% vs 63.3% or 56.4% in controls) and more through glycolysis (68% vs 36.7% or 43.6%), and had reduced basal and maximal respiration. Adding CoQ10 to the culture medium rescued respiration. In galactose medium, the patient's fibroblasts proliferated about 50% less than controls without evidence of apoptosis, and 10 microM CoQ10 partially restored growth.

Smith et al. (2023) predicted that, because the start codon is lost, the COQ7 c.1A-G variant causes translation to begin at methionine-39. The resulting 179-amino acid protein (vs 217 for wildtype) would lack the cleavable N-terminal mitochondrial targeting presequence plus 2 further residues, preventing its incorporation into the inner mitochondrial membrane. The authors explained that because the presequence is normally cleaved, the truncated and wildtype proteins had similar molecular weights on immunoblot.

### Diagnosis

Jacquier et al. (2023) proposed that accumulation of 6-DMQ in plasma could be used to confirm the pathogenicity of COQ7 variants. They also recommended adding COQ7 to the gene panels used to diagnose peripheral neuropathy.

### Clinical Management

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported that the affected sister in their Portuguese family took CoQ10 (30 mg daily) between ages 15 and 18 years. She said her symptoms stabilized during that period and that progression resumed after she stopped. Based on her account and on the in vitro rescue, the authors suggested that oral CoQ10 supplementation be considered as a potential therapy.

### Animal Model

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) showed that the yeast COQ7 ortholog CAT5 is required for growth on oxidative carbon sources. A cat5-null strain had an oxidative growth defect, which wildtype CAT5 fully rescued but CAT5 carrying the equivalents of human pathogenic variants did not. The authors concluded that the yeast model is valid for functional studies of COQ7 variants. Supplementation with 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,4-diHB) rescued the growth defect of both the leaky (hypomorphic) and the severe CAT5 mutants. Overexpressing COQ8 together with 2,4-diHB acted synergistically to restore oxidative growth and correct the respiratory defect.
