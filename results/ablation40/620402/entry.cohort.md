### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is caused by homozygous mutation in the COQ7 gene (601683) on chromosome 16p12.3.

### Description

Autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is a length-dependent, pure motor axonal neuropathy that begins in childhood. Patients have progressive walking difficulties and distal weakness and atrophy, which affect the lower limbs more than the upper limbs, with pes cavus. Sensation is preserved. The disorder is associated with decreased coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) and accumulation of the COQ7 substrate 6-demethoxy-CoQ10 in patient cells (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported 3 affected sibs born to nonconsanguineous healthy Portuguese parents. The index patient (II-2) was a 36-year-old man. At age 12 years he developed walking difficulties, with a sensation of stiffness in the lower limbs and difficulty walking on his heels. On examination he had distal lower limb weakness, hand weakness, and amyotrophy of the lower limbs and hands, with pes cavus. Deep tendon reflexes were brisk in all 4 limbs except for absent Achilles reflexes, and a Hoffman sign was present, without Babinski sign or spasticity. Sensory examination was normal, and there were no fasciculations. Electroneuromyography showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy with fibrillations and a neurogenic pattern. His sister (II-3), a 25-year-old woman, had normal early development. At age 9 years she developed walking difficulties with fatigability and foot drop. She had distal lower limb and hand weakness, pes cavus, and a positive Gowers maneuver. Deep tendon reflexes were normal without a Hoffman sign, but a Babinski sign was present on the left foot. She reported difficulty feeling cold and warm temperatures on her feet. Electroneuromyography also showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy. Their brother (II-1), a 42-year-old man, noticed difficulties walking and biking at age 10 years, with foot drop. He had severe distal-predominant amyotrophy of the lower limbs and amyotrophy of the hand muscles, severe distal and mild proximal lower limb weakness, pes cavus, absent Achilles reflexes, and a positive Gowers maneuver. None of the 3 sibs had cognitive impairment. Jacquier et al. (2023) characterized the disorder as a length-dependent pure motor axonal neuropathy with childhood onset and progressive walking difficulties, and suggested that the pyramidal features indicated upper motor neuron involvement. In 1 patient, brain and spinal MRI, echocardiography, ECG, and audiometry were normal, and CPK was mildly elevated.

Liu et al. (2023) reported distal hereditary motor neuropathy in 2 Chinese families.

Smith et al. (2023) reported 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous Syrian family: a 24-year-old woman (sib 1), a 23-year-old man (sib 2), and a 12-year-old boy (sib 3). All 3 developed distal weakness by 10 to 14 years of age, which progressed. They had distal muscle atrophy affecting the lower more than the upper extremities, with preserved sensation. Nerve conduction studies showed reduced distal motor amplitudes with preserved sensory responses. In sib 1, deep tendon reflexes were preserved and EMG showed distal chronic reinnervation. MRI in sibs 1 and 2 showed distal muscle fat infiltration, and in sib 1 the calves were most affected. A biopsy of the left vastus lateralis in sib 2 showed neurogenic atrophy with wide variation in fiber size and shape and fiber type grouping, without ragged-red fibers. The sibs had no developmental delay or cognitive impairment. Sibs 2 and 3, as well as an unaffected sib (sib 4) who had no neuropathy, had congenital deafness. This was explained by a homozygous GJB2 variant and did not segregate with the neuropathy, and sib 1 was not deaf. Smith et al. (2023) noted that, unlike previously reported patients with COQ7-related CoQ10 deficiency, their patients had a pure length-dependent motor neuropathy without sensory involvement, demyelination, developmental delay, or upper motor neuron signs. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) found decreased CoQ10 in patient fibroblasts compared with controls (12.5 vs 71.5 pmol/mg protein). Patient fibroblasts also accumulated 6-demethoxy-CoQ10 (6-DMQ), the substrate of COQ7, and 6-DMQ was detected in patient plasma. The CoQ10 precursor 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid slightly increased CoQ10 and normalized 6-DMQ levels in fibroblasts.

In fibroblasts from affected sibs, Smith et al. (2023) found reduced CoQ10 and accumulation of DMQ10. In sib 2, muscle CoQ10 was 67% lower than in an unrelated human donor (63 ng/mg protein vs 192.7 ng/mg protein), and muscle showed reduced maximal respiration and reduced complex IV activity.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HMNR9 in the family reported by Jacquier et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The affected sibs were born to nonconsanguineous healthy parents, and the mutation was homozygous in the affected sibs and heterozygous in the unaffected parents. In the consanguineous Syrian family reported by Smith et al. (2023), the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for a COQ7 mutation, whereas the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers.

### Molecular Genetics

By whole-exome sequencing in 3 Portuguese sibs with distal hereditary motor neuropathy, Jacquier et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.3G-T transversion in the start codon of isoform 1 of the COQ7 gene (p.Met1?). The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family, with both unaffected parents heterozygous. The variant was present once in heterozygous state in the gnomAD database. Alternative isoforms 2 and 3 use other start codons and were predicted to be unaffected. In transfected SH-EP neuroblastoma cells, the mutation reduced COQ7 protein levels by 85% without changing mRNA levels or subcellular localization. COQ7 protein was barely detectable in patient fibroblasts, consistent with a defect in translation initiation. A FLAG-tagged isoform 2 localized to mitochondria, suggesting partial compensation.

Liu et al. (2023) reported that biallelic variants in the COQ7 gene caused distal hereditary motor neuropathy in 2 Chinese families.

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Syrian family with hereditary motor neuropathy, Smith et al. (2023) performed duo-exome sequencing on sibs 1 and 2 and identified a shared homozygous c.1A-G transition in the COQ7 gene. The variant was initially classified as of uncertain significance. Affected sib 3 was also homozygous, and the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from gnomAD. It was predicted to abolish the initiating methionine, so that translation would start at methionine-39, removing the cleavable mitochondrial targeting sequence and 2 additional amino acids. Fibroblasts from affected sibs showed severely reduced COQ7 protein.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jacquier et al. (2023) compared the pure distal hereditary motor neuropathy phenotype with the severe multisystem COQ10D8 phenotypes caused by COQ7 mutations in the catalytic domain, which affect all isoforms. They speculated that the milder phenotype resulted because the start-codon mutation affected only isoform 1 and did not completely prevent CoQ10 synthesis.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) described 5 patients with COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency and found 2 distinct presentations. One was neonatal onset with severe neuromuscular, cardiorespiratory, and renal involvement. The other was late onset with progressive neuropathy, lower extremity weakness, abnormal gait, and variable developmental delay. The authors noted an emerging genotype-phenotype correlation.

### Pathogenesis

Jacquier et al. (2023) showed that patient fibroblasts had reduced mitochondrial ATP production with a compensatory increase in glycolysis. Basal and maximal respiration were reduced and were rescued by CoQ10 supplementation. Proliferation in galactose medium was reduced and was partially rescued by 10 microM CoQ10.

Smith et al. (2023) found that fibroblasts from affected sibs had severely reduced COQ7 protein, 87% lower CoQ10, and accumulation of DMQ10, which was absent in cells from the carrier father and carrier sib. Both muscle and fibroblasts showed impaired maximal respiration. Muscle also showed increased H2O2 emission and fewer supercomplexes containing complexes I and IV.

### Clinical Management

Jacquier et al. (2023) noted that 1 patient reported that symptoms stabilized between ages 15 and 18 years while she was treated with CoQ10 (30 mg/day), and that they worsened again after treatment was stopped. The authors proposed oral CoQ10 supplementation as a potential therapy. They also suggested that accumulation of 6-DMQ in plasma could be used to confirm the pathogenicity of COQ7 variants.

### Animal Model

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) found that a yeast strain lacking CAT5 (cat5-delta), the ortholog of COQ7, could not grow on oxidative carbon sources. Wildtype CAT5 fully rescued this defect, but CAT5 carrying the equivalents of human pathogenic variants did not. The equivalents of the human R54Q, R107W, and I66N variants and of the complex allele [T103M;L111P] partially rescued growth, indicating that they are hypomorphic alleles. Supplementation with 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid rescued the growth defect of both the leaky and the severe mutants. Overexpression of COQ8 combined with 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid supplementation acted synergistically to restore oxidative growth and correct the respiratory defect.
