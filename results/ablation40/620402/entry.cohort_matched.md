### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is caused by homozygous mutation in the COQ7 gene (601683) on chromosome 16p12.3.

### Description

Autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is a childhood-onset, length-dependent, pure motor axonal neuropathy. It causes progressive walking difficulty with distal weakness and atrophy, more severe in the lower limbs than in the upper limbs. Some patients have pyramidal signs. Patient cells have decreased coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) and accumulate the COQ7 substrate 6-demethoxy-CoQ10. Unlike earlier reported patients with COQ7 mutations, these patients did not have developmental delay or multiorgan involvement (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported 3 sibs, 2 brothers and a sister, from a nonconsanguineous Portuguese family. All 3 had normal early development, and the 2 brothers took part in sports. The index patient (II-2) was a 36-year-old man who developed walking difficulties at age 12 years, with a sensation of stiffness in the lower limbs and difficulty walking on his heels. He had distal weakness of the lower limbs and hands, amyotrophy of the lower limbs and hands, and pes cavus. His deep tendon reflexes were brisk in all 4 limbs except for absent Achilles reflexes, and he had a Hoffmann sign without a Babinski sign or spasticity. Sensory examination was normal, cranial nerves were normal, and he had no cognitive disorder. Electroneuromyography showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy, and EMG showed fibrillations and a neurogenic pattern. His 25-year-old sister (II-3) developed walking difficulties with fatigability and foot drop at age 9 years. She had distal lower limb and hand weakness, a positive Gowers maneuver, pes cavus, and a Babinski sign on the left foot. Deep tendon reflexes were normal and there was no Hoffmann sign. Sensory examination was normal, although she reported difficulty feeling cold and warm temperatures on her feet. Motor amplitudes were reduced, and EMG showed fibrillations and a neurogenic pattern. Her symptoms stabilized while she was taking CoQ10 (30 mg per day) between 15 and 18 years of age, and they began to worsen again at age 18 years, when supplementation was stopped. Their 42-year-old brother (II-1) noticed walking and biking difficulties with foot drop at age 10 years. He had severe amyotrophy of the lower limbs, predominantly distal, and amyotrophy of the hand muscles. He also had severe distal weakness in the upper and lower limbs with complete loss of foot plantar and dorsal flexion, mild proximal lower limb weakness, a positive Gowers maneuver, pes cavus, and absent Achilles reflexes. He had no Hoffmann or Babinski sign, and his sensory examination was normal. None of the 3 sibs had a cognitive disorder. In 1 patient, brain and spinal cord MRI, echocardiography, ECG, ophthalmic examination, and audiometry were normal apart from known myopia, and creatine kinase was mildly elevated. The authors suggested that the pyramidal signs in some patients reflected upper motor neuron involvement.

Liu et al. (2023) reported distal hereditary motor neuropathy in 2 Chinese families.

Smith et al. (2023) reported 3 affected sibs, a 24-year-old woman and 2 brothers aged 23 and 12 years, born to Syrian parents who were first cousins. The sibs were referred for progressive distal weakness. Distal weakness began by 10 to 14 years of age, and all 3 had muscle atrophy affecting the distal lower more than the upper extremities. Sensation (temperature, fine touch, sharp, vibration, and proprioception) and deep tendon reflexes were preserved. Nerve conduction studies showed reduced distal motor amplitudes with preserved sensory responses, and EMG in sibs 1 and 2 showed distal chronic reinnervation. Muscle MRI in sibs 1 and 2 showed normal proximal muscles, with significant distal muscle wasting and moderate to severe fat infiltration that started at the distal third of the thigh and was most pronounced in the calves bilaterally. A biopsy of the left vastus lateralis in sib 2 showed neurogenic atrophy with scattered angular atrophic fibers, occasionally in groups, and abundant pyknotic nuclear clumps, without ragged red fibers or cytochrome oxidase-negative fibers. The sibs had a pure length-dependent distal motor neuropathy with no sensory involvement and no evidence of demyelination. They had no developmental delay, cognitive impairment, or upper motor neuron features, which set them apart from previously reported patients with COQ7-related CoQ10 deficiency. Affected sibs 2 and 3, and their unaffected sib 4, had been deaf since childhood. The deafness did not segregate with the motor neuropathy and was associated with a homozygous GJB2 c.35delG variant. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

In fibroblasts from patient II-2, Jacquier et al. (2023) found that CoQ10 fell from 71.5 pmol/mg protein in controls to 12.5 pmol/mg protein, and that 6-demethoxy-CoQ10 (6-DMQ) accumulated. 6-DMQ also accumulated in this patient's plasma.

Smith et al. (2023) found accumulation of DMQ10 in cultured fibroblasts from the affected sibs. On average, the sibs' fibroblasts had 87% less CoQ10 than fibroblasts from their father and unaffected sib, and about 85% less COQ7 protein. In sib 2, muscle had no detectable COQ7 and contained 67% less CoQ10 than control muscle (63 vs 192.7 ng/mg protein), but no DMQ10 was detected.

### Inheritance

HMNR9 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. In the family reported by Jacquier et al. (2023), the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for a COQ7 mutation and their unaffected parents were heterozygous. In the consanguineous Syrian family reported by Smith et al. (2023), the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for a COQ7 mutation, while the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 sibs from a nonconsanguineous Portuguese family with HMNR9, Jacquier et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.3G-T mutation in the start codon of COQ7 isoform 1 (p.Met1?). The unaffected parents were heterozygous. The variant was absent from the ExAC database and occurred once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. In transfected SH-EP neuroblastoma cells, the mutant reduced COQ7 protein levels by 85% without changing mRNA levels or mitochondrial localization, and the authors concluded that the mutation most likely reduces translation. In patient fibroblasts, COQ7 protein was barely detectable.

Liu et al. (2023) identified biallelic variants in the COQ7 gene in 2 Chinese families with distal hereditary motor neuropathy.

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Syrian family with HMNR9, Smith et al. (2023) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1A-G start-loss variant in the COQ7 gene. Duo-exome sequencing was performed in sibs 1 and 2, and the variant was then also found in sib 3. The variant segregated with the disorder: the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers. It was absent from gnomAD and was predicted to start translation at methionine-39, which would remove the cleavable mitochondrial targeting sequence. Patient fibroblasts had about 85% less COQ7 protein, and COQ7 was undetectable in patient muscle. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jacquier et al. (2023) speculated that their start-codon mutation causes a milder phenotype than the catalytic-domain COQ7 mutations seen in primary CoQ10 deficiency. They reasoned that it affects only isoform 1 and spares alternative isoforms, which could maintain basal mitochondrial COQ7 levels. An isoform translated from an alternative start codon (isoform 2) also localized to mitochondria, suggesting that it could partially compensate.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) studied 5 new patients with COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency and described 2 distinct presentations, with an emerging genotype-phenotype correlation. One was neonatal onset with severe neuromuscular, cardiorespiratory, and renal involvement. The other was late onset with progressive neuropathy, lower extremity weakness, abnormal gait, and variable developmental delay. In complementation studies with the yeast COQ7 ortholog CAT5, wild-type CAT5 fully rescued the oxidative growth defect of the cat5-delta strain, whereas CAT5 carrying the equivalents of human pathogenic variants did not. Equivalents of p.Arg54Gln, p.Arg107Trp, p.Ile66Asn, and the complex allele p.[Thr103Met;Leu111Pro] partially rescued growth, indicating that these are hypomorphic alleles.

### Pathogenesis

Jacquier et al. (2023) found that patient fibroblasts had reduced mitochondrial ATP production and respiration, increased glycolysis, and reduced proliferation in galactose medium. CoQ10 supplementation rescued respiration and partially restored proliferation. The precursor 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,4-dHB) normalized 6-DMQ levels but only slightly increased CoQ10.

In muscle from sib 2 of their family, Smith et al. (2023) found reduced maximal mitochondrial respiration and complex IV activity, increased H2O2 emission, and fewer supercomplexes containing complexes I and IV. Patient fibroblasts had reduced maximal respiration and showed a shift toward glycolysis with impaired metabolic flexibility. The authors suggested that metabolic dysfunction and mitochondrial oxidative stress may underlie the distal motor phenotype.

### Diagnosis

Jacquier et al. (2023) proposed plasma 6-DMQ as a marker to confirm the pathogenicity of COQ7 variants and recommended adding COQ7 to peripheral neuropathy gene panels.

### Clinical Management

Jacquier et al. (2023) proposed oral CoQ10 supplementation as a potential therapy. One patient reported that her symptoms stabilized while she took CoQ10 (30 mg/day) between 15 and 18 years of age, and that they worsened again after it was stopped. The authors judged 2,4-dHB supplementation impractical for clinical use.

### Animal Model

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) found that 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,4-diHB) rescued the growth defect of both leaky and severe cat5-delta yeast mutants. Overexpression of COQ8 together with 2,4-diHB acted synergistically to restore oxidative growth and correct the respiratory defect.
