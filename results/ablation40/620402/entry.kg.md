### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is caused by homozygous mutation in the COQ7 gene (601683) on chromosome 16p12.3.

### Description

Autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is a progressive length-dependent motor axonal neuropathy. It begins in late childhood, between about 9 and 14 years of age, with walking difficulties and distal weakness and atrophy. The lower limbs are affected more than the upper limbs, and sensation is preserved. Some patients have pes cavus or pyramidal signs (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported 3 Portuguese sibs (2 brothers and a sister, aged 36, 25, and 42 years) with an axonal, recessively inherited distal hereditary motor neuropathy. Their parents were healthy and nonconsanguineous. Onset was between ages 9 and 12 years with walking difficulties. Progressive distal weakness followed, predominantly in the lower limbs, with later hand involvement. All 3 sibs had amyotrophy and pes cavus. Sensory examination was normal, and there was no cognitive disorder. Two patients had pyramidal signs: one had brisk reflexes and a Hoffman sign, and another had a left Babinski sign. Achilles reflexes were abolished in 2 patients. Two patients walked without help, and the eldest used ankle-foot orthoses. In 2 patients, electrophysiologic studies showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy predominantly affecting the distal lower limbs, with fibrillations and neurogenic recruitment. In the index patient, brain and spinal cord MRI, echocardiography, ECG, and audiometry were normal, and creatine kinase was mildly elevated (215 U/l). ⚠︎

Smith et al. (2023) reported 3 sibs (a 24-year-old woman and men aged 23 and 12 years) with a progressive, severe length-dependent motor neuropathy. Their parents were Syrian first cousins. Distal weakness developed by 10 to 14 years of age. Muscle atrophy affected the distal lower limbs more than the upper limbs. Sensation was preserved in all modalities, and deep tendon reflexes were preserved. The patients had no developmental delay, cognitive impairment, or upper motor neuron features. Gene panels for Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, SMA, distal HMN, and mtDNA were negative. In all 3 sibs, electrodiagnostic testing showed reduced distal motor amplitudes with preserved sensory responses. Needle EMG showed distal chronic reinnervation without active denervation, and there was no evidence of demyelination. In 2 sibs, muscle MRI showed normal proximal muscles with distal muscle wasting. Moderate to severe fat infiltration began at the distal third of the thigh and was most pronounced in the calves, particularly the soleus and gastrocnemius. In 1 sib, a vastus lateralis biopsy showed neurogenic atrophy with angular atrophic fibers, pyknotic nuclear clumps, group atrophy, and fiber type grouping. There were no ragged red or cytochrome oxidase-negative fibers.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) reported 5 new patients with COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency and described 2 distinct presentations. One was a neonatal-onset form with severe neuromuscular, cardiorespiratory, and renal involvement. The other was a late-onset form with progressive neuropathy, lower extremity weakness, abnormal gait, and variable developmental delay.

### Other Features

In the Syrian family reported by Smith et al. (2023), 2 of the affected sibs and the unaffected sib had congenital deafness. The deafness did not segregate with the motor neuropathy. It was explained by a homozygous GJB2 c.35delG (p.Gly12ValfsTer2) variant, for which both parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Biochemical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) found decreased CoQ10 in fibroblasts from the index patient (12.5 vs 71.5 pmol/mg protein in controls). The fibroblasts accumulated 6-demethoxycoenzyme Q10 (6-DMQ), the COQ7 substrate, and 6-DMQ was also detected in the patient's plasma. The authors suggested that plasma 6-DMQ accumulation could be used to confirm the pathogenicity of COQ7 variants.

Smith et al. (2023) found that fibroblasts from the 3 affected sibs had on average 87% lower CoQ10 than fibroblasts from the carrier father and the unaffected sib. Patient fibroblasts accumulated the COQ7 substrate DMQ10, which was undetectable in carriers. Muscle from 1 sib had 67% lower CoQ10 than a control (63 vs 192.7 ng/mg protein), and DMQ10 was not detectable in muscle. COQ7 protein was reduced by about 85% in patient fibroblasts and was undetectable in muscle, whereas PDSS2 levels were unchanged. In permeabilized muscle from 1 affected sib, FCCP-induced maximal respiration and complex IV activity were lower than in controls. The same muscle showed higher H2O2 emission and decreased complex I- and IV-containing supercomplexes, whereas complex I and I+II respiration were near normal. Patient fibroblasts had reduced maximal and spare respiratory capacity, increased basal extracellular acidification, a glycolysis-favoring bioenergetic profile, and impaired metabolic flexibility. They nevertheless remained viable in galactose medium.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HMNR9 in the families reported by Jacquier et al. (2023) and Smith et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the nonconsanguineous Portuguese family reported by Jacquier et al. (2023), the affected sibs were homozygous for a COQ7 mutation and both parents were heterozygous. In the consanguineous Syrian family reported by Smith et al. (2023), the affected sibs were homozygous for a COQ7 mutation, and the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers.

### Molecular Genetics

Jacquier et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to study the 3 Portuguese sibs with HMNR9. They identified a homozygous c.3G-T mutation in the start codon of COQ7 isoform 1 (NM_016138.5; p.Met1?). The mutation segregated with the disease, and both parents were heterozygous. It was absent from ExAC and was present once in heterozygous state in gnomAD (allele frequency 0.000004065). Alternative isoforms 2 and 3 use other start codons and were predicted to be unaffected. In transfected SH-EP neuroblastoma cells, the mutation reduced COQ7 protein by 85% without changing mRNA levels or subcellular localization. In patient fibroblasts, COQ7 protein was barely detectable, while mRNA was only modestly decreased (17-21%), suggesting a defect in translation initiation. A FLAG-tagged isoform 2 localized to mitochondria, suggesting that other isoforms could partially compensate.

Liu et al. (2023) reported biallelic variants in the COQ7 gene causing distal hereditary motor neuropathy in 2 Chinese families.

Smith et al. (2023) studied 3 sibs with HMNR9 from a consanguineous Syrian family. Using duo-exome sequencing with Sanger confirmation, they identified a homozygous c.1A-G mutation in the COQ7 gene (NM_016138.5), predicted to abolish the initiating methionine. The parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers, and the variant was absent from gnomAD. Translation was predicted to start at met39, which would remove the cleavable N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence and 2 additional amino acids. COQ7 protein was reduced by about 85% in patient fibroblasts and was undetectable in patient muscle.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) studied previously reported and newly found COQ7 variants in a yeast cat5-deletion model; CAT5 is the yeast ortholog of COQ7. Wildtype CAT5 completely rescued the oxidative growth defect, but yeast CAT5 carrying the equivalent human pathogenic variants did not. Variants equivalent to human p.Arg54Gln, p.Arg107Trp, p.Ile66Asn, and the complex allele p.[Thr103Met;Leu111Pro] partially rescued growth. The authors interpreted these as hypomorphic alleles.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) defined 2 distinct presentations of COQ7-related disorder: a severe neonatal-onset form and a late-onset neuropathy form. They reported an emerging genotype-phenotype correlation in which several variants behaved as hypomorphic (leaky) alleles in yeast. ⚠︎

Smith et al. (2023) stated that their family represented a phenotype distinct from previously reported COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency. Earlier cases had demyelinating and axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, developmental delay, and upper motor neuron features. The authors described their family as the first report of pure distal hereditary motor neuropathy caused by COQ7. Jacquier et al. (2023) speculated that disease caused by their start-codon mutation was milder than disease caused by previously reported catalytic-domain COQ7 mutations because alternative isoforms were spared.

### Pathogenesis

Jacquier et al. (2023) showed that patient fibroblasts had reduced mitochondrial ATP production (42% vs 56-63% in controls) and increased glycolytic ATP production. The fibroblasts also had reduced basal and maximal respiration and reduced proliferation in galactose medium. Treatment with 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid normalized 6-DMQ and slightly increased CoQ10. CoQ10 supplementation rescued respiration and proliferation.

Smith et al. (2023) postulated that the metabolic dysfunction and increased oxidative stress (elevated H2O2 emission) associated with CoQ10 deficiency contribute to the selective distal motor phenotype. They suggested that the increased H2O2 emission may be due to loss of nuclear-located COQ7.

### Clinical Management

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported that the affected sister's symptoms stabilized between ages 15 and 18 years while she was taking CoQ10 (30 mg/day). Progression resumed after she stopped supplementation at 18 years. The authors suggested oral CoQ10 supplementation as a potential therapy.

### Animal Model

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) found that supplementation with 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,4-diHB) rescued the oxidative growth defect of cat5-deleted yeast carrying either leaky or severe COQ7-equivalent mutants. COQ8 overexpression combined with 2,4-diHB acted synergistically to restore oxidative growth and correct the respiratory defect.
