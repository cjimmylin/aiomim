### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is caused by homozygous mutation in the COQ7 gene (601683) on chromosome 16p12.3.

### Description

Autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is a length-dependent, predominantly pure motor axonal neuropathy that begins in late childhood or early adolescence. It causes progressive distal weakness and atrophy, first in the lower limbs and later in the hands, with walking difficulty, foot drop, and pes cavus. Sensation and cognition are preserved (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023). Some patients have pyramidal signs (Jacquier et al., 2023). Patient cells show reduced COQ7 protein and coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), with accumulation of the COQ7 substrate demethoxy-CoQ10 (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported 2 brothers and a sister, born in Portugal to nonconsanguineous healthy Portuguese parents, with axonal distal hereditary motor neuropathy. All 3 had normal early development. The index patient (II-2) was a 36-year-old man. At age 12 years, he developed walking difficulties with stiffness in the lower limbs and difficulty walking on his heels. Weakness worsened gradually and predominated in the distal lower limbs. At age 32 years, hand weakness appeared. On examination he had amyotrophy of the lower limbs and hands and could not walk on his heels or toes. Distal muscles of all 4 limbs were severely weak, foot dorsiflexion most of all. He also had pes cavus and brisk deep tendon reflexes with absent Achilles reflexes and a Hoffman sign, but no Babinski sign or spasticity. Sensory examination was normal and there was no cognitive disorder. His sister (II-3), aged 25 years, developed walking difficulties with fatigability and foot drop at age 9 years. Her weakness worsened rapidly between 12 and 15 years of age, and difficulties with precise hand movements began at age 13. She complained of difficulty feeling cold and warm temperatures on her feet, but sensory examination was normal. She had pes cavus, a positive Gowers maneuver, normal reflexes, and a Babinski sign on the left. The eldest brother (II-1), aged 42 years, had foot drop and walking and biking difficulties from age 10 years. He needed ankle-foot orthoses from about age 20 and developed hand weakness at 22 years. He had severe distal amyotrophy of the lower limbs, hand amyotrophy, complete loss of foot plantar flexion and dorsiflexion, pes cavus, and absent Achilles reflexes. Electroneuromyography in 2 of the sibs showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy that predominantly affected the distal lower limbs, with fibrillations at rest and reduced motor unit recruitment. In 1 patient, brain and spinal cord MRI, echocardiography, ECG, and audiometry were normal, and ophthalmic examination was normal apart from known myopia. Creatine kinase was mildly elevated (215 U/l). The authors noted that the pyramidal features suggested upper motor neuron involvement.

Smith et al. (2023) reported 3 sibs, children of Syrian first-cousin parents: a 24-year-old woman, a 23-year-old man, and a 12-year-old boy. Distal weakness developed by 10 to 14 years of age, with muscle atrophy more marked in the lower than the upper extremities. Sensation and deep tendon reflexes were preserved. Electrodiagnostic testing showed reduced distal motor amplitudes with preserved sensory responses, and needle EMG showed distal chronic reinnervation without active denervation. Muscle MRI in 2 of the sibs showed normal proximal musculature but moderate to severe fat infiltration, beginning in the distal third of the thigh and most pronounced in the calves. A vastus lateralis biopsy showed neurogenic atrophy with group atrophy and fiber type grouping, without ragged red fibers or cytochrome oxidase-negative fibers. The sibs had no developmental delay, cognitive impairment, or upper motor neuron features. Two of the affected sibs and an unaffected brother had been deaf since childhood. This deafness did not segregate with the neuropathy and was explained by a homozygous GJB2 mutation (c.35delG).

### Biochemical Features

In fibroblasts from patient II-2, Jacquier et al. (2023) found that CoQ10 was reduced to 12.5 pmol/mg protein, compared with 71.5 pmol/mg protein in controls. The COQ7 substrate 6-demethoxy-CoQ10 (6-DMQ) appeared in both the patient's fibroblasts and his plasma.

Smith et al. (2023) found demethoxyubiquinone-10 (DMQ10) in cultured fibroblasts from all 3 affected sibs but not from their father or unaffected sib. On average, the affected sibs' fibroblasts had 87% less CoQ10 than those of the father and unaffected sib. Muscle from 1 patient had 67% less CoQ10 than control muscle (63 vs 192.7 ng/mg protein), but no DMQ10 peak was detected.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HMNR9 in the families reported by Jacquier et al. (2023) and Smith et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In both families, affected sibs were homozygous for a COQ7 mutation and the unaffected parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Molecular Genetics

By whole-exome sequencing of a Portuguese family, Jacquier et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.3G-T transversion in the start codon of COQ7 isoform 1 (p.Met1?) in 3 affected sibs with HMNR9. The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and the unaffected parents were heterozygous. It was absent from the ExAC database and present once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. The mutation disrupts the canonical ATG start codon of isoform 1, which is the main isoform in the spinal cord. It is not expected to affect isoforms 2 and 3, which use alternative start codons. In transfected SH-EP neuroblastoma cells, the mutation reduced COQ7 protein by 85% without changing mRNA levels or subcellular localization. In patient fibroblasts, COQ7 protein was barely detectable. Isoform 2 was expressed and colocalized with mitochondria, and the authors suggested that the alternative isoforms might partially compensate.

Liu et al. (2023) reported biallelic variants in the COQ7 gene in 2 Chinese families with distal hereditary motor neuropathy.

By exome sequencing of 2 affected sibs from a consanguineous Syrian family with HMNR9, Smith et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.1A-G transition in the COQ7 gene. The third affected sib was also homozygous. The parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. The variant was absent from gnomAD, and sequencing of muscle cDNA from 1 patient confirmed the change. The mutation was predicted to abolish the initiating methionine so that translation would start at methionine-39, removing the cleavable mitochondrial targeting presequence. Immunoblotting showed COQ7 levels 85 +/- 7% lower in fibroblasts from affected sibs than in carrier relatives, and no COQ7 was detected in muscle from 1 patient.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jacquier et al. (2023) contrasted their patients with previously reported patients carrying COQ7 mutations in the catalytic domain. Those patients had severe phenotypes beginning at birth or in early childhood, with developmental delay and often multiorgan failure. The authors speculated that the start-codon mutation in their family caused a milder, isolated motor neuropathy because it affects only isoform 1 and spares the alternative isoforms, which could maintain a basal level of mitochondrial COQ7.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) described 5 new patients with COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency and defined 2 distinct presentations, noting an emerging genotype-phenotype correlation. One presentation had neonatal onset with severe neuromuscular, cardiorespiratory, and renal involvement. The other had later onset with progressive neuropathy, lower extremity weakness, abnormal gait, and variable developmental delay. In yeast, equivalents of several human variants (p.Arg54Gln, p.Arg107Trp, p.Ile66Asn, and the complex allele p.[Thr103Met; Leu111Pro]) partially rescued the growth defect of the cat5-null strain, indicating that these are hypomorphic alleles.

### Pathogenesis

Jacquier et al. (2023) showed that fibroblasts from a patient with HMNR9 had reduced mitochondrial ATP production and reduced basal and maximal respiration, with increased glycolytic ATP production. Their proliferation was reduced in galactose medium. The CoQ10 precursor 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid normalized 6-DMQ accumulation and slightly increased CoQ10 levels (from 12.5 to 18.8 pmol/mg). CoQ10 supplementation rescued respiration and partially restored proliferation in galactose medium.

In muscle from 1 patient, Smith et al. (2023) found reduced maximal respiration and complex IV activity, increased H2O2 emission, and decreased supercomplexes containing complexes I and IV. Patient fibroblasts had reduced maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity, a bioenergetic profile favoring glycolysis, and impaired flexibility in switching ATP supply. Viability in galactose medium was preserved. The authors suggested that metabolic dysfunction and mitochondrial oxidative stress may underlie the distal motor phenotype.

### Diagnosis

Jacquier et al. (2023) proposed that accumulation of 6-DMQ in plasma could be used to confirm the pathogenicity of COQ7 variants. They recommended including COQ7 in gene panels for peripheral inherited neuropathies.

### Clinical Management

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported that 1 patient described symptom stabilization while taking CoQ10 (30 mg per day) between 15 and 18 years of age, with renewed progressive worsening after supplementation stopped at age 18. The authors suggested considering oral CoQ10 supplementation as a potential therapy. They judged 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid supplementation unfavorable for practical clinical use.
