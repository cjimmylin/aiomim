### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is caused by homozygous mutation in the COQ7 gene (601683) on chromosome 16p12.

### Description

Autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is a length-dependent, pure motor axonal neuropathy. It begins in childhood or early adolescence with walking difficulty and foot drop, followed by slowly progressive distal weakness and atrophy that affect the lower limbs before the hands. Sensation is clinically preserved, and some patients have pyramidal signs. Patient cells show reduced coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) and accumulation of the COQ7 substrate (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported 3 sibs (2 brothers and a sister) from a Portuguese family with dHMN. Their parents were healthy and not consanguineous. All 3 had normal early development. The index patient was a 36-year-old man who developed walking difficulties at age 12 years, with a feeling of stiffness in the lower limbs and difficulty walking on his heels. Weakness worsened gradually and predominated in the distal lower limbs. From age 32 years he had progressive hand weakness. On examination he had amyotrophy of the lower limbs and hands and severe distal weakness in all 4 limbs, most marked for foot dorsiflexion, together with pes cavus. Sensory examination was normal. Deep tendon reflexes were brisk in all 4 limbs except for absent Achilles reflexes, and a Hoffman sign was present without Babinski sign or spasticity. His 25-year-old sister developed walking difficulties with fatigability and foot drop at age 9 years. Her weakness worsened rapidly between 12 and 15 years of age, and her hands were affected from age 13 years. She reported difficulty feeling cold and warm on her feet, but sensory examination was normal. She had pes cavus, normal reflexes and a Babinski sign on the left foot. The 42-year-old eldest brother developed foot drop at age 10 years and needed ankle-foot orthoses from about age 20 years. Hand weakness began at age 22 years. At 42 years he had severe distal amyotrophy, complete loss of foot plantar flexion and dorsiflexion, pes cavus and absent Achilles reflexes. All 3 patients remained ambulant, and none had cranial nerve abnormalities or a cognitive disorder. Electroneuromyography in 2 patients showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy that predominantly affected the distal lower limbs. Needle examination showed fibrillations at rest and reduced motor unit recruitment. In the index patient, brain and spinal cord MRI, echocardiography, ECG and audiometry were normal, and creatine phosphokinase was mildly elevated (215 U/l; normal less than 200 U/l). Jacquier et al. (2023) suggested that the pyramidal features indicated upper motor neuron involvement.

Smith et al. (2023) reported 3 sibs (a 24-year-old woman, a 23-year-old man and a 12-year-old boy) born to Syrian parents who were first cousins. The sibs had a progressive length-dependent motor neuropathy. Distal weakness developed by 10 to 14 years of age, and muscle atrophy affected the distal lower limbs more than the upper limbs. Sensation in all modalities and deep tendon reflexes were preserved. Electrodiagnostic testing showed reduced distal motor amplitudes with preserved sensory responses, and needle EMG showed distal chronic reinnervation without active denervation. Whole-body muscle MRI in 2 sibs showed normal proximal musculature. Distal muscle wasting was significant, with moderate to severe fat infiltration starting at the distal third of the thigh and most pronounced in the calves, particularly the soleus and gastrocnemius. A vastus lateralis biopsy from 1 sib showed neurogenic atrophy, with angular atrophic fibers (occasionally in groups), abundant pyknotic nuclear clumps, group atrophy and fiber type grouping. There were no ragged red fibers or cytochrome oxidase-negative fibers. The sibs had no developmental delay, cognitive impairment or upper motor neuron features. Gene panels for Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, spinal muscular atrophy, distal hereditary motor neuropathy and mtDNA were negative.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) identified 5 new patients with COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency. They described 2 presentations. The first was neonatal-onset, with severe neuromuscular, cardiorespiratory and renal involvement. The second was late-onset, with progressive neuropathy, lower extremity weakness, abnormal gait and variable developmental delay.

### Other Features

In the family reported by Smith et al. (2023), 2 of the 3 affected sibs and their unaffected brother had been deaf since childhood. The deafness did not segregate with the motor neuropathy and was explained by a homozygous c.35delG variant in the GJB2 gene. Both parents were heterozygous carriers of this variant.

### Biochemical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) used HPLC-MS/MS to study fibroblasts from the index patient. CoQ10 was markedly decreased (12.5 pmol/mg protein, compared with 71.5 pmol/mg protein in control fibroblasts), and 6-demethoxycoenzyme Q10 (6-DMQ), the COQ7 substrate, was present. 6-DMQ was also detected in the patient's plasma.

Smith et al. (2023) found that cultured fibroblasts from the 3 affected sibs had on average 87% less CoQ10 than fibroblasts from their father and unaffected sib. The fibroblasts from the affected sibs also contained a significant amount of 5-demethoxyubiquinone-10 (DMQ10), which was not detectable in cells from the father or the unaffected sib. In muscle from 1 affected sib, CoQ10 was 67% lower than in muscle from an unrelated donor (63 vs 192.7 ng/mg protein), but no DMQ10 was detected.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HMNR9 in the families reported by Jacquier et al. (2023) and Smith et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Jacquier et al. (2023) described a nonconsanguineous family with 3 affected sibs who were homozygous for a COQ7 mutation and whose unaffected parents were heterozygous. Smith et al. (2023) described a consanguineous family in which 3 affected sibs were homozygous and the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 Portuguese sibs with HMNR9, Jacquier et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing of the sibs and their parents to identify a homozygous c.3G-T transversion in the COQ7 gene. The variant disrupts the canonical ATG start codon of isoform 1 (NM_016138.5), changing it to ATT (p.Met1?). Sanger sequencing confirmed the genotypes. The unaffected parents were heterozygous. The variant was absent from the ExAC database and present once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. The alternative start codons used by isoforms 2 and 3 were predicted to be unaffected. In transfected SH-EP neuroblastoma cells, mutant COQ7 protein levels were reduced by 85% compared with wildtype, whereas mRNA levels did not differ significantly, which the authors interpreted as a defect in translation. Subcellular localization was similar to wildtype. In fibroblasts from the index patient, COQ7 mRNA was only modestly decreased (by 21% and 17% with 2 primer pairs), but COQ7 protein was barely detectable. The fibroblasts showed reduced CoQ10, accumulation of 6-DMQ and reduced mitochondrial respiration.

Liu et al. (2023) reported biallelic variants in the COQ7 gene in 2 Chinese families with distal hereditary motor neuropathy.

In 3 sibs with HMNR9 from a consanguineous Syrian family, Smith et al. (2023) used duo-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1A-G transition in the COQ7 gene (NM_016138.5). The variant was predicted to result in loss of the initiating methionine. It was confirmed by sequencing of COQ7 cDNA from muscle. The parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from gnomAD. In fibroblasts from the affected sibs, COQ7 protein was 85% lower than in the carrier father and sib, CoQ10 was decreased and DMQ10 accumulated. COQ7 protein was undetectable in muscle from 1 affected sib. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jacquier et al. (2023) noted that previously reported COQ7 mutations were located in the catalytic domain and therefore affected all COQ7 isoforms. These mutations had caused severe phenotypes at birth or in early childhood, with developmental delay. In contrast, the start codon mutation in their family affected only isoform 1. The authors speculated that the alternative isoforms, one of which they showed could reach the mitochondria, maintain a basal level of mitochondrial COQ7, which would explain the milder pure dHMN phenotype.

Smith et al. (2023) noted that earlier patients with COQ7-related CoQ10 deficiency had sensorimotor polyneuropathy with demyelinating and axonal features, developmental delay and upper motor neuron features. In contrast, their patients had a pure length-dependent motor neuropathy without sensory involvement, demyelination, cognitive impairment or upper motor neuron features. The authors cautioned that other features might develop as the sibs get older.

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) defined 2 distinct presentations of COQ7-related disorder: a neonatal-onset form and a late-onset form with progressive neuropathy. Using a yeast model, they showed that cat5-null yeast carrying the equivalents of human COQ7 variants p.Arg54Gln, p.Arg107Trp, p.Ile66Asn and the complex allele p.[Thr103Met;Leu111Pro] partially rescued the oxidative growth defect, indicating that these are hypomorphic alleles. The authors described an emerging genotype-phenotype correlation.

### Pathogenesis

Jacquier et al. (2023) used Seahorse assays on fibroblasts from the index patient. Mitochondrial ATP production was 42%, compared with 63.3% or 56.4% in control fibroblasts, and glycolytic ATP production was 68%, compared with 36.7% or 43.6% in controls. Basal and maximal respiration were reduced, and both were rescued by supplementing the culture medium with CoQ10. In galactose medium, the patient's fibroblasts showed a 50% decrease in cell number compared with controls, with no evidence of apoptosis. Supplementation with 10 microM CoQ10 partially restored growth. The CoQ10 precursor 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid normalized 6-DMQ accumulation and slightly increased CoQ10 levels in the patient's fibroblasts (from 12.5 to 18.8 pmol/mg).

Smith et al. (2023) predicted that the c.1A-G mutation would cause translation to start at methionine-39. The resulting protein would lack the cleavable N-terminal mitochondrial targeting presequence and 2 additional amino acids, preventing its incorporation into the inner mitochondrial membrane. In permeabilized muscle from 1 affected sib, FCCP-induced maximal respiration and complex IV activity were lower than in controls, H2O2 emission was higher, and supercomplexes containing complexes I and IV were decreased. Fibroblasts from the affected sibs had preserved basal respiration but reduced maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity, higher basal extracellular acidification, a bioenergetic profile favoring glycolysis and an impaired ability to switch between ATP production pathways. Viability of the fibroblasts in galactose medium was not reduced. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Jacquier et al. (2023) proposed that accumulation of 6-DMQ in plasma could be used to confirm the pathogenicity of COQ7 variants. They also suggested that COQ7 be included in the gene panels used to diagnose peripheral inherited neuropathies.

### Clinical Management

In the family reported by Jacquier et al. (2023), the affected sister reported that her symptoms stabilized while she was treated with CoQ10 (30 mg per day) between 15 and 18 years of age. Progression resumed at age 18 years, when supplementation was stopped. On the basis of this observation and the rescue of patient cells in vitro, the authors suggested that CoQ10 supplementation be considered as a potential therapy. ⚠︎
