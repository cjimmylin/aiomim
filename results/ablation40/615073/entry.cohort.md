### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11. ⚠︎

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. Onset is usually in adulthood and most often in the neck (cervical dystonia). The dystonia may stay focal or spread to segmental or generalized forms, often involving cranial and speech muscles. Brachial onset has not been reported (Fuchs et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 individuals from 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected, with a mean age at onset of 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 28 mutation carriers with definite dystonia from 8 families. Seven families were of mixed European Caucasian ancestry and 1 was of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite ancestry. Mean age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of carriers, and 93% had neck involvement at their last examination. However, only 46% still had focal dystonia. Cranial involvement was seen in 57% and speech involvement in 44%. No patient had brachial onset, and only 32% developed arm involvement. The authors noted that this pattern distinguished GNAL dystonia from DYT6. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2013) gave details for each family. In family D1, onset in the 6 relatives of the proband ranged from 19 to 50 years. Each had segmental dystonia beginning in the neck, and in 1 relative each the larynx and trunk, or the face, were also involved at onset. The proband, a man, had onset in the legs at age 7 years and developed generalized dystonia. In the Mennonite family P, onset ranged from 25 to 48 years. Five members had neck onset, with focal, segmental, or generalized dystonia. The female proband had leg onset at 25 years and developed generalized dystonia. In family D2, the proband had jaw onset at 39 years. Two relatives had neck onset at 33 and 18 years, and a fourth relative had focal tongue dystonia beginning at 11 years. In family T, 2 men had focal neck dystonia beginning at 25 and 42 years, and a woman had segmental dystonia with laryngeal onset at 25 years. The other families (S, H, B, and N) consisted of patients with focal or segmental dystonia with neck onset, with ages at onset between 17 and 54 years.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DYT25 is autosomal dominant. Fuchs et al. (2013) showed that heterozygous GNAL mutations segregated with the disease in all families for which DNA from multiple affected individuals was available. Dystonia was inherited equally from the maternal and paternal sides.

### Mapping

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that GNAL lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci. They suggested that loss of GNAL may contribute to the dystonia seen in patients with 18p deletions.

### Heterogeneity

Bressman et al. (1994) used linkage data to exclude the DYT1 region on chromosome 9q34 in a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset torsion dystonia that mainly began in the neck. They concluded that a gene other than DYT1 causes some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) used exome sequencing in 2 families with primary torsion dystonia and identified heterozygous mutations in the GNAL gene. Family P carried a nonsense mutation (S293X) and family D1 carried a missense mutation (V137M). Each mutation segregated with the disease in its family. Sanger sequencing of 39 additional multiplex primary torsion dystonia families that were negative for DYT1 and DYT6 found GNAL mutations in 6 more families (19%). These included a missense mutation (E155K), a nonsense mutation (R21X), 2 frameshift insertions, an in-frame 9-bp deletion, and a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C). None of the variants were present in dbSNP135, in about 3,500 European exomes, or in 572 control chromosomes. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2013) studied the mutants with a BRET assay of Galpha-olf coupling to the D1 dopamine receptor. The S293X mutant failed to support any D1R-driven responses. The E155K and V137M mutants showed intermediate phenotypes that were largely consistent with impaired association with G-beta-gamma subunits. A control variant, V16F, behaved like wildtype. The authors suggested that these results pointed to loss of function.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no genotype/phenotype correlation by mutation type, and phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation. There was no apparent phenotypic difference by parental origin of the mutation. However, the authors said that further study was needed to determine whether parental origin affects penetrance or expression.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that Galpha-olf replaces Galpha-s in striatal medium spiny neurons, where it couples D1 dopamine and A2A adenosine receptors to adenylyl cyclase type 5. They suggested that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1R and/or A2AR transmission, located postsynaptically in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DYSTONIA 25**  
GNAL, VAL137MET [c.409G>A; p.V137M]

Fuchs et al. (2013) used exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.409G-A transition in the GNAL gene in family D1, a mixed European Caucasian family with primary torsion dystonia. The mutation results in a val137-to-met (V137M) substitution. It was among 20 missense variants shared by all 4 exome-sequenced members and segregated with the disease in the family, which had 7 affected members. The male proband had leg onset at age 7 years and developed generalized dystonia. The other 6 affected members had segmental dystonia with onset between 19 and 50 years, beginning in the neck. In a BRET assay of D1 dopamine receptor coupling, the V137M mutant showed an intermediate phenotype that was largely consistent with impaired association with G-beta-gamma subunits.
