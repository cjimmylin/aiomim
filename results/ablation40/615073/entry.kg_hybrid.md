### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is a form of primary torsion dystonia (PTD), in which dystonia, with or without tremor, is the only neurologic sign. In the families reported by Fuchs et al. (2013), the disorder was mainly cervical dystonia. Age at onset varied widely, and in more than half of patients the dystonia spread to other muscles, especially the facial muscles.

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 members across 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected. Mean age at onset was 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 28 individuals with definite dystonia from 8 multiplex families with GNAL mutations. All were Caucasian of mixed European ancestry. Mean age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of carriers, and 93% had neck involvement at the last examination. However, most had spread to other sites, and only 46% still had focal dystonia at the last examination. Cranial involvement occurred in 57% and speech involvement in 44%. No patient had brachial onset, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement. The authors noted that this pattern distinguished the disorder from DYT6. In a few patients, onset was in the legs, jaw, tongue, larynx, or trunk, and some patients developed generalized dystonia.

### Inheritance

In the families reported by Fuchs et al. (2013), dystonia was inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Because GNAL had been suggested to be imprinted, the authors examined the parental origin of the mutations. Dystonia was inherited equally from the maternal and paternal sides, and maternally and paternally inherited cases showed no apparent phenotypic differences. The authors noted that further study was needed to determine whether parental origin affects penetrance or expression.

### Cytogenetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that GNAL lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 locus for focal dystonia and the DYT15 locus for myoclonus dystonia. Because dystonia occurs in patients with 18p deletions, they suggested that loss of GNAL may contribute to dystonia in these cases.

### Heterogeneity

In a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset, mainly cervical-onset torsion dystonia, Bressman et al. (1994) used linkage analysis to exclude the region containing the DYT1 locus on chromosome 9q34. They concluded that a gene other than DYT1 is responsible for some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. In family P, a whole-genome scan had previously identified 3 regions of possible linkage (lod scores, 1.5 to 2.1). Within these regions, the authors identified a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the GNAL gene (S293X) that cosegregated with dystonia. In family D1, they identified a heterozygous missense mutation in GNAL (V137M) among the 20 missense variants shared by all 4 sequenced members. This mutation segregated with the disease and was not found in 572 control chromosomes. Neither variant was present in about 3,500 European exomes in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project database.

Fuchs et al. (2013) then used Sanger sequencing to analyze the GNAL coding region in 39 additional multiplex PTD families of mixed European origin who were negative for DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. They identified 6 further novel mutations, accounting for 19% of these families: a nonsense mutation (R21X), 2 frameshift mutations (S95fsX110 and R198fsX210), a missense mutation (E155K), an in-frame deletion of 3 amino acids (P102-V104del), and a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C). None of the variants were found in dbSNP135, about 3,500 European exomes, or 572 control chromosomes. The mutations segregated with the disease in all families for which DNA from multiple affected individuals was available. In a cell-based BRET assay, the S293X mutant failed to support any D1 receptor-driven responses. The V137M and E155K mutants showed intermediate phenotypes that were largely consistent with impaired association with Gbeta-gamma subunits.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no genotype/phenotype correlation by mutation type among 28 affected carriers from 8 families. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same GNAL mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) used a cell-based BRET reporter assay to study the Galpha-olf cycle after stimulation of the dopamine D1 receptor (D1R). The S293X mutant abolished D1R-driven responses, and the E155K and V137M mutants showed intermediate impairment. In contrast, V16F, a variant found in a single control subject, behaved like wildtype. The authors concluded that the functional findings suggested loss of function. They proposed that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1R and/or adenosine A2A receptor transmission, located postsynaptically in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.

Fuchs et al. (2013) studied family P, a family of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage with primary torsion dystonia (DYT25). Using exome sequencing, they identified a heterozygous c.878C-A transversion in the GNAL gene, resulting in a ser293-to-ter (S293X) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with dystonia in the family and was not found in about 3,500 European exomes. In a BRET assay, the S293X mutant failed to support any D1 receptor-driven responses. Six affected family members had onset between 25 and 48 years of age, mostly in the neck.

**.0008 DYSTONIA 25**  
GNAL, IVS3AS, T-C, -5 [c.274-5T>C]

Fuchs et al. (2013) studied family B with primary torsion dystonia (DYT25). They identified a heterozygous c.274-5T-C transition upstream of exon 4 of the GNAL gene (chr18:11,753,820, hg19), a possible splice site mutation. Bioinformatic analysis suggested that the wildtype sequence is 10 times more likely than the mutant sequence to be found near an acceptor splice site. The authors could not confirm this in patient samples because lymphoblasts were the only tissue available and GNAL expression was undetectable in them.
