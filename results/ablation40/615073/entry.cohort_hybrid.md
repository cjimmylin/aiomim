### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant form of primary torsion dystonia in which dystonia is the only neurologic sign. Fuchs et al. (2013) described the phenotype in GNAL mutation carriers as mainly cervical dystonia. Age at onset varied widely, and in over half of carriers the dystonia spread to other muscles, especially the facial muscles. Speech involvement was also common.

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 individuals from 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected, with a mean age at onset of 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) studied 28 individuals with definite dystonia and complete clinical information from 8 multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia and GNAL mutations. All carriers were Caucasian of mixed European ancestry; one family (family P) had mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage. Families D1, P, and S had been reported previously. Mean age at dystonia onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of carriers, and 93% had neck involvement at the final examination. Most carriers progressed to have dystonia at other sites, and only 46% still had focal dystonia at the last examination. Cranial involvement was present in 57% and speech involvement in 44%. No carrier had brachial onset, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement. Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that this distinguished the GNAL phenotype from DYT6. Less common sites of onset included the legs, larynx, jaw, and tongue. In 2 families (D1 and P), the probands had generalized dystonia that began in the legs, at ages 7 and 25 years.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DYT25 is autosomal dominant. Fuchs et al. (2013) found heterozygous GNAL mutations that segregated with dystonia in multiplex families with dominant inheritance. Dystonia was inherited equally through the maternal and paternal lines.

### Mapping

In the family with adult-onset, mainly cervical-onset dystonia that they studied, Bressman et al. (1994) found that linkage data excluded the region containing the DYT1 locus on chromosome 9q34.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported that a previous whole-genome scan of family P had identified 3 regions of possible linkage, with lod scores ranging from 1.5 to 2.1. They noted that GNAL lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci. They also suggested that loss of GNAL may contribute to the dystonia seen in patients with 18p deletions.

### Heterogeneity

Bressman et al. (1994) concluded that a gene other than DYT1 is responsible for some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia (families P and D1). In family P, they found a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the GNAL gene (S293X) that lay within a region of possible linkage and segregated with dystonia. In family D1, they found a heterozygous missense mutation (V137M) that segregated with the disease. The V137M mutation was not found in 572 control chromosomes. Neither mutation was present in about 3,500 European exomes in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project database.

Fuchs et al. (2013) then Sanger-sequenced the entire GNAL coding region in 39 additional multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia. These families were of mixed European origin and negative for DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. Six families (19%) carried heterozygous GNAL mutations: a nonsense mutation (R21X), 2 frameshift mutations (S95fsX110 and R198fsX210), a missense mutation (E155K), an in-frame deletion of 3 amino acids (P102-V104del), and a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C). None of the variants were found in dbSNP135, about 3,500 European exomes, or 572 control chromosomes. In all families with DNA available from multiple affected individuals, the mutations segregated with the disease. The V137 and E155 residues were completely conserved in GNAL orthologs. In a BRET assay of Galpha-olf coupling to the dopamine D1 receptor in HEK293 cells, the S293X mutant supported no D1R-driven responses. The V137M and E155K mutants showed intermediate phenotypes, largely consistent with impaired association with Gbeta-gamma subunits. A V16F variant found in a single control behaved like wildtype. The authors concluded that the findings suggested loss of function.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no genotype/phenotype correlation by mutation type, and phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation. Because GNAL had been suggested to be imprinted, they examined the parental origin of the mutations. They found no apparent phenotypic differences between maternally and paternally inherited cases.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that in striatal medium spiny neurons, Galpha-olf couples dopamine D1 receptors (D1R) and adenosine A2A receptors (A2AR) to the activation of adenylyl cyclase type 5. They proposed that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1R and/or A2AR transmission as a possible cause of dystonia. Unlike the mutations in dopa-responsive dystonia, GNAL mutations would place the primary abnormality postsynaptically, in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.

In affected members of a family with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia (family P), Fuchs et al. (2013) found a heterozygous c.878C-A transversion in the GNAL gene, resulting in a ser293-to-ter (S293X) substitution. The family had mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and segregated with dystonia in the family. It was not present in about 3,500 European exomes. In a BRET assay, the mutant failed to support any D1 receptor-driven responses.

The mutation was found by exome sequencing and segregated with the disease. It was not observed in 572 control chromosomes or in about 3,500 European exomes. The proband was a man who developed focal dystonia with onset in the neck at age 20 years.

**.0008 DYSTONIA 25**  
GNAL, IVS3AS, T-C, -5 [c.274-5T>C]

In affected members of a family with primary torsion dystonia (family B), Fuchs et al. (2013) found a heterozygous c.274-5T-C transition upstream of exon 4 of the GNAL gene, which they described as a possible splice site mutation. Bioinformatic analysis suggested that the wildtype sequence was 10 times more likely than the mutant sequence to be found near an acceptor splice site. However, the authors could not test this in patient samples because GNAL expression was undetectable in lymphoblasts.
