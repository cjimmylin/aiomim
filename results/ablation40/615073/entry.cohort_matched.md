### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant form of primary torsion dystonia. Onset is usually in adulthood but can range from childhood to late adulthood. The disorder mainly begins in the neck as cervical dystonia. In over half of patients it spreads to other muscles, especially the cranial and facial muscles, and speech is often affected (Fuchs et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Fuchs et al. (2013) studied 28 carriers of GNAL mutations with definite dystonia from 8 families. Family P was of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite ancestry, and the other families were Caucasians of mixed European origin. Mean age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of patients, and 93% had neck involvement at final examination. Only 46% still had focal dystonia at last examination, 57% had cranial involvement, and 44% had speech involvement. No patient had onset in the arm, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement. The authors noted that this distinguished the GNAL phenotype from DYT6. Overall, the phenotype was mainly cervical dystonia, with a fairly broad range of onset ages and spread to other muscles, especially facial muscles, in over half of patients. ⚠︎

In family D1 of Fuchs et al. (2013), 7 members were affected, with onset between 7 and 50 years of age. The male proband had dystonia beginning in the legs at age 7 years, and it became generalized; he was 69 years old at last report. The other 6 affected members had segmental dystonia that began in the neck between 19 and 50 years of age. In 1 of them, onset involved the larynx and trunk as well as the neck, and in another it involved the face as well as the neck.

In family P (Fuchs et al., 2013), of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite ancestry, 6 members were affected. The female proband developed dystonia in the leg at age 25 years, and it became generalized. In the other 5 members, dystonia began in the neck between 32 and 48 years of age. It remained focal in 3 of them, was segmental in 1, and became generalized in a man with onset at age 35 years.

Fuchs et al. (2013) also described 6 smaller families of mixed European origin. In family S, 2 males developed neck-onset dystonia at 18 and 17 years of age; it was focal in one and segmental in the other. In family D2, 4 females were affected. Two had segmental dystonia beginning in the neck at 33 and 18 years. The proband had segmental dystonia beginning in the jaw at age 39. The fourth had focal dystonia of the tongue beginning at age 11. In family H, 3 members had focal cervical dystonia, with onset at 31 to 47 years of age. In family B, a man with onset at 54 years had focal neck dystonia, and the female proband had segmental dystonia beginning in the neck at 36 years. In family T, 2 males had focal neck dystonia beginning at 25 and 42 years, and a female had segmental dystonia beginning in the larynx at 25 years. In family N, the male proband had focal neck dystonia beginning at age 20 years.

### Inheritance

Fuchs et al. (2013) showed that the transmission pattern of DYT25 is autosomal dominant. They found heterozygous GNAL mutations in multiplex families, and the mutations segregated with the disease. Dystonia was inherited equally from mothers and fathers.

### Mapping

In family P, a previous genome scan had found 3 regions of possible linkage, with lod scores of 1.5 to 2.1 (Fuchs et al., 2013). Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that GNAL lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci. They suggested that loss of GNAL may contribute to the dystonia seen in patients with deletions of 18p.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia (families P and D1). They found heterozygous mutations in the GNAL gene: a nonsense mutation (S293X) in family P and a missense mutation (V137M) in family D1. Both mutations segregated with the disease. Sanger sequencing of GNAL in 39 additional multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia found 6 more heterozygous mutations (19%). These families were of mixed European origin and negative for DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. The 6 mutations were E155K, an S95fs frameshift, an R198fs frameshift, a splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C), R21X, and an in-frame deletion of pro102 to val104. Segregation analysis confirmed that the mutations were transmitted with the disease. None of the variants were present in dbSNP135, in about 3,500 European exomes, or in 572 control chromosomes. Fuchs et al. (2013) used a BRET assay in HEK293 cells to measure coupling of Galpha-olf to the dopamine D1 receptor (D1R). The S293X mutant supported no D1R-driven responses. The missense mutants E155K and V137M gave intermediate results, consistent with impaired association with Gbeta-gamma subunits. A V16F variant, found in 1 control, behaved like wildtype. The authors concluded that the findings suggested loss of function. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no genotype/phenotype correlation by mutation type, and phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation. Because GNAL had been suggested to be imprinted, they examined the parental origin of the mutations. Dystonia was inherited equally from mothers and fathers, with no apparent difference in phenotype between the two.

### Pathogenesis

In medium spiny neurons, Galpha-olf couples D1R and the adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) to activation of adenylyl cyclase type 5. Fuchs et al. (2013) proposed that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1R and/or A2AR transmission as a possible cause of dystonia. Unlike mutations causing dopa-responsive dystonia, this would place the primary defect postsynaptically, in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.

### History

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 individuals from 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected, with a mean age at onset of 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia. Linkage data excluded the DYT1 region on chromosome 9q34. The authors concluded that a gene other than DYT1 is responsible for some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) used exome sequencing to study family D1, a family of mixed European origin with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. In affected members they found a heterozygous c.409G-A transition in the GNAL gene, resulting in a val137-to-met (V137M) substitution.
