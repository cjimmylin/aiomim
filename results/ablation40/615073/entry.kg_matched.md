### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. Dystonia, with or without tremor, is the only neurologic sign. The disorder usually begins in the neck, and age at onset ranges from childhood to adulthood. In more than half of patients the dystonia spreads to other sites, especially the cranial and facial muscles, and speech is often affected. Arm involvement is less common, and onset in the arm was not seen (Fuchs et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 members across 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected. Mean age at onset was 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 28 individuals with definite dystonia from 8 families with GNAL mutations. All were Caucasian of mixed European ancestry. Mean age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of patients, and 93% had neck involvement at the last examination. In most patients the dystonia had spread to other sites, and only 46% still had focal dystonia at the last examination. Cranial involvement occurred in 57% and speech involvement in 44%. No patient had onset in the arm, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement. The authors noted that this distinguished the disorder from DYT6. They summarized the phenotype as mainly cervical dystonia with a fairly broad range in age at onset. In over half of the patients the dystonia spread to other muscles, especially the facial muscles. Some patients had onset at other sites, including the legs, jaw, tongue, and larynx, and some developed generalized dystonia. Primary torsion dystonia was defined as dystonia, with or without tremor, as the only neurologic sign.

### Inheritance

Fuchs et al. (2013) observed autosomal dominant inheritance of dystonia in families with GNAL mutations. Because GNAL had been suggested to be imprinted, they examined the parental origin of the mutations. Dystonia was inherited equally from the mother's and the father's side, with no apparent phenotypic differences between maternally and paternally inherited cases.

### Cytogenetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that GNAL lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci. Because dystonia occurs in patients with 18p deletions, they suggested that loss of GNAL may contribute to dystonia in these cases.

### Heterogeneity

In a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset torsion dystonia that began mainly in the neck, Bressman et al. (1994) used linkage analysis to exclude the chromosome 9q34 region containing the DYT1 locus. They concluded that a gene other than DYT1 is responsible for some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) used exome sequencing in 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia and identified heterozygous mutations in the GNAL gene, which encodes Galpha-olf. In family P, of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage, they sequenced exomes within 3 regions of possible linkage (lod scores 1.5 to 2.1) and found a nonsense mutation (S293X). In family D1, the missense mutation V137M was among 20 missense variants shared by all 4 sequenced family members. The authors then used Sanger sequencing to screen GNAL in 39 additional multiplex families of mixed European origin who were negative for DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. They found 6 further novel heterozygous mutations, so that mutations were present in 19% of families. These included missense, nonsense, frameshift, in-frame deletion, and possible splice site mutations. None of the variants were in dbSNP135, in about 3,500 European exomes, or in 572 control chromosomes. The mutations segregated with disease in all families where DNA from multiple affected individuals was available. In a cell-based BRET assay, the S293X mutant could not support any D1 receptor-driven responses. The V137M and E155K mutants showed intermediate impairment, which was largely consistent with impaired association with Gbeta-gamma subunits. The authors concluded that the mutations cause loss of function. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no correlation between mutation type and phenotype among 28 affected individuals from 8 families with GNAL mutations. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) used a cell-based BRET assay to study the Galpha-olf cycle after stimulation of the D1 dopamine receptor (D1R). The S293X mutation abolished D1R-driven responses, and the E155K and V137M mutants showed intermediate impairment. The control variant V16F behaved like wildtype. The authors concluded that the findings suggested loss of function. They proposed that GNAL mutations implicate primary abnormalities of D1R and/or A2AR transmission, located postsynaptically in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.

In family B, which had primary torsion dystonia (DYT25), Fuchs et al.
