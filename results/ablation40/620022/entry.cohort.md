### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270) on chromosome 20q13.33.

### Description

Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in COL9A3. In a review of patients with biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants, Rad et al. (2022) found that moderate-to-profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration were consistent features. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred occasionally, and skeletal involvement, such as spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, seemed more variable.

### Clinical Features

Faletra et al. (2014) described a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, which they called the first such family caused by loss-of-function mutations in COL9A3.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy of Indian ancestry, born to consanguineous parents who were third cousins, with Stickler syndrome. He failed his newborn hearing screen and had moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, with mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. His speech delay was attributed to the hearing loss. He had progressive high myopia but no vitreous abnormalities or retinal degeneration. Femoral and tibial bowing were noted at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, normal knee epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly. Other skeletal findings were irregular vertebral endplates, flattening of the acetabula, and irregularity of the medial distal femoral epiphyses. He also had midfacial hypoplasia and autoimmune hypothyroidism. He did not have cleft palate or pes planus. His intelligence was normal, and his growth parameters had been in the normal range. He was last reported at 12 years of age. ⚠︎

Nixon et al. (2019) reported 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome due to homozygous type IX collagen variants. Two of the patients were from 1 family with a COL9A3 variant. All 6 patients were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and all had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy. Nixon et al. (2019) noted that sensorineural hearing loss is more common and more severe in recessive than in dominant Stickler syndrome.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a 3-year-old Russian boy, born to nonconsanguineous parents, with congenital hip dysplasia and congenital high bilateral myopia. He also had speech delay and grade 3-4 bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. His eye findings included vitreoretinal degeneration, with peripheral retinal pigment rearrangement and dystrophic foci, as well as vitreous floaters and a preretinal membrane. His electroretinogram was normal, and he had no retinal detachment. Radiography revealed mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia. This included mild platyspondyly, kyphosis, spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, flattened and irregular femoral heads, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. He had early-onset arthropathy of the lower limbs with joint pain, genu valgum, prominent knee joints, limited hip abduction, waddling gait, lumbar lordosis, planovalgus foot deformity, and long fingers. His facial features included a flattened nasal bridge, small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, and high palate. He did not have cleft palate, and his height was normal.

Rad et al. (2022) reported 5 patients from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. The proband from family 1 was a 28-year-old woman and was the only affected child of 3 born to first-cousin parents. She had high myopia in both eyes, vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, and retinal pigmentary changes. She also had severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss and unilateral absence of the frontal sinus on skull x-ray. Her other findings were knee pain, pes planus, genu valgum, low-set ears, short metacarpals with widened epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly of the thoracic spine. She did not have cleft palate, micrognathia, short stature, retinal detachment, or intellectual disability. ⚠︎

In family 2 of Rad et al. (2022), 2 brothers aged 65 and 57 years were affected among 4 children born to a first-cousin marriage. Both had high myopia, cataract, and a history of multiple vitreoretinal surgeries for recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments resulting from advanced vitreoretinal degeneration. Despite surgery, the older brother was considered blind without light perception in either eye. The younger brother had counting-fingers vision in one eye and no light perception in the other. Both had severe to profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and a herniated cervical disc. The older brother also had muscular atrophy. Neither had short stature.

In family 3 of Rad et al. (2022), 2 of 4 children of first-cousin parents were affected: a boy aged 11.8 years and a girl aged 3.1 years. Both had myopia and congenital, moderate to severe, progressive sensorineural hearing impairment. X-ray and detailed examination showed spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia in both children. Both had pes planus, a depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. Midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures were more pronounced in the girl. The boy complained of knee joint pain, especially when running. He also had genu valgum, bowed tibia, short metacarpals, broad hallux, irregular femoral epiphyses, and flat acetabular roofs with flattened femoral heads. Neither child had cleft palate, and none of the individuals in the study showed signs of intellectual disability.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of STL6 is autosomal recessive. Faletra et al. (2014) described an autosomal recessive Stickler family with COL9A3 mutations. In the family reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), the affected proband was homozygous for a COL9A3 mutation, while his consanguineous parents and an unaffected sister were heterozygous. Markova et al. (2021) showed that the compound heterozygous mutations in their patient were in trans. Rad et al. (2022) identified homozygous mutations in affected members of 3 consanguineous families.

Rad et al. (2022) noted that Nixon et al. had observed mild Stickler phenotypes in carrier parents, but they themselves did not observe mild phenotypes in heterozygous individuals.

### Heterogeneity

Faletra et al. (2014) noted that dominant Stickler syndrome is caused by mutations in COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and that recessive forms are caused by mutations in COL9A1 and COL9A2. Before their report, only heterozygous COL9A3 mutations had been reported in humans, in association with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia type 3, susceptibility to intervertebral disc disease, and hearing loss.

### Molecular Genetics

In an autosomal recessive Stickler family, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270): c.1176_1198del, predicted to cause the frameshift gln393-to-cys (Q393Cfs*25).

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to study the proband from consanguineous Stickler syndrome family R08-308. They identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene. The parents and an unaffected sib were all heterozygous for the change. The authors predicted that the frameshift would cause premature termination of translation and absence of the COL9A3 gene product. The change was not found in public SNP databases. The proband was also heterozygous for an arg127-to-his (R127H) variant in the GJB2 gene (121011), whose significance was uncertain. ⚠︎

In 2 patients from a family with recessive Stickler syndrome, Nixon et al. (2019) identified a homozygous variant in the COL9A3 gene.

Markova et al. (2021) studied a Russian boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome by targeted sequencing of 166 genes associated with congenital skeletal disorders. They identified 2 previously undescribed heterozygous nonsense variants in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (R90X) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (R577X) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing in the family showed that the variants were in trans, and the patient's healthy sibs did not carry the pathogenic COL9A3 variants.

Rad et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to study 5 patients from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. They identified 3 different novel homozygous loss-of-function variants in the COL9A3 gene. The family 1 proband had a 10-bp deletion (c.107_116del) in exon 2, causing a frameshift (P36Rfs*49). The family 2 proband had a c.1204C-T nonsense mutation (R402X) in exon 23. The family 3 proband had a 1-bp deletion (c.355delC) in exon 7, causing a frameshift (L119Sfs*9). With these families, the total number of reported families was 7. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) noted that all Stickler syndrome patients with type IX collagen defects had high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and mild to severe hearing loss. Vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not with COL9A3 mutations. They suggested that loss of the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen produces a phenotype similar to the other recessive type IX collagen forms.

Markova et al. (2021) compared their patient with 6 previously reported patients from 3 families with homozygous COL9A3 mutations. High myopia and sensorineural hearing loss were uniform, and none of the patients had cleft palate. Their compound heterozygous patient had a more severe presentation, with early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration.

In a review of patients with biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants, Rad et al. (2022) found that moderate-to-profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration were consistent features. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred occasionally, and skeletal involvement seemed more variable.

### Diagnosis

Nixon et al. (2019) suggested adding COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 to genetic screening panels for patients with congenital hearing loss. They reasoned that early diagnosis of recessive Stickler syndrome would have a high impact for children at risk of dual sensory impairment.

**.0002 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 1-BP DUP, 650C [c.650dupc]

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) studied a boy of Indian ancestry with STL6 whose parents were third cousins. Using exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC, NM_001853.3) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene. His parents and an unaffected sister were heterozygous. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination of translation and absence of the gene product, and it was not found in public SNP databases. The patient had sensorineural hearing loss, progressive high myopia without vitreoretinal degeneration, femoral and tibial bowing at birth, mild platyspondyly, and midfacial hypoplasia.

**.0003 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG90TER [c.268C>T; p.R90*]

Markova et al. (2021) studied a 3-year-old Russian boy with STL6 using targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes. He was compound heterozygous for 2 nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: a c.268C-T transition in exon 5, resulting in an arg90-to-ter (R90X) substitution, and a c.1729C-T transition in exon 30, resulting in an arg577-to-ter (R577X) substitution. Sanger sequencing in the family confirmed that the mutations were in trans, and healthy sibs did not carry the pathogenic variants. The patient had severe sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration, early-onset arthropathy of the lower limbs, and mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia.

**.0004 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**

**.0006 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG402TER [c.1204C>T; p.R402*]

Rad et al. (2022) studied 2 Iranian brothers with STL6, aged 65 and 57 years, from a first-cousin marriage (family 2). They identified a homozygous c.1204C-T transition (rs989413835) in exon 23 of the COL9A3 gene, resulting in an arg402-to-ter (R402X) substitution. Both brothers had high myopia, recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments from advanced vitreoretinal degeneration, cataract, profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss, and a herniated cervical disc.
