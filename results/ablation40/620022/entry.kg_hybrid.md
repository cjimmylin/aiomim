### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270) on chromosome 20q13.

### Description

Stickler syndrome is a clinically variable and genetically heterogeneous connective tissue disorder with ophthalmic, articular, orofacial, and auditory features. It can be inherited in an autosomal dominant or an autosomal recessive pattern (Faletra et al., 2014). Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive form caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in COL9A3. Rad et al. (2022) found that high myopia and moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss were consistent features in all reported cases, whereas skeletal findings, including spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, were more variable.

### Clinical Features

Faletra et al. (2014) reported a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. In a later review, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) noted that the family had 3 affected sibs. Their phenotype consisted of moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, normal vitreous, midface hypoplasia, tibial rotation, mild irregularities of the capital femoral and metacarpal epiphyses, and pes planus. In a tabulation of previously reported patients, Rad et al. (2022) listed the 3 sibs as Moroccan children of first-cousin parents, aged 4, 11, and 16 years. All 3 had moderate to high myopia and progressive moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, together with epiphyseal dysplasia and midface hypoplasia. None had vitreoretinal degeneration, short stature, or cleft palate.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy (International Skeletal Dysplasia Registry R08-308A) whose parents were third cousins from India. He failed his newborn hearing screen. At 5 months he had moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe sensorineural hearing loss on the left, and the hearing loss remained stable. CT of the temporal bones at 7 months was negative apart from mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months, but by 2 years he had myopia of -6.75 OD and -8 OS, which had progressed to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS by 12 years. No vitreous abnormalities were noted. Femoral and tibial bowing were present at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed flattening of the acetabula, slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, irregularity of the medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly. Growth, gross motor development, and intelligence were normal. His speech was initially delayed, which was thought to be related to the hearing loss. He was found to have autoimmune hypothyroidism at 10 years of age.

Nixon et al. (2019) studied 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome caused by variants in type IX collagen genes; 1 family, with 2 patients, had a variant in COL9A3. All 6 patients were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and all had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy. According to Markova et al. (2021) and Rad et al. (2022), the patient with severe arthropathy was the older of the 2 COL9A3 sibs. This 20-year-old used a wheelchair and had undergone surgery for scoliosis, whereas the younger sib did not show these signs. ⚠︎

Markova et al. (2021) reported a boy born to a healthy nonconsanguineous family. At 1 month he had adductor spasm, hip dysplasia confirmed by ultrasound, and bilateral absence of ossification centers. Congenital high bilateral myopia was found at 3 months, and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss of grade 3-4 was diagnosed at 11 months. His speech was delayed, but motor development was normal. Spine radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, mild platyspondyly, and kyphosis with the apex at T11. Radiographs of the lower limbs at 3 years showed enlarged joint spaces; flattened and irregular femoral heads with broadened, shortened necks; coxa valga; and flattened distal femoral epiphyses, consistent with mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia. On eye examination he had peripheral pigment rearrangement, small whitish dystrophic foci at the periphery, multiple vitreous floaters, and a preretinal membrane, with a normal electroretinogram. At 2 years 5 months he had genu valgum, a waddling gait, rapid fatigability, and pain in the lower limb joints. He also had a slightly flattened nasal bridge, a small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, a high palate, lumbar lordosis, long fingers, and planovalgus feet. His height was 88 cm (25th-50th percentile). The authors considered this a more severe presentation, with early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration.

Rad et al. (2022) reported 5 affected individuals from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families. The proband of family 1 was a 28-year-old woman, the only affected child of first-cousin parents. She had high myopia and vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, and retinal pigmentary changes, as well as severe, progressive sensorineural hearing loss. A skull x-ray showed unilateral absence of the frontal sinus. Radiographs and examination of her joints were normal, although she complained of knee pain. She had none of the typical Stickler craniofacial features. In family 2, 2 brothers aged 65 and 57 years had recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments resulting from advanced vitreoretinal degeneration and had undergone multiple vitreoretinal surgeries. The older brother had no light perception in either eye; the younger could count fingers with one eye and had no light perception in the other. Both had severe, progressive sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, cataract, and a herniated cervical disc, and the older brother had muscular atrophy. In family 3, a boy aged 11.8 years and his sister aged 3.1 years had myopia and congenital, moderate to severe, progressive sensorineural hearing loss. Radiographs showed spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia in both sibs; in the boy this included irregular femoral epiphyses, genua valga, and a flat acetabular roof. Both had pes planus, a depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. Midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures were more pronounced in the girl, and the boy had knee pain. None of the 5 patients had intellectual disability.

### Inheritance

Faletra et al. (2014) reported autosomal recessive inheritance of Stickler syndrome caused by COL9A3 mutation. In the family reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), the affected boy was homozygous and his parents, who were third cousins, and an unaffected sister were heterozygous for the mutation. Markova et al. (2021) showed that the 2 mutations in their patient were in trans. Rad et al. (2022) found homozygous variants in 3 families with first-cousin parents. They noted that Nixon et al. (2019) had observed mild Stickler features in carrier parents, but that they and others had not observed mild phenotypes in heterozygous individuals.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene, c.1176_1198del (p.Gln393Cysfs*25). They described this as the first autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome family caused by COL9A3 mutation; previously, only heterozygous COL9A3 mutations had been reported in humans. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) and Rad et al. (2022) stated that the affected sibs were homozygous for the mutation.

In a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome born to consanguineous Indian parents, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination of translation and absence of the COL9A3 gene product. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and was not found in public SNP databases. Both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. Earlier sequence and deletion/duplication analyses of COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and of specific exons of COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3, had been negative.

Nixon et al. (2019) found a homozygous COL9A3 variant in 2 affected members of 1 family with recessive Stickler syndrome. Rad et al. (2022) listed this variant as a homozygous c.1411C-T nonsense variant (R471X) in exon 28.

In a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) used targeted next-generation sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes to identify compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: R90X in exon 5 and R577X in exon 30. Sanger sequencing of the family showed that the variants were in trans, and the patient's healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants. The authors stated that this was the first reported case of COL9A3-related Stickler syndrome that was not caused by a homozygous mutation.

Rad et al. (2022) performed exome sequencing on the probands of 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with Stickler syndrome. Each proband was homozygous for a different novel loss-of-function variant in the COL9A3 gene: a 10-bp deletion (c.107_116del) in exon 2, a nonsense variant (R402X) in exon 23, and a 1-bp deletion (c.355delC) in exon 7. Segregation was tested by Sanger sequencing in the family members who were available.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reviewed 6 families with 12 known cases of autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome caused by homozygous loss-of-function mutations in COL9A1, COL9A2, or COL9A3. All of these patients had high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and mild to severe hearing loss. Vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported in patients with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not in those with COL9A3 mutations. The authors contrasted these type IX collagen forms with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome caused by COL11A1 mutations, in which hearing loss is severe to profound, cleft palate occurs, and midfacial hypoplasia and orthopedic problems have not been described.

Nixon et al. (2019) concluded that congenital megalophthalmos and myopia occur in both the dominant and the recessive forms of Stickler syndrome, and that sensorineural hearing loss is more common and more severe in the recessive forms. They suggested adding COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 to genetic screening panels for patients with congenital hearing loss.

Markova et al. (2021) compared their patient with the 6 previously reported patients from 3 families with homozygous COL9A3 mutations, all of which led to loss of function. High myopia and sensorineural hearing loss were present in all patients, and cleft palate and retinal detachment were absent in all.

Rad et al. (2022) reviewed 7 families with biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants. They found that moderate to profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration were consistent features, whereas retinal detachment and cataract occurred only occasionally. Skeletal involvement was more variable: the affected brothers aged 65 and 57 years in their family 2 had only myopia, hearing loss, and herniated cervical discs, while the affected children aged 3 and 11 years in family 3 had more prominent skeletal and craniofacial findings.

### Pathogenesis

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) noted that in the knockout mouse, loss of Col9a1 leads to absence of type IX collagen in cartilage, and that a similar consequence has been assumed for loss of the alpha-2 or alpha-3 chains. On this basis they suggested that loss of the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen produces a Stickler syndrome phenotype similar to the autosomal recessive forms caused by mutations in the genes encoding the alpha-1 and alpha-2 chains.

**.0003 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG90TER [c.268C>T; p.R90*; p.Arg90Ter]

In a boy from a nonconsanguineous family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL9A3 gene: a c.268C-T transition in exon 5, resulting in an arg90-to-ter (R90X) substitution, and R577X. The mutations were identified by targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variants and showed that they were in trans, and the healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants.

**.0004 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**

In a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL9A3 gene: a c.1729C-T transition in exon 30, resulting in an arg577-to-ter (R577X) substitution, and R90X. Segregation analysis showed that the variants were in trans. After segregation analysis, the variants were classified as pathogenic under ACMG criteria (PVS1, PM2, PM3).
