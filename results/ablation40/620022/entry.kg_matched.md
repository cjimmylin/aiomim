### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270) on chromosome 20q13.33.

### Description

Stickler syndrome is a clinically variable and genetically heterogeneous disorder with eye, joint, orofacial, and hearing features. It can be inherited in an autosomal dominant or an autosomal recessive pattern (Faletra et al., 2014). Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive form caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in COL9A3 (Rad et al., 2022). In a review of 7 families, Rad et al. (2022) found that moderate to profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration were consistent features. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred only occasionally, and skeletal involvement varied.

### Clinical Features

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) described a boy (International Skeletal Dysplasia Registry R08-308A) of Indian origin with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. He failed his newborn hearing screen. At 5 months he had moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe sensorineural hearing loss on the left, and the hearing loss remained stable. Temporal bone CT showed mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months. By 2 years he had myopia of -6.75 OD and -8 OS, and this had progressed to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS by 12 years. No vitreous abnormalities were noted. He had femoral and tibial bowing at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed flattened acetabula, slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, irregularity of the medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly with irregularities of the vertebral endplates. He also had midfacial hypoplasia. He did not have cleft palate or pes planus. Growth, gross motor development, and intelligence were normal. Early speech delay was attributed to his hearing loss.

Nixon et al. (2019) described 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome caused by homozygous variants in genes encoding type IX collagen. These were COL9A1 (1 family, 1 patient), COL9A2 (2 families, 3 patients), and COL9A3 (1 family, 2 patients). All patients were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel. All had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome born to healthy nonconsanguineous Russian parents. At 1 month he had adductor spasm, hip dysplasia, and bilateral absence of ossification centers. Congenital bilateral high myopia was found at 3 months, and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss of grade 3-4 was diagnosed at 11 months. Speech was delayed, but motor development was normal. Radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, with spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, mild platyspondyly, kyphosis, flattened and irregular femoral heads, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. Eye examination showed peripheral pigment rearrangement, multiple vitreous floaters, and a preretinal membrane, with a normal ERG. At 2 years 5 months he had genu valgum, waddling gait, and pain in the lower limb joints. He also had a flattened nasal bridge, a small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, a high palate, lumbar lordosis, long fingers, and planovalgus feet. His height was in the normal range. ⚠︎

Rad et al. (2022) described a 28-year-old Iranian woman (family 1) who was the only affected child of first-cousin parents. She had high myopia in both eyes and vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, and retinal pigmentary changes. She also had severe, progressive sensorineural hearing loss and unilateral absence of the frontal sinus. Her joints and bones were normal on x-ray and examination, although she had knee pain. She did not have the typical Stickler craniofacial features (midface hypoplasia, cleft palate, micrognathia, depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares). Her height was 157 cm (-0.8 SD).

Rad et al. (2022) also described 2 affected brothers, aged 65 and 57 years, born to Iranian first-cousin parents (family 2). Both had recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments caused by advanced vitreoretinal degeneration and had undergone multiple vitreoretinal surgeries. The older brother was blind with no light perception in either eye. The younger brother could count fingers with one eye and had no light perception in the other. Both had severe, progressive (profound) sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, cataract, and a herniated cervical disc. The older brother also had muscular atrophy.

In a third consanguineous Iranian family (family 3), Rad et al. (2022) described 2 affected sibs, a boy aged 11.8 years and his sister aged 3.1 years, born to first-cousin parents. Both had high myopia and congenital, moderate to severe, progressive sensorineural hearing loss. X-rays showed spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia in both, including irregular femoral epiphyses, genu valgum, and flat acetabular roofs. Both had pes planus, depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. The girl had more marked midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures. The boy had knee pain. Neither sib had intellectual disability. ⚠︎

### Other Features

The patient reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) also had autoimmune hypothyroidism, which was identified at 10 years of age.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of Stickler syndrome type VI is autosomal recessive. Faletra et al. (2014) described the first family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to a COL9A3 mutation. In the family reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), the parents were third cousins from India. Neither the parents nor the older sister had reported hearing loss, myopia, or skeletal anomalies, and all 3 were heterozygous for the COL9A3 mutation, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

Rad et al. (2022) found that the disorder in all 3 Iranian families, each born to first-cousin parents, was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. They noted that Nixon et al. had reported mild Stickler features in carrier parents. However, Rad et al. (2022) and others did not observe mild phenotypes in heterozygous individuals.

### Heterogeneity

Faletra et al. (2014) noted that Stickler syndrome is genetically heterogeneous. Dominant forms are caused by mutations in COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and recessive forms by mutations in COL9A1 and COL9A2. Their finding of a recessive COL9A3 mutation added to the recessive forms a third type IX collagen gene, which encodes the alpha-3 chain of the heterotrimer.

Rad et al. (2022) noted that heterozygous COL9A3 variants are associated with other, allelic disorders. These include nonsyndromic hearing loss, multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, pseudoachondroplasia, and peripheral vitreoretinal degeneration with retinal detachment.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene. This was a 23-bp deletion (c.1176_1198del) predicted to cause a frameshift (Gln393CysfsTer25). The authors described this as the first autosomal recessive Stickler family due to a COL9A3 mutation, noting that only heterozygous COL9A3 mutations had previously been reported in humans.

In a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome born to consanguineous Indian parents, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC; NM_001853.3) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination and absence of the COL9A3 gene product. The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It was not found in public SNP databases. Both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers. Earlier targeted sequencing of specific exons of COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3, and analysis of COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, had been negative.

In 2 affected members of a family with recessive Stickler syndrome, Nixon et al. (2019) identified a homozygous variant in the COL9A3 gene by analyzing genomic DNA from venous blood samples of family members. The authors stated that 3 of the variants found across the 4 families in their study were novel.

In a Russian boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) used targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes to identify compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (R90X) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (R577X) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing of the family showed that the 2 mutations were in trans. The healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants. After segregation analysis, the variants were classified as pathogenic under ACMG criteria (PVS1, PM2, PM3). The authors stated that this was the first reported case of COL9A3-related Stickler syndrome not caused by a homozygous mutation. ⚠︎

Rad et al. (2022) studied 3 consanguineous Iranian families with Stickler syndrome and used exome sequencing to identify a different homozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene in each. In family 1, the proband carried a 10-bp deletion in exon 2 (c.107_116del; NM_001853.3) causing a frameshift (Pro36ArgfsTer49). This variant had an allele frequency of 0.00001390 in gnomAD (2 carriers) and was absent from Iranome, GME, and 1000 Genomes. Sanger sequencing was used to check segregation in available family members. In family 2, 2 affected brothers carried a nonsense mutation in exon 23 (c.1204C-T, R402X; rs989413835). In family 3, 2 affected sibs carried a 1-bp deletion in exon 7 (c.355delC) causing a frameshift. The mutations in families 2 and 3 were not reported in public databases.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) found that their patient's phenotype resembled that of 3 previously reported sibs with homozygous COL9A3 loss-of-function mutations. All Stickler syndrome patients with type IX collagen defects had high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and hearing loss. Vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported in patients with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not in those with COL9A3 mutations. The authors noted that recessive COL11A1-related Stickler syndrome differed from the type IX collagen forms, with severe-profound hearing loss, cleft palate, and no midfacial hypoplasia or orthopedic issues.

Nixon et al. (2019) concluded that congenital megalophthalmos and myopia occur in both dominant and recessive forms of Stickler syndrome. They found that sensorineural hearing loss was more common and more severe in the recessive forms caused by type IX collagen variants. They suggested adding COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 to genetic screening panels for patients with congenital hearing loss.

Markova et al. (2021) compared their patient with 6 previously reported patients from 3 families who had homozygous COL9A3 mutations, all of which led to loss of function. High myopia and sensorineural hearing loss were present in every patient, and cleft palate and retinal detachment were absent in all. The authors concluded that their patient had a more severe presentation, with early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration.

Rad et al. (2022) reviewed their 3 families together with 4 previously reported families with biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants. Moderate to profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration were consistent features. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred only occasionally. Skeletal involvement was more variable: the older adults in family 2 had only myopia, hearing loss, and herniated cervical discs, whereas the young children in family 3 had more prominent skeletal findings. These observations of vitreoretinal degeneration and retinal detachment differ from the earlier summaries by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) and Markova et al. (2021). ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) suggested that loss of the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen causes a Stickler syndrome phenotype similar to the other autosomal recessive forms caused by mutations in the genes encoding the alpha-1 and alpha-2 chains. Their reasoning was that Col9a1 knockout mice lack type IX collagen in cartilage, and a similar consequence has been assumed for loss of the alpha-2 or alpha-3 chains.
