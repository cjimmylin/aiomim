### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene on chromosome 20q13.3.

### Description

Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive connective tissue disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in the COL9A3 gene, which encodes the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen. The features seen in all affected individuals are early-onset, moderate to profound, often progressive sensorineural hearing loss and high myopia. Vitreoretinal degeneration, retinal detachment, and cataract occur in some patients. Skeletal involvement varies and includes epiphyseal or spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, bowing of the long bones, and early-onset arthropathy. Cleft palate has not been reported (Faletra et al., 2014; Hanson-Kahn et al., 2018; Markova et al., 2021; Rad et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Rad et al. (2022) called Stickler syndrome caused by homozygous loss-of-function variants in COL9A3 'STL type VI.'

### Clinical Features

Faletra et al. (2014) described the first family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome caused by loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) summarized the family, which had 3 affected sibs. Their features were moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, normal vitreous, midface hypoplasia, tibial rotation, mild irregularities of the capital femoral and metacarpal epiphyses, and pes planus. The sibs also had moderate to severe intellectual disability. According to Rad et al. (2022), the family was Moroccan and the parents were first cousins. The patients were 4, 11, and 16 years old.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy whose parents were third cousins from India. He failed his newborn hearing screen. At 5 months, audiologic evaluation showed moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe sensorineural hearing loss on the left, from 250 through 4,000 Hz. The hearing loss remained stable. A CT scan of the temporal bones at 7 months was negative apart from mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months. By 2 years, he had myopia of -6.75 OD and -8 OS, which progressed to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS by 12 years. No vitreous abnormalities were noted. Femoral and tibial bowing were present at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, flattening of the acetabula, irregularity of the medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly with irregular vertebral endplates. Growth parameters were in the normal range. Intelligence was normal, and early speech delay was attributed to the hearing loss. Autoimmune hypothyroidism was identified at 10 years. His parents and older sister were reported to be unaffected.

Nixon et al. (2019) described 2 patients from 1 family with recessive Stickler syndrome due to COL9A3 variants, as part of a study of 4 families with type IX collagen defects. All patients in the study were highly myopic, had congenital megalophthalmos with abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy. Markova et al. (2021) and Rad et al. (2022) gave further details of the COL9A3 family. The older sib had a refraction of -23 D in each eye at age 20 years, as well as severe arthropathy of the shoulders and hips, scoliosis that had been treated surgically, and narrowing of the knee joint spaces. This patient used a wheelchair. The younger sib did not have these skeletal findings. Rad et al. (2022) noted that Nixon et al. (2019) had observed mild Stickler phenotypes in carrier parents.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a boy from a nonconsanguineous Russian family. The pregnancy was complicated by oligohydramnios and fetal growth retardation. At 1 month, he had hip dysplasia on ultrasound and bilateral absence of ossification centers. Congenital high bilateral myopia was found at 3 months. Bilateral sensorineural hearing loss of grade 3 to 4 was diagnosed at 11 months, and auditory thresholds were 75 dB on the right and 70 dB on the left. Speech development was delayed. Ophthalmic examination showed peripheral pigment rearrangement, small whitish dystrophic foci at the periphery, multiple vitreous floaters, and a preretinal membrane in the right eye. The electroretinogram was normal. Radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, mild platyspondyly, kyphosis with the apex at T11, flattened and irregular femoral heads with broadened, shortened necks, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses, which were consistent with mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia. At 2 years and 5 months, height was 88 cm (25th to 50th percentile). He had genu valgum, a waddling gait, rapid fatigability, and pain in the lower limb joints from 3 years of age. He also had a slightly flattened nasal bridge, small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, high palate, lumbar lordosis, limited hip abduction, long fingers, and planovalgus foot deformity. The authors considered that this case widened the phenotype to include early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration. ⚠︎

Rad et al. (2022) reported 5 affected individuals from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families. In each family the parents were first cousins. The proband of family 1 was a 28-year-old woman. She had high myopia and vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, and retinal pigmentary changes. Optical coherence tomography showed retinal detachment. She also had severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss and knee pain, but skeletal examination and x-rays were normal. In family 2, 2 sibs aged 65 and 57 years had high myopia and advanced vitreoretinal degeneration with recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments that required multiple surgeries. Both were left with severely reduced vision or blindness. They also had cataract, profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss, and herniated cervical discs. In family 3, a boy aged 11.8 years and his sister aged 3.1 years had myopia, congenital moderate to severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss, and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia. They also had pes planus, a depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. Midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures were more pronounced in the girl. The boy had knee pain, genu valgum, bowed tibiae, short metacarpals, and flattened femoral heads. None of the patients had short stature, cleft palate, or intellectual disability. Rad et al. (2022) concluded that high myopia and moderate to severe hearing loss were consistent features, whereas skeletal findings were more variable. The older patients in family 2 had fewer skeletal findings than the young patients in family 3. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Stickler syndrome type VI shows autosomal recessive inheritance. Faletra et al. (2014) first reported this pattern in a family with a COL9A3 mutation, and Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) confirmed it in a consanguineous family in which both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers. Markova et al. (2021) found the 2 mutations in their patient in trans.

### Mapping

Markova et al. (2021) stated that the COL9A3 gene maps to chromosome 20q13.3.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene, c.1176_1198del (Gln393CysfsTer25). Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) and Rad et al. (2022) stated that the affected sibs were homozygous for this mutation.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to study a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome born to consanguineous Indian parents. They found that he was homozygous for a 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene, which was predicted to cause a frameshift and premature termination. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and showed that both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. The mutation was not found in public SNP databases. Earlier targeted testing of COL2A1, COL11A1, COL11A2, and specific exons of COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 had been negative.

Nixon et al. (2019) identified COL9A3 variants in 1 family with recessive Stickler syndrome, which had 2 affected members. Rad et al. (2022) listed the variant as a homozygous nonsense mutation, c.1411C-T (Arg471Ter).

Markova et al. (2021) studied a Russian boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome using targeted next-generation sequencing of 166 genes associated with skeletal disorders. They found 2 nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (Arg90Ter) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (Arg577Ter) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutations, and segregation analysis showed that they were in trans. The healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants. This was the first patient reported with compound heterozygous rather than homozygous COL9A3 mutations. ⚠︎

Rad et al. (2022) performed exome sequencing on the probands of 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. Each proband was homozygous for a different novel loss-of-function mutation in COL9A3. The family 1 proband had a 10-bp deletion (c.107_116del) that caused a frameshift in exon 2 (Pro36ArgfsTer49); this variant had an allele frequency of 0.00001390 in gnomAD. The family 2 proband had a nonsense mutation, c.1204C-T (Arg402Ter), in exon 23. The family 3 proband had a 1-bp deletion (c.355delC) in exon 7 (Leu119SerfsTer9). The last 2 variants were not found in public databases. Segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing was performed in available family members.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) noted that vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported in patients with recessive Stickler syndrome due to COL9A1 or COL9A2 mutations, but not in patients with COL9A3 mutations. Later reports did describe vitreoretinal degeneration in COL9A3 patients. Nixon et al. (2019) found abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous in all of their patients, and Markova et al. (2021) and Rad et al. (2022) found vitreoretinal degeneration, with retinal detachment in some patients in the latter study.

**.0002 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 1-BP DUP, 650C [c.650dupC]

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC, NM_001853.3) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene in a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome whose parents were consanguineous and from India. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination and absence of the gene product. Both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous, and the variant was not found in public SNP databases. Rad et al. (2022) gave the protein change as Gly217TrpfsTer50 in one table and Pro218AlafsTer49 in another.

**.0004 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**

He was compound heterozygous for 2 mutations in the COL9A3 gene: a c.268C-T transition in exon 5, resulting in an arg90-to-ter (R90X) substitution, and a c.1729C-T transition in exon 30, resulting in an arg577-to-ter (R577X) substitution. The mutations were found by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Segregation analysis in the family showed that they were in trans. ⚠︎

**.0006 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**
