### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270) on chromosome 20q13.33.

### Description

Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in COL9A3, which encodes the alpha-3 chain of heterotrimeric type IX collagen. Rad et al. (2022) noted that the consistent features in these patients were moderate to profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred occasionally, and skeletal involvement, including spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, appeared more variable. Markova et al. (2021) noted that none of the patients they reviewed had cleft palate.

### Clinical Features

Faletra et al. (2014) described a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy of Indian ancestry, born to consanguineous parents, who was last described at 12 years of age. He failed his newborn hearing screen and had moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, with mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had progressive high myopia, but no vitreous abnormalities or retinal degeneration. Femoral and tibial bowing were present at birth. Radiographs showed slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, flattening of the acetabula, irregular medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly with irregular vertebral endplates. He had midfacial hypoplasia but no cleft palate or pes planus. Growth, intelligence, and gross motor development were normal, but speech was delayed. He also had autoimmune hypothyroidism.

Nixon et al. (2019) studied 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome and homozygous variants in type IX collagen genes. Two of these patients came from 1 family with a COL9A3 variant. All 6 patients had high myopia, congenital megalophthalmos, abnormal hypoplastic vitreous gel, and sensorineural hearing loss. One patient in the cohort had severe arthropathy.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a 3-year-old boy born to nonconsanguineous parents. He had fetal growth retardation and congenital bilateral high myopia. At 11 months of age he was diagnosed with grade 3-4 bilateral sensorineural hearing loss and was fitted with hearing aids. Speech development was delayed. He had hip dysplasia with adductor spasm and limited hip abduction. Radiographs showed spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, which included mild platyspondyly, kyphosis with its apex at T11, spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, flattened irregular femoral heads, broadened and shortened femoral necks, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. Eye examination showed peripheral retinal pigment rearrangement with whitish dystrophic foci, multiple vitreous floaters, a preretinal membrane, and vitreoretinal degeneration. The electroretinogram was normal and there was no retinal detachment. Facial features included a flattened nasal bridge, small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, and high palate, without cleft palate. He also had lumbar lordosis, long fingers, deformed prominent knee joints, genu valgum, planovalgus feet, a waddling gait, lower limb joint pain, and rapid fatigability. Markova et al. (2021) compared their patient with 6 previously reported patients from 3 families with COL9A3-related recessive Stickler syndrome. Congenital high myopia and moderately severe bilateral sensorineural hearing loss from a very early age were present in all of them, and none had cleft palate. ⚠︎

Rad et al. (2022) reported 5 patients from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families. In family 1, the affected individual was a 28-year-old woman, the oldest and only affected of 3 children of first-cousin parents. She had high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, retinal pigmentary changes, and severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss. A skull x-ray showed unilateral absence of the frontal sinus. She also had knee joint pain, short metacarpals with widened epiphyses, an irregular radial epiphysis, mild thoracic platyspondyly, genu valgum, and pes planus. She did not have midface hypoplasia, cleft palate, micrognathia, short stature, or intellectual disability.

In family 2 reported by Rad et al. (2022), 2 of 4 children of first-cousin parents were affected: brothers aged 65 and 57 years. Both had high myopia and had undergone multiple vitreoretinal surgeries for recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments resulting from advanced vitreoretinal degeneration. Both also had cataract. Despite surgery, the older brother had no light perception in either eye. The younger brother had counting-finger vision in one eye and no light perception in the other. Both had severe to profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and a herniated cervical disc. The older brother also had muscular atrophy. Neither had short stature or intellectual disability.

In family 3 reported by Rad et al. (2022), 2 of 4 children of first-cousin parents were affected: a boy aged 11.8 years and his sister aged 3.1 years. Both had myopia and congenital moderate to severe progressive sensorineural hearing impairment. X-rays and detailed examination showed spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia in both. Both also had pes planus, depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, midface hypoplasia, and downslanting palpebral fissures, and the last 2 features were more pronounced in the sister. The boy complained of knee joint pain, especially when running. He also had bowed tibiae, genu valgum, short metacarpals, broad big toes, irregular femoral epiphyses, a flat and irregular acetabular roof, flattened femoral heads, and broadened and shortened femoral necks. Neither child had cleft palate or intellectual disability. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of STL6 is autosomal recessive. Faletra et al. (2014) described the first family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome caused by COL9A3 mutation. In the consanguineous family reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), the proband was homozygous for a COL9A3 mutation, and his parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. Markova et al. (2021) showed by segregation analysis that the 2 mutations in their patient were in trans. Rad et al. (2022) found homozygous mutations in 3 consanguineous families.

### Heterogeneity

Faletra et al. (2014) noted that Stickler syndrome is genetically heterogeneous. Dominant forms are caused by mutations in COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and recessive forms are associated with mutations in COL9A1 and COL9A2. Their finding added COL9A3 as a gene for recessive Stickler syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene (c.1176_1198del, p.Gln393Cysfs*25). They noted that only heterozygous COL9A3 mutations had previously been reported in humans, in association with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia type 3, susceptibility to intervertebral disc disease, and hearing loss.

Using exome sequencing, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene in a boy from a consanguineous family with Stickler syndrome. His parents and an unaffected sib were all heterozygous for the mutation. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination of translation and absence of the COL9A3 gene product. The mutation was not found in public SNP databases. The proband also carried a heterozygous R127H variant in GJB2 and a heterozygous intronic variant in SLC6A4, and the authors considered the significance of both uncertain. ⚠︎

Nixon et al. (2019) identified homozygous variants in type IX collagen genes in 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome. Two of these patients came from 1 family with a homozygous COL9A3 variant. The other variants were in COL9A1 (1 family, 1 patient) and COL9A2 (2 families, 3 patients). Three of the variants were novel.

In a 3-year-old boy with Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (R90X) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (R577X) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing of the family showed that the mutations were in trans, and the healthy sibs carried no pathogenic COL9A3 variants. The authors described this patient as the first reported with COL9A3-related recessive Stickler syndrome caused by compound heterozygous, rather than homozygous, loss-of-function mutations.

In 5 patients from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Rad et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify 3 different novel homozygous loss-of-function mutations in the COL9A3 gene. The mutations were a 10-bp deletion in exon 2 (c.107_116del, p.Pro36ArgfsTer49) in family 1, a nonsense mutation in exon 23 (c.1204C-T, R402X) in family 2, and a 1-bp deletion in exon 7 (c.355delC, p.Leu119SerfsTer9) in family 3. With these families, the number of reported families with biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants and Stickler syndrome reached 7. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reviewed 6 families (12 cases) with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome caused by homozygous loss-of-function mutations in COL9A1, COL9A2, or COL9A3. All had high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and mild to severe hearing loss. Vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported in patients with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not in patients with COL9A3 mutations. The authors noted that the type IX collagen-related phenotype appeared distinct from recessive Stickler syndrome caused by COL11A1 mutations. In that form, hearing loss was severe to profound, cleft palate and vitreous abnormalities occurred, and midfacial hypoplasia and orthopedic problems had not been described.

Nixon et al. (2019) noted that congenital megalophthalmos and myopia occur in both dominant and recessive forms of Stickler syndrome. They found sensorineural hearing loss to be more common and more severe in the recessive forms.

Markova et al. (2021) stated that their patient with compound heterozygous COL9A3 mutations had a more severe presentation, with early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration. They suggested that the absence of cleft palate in patients with COL9A3-related recessive Stickler syndrome may help distinguish them from patients with autosomal dominant forms.

Rad et al. (2022) observed that the older affected sibs in one family (aged 65 and 57 years) had only myopia, hearing loss, and herniated cervical discs. In contrast, the much younger sibs in another family (aged 3 and 11 years) had more prominent skeletal findings, including spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia and craniofacial abnormalities. Unlike Nixon et al. (2019), Rad et al. (2022) did not observe mild phenotypes in heterozygous carriers.

### Pathogenesis

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) suggested that loss of the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen results in a Stickler syndrome phenotype similar to the other autosomal recessive forms caused by mutations in the alpha-1 and alpha-2 chains. They noted that this was consistent with knockout mouse data showing that loss of Col9a1 leads to absence of type IX collagen in cartilage.

### Diagnosis

Nixon et al. (2019) suggested adding COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 to genetic screening panels for patients with congenital hearing loss. They reasoned that early diagnosis would matter greatly for children who may develop both visual and hearing impairment.
