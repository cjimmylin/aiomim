### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270) on chromosome 20q13.33.

### Description

Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive connective tissue disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the COL9A3 gene, which encodes the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) described the main features as moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, and skeletal involvement that included epiphyseal changes. Rad et al. (2022) found high myopia and moderate to severe hearing loss in all reported patients. They found skeletal findings more variable, and vitreoretinal degeneration, retinal detachment, and cataract were present in some patients. None of the reported patients had cleft palate (Markova et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Rad et al. (2022) referred to Stickler syndrome caused by homozygous loss-of-function variants in COL9A3 as STL type VI.

### Clinical Features

Faletra et al. (2014) described a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) summarized the family as 3 sibs whose phenotype consisted of moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, normal vitreous, midface hypoplasia, tibial rotation, mild irregularities of the capital femoral and metacarpal epiphyses, and pes planus. The sibs also had moderate to severe intellectual disability. Rad et al. (2022) listed the sibs as Moroccan, born to first-cousin parents, and aged 4, 11, and 16 years.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy born to parents from India who were third cousins. He failed his newborn hearing screen. At 5 months, audiology showed moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe sensorineural hearing loss on the left from 250 through 4,000 Hz, and the hearing loss remained stable. A CT scan of the temporal bones at 7 months was negative apart from mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months. By 2 years his myopia was -6.75 OD and -8 OS, and by 12 years it had progressed to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS. No vitreous abnormalities were noted. Femoral and tibial bowing were present at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, flattening of the acetabula, irregularity of the medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly with irregularities of the vertebral endplates. He also had midfacial hypoplasia. Growth parameters were normal. Speech was initially delayed, which was thought to be related to the hearing loss, and intelligence was normal. Autoimmune hypothyroidism was identified at 10 years. Neither parent nor the patient's sister was reported to have hearing loss, myopia, or skeletal anomalies.

Nixon et al. (2019) reported 6 patients with recessive Stickler syndrome from 4 families. Two of the patients, from 1 family, had variants in COL9A3. All 6 patients were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and all had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy. Markova et al. (2021) and Rad et al. (2022) noted that the oldest affected sib in the COL9A3 family, a 20-year-old, had severe arthropathy of the shoulders and hip, scoliosis that had been operated on, and narrowing of the knee joint spaces, and used a wheelchair. The younger sib had none of these findings. Markova et al. (2021) also noted that this patient's refraction at age 20 years was up to -23 D in each eye.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a boy born to healthy nonconsanguineous parents. The pregnancy was complicated by oligohydramnios and fetal growth retardation, but he was born at term weighing 3,260 g and measuring 51 cm. At 1 month he had adductor spasm, hip dysplasia confirmed by ultrasound, and bilateral absence of ossification centers. Congenital high bilateral myopia was found at 3 months. Speech development was delayed, and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss of grade 3-4 was diagnosed at 11 months. Auditory thresholds were 75 dB on the right and 70 dB on the left. Spinal radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, mild platyspondyly, and kyphosis with the apex at T11. Radiographs of the lower limbs at 3 years showed enlarged joint spaces, flattened and irregular femoral heads, broadened and shortened femoral necks, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. Eye examination showed peripheral pigment rearrangement with small whitish dystrophic foci, multiple vitreous floaters, a preretinal membrane in the right eye, and a normal electroretinogram. At 2 years 5 months he had valgus knee deformity, a waddling gait, rapid fatigability, and pain in the lower limb joints. His height was 88 cm (25th-50th percentile). He had a slightly flattened nasal bridge, a small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, a high palate, lumbar lordosis, limited hip abduction, long fingers, genu valgum, and planovalgus foot deformity. Markova et al. (2021) took the lower limb joint pain that began at age 3 years as a sign of early-onset arthropathy. They regarded the presentation as more severe than previously reported, with early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration.

Rad et al. (2022) reported 5 patients from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families. In each family the parents were first cousins. The patient in family 1 was a 28-year-old woman with high myopia and vitreoretinal degeneration, including empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, and retinal pigmentary changes. She had severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss and unilateral absence of the frontal sinus. She had knee pain, but the text described her joint examination and x-rays as normal. Her radiographs shown in a figure, however, showed short metacarpals with widened epiphyses, an irregular radius epiphysis, mild thoracic platyspondyly, and genua valga. She did not have midface hypoplasia, cleft palate, or micrognathia. Family 2 had 2 affected brothers, aged 65 and 57 years. Both had high myopia and had undergone multiple vitreoretinal surgeries for recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments caused by advanced vitreoretinal degeneration. The older brother was blind without light perception in either eye. The younger brother could count fingers with one eye and had no light perception in the other. Both had cataract, severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss that was profound according to a table, and a herniated cervical disc. The older brother also had muscular atrophy. Family 3 had an affected boy aged 11.8 years and his affected sister aged 3.1 years. Both had myopia, congenital moderate to severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, pes planus, a depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. Midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures were more pronounced in the girl. The boy had knee pain, bowed tibiae, genu valgum, short metacarpals, broad big toes, irregular femoral epiphyses, a flat and irregular acetabular roof, and flattened femoral heads with broadened and shortened necks. None of the 5 patients had intellectual disability.

### Inheritance

Faletra et al. (2014) described the first family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to a COL9A3 mutation. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) found that the patient's consanguineous parents and unaffected sib were all heterozygous for the COL9A3 mutation for which the patient was homozygous. Markova et al. (2021) found 2 COL9A3 mutations in trans in a patient born to nonconsanguineous parents. Rad et al. (2022) reported homozygous mutations in 3 consanguineous families. They noted that Nixon et al. (2019) had observed mild Stickler phenotypes in carrier parents, but they did not observe mild phenotypes in heterozygous individuals themselves.

### Heterogeneity

Faletra et al. (2014) noted that dominant forms of Stickler syndrome are caused by mutations in COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and recessive forms had been associated with mutations in COL9A1 and COL9A2. Their study added COL9A3 as a gene for recessive Stickler syndrome. Nixon et al. (2019) reported recessive Stickler syndrome in families with homozygous variants in COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene (c.1176_1198del; p.Gln393Cysfs*25). They noted that only heterozygous COL9A3 mutations had previously been reported in humans, in association with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia type 3, susceptibility to intervertebral disc disease, and hearing loss.

Earlier testing of the patient reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) showed only a heterozygous GJB2 variant (R127H), with no second GJB2 variant, and sequence and deletion/duplication analysis of COL2A1, COL11A1, COL11A2, and specific exons of COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 was negative. Exome sequencing then showed that the patient was homozygous for a 1-bp duplication in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene (c.650dupC). The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and the parents and an unaffected sib were all heterozygous. The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination of translation and absence of the COL9A3 gene product, and the change was not found in public SNP databases.

Nixon et al. (2019) identified homozygous COL9A3 variants in 2 patients from 1 family with recessive Stickler syndrome. In the same study they found homozygous COL9A1 or COL9A2 variants in other families.

Targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes in a boy with Stickler syndrome led Markova et al. (2021) to identify compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene, R90X and R577X. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans, and the patient's healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants. Markova et al. (2021) described this as the first case of COL9A3-related recessive Stickler syndrome caused by compound heterozygous, rather than homozygous, loss-of-function mutations.

In 5 patients from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with Stickler syndrome, Rad et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to identify 3 different novel homozygous loss-of-function mutations in the COL9A3 gene. Segregation was assessed by Sanger sequencing in available family members. Rad et al. (2022) noted that their families brought the number of reported families with Stickler syndrome due to biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants to 7. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reviewed 6 families with 12 cases of autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to homozygous loss-of-function mutations in COL9A1, COL9A2, or COL9A3. All of the patients had high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and mild to severe hearing loss. Vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported in patients with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not in those with COL9A3 mutations. The authors considered the type IX collagen phenotype distinct from recessive Stickler syndrome caused by COL11A1 mutations, in which hearing loss was severe to profound, cleft palate and vitreous anomalies occurred, and midfacial hypoplasia and orthopedic problems had not been described.

Nixon et al. (2019) noted that congenital megalophthalmos and myopia occur in both dominant and recessive forms of Stickler syndrome. They found sensorineural hearing loss to be more common and more severe in the recessive forms.

Rad et al. (2022) observed that the affected sibs in their family 2, aged 65 and 57 years, had only myopia, hearing loss, and herniated cervical discs. In contrast, the much younger sibs in family 3, aged 3 and 11 years, had more prominent skeletal findings, including spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, and craniofacial abnormalities.

### Pathogenesis

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) noted that knockout mouse studies showed that loss of Col9a1 leads to absence of type IX collagen in cartilage. They suggested that loss of the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen results in a Stickler syndrome phenotype similar to that of the recessive forms caused by mutations in the genes for the alpha-1 and alpha-2 chains.

### Diagnosis

Nixon et al. (2019) suggested that COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 be added to genetic screening panels for patients with congenital hearing loss. They reasoned that early diagnosis would have a high impact for children who may have both visual and hearing impairment.

Markova et al. (2021) suggested that the absence of cleft palate in all reported patients with COL9A3-related recessive Stickler syndrome may help distinguish them from autosomal dominant forms. They also noted that midface hypoplasia tends to become less pronounced with age, so earlier photographs of a patient may be useful in counseling.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 23-BP DEL, NT1176 [c.1176_1198del; p.Q393Cfs; p.Q393fs; p.Gln393Cysfs*25]

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene (c.1176_1198del), resulting in a frameshift (p.Gln393Cysfs*25). Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) noted that the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for the mutation. Rad et al. (2022) listed it as a homozygous frameshift in exon 23 and described the family as Moroccan with first-cousin parents. ⚠︎

**.0002 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 1-BP DUP, 650C [c.650dupc; c.650dupC]

In a boy with STL6 whose parents were third cousins from India, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp duplication in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene (c.650dupC, NM_001853.3). The frameshift was predicted to cause premature termination and absence of the gene product. The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. It was not found in public SNP databases.

**.0003 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG471TER [c.1411C>T; p.Arg471Ter]

In 2 affected members of a family with recessive Stickler syndrome, Nixon et al. (2019) identified a homozygous variant in the COL9A3 gene. Rad et al. (2022) listed this variant as a c.1411C-T transition in exon 28, resulting in an arg471-to-ter (R471X) substitution.

**.0004 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG90TER [c.268C>T; p.R90*; p.Arg90Ter]

In a boy with STL6 born to nonconsanguineous parents, Markova et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL9A3 gene. One was a c.268C-T transition in exon 5, resulting in an arg90-to-ter (R90X) substitution, and the other was a c.1729C-T transition in exon 30, resulting in an arg577-to-ter (R577X) substitution. Both mutations had not been described before. They were found by targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes, and Sanger sequencing of the family showed that they were in trans. The patient's healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants.

COL9A3, ARG577TER [c.1729C>T; p.R577*; p.Arg577Ter]

For discussion of the c.1729C-T transition in the COL9A3 gene, resulting in an arg577-to-ter (R577X) substitution, which was found in compound heterozygous state in a patient with STL6 by Markova et al. (2021), see COL9A3, ARG90TER.

**.0006 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 10-BP DEL, NT107 [c.107_116del; p.P36Rfs; p.P36fs; p.Pro36Argfs*49]

The allele frequency in gnomAD was 0.00001390, with 2 carriers, and the variant was not reported in the Iranome, GME, or 1000 Genomes databases.
