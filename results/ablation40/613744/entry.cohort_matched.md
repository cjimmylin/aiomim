### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

Spastic paraplegia-51 is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder belonging to the AP-4 deficiency syndrome. It is characterized by severe intellectual disability with absent or very limited speech, microcephaly, and infantile hypotonia that progresses to spastic paraplegia or tetraplegia, so that patients do not walk or become wheelchair-bound. Other features include stereotypic laughter, drooling, short stature, and, in some patients, seizures (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) described the disorder as an autosomal recessive cerebral palsy syndrome. They proposed the name 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome' for the autosomal recessive disorder caused by disruption of any of the AP-4 subunits.

Kong et al. (2013) also proposed the term 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome' for the uniform phenotype of type I complex hereditary spastic paraplegia seen in patients with mutations in AP-4 complex subunits. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs, a woman and a man, from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. The sister (IV-4) was born after a full-term pregnancy with an uneventful labor and delivery. At birth she had microcephaly and hypotonia, which progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia by the first year of life. She never developed speech, had been wheelchair-bound since age 5 years, and had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Examination at age 23 showed microcephaly (-3 SD), profound cognitive impairment, adaptive skills with age equivalents below 1 year, drooling, outbursts of stereotypic laughter, absent sphincter control, and a Babinski sign. Her dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face, a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum, an everted full upper vermilion, a prominent antihelix, and facial hypotonia. Brain imaging showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was more pronounced frontally and in the corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing. Eye evaluation and hearing were normal.

Her brother (IV-5) had microcephaly and hypotonia postnatally, and developmental delay was evident by the first year of life. He acquired some dysarthric words but never normal speech. He was able to walk with support, but increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. On examination at 22 years he had microcephaly (-3 SD), severe cognitive deficits, spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia, hyperreflexia and a Babinski sign, outbursts of stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, and absent sphincter control. A head CT scan showed moderate ventriculomegaly with slightly prominent cisterns. Eye evaluation and hearing were normal (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011).

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) compared these sibs with the family carrying an AP4M1 disruption reported by Verkerk et al. (2009). Patients in both families had infantile hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraplegia, microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, stereotypic laughter, drooling, ventriculomegaly, and white matter loss. Epilepsy was present only in the sibs with the AP4E1 deletion.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families. Each family had a mutation in a different AP4 subunit gene, one of which was AP4E1. The patients had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, a shy character, stereotypic laughter, muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation. The abstract did not separate the features of the AP4E1 family from those of the other 2 families. The authors concluded that AP4 deficiency causes a clinically recognizable syndrome.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls (P1 and P2) born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. Both had neonatal asphyxia and early infantile hypotonia. When examined at age 3 years, they had marked developmental retardation and microcephaly (head circumference 46 cm (-2 SD) in P1 and 45.5 cm (-2.5 SD) in P2). Other findings were intellectual disability, spastic paraplegia of the lower limbs, inability to walk unaided, and failure to acquire normal speech. They also had short stature, low body weight, drooling, stereotypic laughter, pseudobulbar signs (jaw jerk and gag reflex), absent sphincter control, and loss of acquired function. Their facial features were coarse, with a prominent bulbous nose and a wide mouth. Brain imaging showed cortical atrophy and atrophy of the inferior vermis, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. Neither twin had seizures, involuntary movements, contractures, hyperreflexia, a Babinski sign, or hypertonia. P1 had slight myopia, astigmatism, and conductive hearing loss on the left side. P2 had slight astigmatism.

At 9 months of age, both twins also had unilaterally enlarged and inflamed axillary lymph nodes, diagnosed as BCG lymphadenitis with acid-fast bacilli. Kong et al. (2013) found no immunologic abnormality that explained the BCG disease. Leukocyte subset counts, immunoglobulins, and complement were normal. The IL-12/IFN-gamma circuit, IFN-gamma-induced GAF DNA-binding activity, and oxidative burst were also normal. No causal mutations were found in known genes for Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2013) reviewed 27 reported patients from 9 kindreds with mutations in AP-4 complex subunits (9 with AP4E1, 9 with AP4B1, 6 with AP4S1, and 3 with AP4M1 mutations). They noted that these patients had a uniform phenotype of type I complex hereditary spastic paraplegia, characterized by severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, progressive spastic paraplegia, growth retardation, and stereotypic laughter.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 in the families reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) and Kong et al. (2013) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In both families the parents were consanguineous, the affected sibs were homozygous for an AP4E1 mutation, and the parents were heterozygous carriers. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) also described autosomal recessive inheritance in consanguineous families.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used chromosomal microarray analysis to identify a homozygous deletion at chromosome 15q21.2 (see). The deletion was 139 kb at minimum and 222 kb at maximum, and fine-mapping defined it as 192 kb (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18). It included the 5-prime end of 2 genes, AP4E1 and SPPL2A, and was confirmed by FISH. The mother and an unaffected older sister were heterozygous carriers, and a younger sib did not carry the deletion. SNRPN methylation analysis showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, so the father was inferred to be a carrier. The authors could not completely rule out a pathogenic role for the codeleted SPPL2A gene. However, they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main genetic mechanism underlying the phenotype.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families with severe autosomal recessive intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia. Using autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing, they found one mutation in each of 3 genes encoding AP4 subunits. One family had a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). This mutation resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). The other 2 families had a nonsense mutation in AP4S1 and a frameshift mutation in AP4B1.

In identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, Kong et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X). The twins had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia with intellectual disability, microcephaly, and short stature. The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The parents were heterozygous, a healthy sib was wild-type, and the variant was absent from 1,050 CEPH controls and 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells, the mutation did not affect AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and AP-4-beta and AP-4-mu levels were also decreased. Native immunoprecipitation showed a more than 95% reduction in assembled AP-4 complex in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells. The AP-4-binding partner tepsin was undetectable in patient fibroblasts. One twin was also heterozygous for missense variants in IFNGR1 (Q190R) and IL12RB2 (K768N), which the authors did not consider causal.

### Pathogenesis

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) cited 3 lines of evidence that loss of any one AP-4 subunit disrupts the whole complex: the very similar phenotypes of families with homozygous AP4E1 and AP4M1 disruptions, antisense data on the mu subunit, and Ap4b1 knockout mice. On this basis they proposed that disruption of any of the 4 subunits causes a distinct autosomal recessive AP-4 deficiency syndrome. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) noted that adaptor protein complexes AP1 to AP4 are ubiquitously expressed, evolutionarily conserved heterotetrameric complexes. These complexes mediate different types of vesicle formation and select the cargo molecules included in the vesicles. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) suggested that AP4-mediated trafficking plays a crucial role in brain development and function.

Kong et al. (2013) noted that the R1105X mutation lies close to the normal termination codon and does not generate a transcript subject to nonsense-mediated decay. They suggested that the resulting C-terminally truncated protein might have dominant-negative or gain-of-function activity. They also proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease in their patients.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) studied 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51. The sibs had microcephaly, infantile hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraplegia, profound or severe intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, seizures, and stereotypic laughter. Using chromosomal microarray analysis, the authors identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion of chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18) in both sibs. The deletion included the 5-prime end of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes and was confirmed by FISH. The mother and an unaffected older sister were heterozygous, and a younger sib did not carry the deletion. Biparental inheritance of chromosome 15 indicated that the father was a carrier. The authors could not completely exclude a role for SPPL2A, but they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main cause of the phenotype.

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105*]

Kong et al. (2013) studied identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. The twins had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia with intellectual disability, microcephaly, and short stature, as well as BCG lymphadenitis. Using whole-exome sequencing, the authors identified a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene in both twins, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.
