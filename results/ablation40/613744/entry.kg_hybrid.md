### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.2.

### Description

SPG51 is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder caused by deficiency of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4). It is characterized by early infantile hypotonia that progresses to spasticity of the lower limbs or of all 4 limbs, profound or severe intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, inability to walk, microcephaly, stereotypic laughter, and drooling (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). Some patients have seizures, short stature or growth retardation, and facial dysmorphism (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) proposed the term 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome' for the autosomal recessive disorder caused by disruption of any of the 4 subunits of AP-4. They first described the disorder in their family as a form of spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy. Kong et al. (2013) regarded AP-4 deficiency syndrome as a subtype of hereditary spastic paraplegia and noted that AP4E1-related disease is classified as SPG51.

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family in which 2 sibs had a disorder described as cerebral palsy. Both were born at term after uneventful pregnancies. The female sib (IV-4) had microcephaly and hypotonia at birth, which progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia by the first year of life. She never developed speech or independent walking. She had been wheelchair-bound since 5 years of age, which was also when generalized tonic-clonic seizures began. On examination at age 23 years she had microcephaly (-3 SD), profoundly impaired cognitive ability, deficient adaptive skills (age equivalents less than 1 year), drooling, and outbursts of stereotypic laughter. Dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face with a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum with an everted full upper vermilion, prominent antehelix of the ears, and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was more pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing of the background. Her brother (IV-5) had microcephaly and hypotonia postnatally, and developmental delay was evident by the first year of life. He acquired some dysarthric words but never normal speech. He walked with support until increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. Seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. At age 22 years he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia and hyperreflexia, stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, and severe cognitive deficits. His dysmorphic features and EEG were very similar to his sister's, and head CT showed moderate ventriculomegaly with slightly prominent cisterns. Neither sib had sphincter control, and both had a Babinski sign and normal eye and hearing evaluations.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families, 1 of which had an AP4E1 mutation. The affected individuals had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, a shy character, stereotypic laughter, muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. The pregnancy had no complications, but neonatal asphyxia was noted at delivery. At 3 years of age the twins had marked developmental retardation, with microcephaly (head circumference -2 SD and -2.5 SD), an inability to walk unaided, abnormal speech, and abnormal circadian development. They smiled or laughed for no obvious reason and had abnormal drooling. Both had early infantile hypotonia and spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities, without hypertonia, hyperreflexia, or Babinski sign. Other pseudobulbar signs included a jaw jerk and gag reflex. Both lacked sphincter control and had no seizures. They had short stature, low body weight, and a facial gestalt with a prominent bulbous nose, wide mouth, and coarse features. EEG showed diffuse slow waves, and CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy. One twin had slight myopia and astigmatism and conductive deafness on the left side; the other had slight astigmatism. The twins were diagnosed with type I complex hereditary spastic paraplegia. Both also had mycobacterial disease (see Other Features).

### Other Features

Kong et al. (2013) reported that the Moroccan twins with AP4E1-related spastic paraplegia had been vaccinated with BCG a few days after birth. Both developed unilaterally enlarged and inflamed axillary lymph nodes at 9 months of age. The nodes were removed surgically, and biopsy showed acid-fast bacilli, consistent with mycobacterial infection. Neither twin had had another episode of mycobacterial infection by age 3 years. Counts of neutrophils, monocytes, B cells, T-cell subsets, and NK cells were normal, as were immunoglobulins and complement. The IL-12/IFN-gamma circuit, IFN-gamma-induced GAF DNA-binding activity, and superoxide production were also normal. Exome data showed no causal mutation in known genes for Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD). A heterozygous IFNGR1 variant (Q190R) and a heterozygous IL12RB2 variant (K768N) were judged to have no functional effect. Kong et al. (2013) proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease in these patients. They noted that mycobacterial disease had not been reported in other patients with AP-4 deficiency. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) later studied Moroccan monozygotic twin sisters born to first-cousin parents who had hereditary spastic paraplegia due to a homozygous R1105X mutation in AP4E1. Both twins had BCG disease, presenting as axillary lymphadenitis at 9 months of age. By whole-exome sequencing and genome-wide linkage analysis, Kong et al. (2018) identified a homozygous splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene (c.733+1G-A) in both twins. They concluded that the twins had MSMD caused by autosomal recessive SPPL2a deficiency.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 in the family reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Both affected sibs were homozygous for a deletion. FISH showed that the mother and an older unaffected sister were heterozygous carriers, and a younger unaffected sib did not carry the deletion. No paternal sample was available. SNRPN methylation analysis in both patients showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, so the father was inferred to be a carrier.

In the Moroccan family reported by Kong et al. (2013), the AP4E1 mutation segregated with autosomal recessive disease. The affected twins were homozygous, both parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was homozygous for the wildtype allele.

### Heterogeneity

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that their family with an AP4E1 deletion closely resembled a Moroccan sibship with a homozygous mutation in AP4M1, which encodes another subunit of AP-4. Shared features included infantile hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraplegia, microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, stereotypic laughter, drooling, ventriculomegaly, and white matter loss. Epilepsy was present in both AP4E1 patients but in none of the AP4M1 patients.

In the other 2 consanguineous families with the AP4-deficiency phenotype, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a nonsense mutation in the AP4S1 gene and a frameshift mutation in the AP4B1 gene. Kong et al. (2013) noted that 27 patients from 9 kindreds with mutations in AP4E1, AP4B1, AP4S1, or AP4M1 had a uniform phenotype of type I complex hereditary spastic paraplegia. The phenotype was characterized by severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, progressive spastic paraplegia, growth retardation, and stereotypic laughter.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used chromosomal microarray analysis to identify a homozygous deletion on chromosome 15q21.2. High-resolution array analysis refined the deletion to 192 kb (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18), and it was confirmed by FISH. The deletion included the 5-prime ends of 2 genes, AP4E1 and SPPL2A. The authors could not completely rule out a pathogenic role for SPPL2A or a contribution of its deletion to the phenotype. However, they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main underlying genetic mechanism, citing the similarity to the phenotype of a family with a homozygous AP4M1 mutation.

In 1 of 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a splice mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The mutation resulted in r.421_542del and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20). It was found by autozygosity mapping combined with either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or next-generation exome sequencing.

In Moroccan twins with SPG51 born to first-cousin parents, Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X) by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed it by Sanger sequencing. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family. It was not found in 1,050 controls from 51 ethnic groups in the CEPH panel or in 69 additional Moroccan controls. In patient cells, the mutation did not affect AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing, but it caused loss of AP-4-epsilon protein and of the assembled AP-4 complex (see Pathogenesis).

### Pathogenesis

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that families with homozygous disruption of either the epsilon or the mu subunit of AP-4 had extremely similar phenotypes. They also noted that knockout mice lacking the beta subunit have no functional AP-4. On this basis, they proposed that disruption of any one of the 4 AP-4 subunits abolishes the function of the complex. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) concluded that their findings supported a crucial role for AP4 complex-mediated trafficking in brain development and functioning.

In EBV-transformed B cells from a patient homozygous for the AP4E1 R1105X mutation, Kong et al. (2013) found that mRNA levels for AP4E1, AP4B1, AP4M1, and AP4S1 did not differ significantly from controls, and no aberrant AP4E1 splicing was detected. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta were also decreased. Native immunoprecipitation showed that the levels of assembled AP-4 in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells were more than 95% lower than in controls. Immunofluorescence confirmed loss of AP-4 and of its binding partner tepsin in patient fibroblasts. The authors concluded that the C-terminal part of AP-4-epsilon plays an important role in maintaining the integrity of the AP-4 complex.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL [192 kb deletion, chr15:48835480-49028171 (hg18)]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18) that included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The deletion was detected by chromosomal microarray analysis, refined with a high-resolution array, and confirmed by FISH. The mother and an unaffected sister were heterozygous carriers, and the father was inferred to be a carrier.

The mutation resulted in r.421_542del and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20). It was found by autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing.

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105*; p.R1105X]

In identical twin girls with SPG51, born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene, resulting in an arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) substitution. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was wildtype. The variant was absent from 1,050 CEPH controls and 69 Moroccan controls. Patient cells had normal AP4E1 mRNA levels, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, other AP-4 subunits were reduced, and assembled AP-4 was more than 95% lower than in controls. The twins also had BCG lymphadenitis, which Kong et al. (2013) proposed was most likely caused by the AP4E1 mutation.

Kong et al. (2018) studied Moroccan monozygotic twin sisters with hereditary spastic paraplegia due to homozygous R1105X mutation in AP4E1. In these twins they identified a homozygous SPPL2A splice site mutation (c.733+1G-A), and they concluded that the twins' BCG disease was caused by autosomal recessive SPPL2a deficiency.
