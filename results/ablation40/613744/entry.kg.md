### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.2.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 is a complex form of hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP). It is characterized by severe intellectual disability with absent speech, microcephaly, stereotypic laughter, and muscular hypotonia in infancy that progresses to spastic paraplegia or tetraplegia with inability to walk. Growth retardation is also part of the disorder. Kong et al. (2013) noted that 27 patients from 9 kindreds with mutations in genes encoding AP-4 subunits (AP4E1, AP4B1, AP4S1, and AP4M1) shared this uniform phenotype of complex HSP, which they called 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.' ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) described 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family, a female (IV-4) examined at age 23 years and a male (IV-5) examined at age 22 years. Both had autosomal recessive spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy with profound intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, and white matter loss. Both were born at term after uneventful pregnancies and had microcephaly and hypotonia from birth or soon after. The hypotonia progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia, hyperreflexia, and Babinski signs. Both later developed generalized tonic-clonic seizures, stereotypic laughter, drooling, absent normal speech, and absent sphincter control, and both became wheelchair-bound. The affected female had dysmorphic features: bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face with a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum with a full everted upper vermilion, prominent antehelices, and facial hypotonia. Her brother's dysmorphic features were described as very similar. Her brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was most pronounced frontally and in the corpus callosum. Her EEG showed generalized theta slowing of the background. Her brother's CT scan showed ventriculomegaly with prominent cisterns. Eye and hearing evaluations were normal in both sibs.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation. The authors concluded that these findings defined a clinically recognizable syndrome due to deficiency of the AP4 complex.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical female twins, born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, who had infancy-onset complex hereditary spastic paraplegia. At age 3 years they had marked developmental delay, microcephaly, inability to walk, absent speech, stereotypic laughter, drooling, muscular hypotonia, short stature and low body weight, and spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities. They did not have seizures. They had a prominent bulbous nose, wide mouth, and coarse features. CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. At 9 months of age, after BCG vaccination, both twins developed unilateral enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph nodes, and biopsy showed acid-fast bacilli. Immune cell counts, immunoglobulins, and complement were normal, as were the IL-12/IFN-gamma axis and the oxidative burst. The authors proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) restudied the twin sisters (P1 and P2) reported by Kong et al. (2013), then 8 years old, primarily for Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease. In addition to hereditary spastic paraplegia, they had BCG disease. They presented at 9 months of age with unilaterally enlarged inflammatory axillary lymph nodes after BCG vaccination at birth, and biopsy confirmed acid-fast bacilli. Neonatal asphyxiation had been noted during delivery. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 is autosomal recessive. The affected sibs reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) were born to consanguineous parents and carried a homozygous deletion. Their mother was a heterozygous carrier, and the father was inferred to be a carrier. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported the disorder in consanguineous families. In the twins reported by Kong et al. (2013), who were born to first-cousin parents, the homozygous mutation segregated with the disorder: both parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib was wildtype.

### Cytogenetics

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) found that both affected sibs in a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family carried a homozygous 192-kb deletion at chromosome 15q21.2, which they confirmed by metaphase FISH using the RP11-147B19 probe. The mother and the older sister carried the deletion heterozygously, and the younger sib did not carry the deletion.

### Heterogeneity

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) found that the same AP4-deficiency phenotype seen in a family with an AP4E1 mutation was caused in 2 other consanguineous families by mutations in genes encoding other AP4 subunits. One family had a nonsense mutation in the AP4S1 gene (c.124C-T, Arg42Ter), and the other had a frameshift mutation in the AP4B1 gene (c.487_488insTAT, Glu163_Ser739delinsVal). They noted that mutations in AP4M1 and AP4E1 had recently been found to cause cerebral palsy associated with severe intellectual disability, supporting a crucial role of AP4-mediated trafficking in brain development and function.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used chromosomal microarray analysis to identify a homozygous deletion on chromosome 15q21.2. High-resolution array mapping defined the deletion as 192 kb (chr15:48,835,480-49,028,171, hg18). The deletion removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes, and FISH confirmed the result. FISH showed that the mother was a heterozygous carrier. SNRPN methylation analysis showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, which indicated that the father was also a carrier. The authors proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main cause of the phenotype, although they could not rule out a contribution from loss of SPPL2A.

In affected members of a consanguineous family with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The mutation caused skipping of sequence (r.421_542del) and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20). It was found by a combination of autozygosity mapping and either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing.

In twins with SPG51 born to consanguineous Moroccan parents, Kong et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family and was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells, the mutation did not affect AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta levels were also reduced. The amount of assembled AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells, and the AP-4 binding partner tepsin was undetectable in patient fibroblasts. The authors suggested that the C-terminal part of AP-4-epsilon is important for the integrity of the AP-4 complex.

Kong et al. (2018) reported that the monozygotic twin sisters described by Kong et al. (2013), born in 2009 to first-cousin Moroccan parents, had hereditary spastic paraplegia due to the homozygous R1105X mutation in AP4E1.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) compared their family with the AP4E1 deletion with a Moroccan family in which Verkerk et al. had reported a homozygous AP4M1 mutation. Patients in both families had infantile hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraplegia, microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, stereotypic laughter, drooling, ventriculomegaly, and white matter loss. Epilepsy was present in the AP4E1 patients but not in the AP4M1 patients. The authors proposed that disruption of any of the 4 AP-4 subunits abolishes the function of the complex and causes a distinct autosomal recessive 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.'

Kong et al. (2013) noted that the R1105X mutation in AP4E1 lies close to the normal termination codon and does not trigger nonsense-mediated decay. They speculated that the resulting C-terminally truncated protein might have dominant-negative or gain-of-function activity. This could explain why mycobacterial disease occurred in their patients but not in other AP-4-deficient patients, most of whom carry probable null mutations. ⚠︎
