### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder. Hypotonia in infancy progresses to spastic paraplegia or tetraplegia. Patients have microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, inability to walk, stereotypic laughter, drooling and lack of sphincter control. Some patients have seizures and brain atrophy on imaging (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs, a 23-year-old woman (IV-4) and a 22-year-old man (IV-5), from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. The sister had microcephaly (-3 SD) and hypotonia at birth. By the first year of life these had progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia, and she had a Babinski sign. She never developed speech or independent walking. She had been wheelchair-bound since 5 years of age, when generalized tonic-clonic seizures also began. She had profound intellectual disability, drooling, stereotypic laughter and lack of sphincter control. Dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face, a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum, an everted full upper vermilion, a prominent antihelix and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was more pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing. Eye evaluation and hearing were normal. Her brother had microcephaly (-3 SD), neonatal hypotonia and developmental delay. He acquired some dysarthric words but never normal speech. He was able to walk with support, but increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures started as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. On examination at 22 years, he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia, hyperreflexia and a Babinski sign, outbursts of stereotypic laughter, drooling and jerky nystagmus. He also had severe intellectual disability and lacked sphincter control. Imaging showed moderate ventriculomegaly and prominent cisterns. His hearing was normal. ⚠︎

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families. Each family carried a mutation in a different AP4 subunit gene, and one of the families had an AP4E1 mutation. The patients had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, and muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia. They also had microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk and growth retardation.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical female twins (P1 and P2) born to first-cousin Moroccan parents and last assessed at age 3 years. Both had early infantile hypotonia, intellectual disability, microcephaly (head circumference 46 cm (-2 SD) and 45.5 cm (-2.5 SD)) and spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities. Neither twin ever walked or spoke. Both had absent sphincter control, stereotypic laughter, drooling, short stature and low body weight. Their facial gestalt included a prominent bulbous nose, a wide mouth and coarse features. Imaging showed atrophy of the inferior vermis and cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. Both twins had astigmatism. P1 also had myopia and left-sided conductive hearing loss. Neither had seizures, hyperreflexia, a Babinski sign or contractures. In addition, at 9 months of age both twins had unilaterally enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph nodes, which represented BCG lymphadenitis. Kong et al. (2013) found no immunologic abnormality to explain the mycobacterial disease. Lymphocyte subsets, immunoglobulins, complement, the IL-12/IFN-gamma circuit and oxidative burst were all normal. The authors therefore proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) restudied the twins reported by Kong et al. (2013), then aged 8 years. They noted that neonatal asphyxia had occurred at delivery. They attributed the twins' hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 mutation. In contrast, they attributed the BCG disease, with enlarged inflammatory axillary lymph nodes containing acid-fast bacilli, to a separate Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease caused by a homozygous SPPL2A mutation. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

SPG51 follows autosomal recessive inheritance. In the consanguineous family reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), the affected sibs were homozygous for the deletion and their mother was a heterozygous carrier. SNRPN methylation analysis showed that the probands had inherited chromosome 15 from both parents, so the father was inferred to be a carrier. Kong et al. (2013) found that the affected twins born to first-cousin parents were homozygous for the AP4E1 mutation and that both parents were heterozygous.

### Heterogeneity

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) found mutations in 3 different AP4 subunit genes (AP4S1, AP4B1 and AP4E1) in 3 consanguineous families. They concluded that mutations in different AP4 subunit genes cause a clinically recognizable syndrome due to deficiency of the AP4 complex.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous deletion of chromosome 15q21.2, estimated at a minimum of 139 kb and a maximum of 222 kb. The deletion included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. High-resolution arrays defined the deletion as 192 kb, and FISH confirmed it. The mother and an older sister were heterozygous carriers, and a younger sib did not carry the deletion. Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) could not completely exclude a contribution from deletion of SPPL2A, but they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main disease mechanism.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families using autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. They identified one mutation in each of 3 genes encoding AP4 subunits. One of these was a splice site mutation in AP4E1 (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA) that resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20).

Kong et al. (2013) performed whole-exome sequencing in Moroccan identical twins born to first-cousin parents who had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia and BCG lymphadenitis. They identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in AP4E1 (R1105X). The parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib was homozygous for the wildtype allele. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or 69 Moroccan controls. The mutation did not change AP4E1 mRNA levels in patient EBV-B cells. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta were also reduced. Assembled AP-4 complex in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells was more than 95% lower than in controls. The AP-4 binding partner tepsin was not detectable in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) found that the twins reported by Kong et al. (2013) also carried a homozygous splice donor site mutation in intron 6 of the SPPL2A gene (c.733+1G-A), which Sanger sequencing confirmed. They attributed the twins' mycobacterial disease to this SPPL2A mutation and their hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that their family with the AP4E1 deletion closely resembled a family with a homozygous AP4M1 mutation. They proposed that loss of any one of the 4 AP-4 subunits disrupts the whole complex, causing a distinct autosomal recessive 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.' Consistent with this proposal, Kong et al. (2013) found that the AP4E1 R1105X mutation reduced the levels of the other AP-4 subunits and more than 95% of the assembled AP-4 complex was lost in patient cells.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion of chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18) that removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The sibs had microcephaly, spastic tetraplegia, severe to profound intellectual disability and seizures. The deletion was defined by high-resolution arrays and confirmed by FISH. The mother and an older sister were heterozygous carriers, and the father was inferred to be a carrier. The authors could not completely exclude a contribution from the loss of SPPL2A, but they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main mechanism.

**.0002 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 4-BP DEL, NT542+1 [c.542+1_542+4delgtaa; p.E181Gfs; p.E181fs]

In affected members of a consanguineous family with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). They found it using autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger sequencing or exome sequencing. The mutation resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20). ⚠︎

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105*]

In identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, Kong et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene. The twins had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, intellectual disability, absent speech and BCG lymphadenitis. The parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib was homozygous for the wildtype allele. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells the mutation did not change AP4E1 mRNA levels, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable. The assembled AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower in patient cells than in controls. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) found that the same twins were also homozygous for a splice site mutation in SPPL2A (c.733+1G-A). They attributed the twins' mycobacterial disease to the SPPL2A mutation and their hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.
