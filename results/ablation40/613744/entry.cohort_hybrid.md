### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 is a neurodevelopmental disorder in which early infantile hypotonia progresses to spasticity of the lower limbs or of all 4 limbs. Affected individuals also have microcephaly, severe intellectual disability with absent or very limited speech, and an inability to walk. Other features include stereotypic laughter, drooling, and absent sphincter control. Some patients have seizures, and brain imaging may show ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, and white matter loss (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) described the disorder as an autosomal recessive form of spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy. They proposed that disruption of any one of the 4 subunits of the AP-4 complex causes a distinct autosomal recessive disorder, which they called 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.' Kong et al. (2013) regarded AP-4 deficiency syndrome as a subtype of hereditary spastic paraplegia. They noted that disorders caused by mutations in the genes encoding the 4 AP-4 subunits are designated SPG47 (AP4B1), SPG50 (AP4M1), SPG51 (AP4E1), and SPG52 (AP4S1).

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family who had been described as having cerebral palsy. Patient IV-4, a woman, was born at term after an uneventful delivery. At birth she had microcephaly and hypotonia, which progressed by the first year of life to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia. She never developed speech or walked independently. She had been wheelchair-bound since age 5 years, which was also when generalized tonic-clonic seizures began. At age 23 years she had microcephaly (-3 SD), profoundly impaired cognitive ability, drooling, and outbursts of stereotypic laughter. Her dysmorphic features were bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face with a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum with an everted full upper vermilion, prominent antehelix, and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was more pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing. Her brother, patient IV-5, had microcephaly and hypotonia postnatally, and developmental delay was evident by the first year of life. He acquired some dysarthric words but never normal speech. He could walk with support, but increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. At age 22 years he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia and hyperreflexia, outbursts of stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, and severe cognitive deficits. His dysmorphic features and EEG were very similar to his sister's. Head CT showed moderate ventriculomegaly with slightly prominent cisterns. Both sibs had a Babinski sign and lacked sphincter control, and their eye and hearing evaluations were normal. Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that the phenotype closely resembled that of a Moroccan sibship with a homozygous AP4M1 mutation reported by Verkerk et al. (2009). Both families had infantile hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraplegia, microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, stereotypic laughter, drooling, ventriculomegaly, and white matter loss, but only the AP4E1 sibs had epilepsy.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families with mutations in AP4E1, AP4S1, or AP4B1. They had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, and muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia. Other features were microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation. The authors concluded that deficiency of the AP4 complex causes a clinically recognizable syndrome.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. Neonatal asphyxia was noted at delivery. When examined at 3 years of age, the twins had marked developmental retardation, microcephaly (head circumference -2 SD and -2.5 SD), inability to walk unaided, and abnormal speech. They smiled or laughed for no obvious reason and drooled. Both had muscular hypotonia and spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities without hypertonia, hyperreflexia, or Babinski sign. Neither had seizures or involuntary movements. Their facial gestalt included a prominent bulbous nose, a wide mouth, and coarse features. Both twins had short stature, low body weight, absent sphincter control, loss of acquired function, and pseudobulbar signs. CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. One twin had slight myopia, astigmatism, and left-sided conductive deafness, and the other had slight astigmatism. Both had been vaccinated with BCG a few days after birth. At 9 months of age both developed unilateral enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph nodes, and biopsy showed acid-fast bacilli. The twins had normal leukocyte subset counts and no immunoglobulin or complement defect. The IL-12/IFN-gamma circuit, IFN-gamma-induced GAF DNA-binding activity, and oxidative burst were also normal. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) restudied these twins as part of a study of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD). Whole-exome sequencing and genomewide linkage analysis showed that both twins carried a homozygous splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene (c.733+1G-A), which caused skipping of exon 6. The authors found that SPPL2a deficiency is an autosomal recessive cause of MSMD. They noted that, in addition to MSMD, the twins had hereditary spastic paraplegia due to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 is autosomal recessive. In the consanguineous family reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), both affected sibs were homozygous for the deletion and their mother was a heterozygous carrier. In the consanguineous Moroccan family reported by Kong et al. (2013), the affected twins were homozygous for the mutation and both parents were heterozygous.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used chromosomal microarray analysis to identify a homozygous deletion at chromosome 15q21.2 that included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. High-resolution array analysis defined the deletion as 192 kb (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18), and FISH confirmed it. The mother was a heterozygous carrier. A paternal sample was not available, but SNRPN methylation analysis showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, so the father was inferred to be a carrier. An unaffected older sister was a heterozygous carrier, and a younger sib did not carry the deletion. The authors could not completely rule out a pathogenic role for the codeleted SPPL2A gene, but they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main mechanism underlying the phenotype.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia using autozygosity mapping combined with either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. They found one mutation in each of 3 genes encoding AP4 subunits. In AP4E1, this was a splice mutation (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA) that resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). The other 2 mutations were a nonsense mutation in AP4S1 and a frameshift mutation in AP4B1.

In identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, Kong et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X), which they confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib did not carry the mutation. The mutation was not found in 1,050 controls from the CEPH panel or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells, the mutation had no effect on AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of the AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta subunits were also reduced. Native immunoprecipitation in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells showed that levels of assembled AP-4 complex were more than 95% lower than in controls. In patient fibroblasts, the AP-4-binding partner tepsin was not detectable.

### Pathogenesis

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) proposed that disruption of any one of the 4 AP-4 subunits abolishes the function of the whole complex. Their evidence was the very similar phenotypes of families with homozygous disruption of AP4E1 or AP4M1, antisense downregulation data for the mu subunit, and knockout mice lacking the beta subunit.

Kong et al. (2013) concluded that the C-terminal part of the AP-4-epsilon subunit plays an important role in maintaining the integrity of the AP-4 complex. They noted that the R1105X mutation lies close to the normal termination codon and does not produce a transcript subject to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. They suggested that the resulting C-terminally truncated protein might have dominant-negative or gain-of-function activity, and they proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease in their patients. Kong et al. (2018) later found that these twins were also homozygous for a loss-of-function splice mutation in the SPPL2A gene, and they identified autosomal recessive SPPL2a deficiency as a cause of mycobacterial disease.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL [chr15:48835480-49028171 (hg18) deletion]

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) studied 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy, profound intellectual disability, microcephaly, and epilepsy. In both sibs they identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion at chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18) that included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The deletion was found by chromosomal microarray analysis and confirmed by FISH. The mother and an unaffected sister were heterozygous carriers, and the father was inferred to be a carrier.

**.0002 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 4-BP DEL, 542+1 [c.542+1_542+4delgtaa; p.E181Gfs; p.E181fs; NM_007347.3:c.542+1_542+4delGTAA; r.421_542del; p.Glu181Glyfs*20]

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 1 of 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability, progressive spastic paraplegia, shy character, and short stature. In this family they identified a splice mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA) that resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). The mutation was found by autozygosity mapping combined with either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing.

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105*; p.R1105X]

Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene in identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. The twins had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia with intellectual disability, microcephaly, and short stature, as well as BCG lymphadenitis. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous, a healthy sib did not carry the mutation, and the mutation was absent from 1,050 CEPH controls and 69 Moroccan controls. In patient cells, AP4E1 mRNA was unaffected, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable and levels of assembled AP-4 complex were more than 95% lower than in controls. Kong et al. (2018) later found that the twins were also homozygous for a splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene, which they linked to the mycobacterial disease.
