### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.2.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 is a complex neurologic disorder that begins in infancy. Hypotonia progresses to spastic paraplegia or tetraplegia, and patients have microcephaly, severe to profound intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, inability to walk, stereotypic laughter, drooling, and growth retardation. Some patients have seizures (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). The disorder belongs to a group of clinically similar conditions, called 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome', that are caused by mutations in genes encoding subunits of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4) (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) described a brother and sister from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with an autosomal recessive form of spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy. Both sibs also had profound intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, stereotypic laughter, drooling, and similar facial dysmorphism. Neither sib developed normal speech or sphincter control, and both became wheelchair-bound.

The female sib (IV-4) in the family reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) was born at term and had microcephaly and hypotonia at birth. By the first year of life this had progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia. She never spoke or walked independently. She had been wheelchair-bound since age 5 years, when generalized tonic-clonic seizures began. At age 23 she had microcephaly (-3 SD), profound cognitive impairment, drooling, stereotypic laughter, and facial dysmorphism. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was most pronounced frontally and in the corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing.

Her brother (IV-5) was also born at term and had microcephaly and hypotonia after birth, with developmental delay evident by the first year of life (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011). He acquired only some dysarthric words and could walk with support, but increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic seizures at age 15. At age 22 he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia and hyperreflexia, stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, and severe cognitive deficits. Head CT showed moderate ventriculomegaly with slightly prominent cisterns.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families. They had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, a shy character, stereotypic laughter, muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation. One of these families carried an AP4E1 mutation (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

Kong et al. (2013) reported 3-year-old identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. The twins had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia with onset in infancy. Features included marked developmental delay, microcephaly (head circumference -2 SD and -2.5 SD), severe intellectual disability, inability to walk, absent speech, early infantile hypotonia, and spastic paraplegia of the lower limbs. They also had stereotypic laughter, drooling and other pseudobulbar signs, short stature, and low body weight. Their facial gestalt included a prominent bulbous nose, a wide mouth, and coarse features. CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. The twins had no seizures, hyperreflexia, or Babinski signs. Kong et al. (2018) later reported the same twins at age 8 years and noted that their pregnancy had no complications, but that neonatal asphyxia was noted at delivery.

### Other Features

Both twins reported by Kong et al. (2013) had received BCG vaccination a few days after birth. At 9 months of age each developed a unilateral enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph node. Biopsy of the surgically removed nodes showed acid-fast bacilli consistent with mycobacterial infection. Neither twin had another mycobacterial episode by age 3 years. Kong et al. (2013) proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease (see GENOTYPE/PHENOTYPE CORRELATIONS). However, Kong et al. (2018) later attributed the Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) in these twins to a homozygous SPPL2A splice mutation (c.733+1G-A). They stated that the twins' hereditary spastic paraplegia was due to the homozygous AP4E1 mutation.

### Biochemical Features

Kong et al. (2013) found no immunologic abnormality to explain the mycobacterial disease in the twins with SPG51. Leukocyte subsets, immunoglobulins, and complement were normal. The IL-12/IFN-gamma circuit, IFN-gamma-induced GAF DNA binding, and superoxide production were also normal. Exome data showed no causative mutation in known MSMD genes. A heterozygous IFNGR1 Q190R variant and an IL12RB2 K768N variant were judged nonfunctional.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 in the families reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), Abou Jamra et al. (2011), and Kong et al. (2013) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

In the Palestinian-Jordanian family, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used FISH to show that the mother of the affected sibs was a heterozygous carrier of the deletion. No paternal sample was available. SNRPN methylation analysis in both patients showed both methylated and unmethylated alleles, consistent with biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, so the father was inferred to be a carrier. Of the unaffected relatives tested, the older sister was a heterozygous carrier and the younger sib did not carry the deletion.

In the Moroccan family reported by Kong et al. (2013), both affected twins were homozygous for the AP4E1 mutation, the consanguineous parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was wildtype.

### Heterogeneity

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that their family with the AP4E1 deletion closely resembled 5 Moroccan sibs with a homozygous AP4M1 mutation reported by Verkerk et al. (2009). The shared features were infantile hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraplegia, microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, stereotypic laughter, drooling, ventriculomegaly, and white matter loss. Unlike the AP4M1 patients, both AP4E1 patients had epilepsy.

In 2 consanguineous families with the same phenotype as their AP4E1 family, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a nonsense mutation in the AP4S1 gene (c.124C-T, arg42-to-ter, R42X) and a frameshift mutation in the AP4B1 gene (c.487_488insTAT, Glu163_Ser739delinsVal). These findings showed that mutations in different AP4 subunit genes cause the disorder.

Kong et al. (2013) noted that 27 patients from 9 kindreds with mutations in AP4E1, AP4B1, AP4S1, or AP4M1 shared a uniform phenotype of complex hereditary spastic paraplegia. They described it as an 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome' with severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, progressive spastic paraplegia, growth retardation, and stereotypic laughter.

### Molecular Genetics

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used chromosomal microarray analysis to identify a homozygous deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 in both affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51. High-resolution array analysis refined the deletion to 192 kb (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18), and FISH confirmed it. The deletion included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The authors could not completely rule out a contribution from SPPL2A, but proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main genetic mechanism underlying the phenotype.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia. They used autozygosity mapping combined with either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. In 1 family they identified a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The mutation resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20). In the other 2 families they found mutations in other AP4 subunit genes (see HETEROGENEITY).

Kong et al. (2013) performed whole-exome sequencing on one of the affected Moroccan twins with SPG51. They identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family. It was absent from 1,050 CEPH controls from 51 ethnic groups and from 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells, the mutation did not alter mRNA levels or splicing of AP4E1 or of the other AP-4 subunit genes. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta were reduced. Native immunoprecipitation showed that assembled AP-4 in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells was more than 95% lower than in controls. Immunofluorescence confirmed the loss of AP-4 and of its binding partner tepsin. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kong et al. (2013) noted that the AP4E1 R1105X mutation lies close to the normal stop codon and is not predicted to trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. They suggested that it might produce a C-terminally truncated protein with dominant-negative or gain-of-function activity. In their view, this could explain the mycobacterial phenotype in their patients, which had not been seen in other AP4E1 patients. Kong et al. (2018) later attributed the mycobacterial disease in these twins to a homozygous SPPL2A mutation (see OTHER FEATURES).

### Pathogenesis

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) cited the similar phenotypes of families with disruptions in the epsilon (AP4E1) and mu (AP4M1) subunits, together with a mouse knockout of the beta subunit that lacks functional AP-4. On this basis they proposed that disruption of any one of the 4 AP-4 subunits abolishes the function of the complex, causing a distinct autosomal recessive disorder that they termed 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome'. They noted that AP-4 selectively transports AMPA and delta-2 glutamate receptors from the trans-Golgi network to the somatodendritic domain.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) concluded that their results supported the hypothesis that trafficking mediated by the AP4 complex plays a crucial role in brain development and function. They also concluded that deficiency of the AP4 complex causes a clinically recognizable syndrome.

Kong et al. (2013) found that the AP4E1 R1105X mutation reduced AP-4-epsilon protein to barely detectable levels and also reduced levels of the AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta subunits. It reduced assembled AP-4 complex by more than 95% and led to loss of the AP-4 binding partner tepsin. These results indicated that the C-terminal part of AP-4-epsilon is important for the integrity of the complex.

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105*]

Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene in identical twin girls, born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, who had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia with onset in infancy. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib was wildtype. The mutation was absent from 1,050 CEPH controls from 51 ethnic groups and from 69 Moroccan controls. It did not alter AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing in patient cells, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable. Assembled AP-4 complex was reduced by more than 95% in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells, and the AP-4 binding partner tepsin was lost. The twins also had BCG-related mycobacterial lymphadenitis, which Kong et al. (2013) suggested might result from a dominant-negative or gain-of-function effect of the truncated protein. Kong et al. (2018) later attributed the mycobacterial disease to a coexisting homozygous SPPL2A splice mutation and stated that the spastic paraplegia was due to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.
