### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene (611507) on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is an autosomal recessive disorder. It was first described in consanguineous Jordanian families with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy. Unlike the classic form of Wolfram syndrome, these families had no diabetes insipidus, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding (El-Shanti et al., 2000). A later patient had optic neuropathy without optic atrophy, high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss and a platelet aggregation defect (Mozzillo et al., 2014). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients from 4 families who had the Wolfram syndrome phenotype of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy, along with features not previously reported. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) described a Caucasian Italian girl with WFS2 whose parents said they were not consanguineous. From age 5 years she had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. At 14 years she was diagnosed with malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception, which needed resection. At 16 years she developed reduced visual acuity, dyschromatopsia and bilateral optic disc pallor. Nonautoimmune diabetes mellitus began at 17 years and was treated with insulin. She also had bilateral high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, slow intestinal transit and entero-gastric bile reflux.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) concluded that this patient had optic neuropathy rather than optic atrophy. Fundus examination showed slight bilateral optic disc pallor, and pupillary light reflexes were preserved. Pattern VEPs were normal, and brain MRI showed normal optic nerve caliber. Visual field testing, ERG, microperimetry and OCT showed moderate involvement of the optic nerve in both eyes. These findings were stable over 5 years of follow-up, and at age 22 years she had no optic atrophy.

### Biochemical Features

Mozzillo et al. (2014) found that their WFS2 patient had a reduced, reversible platelet aggregation defect in response to ADP at concentrations up to 10 microM, and no platelet secretion in response to ADP. Aggregation in response to collagen, epinephrine and ristocetin was normal. Her bleeding score was 3 (normal 3 or less), and other coagulation tests were normal. Her heterozygous parents and brother also had a reversible platelet aggregation defect in response to ADP, but none of them had ophthalmologic abnormalities, diabetes or deafness. The authors noted that the Jordanian patients had been reported to have defective aggregation to collagen rather than to ADP.

### Inheritance

El-Shanti et al. (2000) mapped WFS2 by homozygosity mapping in families with Wolfram syndrome, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous mutation in 3 consanguineous Jordanian families.

The parents of the patient reported by Mozzillo et al. (2014) said they were not consanguineous, although their grandparents were born on the same small Italian island where the family still lives. Long runs of homozygosity in SNP-array data showed no degree of parental relationship. However, microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor, and the patient's estimated inbreeding coefficient was 0.0087. Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers of the deletion for which she was homozygous.

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 4 families with Wolfram syndrome using 3 microsatellite markers linked to the 4p16.1 Wolfram syndrome locus (D4S432, D4S3023 and D4S2366). They significantly excluded linkage to that region in 3 of the families. In those 3 families, homozygosity mapping showed linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24.

Amr et al. (2007) noted that the 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome had no WFS1 mutations and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus on chromosome 4q22-q25.

### Heterogeneity

In 1 of the 4 families studied by El-Shanti et al. (2000), the 2 affected individuals were homozygous for all 3 markers from the 4p16.1 linkage region. The other 3 families showed linkage to 4q22-q24, which the authors presented as further evidence of genetic heterogeneity in Wolfram syndrome.

Because WFS1 mutations can also cause nonsyndromic or isolated hearing phenotypes, Amr et al. (2007) screened 377 probands with hearing loss for mutations in the WFS2 gene (ZCD2, now CISD2). They found none.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) argued that the 'asymptomatic' optic atrophy described in the Jordanian WFS2 patients was not fully supported, and that their ocular disease was probably optic neuropathy. Because optic atrophy is a main diagnostic criterion for Wolfram syndrome, they suggested, with caution, that the so-called WFS2 may not be a subtype of Wolfram syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with Wolfram syndrome. These families had previously been shown not to carry WFS1 mutations and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus. In all 3 families, Amr et al. (2007) identified a homozygous mutation in the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene: a G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 (c.109G-C) predicting a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution at a conserved residue. They concluded that the mutation causes disease by disrupting mRNA splicing, which eliminates exon 2 and introduces a premature stop codon.

In a Caucasian Italian girl with WFS2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a novel homozygous intragenic deletion of the CISD2 gene that removed all of exon 2. They detected it because exon 2 failed to amplify by PCR, and they confirmed it by cDNA sequencing. SNP-array analysis of the trio limited the deletion to a maximum size of 2,050 bp, extending from intron 2 to intron 3, with a minimum size of 739 bp (GRCh38). Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers. The authors noted that the deleted region includes the codons for amino acids WRSKT (residues 102 to 106) within the 2Fe-2S domain, which may result in a nonfunctional protein. ⚠︎

Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a novel mutation in the CISD2 gene in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome 2.

### Pathogenesis

Amr et al. (2007) showed that ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein), the protein encoded by ZCD2, localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum, as wolframin does, but does not interact directly with wolframin. When stimulated with thapsigargin, lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals showed a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than control cells. Resting intracellular calcium concentrations did not differ.

Chen et al. (2009) showed that Cisd2 is located mainly in the mitochondria. In Cisd2 knockout mice, mitochondrial degeneration appeared to directly trigger accelerated aging. The authors concluded that their findings support the earlier clinical hypothesis that Wolfram syndrome is partly a mitochondria-mediated disorder, and they proposed that CISD2 mutation causes WFS2 in humans as a mitochondria-mediated disorder.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction, together with autophagic cell death. These events came before the earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration, and together they produced features suggestive of premature aging. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased in parallel with the development of the premature aging phenotype. The authors proposed the Cisd2 knockout mouse as an animal model of WFS2.
