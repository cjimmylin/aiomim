### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome-2 is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder. As with other forms of Wolfram syndrome, the minimal criteria for diagnosis are insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy (El-Shanti et al., 2000; Mozzillo et al., 2014). WFS2 differs from WFS1 because it lacks diabetes insipidus and psychiatric disorders and includes bleeding upper intestinal ulcers and defective platelet aggregation (El-Shanti et al., 2000; Mozzillo et al., 2014).

### Nomenclature

Amr et al. (2007) referred to the WFS2 gene as ZCD2 and named its protein product ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein). Chen et al. (2009) and Mozzillo et al. (2014) referred to the gene as CISD2.

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients with the Wolfram syndrome phenotype from 4 families. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus. Several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding, which had not previously been reported in Wolfram syndrome.

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with Wolfram syndrome. These families had no mutations in WFS1 and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) reported a 17-year-old Caucasian girl born to parents who reported no consanguinity. They described her as the first Caucasian patient with WFS2. Recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers had begun at 5 years of age. At 14 years, she was diagnosed with malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception and underwent resection of part of the duodenum with duodenal-jejunal anastomosis. At 16 years, she developed reduced visual acuity (20/40 right, 20/50 left) and dyschromatopsia. She had bilateral optic disc pallor with preserved pupillary light reflexes. Pattern visual evoked potentials were normal, and brain MRI showed normal optic nerve caliber. At 17 years, she developed nonautoimmune diabetes and started insulin. Further studies showed bilateral high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, slow intestinal transit, and entero-gastric bile reflux. Ocular findings were unchanged after 5 years of follow-up. Visual field testing, electroretinography, and OCT of the macula and retinal nerve fiber layer showed moderate bilateral optic nerve involvement. These findings were compatible with optic neuropathy, and optic atrophy was excluded. Platelets showed reduced, reversible aggregation in response to ADP and absent secretion in response to ADP. Aggregation in response to collagen, epinephrine, and ristocetin was normal. At 22 years, she had diabetes, optic neuropathy with preserved visual acuity, tinnitus, high-frequency hearing loss, bile reflux, slow intestinal transit, the platelet aggregation defect, and bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. Her heterozygous parents and brother were apparently healthy, but they also showed reversible defective platelet aggregation in response to ADP.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) noted that in the earlier Jordanian WFS2 patients, a bleeding tendency and gastric ulcers had been observed in 79% and 90%, respectively, and defective platelet aggregation had been reported with collagen. Optic atrophy in those patients had been seen after childhood or described as 'asymptomatic.' The authors suggested that the ocular disease in the Jordanian patients was probably optic neuropathy rather than optic atrophy. If so, they argued, WFS2 might not be a subtype of Wolfram syndrome, since optic atrophy is a main diagnostic criterion. They proposed that the difference in platelet findings (ADP versus collagen) might reflect differences in experimental conditions or variability between patients.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) reported a novel CISD2 mutation in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome-2.

### Inheritance

El-Shanti et al. (2000) described Wolfram syndrome as an autosomal recessive disorder. Autosomal recessive inheritance of WFS2 was supported by the homozygous CISD2 mutations found in consanguineous Jordanian families (Amr et al., 2007). It was also supported by an Italian patient with a homozygous deletion whose parents and brother were heterozygous carriers (Mozzillo et al., 2014).

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 4 families with Wolfram syndrome using 3 microsatellite markers (D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366) previously linked to the 4p16.1 locus. They significantly excluded linkage to this region in 3 of the 4 families. Linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24 was then demonstrated in these 3 families.

### Heterogeneity

El-Shanti et al. (2000) found that, in 1 of their 4 families, the 2 affected individuals were homozygous for all 3 markers from the 4p16.1 region linked to Wolfram syndrome. The other 3 families showed linkage to 4q22-q24, which supported genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with WFS2 mapped to 4q22-q25. They identified a homozygous missense mutation (E37Q) in the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene. The mutation also disrupted mRNA splicing, eliminating exon 2 and introducing a premature stop codon. The authors concluded that this aberrant splicing was the basis of the disorder. Screening of 377 probands with hearing loss found no mutations in the gene. The encoded protein, ERIS, localized to the endoplasmic reticulum but did not interact directly with wolframin. Lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals had normal resting intracellular calcium. However, when stimulated with thapsigargin, they showed a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than controls.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a homozygous intragenic deletion of the entire CISD2 exon 2 in an Italian girl with WFS2. The deletion was found by failure to PCR-amplify exon 2 and by cDNA sequencing. SNP array analysis of the trio confirmed it and showed that her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers. The parents reported no consanguinity, and runs of homozygosity showed no parental relationship. However, microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a novel CISD2 mutation in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome-2.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2-deficient mice had features suggestive of premature aging. Mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction with autophagic cell death occurred before the earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration. Cisd2 localized mainly to the mitochondria. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased in parallel with the premature aging phenotype. The authors proposed that WFS2 is a mitochondria-mediated disorder.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, GLU37GLN [c.109G>C; p.Glu37Gln; E37Q]

Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 of the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene in affected members of 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome-2. The change predicts a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution at a conserved residue. The mutation also disrupted mRNA splicing, eliminating exon 2 and introducing a premature stop codon. The authors proposed this splicing defect as the basis of the disorder. Lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals showed a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than controls after stimulation with thapsigargin.

**.0002 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, EX2DEL

Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a homozygous intragenic deletion of the entire exon 2 of the CISD2 gene in an Italian girl with WFS2. Her features included diabetes mellitus, optic neuropathy, bleeding upper intestinal ulcers, sensorineural hearing loss, and defective platelet aggregation to ADP. The deletion was detected by failure to PCR-amplify exon 2 and by cDNA sequencing, and SNP array analysis confirmed it. Its maximum size was restricted to 2,050 bp between intron 2 and intron 3. The deleted region includes the codons for amino acids 102 to 106, within the 2Fe-2S domain. Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers.
