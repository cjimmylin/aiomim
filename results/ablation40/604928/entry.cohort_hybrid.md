### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene (611507) on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome-2 is an autosomal recessive form of Wolfram syndrome. El-Shanti et al. (2000) noted that only insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy are needed to diagnose Wolfram syndrome. In the families they studied, the WFS2 phenotype differed from the classic syndrome in 2 ways: none of the affected members had diabetes insipidus, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding. Mozzillo et al. (2014) also described defective platelet aggregation in WFS2. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients with the Wolfram syndrome phenotype from 4 families. They reported features that had not been described before in the syndrome. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) reported a Caucasian girl with WFS2, the first child of parents who said they were not consanguineous. She had had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers since age 5 years. At age 14, she was diagnosed with malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception and underwent resection of a duodenal tract with duodenal-jejunal anastomosis. At age 16, she developed reduced visual acuity and dyschromatopsia. Best corrected visual acuity was 20/40 in the right eye and 20/50 in the left. Fundus examination showed optic disc pallor with preserved pupillary light reflexes, pattern visual evoked potentials were normal, and brain MRI showed normal optic nerve caliber. Non-autoimmune diabetes developed at age 17 years, and she started insulin. Further studies found bilateral sensorineural hearing loss in the high frequencies, slow intestinal transit, and entero-gastric bile reflux. Visual findings were unchanged after 5 years of follow-up. Visual field testing, ERG, and OCT of the macula and retinal nerve fiber layer then showed moderate involvement of the optic nerve in both eyes. The authors considered this compatible with optic neuropathy and excluded optic atrophy. Platelet aggregation to ADP was reduced and reversible up to 10 microM, and platelet secretion in response to ADP was absent. Responses to collagen, epinephrine, and ristocetin were normal. At age 22 years, she had diabetes, optic neuropathy with preserved visual acuity, tinnitus from the high-frequency hearing loss, bile reflux, slow intestinal transit, defective platelet aggregation to ADP, and bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. Her parents and brother, who were heterozygous carriers, had no ophthalmologic abnormalities, diabetes, or deafness, but they did have a reversible platelet aggregation defect in response to ADP.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) suggested that the 'asymptomatic' optic atrophy reported in Jordanian WFS2 patients may also have been optic neuropathy. Because optic atrophy is a main diagnostic criterion for Wolfram syndrome, they suggested that the so-called WFS2 might not be a subtype of Wolfram syndrome. They also noted that their patient had impaired platelet aggregation to ADP rather than to collagen, as had been reported in the Jordanian patients. Since both collagen- and ADP-induced aggregation are calcium-mediated, they proposed that different experimental conditions or variability between patients could explain the difference.

### Inheritance

El-Shanti et al. (2000) described Wolfram syndrome as an autosomal recessive disorder. Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous mutation in 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with WFS2, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Mozzillo et al. (2014) found a homozygous CISD2 deletion in an affected girl, while her parents and brother were heterozygous.

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) used homozygosity mapping in 16 patients with Wolfram syndrome from 4 families. With 3 microsatellite markers linked to the chromosome 4p16.1 locus (D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366), they significantly excluded linkage in 3 of the families. In those 3 families, they showed linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24.

Amr et al. (2007) noted that the 3 consanguineous Jordanian families had no mutations in the WFS1 gene and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus at 4q22-q25.

### Heterogeneity

El-Shanti et al. (2000) found further evidence for genetic heterogeneity of Wolfram syndrome. In 1 of their 4 families, the 2 affected members were homozygous for all 3 markers from the 4p16.1 linkage region. The other 3 families showed linkage to 4q22-q24.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with WFS2, Amr et al. (2007) identified a homozygous mutation in the ZCD2 gene (CISD2): a G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 predicting a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution. The amino acid is conserved, but the authors concluded that the mutation caused disease by aberrant splicing. The mutation removed exon 2 from the mRNA and introduced a premature stop codon. They found no mutations in the gene when they screened 377 probands with hearing loss.

In a Caucasian girl with WFS2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a homozygous intragenic deletion in the CISD2 gene that removed exon 2. They found it by PCR and cDNA sequencing and confirmed it by SNP-array analysis. Her parents and brother were heterozygous for the deletion. The parents said they were not consanguineous, and runs of homozygosity showed no parental relationship, but microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a novel CISD2 mutation in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome-2.

### Pathogenesis

Amr et al. (2007) showed that the ZCD2-encoded protein, which they named ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein), localized to the endoplasmic reticulum but did not interact directly with wolframin. After stimulation with thapsigargin, lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals had a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than control cells. Resting intracellular calcium concentrations did not differ.

Chen et al. (2009) found that the Cisd2 protein was located mainly in the mitochondria. They stated that their knockout mouse work supported the hypothesis that Wolfram syndrome is in part a mitochondria-mediated disorder, and they proposed that CISD2 mutation causes the mitochondria-mediated disorder WFS2 in humans.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction with autophagic cell death. These changes preceded the earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration, and together they led to phenotypic features suggestive of premature aging. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased in parallel with the premature aging phenotype.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, GLU37GLN [c.109G>C; p.E37Q]

In affected members of 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome-2, Amr et al. (2007) identified a homozygous G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 of the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene, predicting a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution. The amino acid is conserved, but the mutation disrupted mRNA splicing by removing exon 2, which introduced a premature stop codon. Lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals had a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium after thapsigargin stimulation than control cells.

**.0002 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, EX2DEL [homozygous deletion affecting the whole exon 2 of CISD2]

In a Caucasian girl with Wolfram syndrome-2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a homozygous intragenic deletion that removed all of CISD2 exon 2. Using SNP-array analysis, they estimated a minimum deletion size of 739 bp and restricted the maximum to a 2,050-bp region spanning from intron 2 to intron 3. The deleted region included the codons for amino acids 102 to 106 within the 2Fe-2S domain, which the authors suggested may produce a nonfunctional protein. Her parents and brother were heterozygous and had no ophthalmologic abnormalities, diabetes, or deafness, but they had a reversible platelet aggregation defect in response to ADP.
