### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene (611507) on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome-2 is an autosomal recessive disorder. Its features include insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy, without diabetes insipidus. Some patients have profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding. A defect in platelet aggregation has also been reported.

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients with Wolfram syndrome from 4 families. Their patients had the Wolfram syndrome phenotype, with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy, and also had features that had not been reported before. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus. Several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) reported a Caucasian Italian girl (FS) who was the first child of nonconsanguineous parents. From the age of 5 years she had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. She also had intestinal malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception. Ophthalmologic findings included dyschromatopsia, optic disc pallor, visual field defects, and optic neuropathy. At 22 years of age she had non-autoimmune diabetes mellitus and optic neuropathy, with preserved visual acuity and electrophysiologic tests and no optic atrophy. Other findings at that age were tinnitus due to bilateral high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss triggered by loud environments, entero-gastric bile reflux, slow intestinal transit, and bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. Her platelets showed a reduced and reversible aggregation defect in response to ADP, with absent platelet secretion in response to ADP. Aggregation in response to collagen was normal. Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers. They had no ophthalmologic abnormalities, diabetes, or deafness, but they did have a reversible platelet aggregation defect in response to ADP.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) pointed out that their patient had optic neuropathy rather than optic atrophy at age 22 years. They suggested that the 'asymptomatic' optic atrophy reported in Jordanian WFS2 patients may also have been optic neuropathy. Because optic atrophy is a main diagnostic criterion for Wolfram syndrome, they cautioned that the so-called WFS2 might not be a subtype of Wolfram syndrome. The Jordanian patients had been reported with a platelet aggregation defect in response to collagen, whereas their patient's defect was in response to ADP. Mozzillo et al. (2014) noted that aggregation to both agonists is calcium-mediated, and they proposed that different experimental conditions or variability between patients could explain the discrepancy.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) reported Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome-2.

### Inheritance

Wolfram syndrome-2 shows autosomal recessive inheritance. El-Shanti et al. (2000) mapped the locus by homozygosity mapping in affected families. Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous mutation in 3 consanguineous Jordanian families. Mozzillo et al. (2014) found a homozygous deletion in an affected girl whose parents and brother were heterozygous.

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients with Wolfram syndrome from 4 families. Using 3 microsatellite markers linked to the 4p16.1 locus (D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366), they significantly excluded linkage in 3 of the families. Homozygosity mapping then showed linkage to chromosome 4q22-24 in those 3 families.

Amr et al. (2007) noted that 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome had been shown not to carry WFS1 mutations and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus at 4q22-q25.

### Heterogeneity

El-Shanti et al. (2000) concluded that their results gave further evidence for genetic heterogeneity of Wolfram syndrome. In 1 of their 4 families, the 2 affected members were homozygous for all 3 markers in the 4p16.1 linkage region. The authors also stated that they had found a new locus involved in the etiology of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus.

### Molecular Genetics

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with Wolfram syndrome-2. In all 3 families they identified a single homozygous missense mutation in a novel, highly conserved zinc-finger gene, ZCD2 (CISD2). The mutation was a G-C transversion at nucleotide 109, predicting a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution. Amr et al. (2007) found that the mutation caused disease through aberrant splicing rather than through the amino acid change: it removed exon 2 from the mRNA and introduced a premature stop codon. They found no mutations in the WFS2 gene when they screened 377 probands with hearing loss.

In an Italian girl with Wolfram syndrome-2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a homozygous intragenic deletion in the CISD2 gene affecting the whole of exon 2. Her parents and brother were heterozygous for the deletion. Mapping by SNP-array analysis restricted the maximum deletion size to a 2,050-bp region spanning from intron 2 to intron 3, with a minimum size of 739 bp. The deleted region included the codons for amino acids 102 to 106 within the 2Fe-2S domain, which the authors suggested may result in a nonfunctional protein. ⚠︎

Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a novel CISD2 mutation in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome-2.

### Pathogenesis

Amr et al. (2007) found that the protein encoded by ZCD2 (CISD2), which they named ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein), localized to the endoplasmic reticulum but did not interact directly with wolframin. In lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals, stimulation with thapsigargin produced a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than in control cells. Resting intracellular calcium concentrations did not differ.

Chen et al. (2009) found that the Cisd2 protein was located mainly in the mitochondria and that CISD2 is involved in controlling mammalian life span. They stated that their findings supported the hypothesis that Wolfram syndrome is in part a mitochondria-mediated disorder, and they proposed that mutation of CISD2 causes the mitochondria-mediated disorder WFS2 in humans.

### Population Genetics

The parents of the patient reported by Mozzillo et al. (2014) said they were not consanguineous, although their grandparents had been born on the same small Italian island. Runs of homozygosity in the SNP-array data did not show any parental relationship, but microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction with autophagic cell death. These changes came before the earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration, and together they led to phenotypic features suggestive of premature aging. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased in parallel with the premature aging phenotype.
