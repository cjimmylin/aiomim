### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene (611507) on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome-2 is an autosomal recessive disorder. Like Wolfram syndrome, it is defined by insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy. In the families in which it was first described, diabetes insipidus was absent and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding (El-Shanti et al., 2000). Mozzillo et al. (2014) noted that WFS2 differs from WFS1 in that it lacks diabetes insipidus and psychiatric disorders and includes bleeding upper intestinal ulcers and defective platelet aggregation. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Mozzillo et al. (2014) suggested that the ocular disease in the Jordanian WFS2 patients was probably optic neuropathy rather than optic atrophy, because the 'asymptomatic' optic atrophy described in them was not fully supported. Optic atrophy is a main diagnostic criterion for Wolfram syndrome. Mozzillo et al. (2014) therefore suggested, with caution and noting that theirs was a single case report, that the so-called WFS2 might not be a subtype of Wolfram syndrome.

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients from 4 families who had the Wolfram syndrome phenotype. They also had features that had not been reported before. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) described a Caucasian girl with WFS2. She was the first child of parents who said they were not consanguineous. From age 5 years she had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. At 14 years she was diagnosed with malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception, and a duodenal segment was resected with duodenal-jejunal anastomosis. At 16 years she developed reduced visual acuity and dyschromatopsia. Best corrected visual acuity was 20/40 in the right eye and 20/50 in the left. Fundus examination showed optic disc pallor with preserved pupillary light reflexes bilaterally. Pattern VEPs were normal, and brain MRI showed normal optic nerve caliber. Nonautoimmune diabetes began at 17 years and was treated with insulin. She also had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss in the high frequencies, slow intestinal transit, and entero-gastric bile reflux. Her ocular findings were unchanged after 5 years of follow-up. Visual field testing, ERG, microperimetry, and OCT then showed moderate involvement of the optic nerve in both eyes, consistent with optic neuropathy, and excluded optic atrophy. At 22 years she had diabetes mellitus, optic neuropathy with preserved visual acuity and normal electrophysiologic tests, no optic atrophy, tinnitus, entero-gastric bile reflux, slow intestinal transit, and bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. Her parents and brother had no ophthalmologic abnormalities, diabetes, or deafness.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) reported Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome-2.

### Biochemical Features

Mozzillo et al. (2014) found that their patient had a reduced, reversible platelet aggregation defect in response to ADP at concentrations up to 10 microM, and her platelets showed no secretion in response to ADP. Aggregation in response to collagen, epinephrine, and ristocetin was normal. Her bleeding score was 3 (normal 3 or less), and other main coagulation tests were normal. Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers and also had a reversible platelet aggregation defect in response to ADP. The authors noted that Jordanian WFS2 patients had been described with defective aggregation to collagen rather than to ADP. Because collagen- and ADP-induced aggregation are both calcium-mediated, they suggested that differences in experimental conditions or variability between patients might explain the discrepancy.

### Inheritance

El-Shanti et al. (2000) described Wolfram syndrome as an autosomal recessive disorder and mapped this form by homozygosity mapping. Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous mutation in 3 consanguineous Jordanian families, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the patient reported by Mozzillo et al. (2014), the CISD2 deletion was homozygous, and her unaffected parents and brother were heterozygous. The parents reported that they were not consanguineous, but their grandparents had been born on the same small Italian island where the family still lived. Long runs of homozygosity in the SNP-array data showed no degree of parental relationship. However, microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor.

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) tested 4 families with Wolfram syndrome using 3 microsatellite markers (D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366) reported to be linked to the chromosome 4p16.1 locus. They significantly excluded linkage to this locus in 3 of the 4 families, and in those 3 families they demonstrated linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24.

Amr et al. (2007) noted that the 3 consanguineous Jordanian families had no WFS1 mutations and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus on chromosome 4q22-q25.

### Heterogeneity

In 1 of the 4 families studied by El-Shanti et al. (2000), the 2 affected individuals were homozygous for all 3 markers from the chromosome 4p16.1 linkage region. Linkage of the other 3 families to chromosome 4q22-q24 provided further evidence of genetic heterogeneity in Wolfram syndrome.

Mutations in WFS1 had also been found to cause low-frequency nonsyndromic hearing loss, progressive hearing loss, and isolated optic atrophy with hearing loss. Amr et al. (2007) screened 377 probands with hearing loss and found no mutations in the WFS2 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with WFS2, Amr et al. (2007) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene. The mutation was a G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 that predicted a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution at a conserved residue. The families had been shown not to carry WFS1 mutations and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus. Although the change was nonsynonymous, Amr et al. (2007) concluded that it causes disease through aberrant splicing: it disrupted mRNA splicing by eliminating exon 2, which introduced a premature stop codon.

In a Caucasian girl with WFS2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) identified a novel homozygous intragenic deletion of the CISD2 gene that removed the whole of exon 2. PCR failed to amplify exon 2 in the patient, and cDNA sequencing showed the deletion. SNP-array analysis of the trio confirmed the deletion and showed that her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers. Combined with the PCR data, the array results restricted the maximum size of the deletion to 2,050 bp, spanning intron 2 to intron 3; the minimum size was 739 bp. The deleted region included the codons for amino acids WRSKT (residues 102 to 106), within the 2Fe-2S domain. The authors suggested that this may result in a nonfunctional protein.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a novel mutation in the CISD2 gene in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome-2.

### Pathogenesis

Amr et al. (2007) showed that ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein), the protein encoded by ZCD2, localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum but does not interact directly with wolframin. When stimulated with thapsigargin, lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals showed a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than control cells. Resting intracellular calcium concentrations did not differ.

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 is located mainly in the mitochondria. They concluded that their knockout mouse findings support an earlier clinical hypothesis that Wolfram syndrome is in part a mitochondria-mediated disorder, and they proposed that mutation of CISD2 causes WFS2 in humans as a mitochondria-mediated disorder.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction accompanied by autophagic cell death. These events preceded the 2 earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration, and together they produced a set of features suggestive of premature aging. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased in parallel with the development of the premature aging phenotype. The authors proposed the knockout mouse as an animal model for WFS2.
