### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that auriculocondylar syndrome-4 (ARCND4) is caused by a 430-kb tandem duplication covering most of the HDAC9 gene (606543) on chromosome 7p21.1. The duplication lies telomeric to TWIST1 and includes TWIST1 regulatory elements.

### Description

Auriculocondylar syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder of the first and second pharyngeal arches. It is characterized by malformed 'question mark' ears, prominent cheeks, microstomia, an abnormal temporomandibular joint, and hypoplasia of the mandibular condyle. Penetrance appears complete, but expression varies widely within and between families (summary by Masotti et al., 2008).

### Clinical Features

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) studied a nonconsanguineous four-generation Brazilian family (F1) in which 11 members had the typical features of auriculocondylar syndrome. Of the documented individuals, 8 of 10 had question mark ears, 8 of 10 had microstomia, and 6 of 10 had micrognathia. Intrafamilial variability was considerable. Large, open fontanelles were not present.

### Inheritance

In the family reported by Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022), auriculocondylar syndrome-4 was transmitted in an autosomal dominant pattern. The duplication segregated with the phenotype, and all unaffected family members were negative for it, suggesting full penetrance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) found no pathogenic variants in PLCB4, GNAI3, or EDN1 in a four-generation Brazilian family with auriculocondylar syndrome. Linkage analysis assuming autosomal dominant inheritance gave a maximum LOD score of 2.88 (maximum theoretical 2.93) in a 17.6-Mb region on chromosome 7. Microsatellite markers narrowed this region to chr7:14395902-28158440 (hg38).

### Heterogeneity

Masotti et al. (2008) performed genome-wide linkage analysis in 2 large families with auriculocondylar syndrome. They found linkage to 1p21.1-q23.3 in one family (F2), with a maximum lod score of 3.01 at theta = 0, and named this locus ACS1. The locus was not linked to the phenotype in the second family (F1), a multigeneration family with 11 affected individuals in whom facial asymmetry was highly prevalent. Masotti et al. (2008) concluded that at least 2 loci are responsible for auriculocondylar syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) performed targeted resequencing in the Brazilian family with auriculocondylar syndrome. They identified a 430-kb tandem duplication (NC_000007.14:g.18437239_18867540dup) that began about 280 kb telomeric of TWIST1 and covered most of the HDAC9 gene. Several shorter HDAC9 isoforms were duplicated in full, whereas the full-length, catalytically active isoforms were predicted to be disrupted. Breakpoint amplification and Sanger sequencing showed that the duplication segregated with the phenotype in the family. All unaffected individuals were negative for it.

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) also found 19 patients in DECIPHER with copy-number gains in this region. Seven had ear malformations and 7 had micrognathia, and most of these duplications included the TWIST1 enhancer eTW6. One patient without features of auriculocondylar syndrome carried a 179-kb duplication that lay entirely within the family's duplication but did not include the TWIST1 regulatory elements.

### Pathogenesis

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) performed Capture-C from the TWIST1 promoter in human embryonic calvarial mesenchymal cells. The most frequent cis interactions were telomeric of TWIST1, within HDAC9 and the duplicated region. This suggested that the region contains TWIST1 regulatory elements and pointed to a possible pathogenic mechanism for the duplication.

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) derived neural crest cells from patient induced pluripotent stem cells. Compared with controls, these cells had higher expression of HDAC9 (3.15-fold) and TWIST1 (2.03-fold) and showed reduced migration (4.3-fold decrease). During osteogenic differentiation, patient mesenchymal cells had lower alkaline phosphatase activity and less matrix mineralization, which the authors took to indicate delayed or impaired osteogenesis.
