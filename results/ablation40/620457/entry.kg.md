### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that auriculocondylar syndrome-4 (ARCND4) is caused by a tandem duplication on chromosome 7p21.1 that covers most of the HDAC9 gene (606543) and is thought to contain TWIST1 regulatory elements.

### Description

Auriculocondylar syndrome is characterized by malformed 'question mark' ears, prominent cheeks, microstomia, an abnormal temporomandibular joint, and hypoplasia of the mandibular condyle (Masotti et al., 2008). Features vary considerably within families, and micrognathia and facial asymmetry are also reported (Masotti et al., 2008; Romanelli Tavares et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Masotti et al. (2008) described a multigeneration family (F1) in which 11 individuals had auriculocondylar syndrome, with variable features within the family. Most of the patients had facial asymmetry. The authors noted that earlier reports of auriculocondylar syndrome had not highlighted this feature. ⚠︎

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) studied a previously reported nonconsanguineous 4-generation Brazilian family in which 11 members had typical features of auriculocondylar syndrome. Ten affected members were documented. Of these, 8 had question mark ears, 8 had microstomia, and 6 had micrognathia, and features varied considerably within the family. None of the patients had large, open fontanelles, which are the hallmark of trisomy 7p. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of ARCND4 in the 4-generation Brazilian family studied by Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. The duplication segregated with the phenotype, and the findings suggested full penetrance. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) identified 19 patients in the DECIPHER database with copy-number gains in the region. Seven had ear malformations and 7 had micrognathia. Most of these patients had larger duplications that included the TWIST1 enhancer eTW6. One 179-kb duplication (patient 276644) lay entirely within the region duplicated in the ARCND family but did not span the TWIST1 regulatory elements. This duplication was not associated with ARCND features. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) studied a Brazilian family with auriculocondylar syndrome in which Sanger sequencing of the PLCB4, GNAI3, and EDN1 genes had found no pathogenic variants. They performed genomewide SNP linkage analysis in 9 affected and 3 unaffected family members under an autosomal dominant model. They found a maximum lod score of 2.88 (maximum theoretical 2.93) in a 17.6-Mb region on chromosome 7 (chr7:14,395,902-32,017,194, hg38). Microsatellite markers narrowed the region to chr7:14,395,902-28,158,440. Gene prioritization ranked TWIST1 as the top candidate gene in the region.

### Heterogeneity

Masotti et al. (2008) performed linkage analysis in 2 large families with auriculocondylar syndrome, F1 and F2. Segregation analysis excluded the loci for Treacher Collins syndrome, oculoauriculovertebral spectrum, and Townes-Brocks syndrome. A genomewide search found evidence of linkage to 1p21.1-q23.3 in family F2 (maximum lod score of 3.01 at theta = 0), which defined the first ACS locus. This locus was not linked to the phenotype in family F1. The authors concluded that auriculocondylar syndrome is genetically heterogeneous, with at least 2 loci responsible for the phenotype.

### Molecular Genetics

In the Brazilian family with ARCND4, Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) found no causative variants by whole-exome sequencing of 4 affected members; a synonymous variant in the TRIL gene did not segregate with the disorder. Targeted resequencing of a 2.4-Mb region around TWIST1 identified a 430,302-bp tandem duplication (NC_000007.14:g.18437239_18867540dup). The duplication began about 280 kb telomeric to TWIST1 and covered most of the HDAC9 gene. Breakpoint PCR and Sanger sequencing showed that the duplication segregated with the phenotype. All unaffected individuals were negative for the duplication, suggesting full penetrance. The duplication was predicted to disrupt the full-length, catalytically active HDAC9 isoforms.

### Pathogenesis

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) performed Capture-C analysis in human embryonic calvarial mesenchymal cells, using the TWIST1 promoter as the viewpoint. This showed multiple cis contacts, with the highest frequency of contacts telomeric to TWIST1, within HDAC9 and the duplicated region. These results implied that the duplicated region contains TWIST1 regulatory elements, including the enhancer eTw6, and suggested a possible pathogenic mechanism. ⚠︎

Romanelli Tavares et al. (2022) generated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from 3 affected family members and from controls. They differentiated these cells into neural crest cells (iNCCs) and mesenchymal stem-like cells (nMSCs). Compared with controls, patient iNCCs had increased mRNA levels of HDAC9 (3.15-fold) and TWIST1 (2.03-fold) and a 4.3-fold decrease in migratory capacity, with no difference in the cell cycle. Patient nMSCs had reduced alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity (a 20.3-fold decrease), reduced ALP expression, and subtly reduced matrix mineralization, indicating delayed or impaired osteogenic differentiation. The authors suggested that deregulation of TWIST1 contributes to ARCND by compromising neural crest cell migration and osteogenesis.
