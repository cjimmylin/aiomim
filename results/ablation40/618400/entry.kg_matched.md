### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that axonal Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 2EE (CMT2EE) is caused by homozygous mutation in the MPV17 gene (137960) on chromosome 2p23.3.

### Description

Axonal Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 2EE (CMT2EE) is an autosomal recessive axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy that begins in childhood, adolescence, or adulthood. It causes distal weakness and atrophy, mainly in the lower limbs, along with distal sensory loss and absent tendon reflexes. Some patients have ragged-red fibers and other mitochondrial abnormalities in muscle. Most reported patients have not had the liver or brain involvement seen in Navajo neurohepatopathy, another disorder caused by MPV17 mutation (Choi et al., 2015; Baumann et al., 2019). However, Blakely et al. (2012) described a patient whose neuropathy was accompanied by leukoencephalopathy and mild liver abnormalities. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Blakely et al. (2012) reported a 21-year-old Pakistani man, born to nonconsanguineous parents, who began having difficulty walking at age 14 years. Over several years this progressed to bilateral foot drop requiring orthoses. He had symmetric distal numbness that began in the feet and ascended to the knees, with milder involvement of the hands. On examination he had distal muscle wasting, clawed toes without pes cavus, a slow unsteady gait, and decreased tone. Distal weakness was severe (MRC grade 0/5 in some hand muscles and in the anterior tibialis), and hip flexion was mildly weak. Tendon reflexes were absent and sensation was lost distally in the lower limbs. He had no significant cognitive impairment. Nerve conduction studies showed an axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy. Sural nerve biopsy showed a severe chronic axonal neuropathy with only occasional surviving myelinated axons. An MRI at age 9 years had been normal, but later cranial MRI showed diffuse T2 hyperintensity in the cerebral white matter and cerebellum, suggesting leukoencephalopathy. CSF protein (1963 mg/L) and lactate (4.3 mmol/L) were increased. He also had mild hepatomegaly, abnormal liver echogenicity, and a minimally raised gamma-GT. Liver biopsy showed patchy steatosis (about 20%) with early bridging fibrosis, although he had no clinically significant chronic liver disease. He had recovered fully from hepatitis A at age 3 years. His sister developed a progressive, fatal hepatitis at age 9 years. Their father had an episode of hepatitis in childhood and recovered fully. The mother and an older brother were well.

Choi et al. (2015) reported 2 unrelated Korean patients, each born to healthy nonconsanguineous parents. Patient 1 (family FC26) was a 34-year-old woman whose early motor milestones were normal. She first noticed distal lower limb weakness at age 9 years and began walking with short leg braces at age 12. At age 34 she had distal weakness and atrophy, mainly in the lower limbs. Atrophy of the intrinsic hand, foot, and calf muscles was severe. She also had flexion deformities of the interphalangeal joints, ankle deformity, and scoliosis. Vibration and position sense were more affected than pain and touch, and knee and ankle jerks were absent. She had no liver involvement and no other features of Navajo neurohepatopathy, but her serum lactate was mildly raised (2.1 mmol/L). Patient 2 (family FC355) was a 22-year-old man who had had gait and balance problems since age 13 years and began walking with short leg braces at age 20. He had distal weakness and atrophy, atrophy of the intrinsic hand, foot, and calf muscles, and flat feet. Vibration and position sense were more impaired than pain and touch, and deep tendon reflexes were absent. He had no other features of Navajo neurohepatopathy. Apart from a slightly raised serum lactate (1.9 mmol/L), his laboratory results were normal.

Electrophysiologic findings were similar in the 2 patients of Choi et al. (2015). Sural sensory nerve action potentials were lost early in the disease, and no sensory responses could be elicited in either patient. Peroneal and tibial CMAPs were absent, and median and ulnar motor conduction velocities and CMAPs were reduced. Visual and brainstem auditory evoked potentials were normal, and brain MRI showed no abnormality. On muscle MRI, the soleus muscles showed severe atrophy and fatty replacement, while the tibialis posterior and lateral gastrocnemius were relatively spared. Thigh and hip muscles were much less affected, a pattern consistent with length-dependent axonal degeneration. The authors noted that the preferential involvement of the soleus resembled CMT2A. Sural nerve biopsy from patient 1 at age 34 showed near-complete loss of large and medium-sized myelinated fibers and atrophy and vesicular changes in unmyelinated axons. There was no demyelination and there were no onion bulbs, consistent with an axonal neuropathy. A vastus lateralis biopsy from patient 2 at age 22 showed a few ragged-red fibers and subsarcolemmal accumulation of enlarged mitochondria.

Baumann et al. (2019) described 5 patients from 2 unrelated families who had juvenile- or adult-onset axonal sensorimotor neuropathy. None had the hepatocerebral involvement seen in Navajo neurohepatopathy.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by Blakely et al. (2012), skeletal muscle biopsy showed ragged-red fibers (about 1%) and about 20% COX-deficient fibers in a mosaic pattern. On electron microscopy there were subsarcolemmal abnormal mitochondria with paracrystalline ('parking lot') inclusions. Muscle mtDNA copy number was normal, but long-range PCR showed multiple mtDNA deletions. Single-fiber real-time PCR showed high levels of clonally expanded mtDNA deletions in COX-deficient fibers. The authors noted that multiple mtDNA deletions had not previously been reported in patients with MPV17 mutations.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CMT2EE in the families reported by Blakely et al. (2012), Choi et al. (2015), and Baumann et al. (2019) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Choi et al. (2015) described their 2 Korean families as autosomal recessive: in each family the affected individual was homozygous for the MPV17 mutation and was born to healthy nonconsanguineous parents. Blakely et al. (2012) noted that their patient's parents were nonconsanguineous but of the same Asian ethnicity. They suggested that the homozygous mutation pointed either to a shared common ancestor or to a high population prevalence of this recessive mutation in that Asian group, and they considered a common founder most likely.

### Molecular Genetics

Blakely et al. (2012) sequenced the POLG, DGUOK, and MPV17 genes in a Pakistani man with axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy and leukoencephalopathy. They identified a homozygous missense mutation in MPV17, pro98-to-leu (P98L). The nucleotide change was given as c.2898C-T in the text and as c.298C-T in the figure legend. The affected residue is conserved from human to yeast, and the variant had previously been reported as a recessive mutation associated with mtDNA depletion in liver. Parental samples were not available to confirm carrier status.

In 2 affected individuals from 2 unrelated Korean families, Choi et al. (2015) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.122G-A transition in the MPV17 gene. The mutation results in an arg41-to-gln (R41Q) substitution at a conserved residue, and it was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Each parent was presumed to carry 1 mutant allele. The variant was absent from 300 controls, from in-house exome data (n = 302), and from the dbSNP142 and 1000 Genomes databases. It was present at very low frequency in ESP (0.00008) and ExAC (0.00002471). In NSC34 mouse motor neuronal cells, siRNA knockdown of Mpv17 significantly reduced cell proliferation and viability under oxidative stress (H2O2) and affected OXPHOS levels. Overexpressing the R41Q mutant significantly inhibited proliferation.

Baumann et al. (2019) studied 2 unrelated families with axonal sensorimotor neuropathy. In the affected members of the first family they found a homozygous R41Q mutation in the MPV17 gene. In the affected members of the second family they found a novel homozygous near-splice variant in MPV17, c.376-9T-G, which they showed caused an in-frame deletion of 11 amino acids. The authors noted that MPV17 mutations were first reported in Navajo neurohepatopathy, an autosomal recessive mtDNA depletion syndrome. They concluded that MPV17 mutations should also be considered in patients with pure, nonsyndromic axonal neuropathy.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Blakely et al. (2012) considered their patient with the P98L mutation to have a phenotype similar to the classic form of Navajo neurohepatopathy caused by the MPV17 R50Q mutation. However, the muscle involvement, with ragged-red fibers and mosaic COX deficiency, was unusual. They noted that P98L, like R50Q, is among the MPV17 mutations that may be associated with longer survival.

Choi et al. (2015) noted that previously reported MPV17 patients with neuropathy also had hepatopathy or brain involvement. By contrast, their 2 patients with the homozygous R41Q mutation had only axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, without liver or brain involvement at ages 34 and 22 years. They noted that a different substitution at the same residue (R41W) also impaired proliferation of NSC34 cells, and they suggested that mutations at arg41 predominantly cause peripheral neuropathy.

### Pathogenesis

Blakely et al. (2012) concluded that MPV17 mutation can cause mtDNA depletion and can also cause clonally expanded mtDNA deletions and focal COX deficiency in skeletal muscle. They suggested that the MPV17 protein is involved in both mtDNA replication and repair, and in both quantitative and qualitative mtDNA abnormalities.

Choi et al. (2015) found that overexpression of the R41Q, R50Q, or L143* MPV17 mutants significantly inhibited proliferation of NSC34 mouse motor neuronal cells, and that siRNA knockdown of Mpv17 reduced proliferation and viability under oxidative stress. They suggested that neuronal function might be impaired both by loss of normal MPV17 function and by harmful gain-of-function effects of the mutant protein.
