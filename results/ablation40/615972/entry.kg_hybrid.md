### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nanophthalmos-4 (NNO4) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TMEM98 gene (615949) on chromosome 17q11.2.

### Description

Nanophthalmos is a congenital disorder characterized by small eyes. Its main complications are severe hyperopia and angle-closure glaucoma (Awadalla et al., 2014). The eye is structurally normal but smaller than usual. Patients have high hyperopia, a greater incidence of angle-closure glaucoma, and an increased risk of surgical complications (Khorram et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Yu et al. (2009) described a 5-generation Chinese family with isolated simple microphthalmia. There were no other physical anomalies in the family. The 9 affected members were aged 6 to 66 years (mean, 42.8 years), and all had bilateral disease present from birth. Hyperopia ranged from +4.50 to +11.50 diopters (mean, +8.00 diopters), and axial length ranged from 18.22 mm to 20.26 mm (mean, 19.29 mm). Best corrected visual acuity was less than 0.5 in most eyes. Three patients, all older than 50 years, had angle-closure glaucoma or occludable anterior chamber angles. They were treated by trabeculectomy, but the disease kept progressing, and 2 of them developed atrophia bulbi despite repeated surgeries. The authors also observed learning disabilities in one-third of the cases. They suggested that lens thickening after middle age and gradually occludable anterior chamber angles could produce refractory glaucoma, and that most patients would eventually lose visual acuity. Yu et al. (2009) examined full-thickness sclera on histology. They found diffusely swollen collagen fibers that were twisted or frayed and mixed with normal fibers. In the 2 atrophied globes, the alignment of collagen fibers was disrupted. The sclera contained many coil-like amorphous deposits and was 3 times the thickness of normal sclera. The corneas were also thickened, pigment cells had proliferated, and bone-like tissue had formed. ⚠︎

Hu et al. (2011) studied a Chinese family with congenital simple microphthalmia inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern.

Awadalla et al. (2014) studied a large white family (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, recruited through ophthalmology clinics in Adelaide, South Australia. They defined nanophthalmos as an axial length less than 20.0 mm and/or a refractive error greater than +7.00. Sixteen (46%) of the 35 available family members had nanophthalmos. The mean refraction of affected members was +11.8 D, and their mean axial length was 17.6 mm.

Khorram et al. (2015) described a Caucasian family of German heritage living in the United States (pedigree 991-F) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos spanning at least 4 generations. Six of the 10 examined members had nanophthalmos, and 3 more were affected according to the family history report. In the affected members whose axial length was measured, mean axial eye length was 17.1 mm +/- 0.31 mm. Affected members were highly hyperopic, with spherical equivalent refractions of +13.0 +/- 0.89 diopters, compared with +0.71 +/- 0.81 diopters in unaffected members.

Khorram et al. (2015) also described a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage from the Mariana Islands (pedigree 951-N) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. Eight of the 10 examined members had nanophthalmos, with an average axial eye length of 17.4 mm +/- 0.94 mm. In unaffected members, axial length was 22.7 +/- 0.38 mm. Affected members also had hyperopia, with spherical equivalent refractions of +3.93 +/- 1.57 diopters. Three more family members were affected according to the family history report. Across both families, axial eye lengths in patients with nanophthalmos ranged from 15.9 to 18.4 mm.

### Inheritance

Yu et al. (2009) and Hu et al. (2011) observed autosomal dominant transmission of simple microphthalmia in Chinese families. Awadalla et al. (2014) observed autosomal dominant transmission of nanophthalmos in a large white family. Khorram et al. (2015) found autosomal dominant transmission with high penetrance, spanning at least 4 generations, in a family from the United States and a family from the Mariana Islands.

### Cytogenetics

Yu et al. (2009) found a normal karyotype in all 9 affected members of a Chinese family with autosomal dominant simple microphthalmia. They concluded that the disease was most probably caused by a single-gene mutation.

### Mapping

Yu et al. (2009) studied 34 members (9 affected and 25 unaffected) of a Chinese family with autosomal dominant simple microphthalmia. They genotyped 23 microsatellite markers from 5 autosomes covering known microphthalmia loci. Two-point lod scores were less than 1 for all markers. The authors therefore excluded linkage to the candidate genes CHX10, PAX6, and MFRP and to the loci on chromosomes 14q32, 11p, and 2q11-q14. They predicted that a novel locus was responsible and suggested that this was further evidence of high genetic heterogeneity in microphthalmia.

Hu et al. (2011) studied a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia. After excluding reported microphthalmia candidates, they performed a whole-genome scan with 382 microsatellite markers, followed by fine mapping. The highest lod score was at D17S1824 (maximum lod score, 4.97 at theta = 0.00). Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 21.57-cM interval between D17S900 and D17S1872 on chromosome 17p12-q12.

Awadalla et al. (2014) performed a genomewide linkage scan with a 10K SNP array in family NNO-SA1. They found significant linkage to chromosome 17 between SNPs rs2323659 and rs967293, with a maximum location score of 4.1.

Khorram et al. (2015) performed genomewide SNP linkage analysis in 2 families. In pedigree 991-F, a 20.2-Mbp segment of chromosome 17p12-q12, bounded by rs530664 and rs16523, was transmitted with nanophthalmos in all members of the family. In pedigree 951-N, an overlapping 19.7-Mb segment bounded by rs917541 and rs16523 was coinherited with the disease. The authors concluded that the gene lay between rs917541 and rs16523. They noted that this pericentric region of chromosome 17 had previously been linked to autosomal dominant nanophthalmos in a Chinese pedigree.

### Molecular Genetics

In a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia mapped to chromosome 17p12-q12, Hu et al. (2011) screened 14 candidate genes, including CRYBA1 and NCOR1, by PCR and direct sequencing. They also looked for copy number variation by genomewide SNP genotyping. No mutation or CNV responsible for the phenotype was identified.

Awadalla et al. (2014) performed whole exome sequencing in family NNO-SA1, a large white kindred with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos linked to 17p12-q12. Within the linked region, they identified a single novel missense variant that segregated with the disease: a c.577G-C change in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene, resulting in an ala193-to-pro (A193P) substitution. The variant was absent from the Exome Variant Server database and from 285 local white controls. By RT-PCR, TMEM98 was expressed in all ocular tissues tested, including the sclera and optic nerve head. The authors concluded that TMEM98 most likely represented the cause of disease in this family.

Khorram et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing in an affected member of a US family with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos (pedigree 991-F) linked to chromosome 17. They found 4 nonsynonymous variants within the linked region. Only one was coinherited with nanophthalmos, absent from healthy family members, and predicted pathogenic by SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62: a his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in the TMEM98 gene. Sanger sequencing showed that all other affected members of the family also carried the mutation. The authors then used Sanger sequencing to screen TMEM98 in the Mariana Islands family (pedigree 951-N), testing 11 affected and 4 healthy members. All affected members carried a heterozygous 34-bp deletion spanning the end of exon 4 and the start of intron 4, which was absent from the healthy members. Neither mutation was found in the Exome Variant Server or the 1000 Genomes Project.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, ALA193PRO [c.577G>C; p.A193P; p.Ala193Pro]

Awadalla et al. (2014) studied a large white family (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. In this family they identified a c.577G-C change in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene, resulting in an ala193-to-pro (A193P) substitution. The variant was found by whole exome sequencing within the linked region on chromosome 17p12-q12 and segregated with the disease. It was absent from the Exome Variant Server database and from 285 local white controls.

**.0002 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, HIS196PRO [p.H196P; p.His196Pro]

Khorram et al. (2015) studied a Caucasian family of German heritage from the United States (pedigree 991-F) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. Exome sequencing within the linked chromosome 17 region identified a his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in the TMEM98 gene. The mutation was coinherited with nanophthalmos, absent from healthy family members, and predicted damaging by SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62. It altered a histidine that is highly conserved across species and was absent from the Exome Variant Server and the 1000 Genomes Project.

**.0003 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, 34-BP DEL, NT694 [c.694_721delagaatgaagactggatcgaagatgcctcgtaagg; c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg]

Khorram et al. (2015) studied a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage from the Mariana Islands (pedigree 951-N) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. By Sanger sequencing, they identified a heterozygous 34-bp deletion in the TMEM98 gene (c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg) in all affected members of the family. The deletion spans the last 28 bp of exon 4 and the first 6 bp of intron 4 and eliminates a splice site, resulting in premature termination of translation. It was coinherited with nanophthalmos and absent from all healthy family members. It was also absent from the Exome Variant Server and the 1000 Genomes Project.
