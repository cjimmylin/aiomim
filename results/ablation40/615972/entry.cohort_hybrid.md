### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nanophthalmos-4 (NNO4) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TMEM98 gene (615949) on chromosome 17q11.2.

### Description

Nanophthalmos is a congenital disorder characterized by small eyes, with the main complications being severe hyperopia and angle-closure glaucoma (Awadalla et al., 2014). Khorram et al. (2015) described nanophthalmos as a structurally normal but smaller eye with short axial length (less than 20 mm), high hyperopia, and small corneal diameter. These anatomic features result in a crowded anterior chamber and a high incidence of angle-closure glaucoma, and the thick, impermeable sclera has been associated with a high risk of choroidal effusions after intraocular surgery (Khorram et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Yu et al. (2009) reported a 5-generation Chinese family in which 9 members, aged 6 to 66 years, had isolated bilateral microphthalmia present from birth. No family member had any other physical anomaly. Hyperopia ranged from +4.50 to +11.50 diopters (mean, +8.00 diopters), and axial length ranged from 18.22 to 20.26 mm (mean, 19.29 mm), shorter than the normal length of 23 to 24 mm. Best corrected visual acuity was less than 0.5 in most eyes. Angle-closure glaucoma or occludable anterior chamber angles were present in 3 patients, all older than 50 years of age, and the condition kept progressing despite trabeculectomy; 2 of these patients eventually developed atrophia bulbi despite repeated surgeries. Learning disabilities were observed in one-third of the cases. All 9 affected members had a normal karyotype. Histologic examination of scleras from affected members showed diffusely swollen collagen fibers that were twisted or frayed and mixed with normal fibers. In the 2 atrophied globes, the alignment of collagen fibers was disrupted, there was abundant coil-like amorphous material in scleras that were 3 times the normal thickness, the corneas were thickened, pigment cells had proliferated, and bone-like tissue had formed. Yu et al. (2009) suggested that the abnormal collagen fibers might directly prevent early development of the globe.

Awadalla et al. (2014) studied a large white family from South Australia (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, defined as an axial length less than 20.0 mm and/or a refractive error greater than +7.00. Of 35 available family members, 16 had nanophthalmos, with a mean refraction of +11.8 D and a mean axial length of 17.6 mm.

Khorram et al. (2015) described 2 large pedigrees with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos spanning at least 4 generations: a Caucasian family of German heritage living in the United States (pedigree 991-F) and a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage living in the Mariana Islands (pedigree 951-N). In pedigree 991-F, 6 of 10 examined members were diagnosed with nanophthalmos and an additional 3 members were affected according to family history. Affected members had a mean axial eye length of 17.1 +/- 0.31 mm and high hyperopia, with spherical equivalent refractions of +13.0 +/- 0.89 diopters, compared with +0.71 +/- 0.81 diopters in unaffected family members. In pedigree 951-N, 8 of 10 examined members had nanophthalmos, with an average axial eye length of 17.4 +/- 0.94 mm, compared with 22.7 +/- 0.38 mm in unaffected members; spherical equivalent refractions in affected members were +3.93 +/- 1.57 diopters. Three additional members of pedigree 951-N were affected according to family history but were not available for examination. Overall, axial eye lengths in affected individuals ranged from 15.9 to 18.4 mm.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant transmission of the disorder was observed in the 5-generation Chinese family reported by Yu et al. (2009), in the white family reported by Awadalla et al. (2014), and in the 2 pedigrees reported by Khorram et al. (2015), which showed high penetrance over at least 4 generations.

### Mapping

In a 5-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant microphthalmia, Yu et al. (2009) tested 23 microsatellite markers at previously reported microphthalmia candidate genes (CHX10, PAX6, and MFRP) and loci (14q32, 11p, and 2q11-q14). All 2-point lod scores were less than 1, so linkage to these loci was excluded, and the authors predicted that a novel locus was responsible for the disorder in this family, suggesting high genetic heterogeneity of microphthalmia.

By a genomewide scan using 382 microsatellite markers in a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia, after exclusion of reported candidate loci, Hu et al. (2011) obtained a maximum lod score of 4.97 at D17S1824 (theta = 0.00). Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 21.57-cM interval between D17S900 and D17S1872 on chromosome 17p12-q12.

In a large white family with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Awadalla et al. (2014) performed a genomewide linkage scan using a 10K SNP array and detected significant linkage on chromosome 17 between SNP markers rs2323659 and rs967293, with a maximum location score of 4.1.

Khorram et al. (2015) found that a 20.2-Mbp segment of chromosome 17p12-q12, bounded by SNPs rs530664 and rs16523, was transmitted with nanophthalmos in all members of a US pedigree, and that an overlapping 19.7-Mb segment, bounded by rs917541 and rs16523, was coinherited with nanophthalmos in a Mariana Islands pedigree, placing the disease gene between rs917541 and rs16523. They noted that this pericentric region of chromosome 17 had previously been linked to autosomal dominant nanophthalmos in a Chinese pedigree.

### Molecular Genetics

In a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia mapping to 17p12-q12, Hu et al. (2011) sequenced 14 candidate genes, including CRYBA1 and NCOR1, and performed genomewide SNP genotyping for copy number variation, but identified no mutation or CNV responsible for the phenotype.

In affected members of a large white family with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos linked to chromosome 17p12-q12, Awadalla et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a single novel segregating missense variant within the linkage region, a c.577G-C transversion in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene, resulting in an ala193-to-pro (A193P) substitution. The variant was absent from the Exome Variant Server database and from 285 local white controls. By RT-PCR, TMEM98 was expressed in all ocular tissues tested, including the sclera and optic nerve head. The authors concluded that TMEM98 most likely represented the cause of the disease in this family. ⚠︎

In a US pedigree with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos (991-F), Khorram et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing in an affected member and found 4 nonsynonymous variants within the linked region on chromosome 17, in the ZNF286A, ZNF287, SLC47A1, and TMEM98 genes. Only a his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in TMEM98 was coinherited with nanophthalmos, absent from healthy family members, and predicted to be pathogenic by SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62; Sanger sequencing showed that all other affected members of the pedigree carried the mutation. The histidine residue was highly conserved across species. In a Mariana Islands pedigree (951-N), Sanger sequencing of TMEM98 in 11 affected and 4 healthy members identified a heterozygous 34-bp deletion spanning the last 28 bp of exon 4 and the first 6 bp of intron 4 (c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg) in all affected members; the deletion was absent from healthy members and was predicted to eliminate a splice site and result in premature termination of translation. Neither mutation was found in the Exome Variant Server or 1000 Genomes Project databases. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, ALA193PRO [c.577G>C; p.A193P; p.Ala193Pro]

In affected members of a large white family from South Australia with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos (NNO4), Awadalla et al. (2014) identified a c.577G-C transversion in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene, resulting in an ala193-to-pro (A193P) substitution. The mutation, which was found by exome sequencing within a chromosome 17 linkage region, segregated with the disorder in the family and was absent from the Exome Variant Server database and from 285 local white controls.

**.0002 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, HIS196PRO [p.H196P; p.His196Pro]

In affected members of a Caucasian family of German heritage living in the United States with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos (NNO4), Khorram et al. (2015) identified a his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in the TMEM98 gene. The mutation was found by exome sequencing of an affected member within the chromosome 17 linked region and was confirmed by Sanger sequencing in all other affected family members. It was absent from healthy family members and from the Exome Variant Server and 1000 Genomes Project databases, affected a histidine residue highly conserved across species, and was predicted to be damaging by SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62.

**.0003 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**

In affected members of a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage from the Mariana Islands with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos (NNO4), Khorram et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous 34-bp deletion in the TMEM98 gene spanning the last 28 bp of exon 4 and the first 6 bp of intron 4 (c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg). The deletion, found by Sanger sequencing, was present in all 11 affected members tested and absent from 4 healthy family members, and was not found in the Exome Variant Server or 1000 Genomes Project databases. It was predicted to eliminate a splice site and result in premature termination of translation.
