### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nanophthalmos-4 (NNO4) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TMEM98 gene (615949) on chromosome 17q11.2.

### Description

Nanophthalmos-4 is an autosomal dominant ocular disorder in which affected individuals have small eyes with short axial length, usually with hyperopia that is often high (Awadalla et al., 2014; Khorram et al., 2015). The degree of hyperopia varied between families (Khorram et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Yu et al. (2009) reported a 5-generation Chinese family in which 9 members, aged 6 to 66 years, had isolated bilateral microphthalmia from birth. Hyperopia ranged from +4.50 to +11.50 diopters (mean +8.00), and axial length ranged from 18.22 to 20.26 mm (mean 19.29 mm). Three patients older than 50 years had angle-closure glaucoma or occludable angles, with raised intraocular pressure, which progressed despite trabeculectomy. Two of them (III10 and III15) developed atrophia bulbi despite repeated surgeries and had no light perception in the affected eye. Learning disabilities were seen in one-third of cases. All 9 patients had a normal karyotype. On histology, the sclera showed diffusely swollen, twisted or frayed collagen fibers mixed with normal fibers; in the atrophied globes, collagen alignment was disrupted, the sclera was about 3 times normal thickness, and pigment cell proliferation and bone-like tissue were seen. ⚠︎

Hu et al. (2011) studied a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia.

Awadalla et al. (2014) studied a large white family from South Australia with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos (family NNO-SA1). Of the 35 available family members, 16 (46%) had a diagnosis of nanophthalmos. Their mean refraction was +11.8 D and their mean axial length was 17.6 mm.

Khorram et al. (2015) reported 2 pedigrees with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, both showing high penetrance over at least 4 generations. In pedigree 991-F, a Caucasian family of German heritage from the United States, 6 of 10 examined members were diagnosed with nanophthalmos and 3 more were affected according to the family history. Where measured, axial lengths ranged from 16.7 to 17.4 mm, and refractions ranged from +11.125 to +14.125 D. Mean axial length was 17.1 mm and hyperopia was about +13 D.

Khorram et al. (2015)'s second pedigree, 951-N, was a Micronesian Pacific Islander family from the Mariana Islands. Eleven affected members were tested, and 3 more were affected according to the family history but could not be examined. Affected members had a mean axial length of 17.4 mm, with individual values from 15.86 to 18.43 mm. Their hyperopia was less marked than in the US pedigree (mean +3.93 D), with values from +1.88 to +7.00 D in those measured.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of microphthalmia or nanophthalmos was observed in the Chinese families reported by Yu et al. (2009) and Hu et al. (2011), in the white kindred reported by Awadalla et al. (2014), and in the 2 pedigrees reported by Khorram et al. (2015). Khorram et al. (2015) noted high penetrance over at least 4 generations.

### Mapping

In their 5-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant microphthalmia, Yu et al. (2009) excluded linkage to the candidate genes CHX10, PAX6, and MFRP and to loci at 14q32, 11p, and 2q11-q14. LOD scores were below 1 for all 23 markers tested, and the authors predicted that a novel locus was responsible.

Through a genomewide scan in a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia, Hu et al. (2011) obtained a maximum lod score of 4.97 at D17S1824 (theta = 0.00). Haplotype analysis placed the disease locus in a 21.57-cM interval on chromosome 17p12-q12, between D17S900 and D17S1872.

In the large white family NNO-SA1 with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Awadalla et al. (2014) found significant linkage to chromosome 17 between SNP markers rs2323659 and rs967293, with a maximum location score of 4.1.

Khorram et al. (2015) found that a 20.2-Mb segment of chromosome 17p12-q12 (rs530664-rs16523) cosegregated with nanophthalmos in pedigree 991-F. An overlapping 19.7-Mb segment (rs917541-rs16523) cosegregated with the disorder in pedigree 951-N. Together these placed the disease gene between rs917541 and rs16523, a region overlapping one previously linked in a Chinese pedigree.

### Molecular Genetics

Hu et al. (2011) screened 14 candidate genes in the linked region on chromosome 17p12-q12, including CRYBA1 and NCOR1, and performed genomewide SNP genotyping in their Chinese family with congenital simple microphthalmia. They found no mutation or copy number variation responsible for the phenotype. ⚠︎

In affected members of the large white South Australian family NNO-SA1 with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Awadalla et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.577G-C transversion in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene, resulting in an ala193-to-pro (A193P) substitution. Exome sequencing found this to be the only novel missense variant in the linkage region that segregated with the disease. It was absent from the Exome Variant Server database and from 285 local white controls. By RT-PCR, TMEM98 was expressed in all ocular tissues tested, including sclera and optic nerve head. The authors concluded that TMEM98 was most likely the cause of disease in this family, and described it as the first gene identified for autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. ⚠︎

Khorram et al. (2015) studied 2 pedigrees with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. In pedigree 991-F, from the United States, they performed exome sequencing in an affected member (IV-3) and identified a his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in the TMEM98 gene. Of the 4 candidate variants, only H196P was coinherited with nanophthalmos, was absent from healthy family members, and had pathogenic scores on all 3 sequence analysis systems. Sanger sequencing showed that all other affected members of the pedigree carried the mutation. ⚠︎

In the second pedigree studied by Khorram et al. (2015), 951-N from the Mariana Islands, Sanger sequencing identified a novel 34-bp heterozygous deletion in the TMEM98 gene (c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg). The deletion spanned the last 28 bp of exon 4 and the first 6 bp of intron 4. It was found in all 11 affected members tested and was absent from 4 healthy family members. It was predicted to eliminate a splice site and cause premature termination. Both TMEM98 mutations found by Khorram et al. (2015) were absent from public databases and affected conserved sequences.

It was the only novel missense variant in the linkage region on chromosome 17 that segregated with the disease. It was absent from the Exome Variant Server database and from 285 local white controls. Sixteen of 35 available family members had nanophthalmos, with a mean refraction of +11.8 D and a mean axial length of 17.6 mm. ⚠︎
