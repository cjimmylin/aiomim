### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene (613469) on chromosome 10q24.2.

### Description

Urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its features are congenital obstructive uropathy and an abnormal facial expression, with facial grimacing when the patient attempts to smile (Wang et al., 1997; Daly et al., 2010). Patients have enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis and voiding dysfunction caused by neurogenic bladder (Wang et al., 1997). Daly et al. (2010) noted that the urinary bladder fails to void completely even though there is no anatomical obstruction of bladder outflow and no overt neurological damage. Affected individuals often have reflux of infected urine to the upper renal tract, with a risk of kidney damage and renal failure. Aydogdu et al. (2010) noted that the condition occurs in both sexes.

### Nomenclature

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) proposed the term 'urofacial syndrome' for the combination of an inverted facial expression on laughing and neuropathic bladder.

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families, one of which was consanguineous (first cousins). The patients had peculiar facies and gestures while smiling and crying, together with hydronephrosis, hydroureter and intravesical stenosis of the ureter. Other findings included abnormal caliber of the urethra in its prostatic and membranous portions, urethral valves, and an abnormal bladder with trabeculation and diverticula. The bladder showed severe hypertrophy of the mucosa with sclerotic changes.

Between 1965 and 1986, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) saw 36 children who had enuresis and urinary tract infection together with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. On urologic work-up, all patients had characteristic findings of mild neuropathic bladder, and most had severe urinary tract damage. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation.

Chauve et al. (2000) reported the first European cases of urofacial syndrome, in a French family with uropathy and facial abnormalities.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European cases of urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome. They noted that the syndrome was first described in Colombian patients and had rarely been reported from other countries. They suggested that cases may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing goes unrecognized.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported the first published case of urofacial syndrome in Turkey. The patient had been followed for recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux and was diagnosed with the syndrome at 16 years of age. The authors described the inverted facial expression, in which patients appear to cry when they smile. They noted that the urinary tract problems, including enuresis, urinary tract infection and hydronephrosis, are variable and can progress to hypertension and end-stage renal disease.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 Turkish families, followed for a median of 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months).

### Inheritance

From genetic analysis of 3 families, one of which was consanguineous (first cousins), Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance.

In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that urofacial syndrome is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Their evidence was multiple affected sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity and an equal sex ratio. Later reports also described the disorder as autosomal recessive (Wang et al., 1997; Wang et al., 1999; Wang et al., 2003). Pang et al. (2010) and Daly et al. (2010) found that it is caused by loss-of-function mutations in HPSE2, which were homozygous in the families studied by Daly et al. (2010).

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) carried out a genome screen in 20 selected UFS patients using homozygosity mapping and DNA pooling. Marker D10S677 was linked to and associated with UFS. Patients had a significant excess of homozygosity at this marker compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)), and allele frequencies differed between the patient pool and the control pools. All 31 patients studied were homozygous for 2 closely linked markers, D10S1726 and D10S198, which lie about 5 cM telomeric to D10S677. Only 12% of unaffected relatives were homozygous for both markers (P less than 10(-19)). Recombinations at the flanking markers D10S1433 and D10S603 placed the gene within a 1-cM interval on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) built a physical map of the UFS critical region on 10q23-q24 and identified new polymorphic markers in it. Haplotype analysis with these markers placed the UFS gene within a single 1,410-kb YAC clone.

Chauve et al. (2000) carried out haplotype analysis in a French family and compared the results with haplotypes previously reported in patients from Colombia and America. The findings were compatible with the same 10q23-q24 critical region.

Wang et al. (2003) built a genomic sequence map covering the entire UFS interval on chromosome 10q23-q24. This narrowed the disease interval from about 360 kb to a 220-kb region containing the ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene.

Pang et al. (2010) noted that they had previously used homozygosity mapping to localize the UFS gene to chromosome 10q24. By whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual, Daly et al. (2010) defined a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (1999) used homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish descent to show that urofacial syndrome is genetically homogeneous. The locus had previously been mapped to 10q23-q24 in Colombian families. Chauve et al. (2000) found that haplotypes in a French family favored genetic homogeneity. Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q is responsible for UFS in all patients from multiple ethnic groups.

In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) did not find HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome, which indicated genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) found that the glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase gene (GOT1) lay within the new UFS critical region and determined its genomic structure. Mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) screened the ACDP1 gene in UFS patients, covering the coding region, the 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, the promoter and the exon/intron junctions. They found no pathogenic mutation. ⚠︎

Pang et al. (2010) found loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all of the UFS patients they studied, who came from Colombia, the United States and France.

Daly et al. (2010) used whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual to define a 16-Mb autozygous region on 10q23-q24. Within this region they found a homozygous 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene. They then found homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations and frameshift mutations in HPSE2 in 5 further unrelated families, which confirmed HPSE2 as the causative gene.

### Pathogenesis

Because UFS patients have complete loss of HPSE2 function, Pang et al. (2010) suggested that HPSE2 may be important for the synergic action of the muscles involved in facial expression and urine voiding.

Daly et al. (2010) showed that HPSE2 is expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system, where it might be involved in controlling facial expression and urinary voiding. It is also expressed in bladder smooth muscle, which is consistent with a role in renal tract morphology and function.

### Diagnosis

Elejalde (1979) proposed that the syndrome reflected alteration of the facial and urinary developmental fields. He noted that the peculiar facies allows early recognition, which could help with early assessment and treatment and perhaps lead to a better prognosis.

Because of the clear link between the facial distortion and a neuropathic bladder that damages the genitourinary tract, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) recommended urological evaluation of individuals with 'inversion' of facial expression.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) noted that early diagnosis has important consequences for the management and prognosis of the urinary problems. Derbent et al. (2009) advised physicians to consider urofacial syndrome in any patient with dysfunctional voiding, particularly in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage.

### Clinical Management

Aydogdu et al. (2010) emphasized that early diagnosis of urofacial syndrome is crucial for the management and prognosis of urinary problems due to a dysfunctional bladder. Derbent et al. (2009) likewise stressed that early diagnosis is important for managing the urinary problems.
