### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene (613469) on chromosome 10q24.2.

### Description

Urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its features are an abnormal, 'inverted' facial expression, in which patients grimace or appear to be crying when they try to smile, together with urinary tract disease (Wang et al., 1997; Derbent et al., 2009). The bladder fails to empty completely even though there is no anatomical bladder outflow obstruction or overt neurological damage (Daly et al., 2010). Patients present with enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and voiding dysfunction caused by neurogenic bladder (Wang et al., 1997). Reflux of infected urine to the upper renal tract puts the kidneys at risk, and some severely affected patients develop hypertension and progress to end-stage renal disease (Derbent et al., 2009; Daly et al., 2010).

### Nomenclature

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) proposed the name urofacial syndrome for this disorder.

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families. In one family the parents were first cousins. The patients had peculiar facies and gestures when smiling and crying. They also had urinary tract abnormalities: hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal urethral caliber in the prostatic and membranous portions, urethral valves, and a trabeculated bladder with diverticula and severe mucosal hypertrophy with sclerotic changes.

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) reported 36 children, seen between 1965 and 1986, who had enuresis and urinary tract infection along with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic workup found mild neuropathic bladder in every case, and most patients had severe urinary tract damage. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation.

Chauve et al. (2000) reported a French family, the first European cases of urofacial syndrome. The authors described the disorder as a combination of a uropathy and facial abnormalities.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European patients with urofacial syndrome. They noted that the condition was originally described in Colombian patients and has rarely been reported from other countries. They suggested that cases may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing goes unrecognized.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported the first published case of urofacial syndrome in Turkey. The patient had been followed for recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux, and urofacial syndrome was not diagnosed until age 16 years.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 families from Turkey, followed for a median of 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months). The condition affected both sexes and was marked by uropathy and facial abnormalities. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

From genetic analysis of 3 families, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance, even though one of the families was consanguineous.

In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that urofacial syndrome is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Their evidence was multiple affected sibs, unaffected parents, an increased rate of parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio. Later reports also described the disorder as autosomal recessive (Wang et al., 1997; Daly et al., 2010). Daly et al. (2010) found homozygous mutations in the HPSE2 gene in affected families.

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) carried out a genome screen that combined homozygosity mapping with DNA pooling. The study used 20 selected UFS patients, one patient pool, and three control pools of unaffected relatives. Of 36 randomly chosen markers, D10S677 showed linkage to and association with UFS. Patients had a significant excess of homozygosity at this marker compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)), and allele frequencies differed between patient and control pools. The authors then typed 10 more markers spanning 22 cM around D10S677 for haplotype (linkage disequilibrium) analysis. All 31 patients were homozygous for D10S1726 and D10S198, which lie about 5 cM telomeric to D10S677, compared with 12% of unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-19)). Recombinations in several patients who were heterozygous at the flanking markers D10S1433 (centromeric) and D10S603 (telomeric) placed the UFS gene in a 1-cM interval on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) built a physical map of the 10q23-q24 critical region and identified new polymorphic markers in it. Haplotype analysis with these markers placed the UFS gene within a single 1,410-kb YAC clone.

In a French family with urofacial syndrome, Chauve et al. (2000) performed haplotype analysis and compared the results with published haplotypes of patients from Colombia and America. The findings were consistent with the same critical region on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Homozygosity mapping by Wang et al. (2003) had placed the disease gene in a region of about 360 kb. The authors built a genomic sequence map covering the whole interval and narrowed it to a 220-kb region. This region contains the ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene. ⚠︎

By whole-genome SNP mapping in one individual with urofacial syndrome, Daly et al. (2010) defined a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (1999) used homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage to show that urofacial syndrome is genetically homogeneous. The locus had previously been mapped to 10q23-q24 in families from Colombia. Chauve et al. (2000) also concluded that their haplotype findings in a French family favored genetic homogeneity. Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q causes urofacial syndrome in all patients, including patients of multiple ethnic groups.

In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome, which pointed to genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) found that the GOT1 gene, which encodes glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase, lies within the UFS critical region and determined its genomic structure. Mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) searched the ACDP1 gene in urofacial syndrome patients for mutations, covering the coding region, the 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, the promoter region, and the exon/intron junctions. They found no pathogenic mutation. ⚠︎

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all of the urofacial syndrome patients they studied. The patients came from Colombia, the United States, and France.

Within the 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24, Daly et al. (2010) identified a 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene in an individual with urofacial syndrome. They then found homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations in HPSE2 in 5 further unrelated families with the disorder.

### Pathogenesis

Because UFS patients have complete loss of HPSE2 function, Pang et al. (2010) suggested that HPSE2 may be important for the coordinated action of the muscles involved in facial expression and urine voiding.

Daly et al. (2010) showed that HPSE2 is expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system, where they suggested it might help control facial expression and urinary voiding. HPSE2 is also expressed in bladder smooth muscle, which fits with a role in the structure and function of the renal tract.

### Diagnosis

Elejalde (1979) suggested that the syndrome reflects altered facial and urinary developmental fields. Because the peculiar facies allows early recognition, the author proposed that early assessment and treatment might improve the prognosis.

Because 'inversion' of facial expression is clearly linked to neuropathic bladder and the resulting damage to the genitourinary tract, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) recommended urologic evaluation for anyone with this facial sign.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) stressed that early diagnosis has important consequences for the management and prognosis of the urinary problems. Aydogdu et al. (2010) made the same point about urinary problems caused by a dysfunctional bladder. Derbent et al. (2009) noted that the diagnosis can be missed because the urinary problems vary and the facial grimacing goes unrecognized. They recommended that physicians consider urofacial syndrome in any patient with dysfunctional voiding, especially in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage.
