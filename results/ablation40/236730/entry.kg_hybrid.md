### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene (613469) on chromosome 10q24.2.

### Description

Urofacial syndrome (UFS) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by congenital obstructive uropathy and abnormal facial expression (Wang et al., 1997). Patients have an 'inverted' facial expression, in which they appear to be crying when they smile (Derbent et al., 2009). Daly et al. (2010) described facial grimacing when attempting to smile and failure of the urinary bladder to void completely despite a lack of anatomic bladder outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage. Affected individuals present with enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and voiding dysfunction resulting from neurogenic bladder (Wang et al., 1997). They often have reflux of infected urine from the bladder to the upper renal tract, with a risk of kidney damage and renal failure (Daly et al., 2010), and some severely affected patients become hypertensive and progress to end-stage renal disease (Derbent et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Elejalde (1979) referred to the disorder as 'the Ochoa syndrome.' Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) suggested the term urofacial syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families, one of which was consanguineous (first cousins). The patients had peculiar facies and gestures when smiling and crying. They also had hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal caliber of the urethra in the prostatic and membranous portions, urethral valves, and an abnormal trabeculated bladder with diverticula associated with severe hypertrophy of the mucosa with sclerotic changes. Elejalde (1979) suggested that the syndrome represents alteration of the facial and urinary developmental fields.

Between 1965 and 1986, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) saw 36 children with enuresis and urinary tract infection in association with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic workup showed characteristic findings of mild neuropathic bladder in all cases, and most patients had severe urinary tract damage. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation.

Chauve et al. (2000) reported a French family with urofacial syndrome, the first European cases of the disorder.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European cases of urofacial syndrome. They noted that the entity had originally been described in Colombian patients and that very few cases had been reported from other countries. They suggested that cases may be missed because the urinary problems are variable and the characteristic facial grimacing is not recognized.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported the first published case of urofacial syndrome in Turkey. The patient had been followed with a diagnosis of recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux, and urofacial syndrome was not diagnosed until 16 years of age.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 families in Turkey, with a median follow-up of 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months). The condition occurred in both sexes. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

From genetic analysis of 3 families, one of them consanguineous, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance.

In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that urofacial syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder. They based this on the occurrence of the disorder in multiple sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio. Daly et al. (2010) identified homozygous mutations in the HPSE2 gene in affected individuals, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) performed a genome screen in 20 selected patients with urofacial syndrome, using a combination of homozygosity mapping and DNA pooling with one patient pool and three control pools of unaffected relatives. Of 36 randomly chosen markers, D10S677 was linked to and associated with UFS: patients had a significant excess of homozygosity compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)), and allele frequencies differed between the patient pool and the control pools. The authors then analyzed 10 additional markers flanking D10S677 and covering a 22-cM region. All 31 patients were homozygous for 2 closely linked markers, D10S1726 and D10S198, located approximately 5 cM telomeric to D10S677, whereas only 12% of unaffected relatives were homozygous for both markers (P less than 10(-19)). Several patients were heterozygous at the 2 markers immediately flanking D10S1726/D10S198, D10S1433 on the centromeric side and D10S603 on the telomeric side. These recombinations placed the UFS gene within a 1-cM interval on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) constructed a physical map and identified novel polymorphic markers in the UFS critical region. Haplotype analysis using the new markers placed the UFS gene within a single YAC clone of 1,410 kb.

Chauve et al. (2000) performed haplotype analysis in a French family with urofacial syndrome and compared the results with those previously described in patients from Colombia and America. The results were compatible with the same location of the critical region on 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (2003) stated that their previous homozygosity mapping studies had located the UFS gene to a genomic interval of approximately 360 kb on chromosome 10q23-q24. They constructed a genomic sequence map covering the entire UFS interval and narrowed the disease interval to a 220-kb region that harbored the newly identified ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene.

By whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 individual with urofacial syndrome, Daly et al. (2010) defined an autozygous region of 16 Mb on chromosome 10q23-q24.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (1999) used homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage to show genetic homogeneity of urofacial syndrome; the locus had previously been mapped using families from Colombia. Chauve et al. (2000) concluded that their haplotype results in a French family favored the hypothesis of genetic homogeneity. Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q is responsible for UFS in all patients from multiple ethnic groups.

In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) did not identify HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome, indicating genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) determined the precise location of the GOT1 gene, which encodes glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase, within the UFS critical region and determined its genomic structure. Mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) searched extensively for mutations in the ACDP1 gene in patients with urofacial syndrome, covering the coding region, the 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, the promoter region, and the exon/intron junctions. They found no pathogenic mutation. ⚠︎

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all patients with urofacial syndrome they studied, who originated from Colombia, the United States, and France.

Within an autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24 in an individual with urofacial syndrome, Daly et al. (2010) identified a 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene. They identified homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations in HPSE2 in 5 further unrelated families with UFS, confirming HPSE2 as the causative gene. Mutations were not identified in 4 additional UFS patients.

### Pathogenesis

Pang et al. (2010) suggested that complete loss of HPSE2 function in patients with urofacial syndrome indicates that HPSE2 may be important for the synergic action of the muscles involved in facial expression and urine voiding.

Daly et al. (2010) showed that HPSE2 is expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system. They suggested that it might be involved there in controlling facial expression and urinary voiding. HPSE2 is also expressed in bladder smooth muscle, which they considered consistent with a role in renal tract morphology and function.

### Diagnosis

Elejalde (1979) noted that the peculiar facies allows early recognition of the condition, which can help with early assessment and treatment and may lead to a better prognosis. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) recommended urologic evaluation for individuals with 'inversion' of facial expression because of the clear association between this distorted facial expression and neuropathic bladder with resulting damage to the genitourinary tract. Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) stressed that early diagnosis has important consequences for the management and prognosis of the urinary problems. Derbent et al. (2009) recommended that physicians consider the syndrome in any patient who presents with dysfunctional voiding, particularly in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage.
