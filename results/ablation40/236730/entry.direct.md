### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene on chromosome 10q24.

### Description

Urofacial syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are an unusual 'inverted' facial expression, in which affected people appear to be crying when they try to smile, and failure of the urinary bladder to empty completely, without anatomic bladder outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage (Daly et al., 2010; Derbent et al., 2009). Patients have enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and voiding dysfunction caused by neurogenic bladder (Wang et al., 1997). Reflux of infected urine to the upper renal tract can damage the kidneys and lead to renal failure (Daly et al., 2010).

### Nomenclature

Elejalde (1979) used the term 'the Ochoa syndrome' for the condition. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) suggested the term 'urofacial syndrome.' ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families. One family was consanguineous (first cousins). The patients had a peculiar facies and peculiar gestures when smiling and crying. Their urinary findings were hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal caliber of the urethra in the prostatic and membranous portions, urethral valves, and an abnormal bladder with trabeculation and diverticula. The diverticula were associated with severe hypertrophy of the mucosa with sclerotic changes. Elejalde (1979) noted that the peculiar facies allows early recognition of the condition.

Between 1965 and 1986, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) saw 36 children who had enuresis and urinary tract infection together with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. In all cases, urologic work-up showed characteristic findings of mild neuropathic bladder, and most patients had severe urinary tract damage. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation. The authors suggested that individuals with 'inversion' of facial expression should have a urologic evaluation.

Chauve et al. (2000) reported the first European cases of urofacial syndrome, which occurred in a French family.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 further European cases of urofacial syndrome. They noted that cases may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing is not recognized. They also noted that early diagnosis has important consequences for the management and prognosis of the urinary problems.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported the first published case of urofacial syndrome from Turkey. The patient had been followed for recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux and was diagnosed with the syndrome at 16 years of age. The authors noted that some severely affected patients become hypertensive and progress to end-stage renal disease.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 families in Turkey. The median follow-up was 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months). The condition occurred in both sexes. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

From their analysis of 3 families, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant, with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that inheritance was autosomal recessive. Their evidence was the occurrence of the disorder in multiple sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio. ⚠︎

The homozygous HPSE2 mutations found in patients with urofacial syndrome by Daly et al. (2010) are consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) performed a genome screen in 20 selected patients from Colombia, combining homozygosity mapping with DNA pooling against 1 patient pool and 3 control pools of unaffected relatives. After 36 randomly chosen markers had been analyzed, they found that D10S677 was linked to and associated with UFS. Patients showed a significant excess of homozygosity at this marker compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)). Fine mapping with 10 additional markers covering a 22-cM region showed that all 31 patients were homozygous at 2 closely linked markers, D10S1726 and D10S198, which lie about 5 cM telomeric to D10S677. Only 12% of unaffected relatives were homozygous at both markers (P less than 10(-19)). Recombination events in several patients placed the UFS gene within a 1-cM interval between D10S1433 and D10S603 on chromosome 10q23-q24. ⚠︎

Wang et al. (1999) used homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage. They built a physical map of the region, identified new polymorphic markers, and used haplotype analysis to place the UFS gene within a single 1,410-kb YAC clone.

Wang et al. (2003) constructed a genomic sequence map covering the whole UFS interval, which had previously been localized to about 360 kb on chromosome 10q23-q24. They narrowed the disease interval to a 220-kb genomic region.

Using whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual, Daly et al. (2010) defined an autozygous region of 16 Mb on chromosome 10q23-q24.

### Heterogeneity

Several studies supported genetic homogeneity of urofacial syndrome. Wang et al. (1999) demonstrated genetic homogeneity through homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage, whose disease mapped to the same region of 10q23-q24 as in the Colombian families. In a French family, Chauve et al. (2000) found haplotypes compatible with the same critical region, which favored genetic homogeneity. Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q is responsible for the disorder in all UFS patients from multiple ethnic groups. ⚠︎

In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome, which indicated genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) mapped the glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase gene (GOT1) within the UFS critical region and determined its genomic structure. Mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) searched for mutations in the ACDP1 gene, which lies within the narrowed UFS interval. They examined the coding region, the 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, the promoter region, and the exon/intron junctions, but found no pathogenic mutation in UFS patients.

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all patients with urofacial syndrome originating from Colombia, the United States, and France. HPSE2 encodes a 592-amino acid protein. It contains a domain with sequence homology to the glycosyl hydrolase motif of the heparanase (HPSE) gene. The authors suggested that HPSE2 may be important for the coordinated action of muscles involved in facial expression and urine voiding.

Within an autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24, Daly et al. (2010) identified a 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene in an individual with urofacial syndrome. In 5 further unrelated families, they found homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations in HPSE2, confirming it as the causative gene. HPSE2 was expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system and in bladder smooth muscle.

### Diagnosis

Elejalde (1979) and Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) both emphasized that the characteristic facial expression allows early recognition of the disorder and should prompt urologic evaluation. Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) and Derbent et al. (2009) noted that the diagnosis may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the facial grimacing goes unrecognized. Derbent et al. (2009) recommended considering the syndrome in any patient with dysfunctional voiding, particularly in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage.
