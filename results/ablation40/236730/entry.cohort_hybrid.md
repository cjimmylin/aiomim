### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene (613469) on chromosome 10q24.2.

### Description

Urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by congenital obstructive uropathy and abnormal facial expression. Patients present with enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and voiding dysfunction as a result of neurogenic bladder (Wang et al., 1997). Daly et al. (2010) characterized the disorder by facial grimacing when attempting to smile and failure of the urinary bladder to void completely despite a lack of anatomic bladder outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage; affected individuals often have reflux of infected urine from the bladder to the upper renal tract, with a risk of kidney damage and renal failure.

### Nomenclature

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) suggested the term 'urofacial syndrome' for the association of 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing and neuropathic bladder.

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families, 1 of them consanguineous (first cousins), who had peculiar facies and gestures while smiling and crying. Urinary findings included hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal caliber of the urethra in the prostatic and membranous portions, urethral valves, and an abnormal bladder with trabeculation and diverticula associated with severe hypertrophy of the mucosa with sclerotic changes. Elejalde (1979) considered that the syndrome represented alteration of the facial and urinary developmental fields.

Between 1965 and 1986, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) saw 36 children with enuresis and urinary tract infection in association with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic workup disclosed characteristic findings of mild neuropathic bladder in all cases, with severe urinary tract damage in most of them. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation.

Chauve et al. (2000) reported a French family with urofacial syndrome, which they described as the first European cases of the disorder.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European cases of urofacial syndrome. They noted that the entity was originally described in Colombian patients and that very few cases had been reported from other countries, likely because of variability of the urinary problems and failure to recognize the characteristic facial grimacing.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported what they described as the first published case of urofacial syndrome in Turkey. The patient was diagnosed at 16 years of age, after having been followed with the diagnosis of recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux. The authors noted that the facial expression in this disorder is 'inverted,' such that patients appear to be crying when they smile, and that the urinary problems usually result in enuresis, urinary tract infection, and hydronephrosis, with some severely affected patients becoming hypertensive and progressing to end-stage renal disease.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 families from Turkey, with a median follow-up of 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months).

### Inheritance

From genetic analysis of 3 families, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant, with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance.

In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that the occurrence of the disorder in multiple sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio indicated autosomal recessive inheritance. Homozygosity for markers in the critical region in all 31 patients studied by Wang et al. (1997) and the identification of homozygous HPSE2 mutations by Daly et al. (2010) were consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) performed a genome screen using a combination of homozygosity mapping and DNA pooling in 20 selected patients with urofacial syndrome, 1 patient pool, and 3 control pools of unaffected relatives. After analysis of 36 randomly chosen markers, D10S677 was identified as linked to and associated with UFS, as suggested by a significant excess of homozygosity in patients compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)) and by allele frequency differences between the patient pool and control pools. Fine mapping with 10 additional markers covering a 22-cM region by haplotype (linkage disequilibrium) analysis showed that all 31 patients were homozygous for 2 closely linked markers (D10S1726 and D10S198) located approximately 5 cM telomeric to D10S677, whereas only 12% of unaffected relatives were homozygous for both markers (P less than 10(-19)). Recombinational events at the flanking markers D10S1433 and D10S603 placed the UFS gene within a 1-cM interval on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) established a physical map and identified novel polymorphic markers in the UFS critical region. Haplotype analysis using the new markers mapped the UFS gene within a single YAC clone of 1,410 kb.

Chauve et al. (2000) compared haplotypes in a French family with urofacial syndrome with those previously described in patients from Colombia and America; the results were compatible with the same localization of the critical region on 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (2003) constructed a genomic sequence map covering the entire UFS interval, previously defined as approximately 360 kb on chromosome 10q23-q24, and narrowed the disease interval to a 220-kb genomic region harboring the ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene.

By whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual, Daly et al. (2010) defined an autozygous region of 16 Mb on chromosome 10q23-q24.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (1999) demonstrated genetic homogeneity of urofacial syndrome through homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage, the gene having previously been mapped to 10q23-q24 using families from Colombia. Chauve et al. (2000) concluded that haplotype results in a French family favored the hypothesis of genetic homogeneity, and Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q is responsible for UFS in all patients from multiple ethnic groups.

In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) did not identify HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome, indicating genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) determined the precise location of the glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase gene (GOT1) within the UFS critical region and determined its genomic structure, but mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) searched the ACDP1 gene for mutations in patients with urofacial syndrome, including the coding region, the 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, the promoter region, and the exon/intron junctions, but failed to identify a pathogenic mutation.

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all patients with urofacial syndrome originating from Colombia, the United States, and France.

In an individual with urofacial syndrome, Daly et al. (2010) identified a 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene within a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24 defined by whole-genome SNP mapping. Homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations in HPSE2 in 5 further unrelated families confirmed HPSE2 as the causative gene. Mutations were not identified in 4 additional patients with UFS.

### Pathogenesis

Pang et al. (2010) noted that HPSE2 encodes a 592-amino acid protein containing a domain with sequence homology to the glycosyl hydrolase motif of heparanase. They suggested that complete loss of HPSE2 function in UFS patients indicates that HPSE2 may be important for the synergic action of muscles implicated in facial expression and urine voiding.

Daly et al. (2010) showed that HPSE2 is expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system, where it might be implicated in controlling facial expression and urinary voiding, and also in bladder smooth muscle, consistent with a role in renal tract morphology and function.

### Diagnosis

Elejalde (1979) noted that the peculiar facies allows early recognition of the condition, which could be helpful for early assessment and treatment, perhaps leading to a better prognosis. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) stated that the clear association of distortion in facial expression with neuropathic bladder and resultant damage to the genitourinary tract should prompt urologic evaluation of individuals with 'inversion' of facial expression.

Derbent et al. (2009) advised that urofacial syndrome be considered in any patient who presents with dysfunctional voiding, particularly in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage.

### Clinical Management

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) and Aydogdu et al. (2010) emphasized that early diagnosis of urofacial syndrome has important consequences for the management and prognosis of urinary problems due to a dysfunctional bladder.
