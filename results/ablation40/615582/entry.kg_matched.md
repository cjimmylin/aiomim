### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also called Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (190230) on chromosome 14q24.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is a connective tissue disorder with features that overlap Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Reported features include aneurysms and dissections of the aorta and other arteries, mitral valve disease, craniofacial findings such as hypertelorism, bifid uvula, and cleft palate, and skeletal findings such as tall stature, arachnodactyly, kyphoscoliosis, and joint hypermobility (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015; Kuechler et al., 2015). Variability within and between families is striking. Some affected individuals have no cardiovascular involvement, and 1 reported patient had growth retardation, low muscle mass, and distal arthrogryposis (Rienhoff et al., 2013).

### Nomenclature

Kuechler et al. (2015) noted that the emerging connective tissue disorder caused by TGFB3 mutations had tentatively been called Rienhoff syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) described a European American girl, born to nonconsanguineous healthy parents, whom they followed to age 9 years. At birth she had distal arthrogryposis, with contractures of the right hand (most severe in the 3rd and 4th fingers) and of all toes. She also had mild hypotonia and a midline facial nevus flammeus. She had failure to thrive and delayed gross motor development, and at 17 months her weight was below the 1st centile. At that age she had hypertelorism, prominent eyes, blue sclerae, bifid uvula with an intact hard palate, mild retrognathia, a metopic ridge, pectus excavatum, pes planus, hyperextensible large joints, and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. Muscle bulk was decreased, with low tone, diminished reflexes, and strength of 1/5. She stood independently at 20 months with a positive Gowers sign and walked at 24 months with a 'hip waddle' gait. Her neurocognitive development was normal. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. At age 7 years her weight was still below the 1st centile, she had minimal subcutaneous fat, and her elbows and knees were markedly hyperextensible. A quadriceps biopsy at age 7 showed type 1 fiber predominance with mild focal fiber-type disproportion consistent with disuse, without dystrophic or inflammatory changes. Echocardiograms performed yearly from 18 months showed no cardiac defect, and aortic root dimensions were normal at 6.5 years.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with features overlapping Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Seven family members, aged 40 to 68 years, had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three of them died from aortic dissection and rupture. Four members had mitral valve abnormalities, ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation that required surgery. Craniofacial features were subtle: long face, high-arched palate, and retrognathia. Other findings included pectus deformity, scoliosis, velvety skin, varices, and hiatal hernia, as well as autoimmune features (HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease, and celiac disease).

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized 43 patients (23 male and 20 female; median age 34 years) from 11 families with TGFB3 mutations. Clinical overlap with Loeys-Dietz syndrome was significant, and variability within and between families was striking. Vascular involvement ranged from none at age 64 to type A dissection (median age 51 years), type B dissection (median age 44.5 years), abdominal aortic dissection, and death from cerebral aneurysm dissection at age 55. No patient had early dissection, dissection at a small aortic size, or striking arterial tortuosity. Other features included mitral valve disease and septal defects. Skeletal findings included tall stature (12 patients), arachnodactyly (16), kyphoscoliosis (11), joint hypermobility (9), and pectus deformity (8). Loeys-Dietz syndrome features included hypertelorism (14), bifid uvula (11), cleft palate (5), cervical spine instability, and clubfoot. No patient had ectopia lentis, and only 2 had early-onset osteoarthritis.

Kuechler et al. (2015) described a family in which a father and his 2 children had features overlapping Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome, including tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula. None of them had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation, or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) observed autosomal dominant inheritance in a large Dutch family, and Kuechler et al. (2015) observed it in a father and his 2 children. In the patients reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013) and Matyas et al. (2014), the heterozygous TGFB3 mutation had occurred de novo. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed genomewide SNP linkage analysis on 6 patients from the Dutch family 1. All affected patients shared 2 large genomic regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15. TGFB3, on chromosome 14q24, was the most prominent candidate gene in these regions.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on a girl with low muscle mass, growth retardation, distal arthrogryposis, and features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, and on her parents and 2 unaffected sibs. In the patient they found a de novo heterozygous mutation in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene (C409Y), and Sanger sequencing confirmed that it was de novo. The variant was absent from dbSNP130, the EVS database, and 1000 Genomes. The substituted cysteine is conserved across TGF-beta family ligands and is part of the 'cysteine knot' needed for ligand activity. In functional studies, the mutant ligand did not activate a TGF-beta reporter. In Xenopus embryos, coinjection of mutant and wildtype RNA reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals, which suggested a dominant-negative effect. The patient also carried a second de novo heterozygous variant, a nonsense change in CDH2 (114020) (c.208C-T, Q70X). N-cadherin levels in her dermal fibroblasts did not differ significantly from those in controls, and Cdh2 heterozygous null mice are phenotypically normal. The authors therefore concluded that the CDH2 variant was unlikely to drive the phenotype, although they could not exclude a modifying effect.

Matyas et al. (2014) found a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

In affected members of the Dutch family 1, sequencing of all known thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (TAAD) genes found no causal mutation. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) then identified a heterozygous splice site mutation in TGFB3 (c.754+2T-C) in the family. The mutation was absent from variant databases and segregated with the phenotype. cDNA sequencing showed skipping of exon 4, which results in an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids. The same authors screened TGFB3 in 470 additional probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic TAAD, 350 by Sanger sequencing and 120 by exome sequencing. They found heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 more probands: 4 missense mutations, 1 nonsense mutation, and 2 frameshift mutations. All of these mutations were absent from controls in EVS, 1000 Genomes, and Genome of the Netherlands. The missense mutations were D263H (family 3), R300W (families 2, 4, 5, and 11), I322T (family 10), and L401P (family 8). The other mutations were a de novo nonsense mutation, Y365X (family 6), and 2 single-base deletions causing frameshifts, L386RfsX21 (family 7) and N235MfsX11 (family 9). The proband in family 6 also carried a SMAD3 (603109) variant of unknown significance (A250T). It had been inherited from his or her mother, who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement, and the authors considered its contribution to pathogenesis unclear.

Kuechler et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 of the 3 affected members of a family with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. They identified a novel heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and showed that it segregated with the phenotype in the family.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found that TGFB3 mutations affecting arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula. TGFB3 mutations also appeared to have opposing effects on height. One patient with the D263H mutation had short stature and received growth hormone therapy, whereas several other patients were tall.

Kuechler et al. (2015) noted that a previously reported heterozygous loss-of-function TGFB3 mutation (C409Y) was associated with growth retardation. In contrast, arg300 mutations, which they presumed increase TGF-beta signaling, were associated with overgrowth or tall stature. They suggested that the short or tall stature in patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of the mutations on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) suggested that the phenotype in their patient with the C409Y mutation resulted from reduced, rather than excess, TGF-beta signaling. They proposed that TGFB3 deficiency during early myogenesis could lower the number of muscle precursor cells and cause hypomyoplasia, possibly through incomplete epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition of the dermomyotome. They also concluded that normal muscle mass and complete palatal fusion appear to require a minimum level of TGFB3 signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) examined dissected aortic wall from a patient with the D263H mutation. It showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition, resembling Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. Immunohistochemistry showed paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling, with increased canonical (pSMAD2) and noncanonical (pERK) signaling and elevated TGFB1 mRNA. Pathology reports from 2 deceased patients in family 1 described extensive elastic fiber fragmentation and aortic medial degeneration. The authors had observed 3 truncating mutations and an in-frame splice site mutation, and on that basis they hypothesized that TGFB3 mutations cause loss of function. They noted that missense mutations in the LAP domain alter residues important for integrin activation and for processing. They concluded that TGFB3 loss-of-function mutations are associated with aortic and arterial aneurysms and dissections and with mitral valve disease. They suggested that previously reported patients may have lacked cardiovascular disease because of their young age.

### Diagnosis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) noted that their patient shared features with Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, and Beals-Hecht syndrome but did not meet the diagnostic criteria for any of them. Distinguishing features included marked growth retardation, absence of cardiovascular findings, and distinct muscle histopathology. Analysis of TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1, and FBN2 found no mutation.

### Clinical Management

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported that a 3-year trial of losartan, begun at age 3 years at doses up to 2.0 mg/kg/day, did not change their patient's muscle strength or mass. They suggested that this pointed to a pathophysiology distinct from that of mutations that enhance TGF-beta signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers, with at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches. They stated that imaging of the brain vessels might be indicated depending on family history.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.C409Y]

Rienhoff et al. (2013) studied a European American girl with low muscle mass, growth retardation, distal arthrogryposis, and features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. By exome sequencing of the patient, her parents, and 2 unaffected sibs, they identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the change was de novo, and the variant was absent from dbSNP130, the EVS database, and 1000 Genomes. The cysteine is conserved across TGF-beta family ligands and is part of the 'cysteine knot' needed for ligand activity. Mutant ligand expressed in 293T cells did not activate a TGF-beta reporter (p3TP-Lux), whereas wildtype ligand did. In Xenopus embryos, coinjection of mutant and wildtype TGFB3 RNA at a 1:1 ratio reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals to about 40% and 60% of wildtype, respectively, with no effect on BMP-induced SMAD1 phosphorylation. The authors concluded that the mutant has a dominant-negative effect on TGF-beta signaling and suggested that it is a hypomorphic allele. ⚠︎

**.0002 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, IVS4DS, T-C, +2 [c.754+2T>C; p.E216_K251del]

In affected members of a large Dutch family (family 1) with autosomal dominant Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.754+2T-C mutation in the canonical donor splice site of exon 4 of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation was absent from variant databases and segregated with the phenotype. cDNA sequencing in 2 patients showed skipping of exon 4. This results in an in-frame deletion of 108 nucleotides and a predicted deletion of 36 amino acids (Glu216_Lys251del), which homology modeling predicted would remove a central beta-strand of the latency-associated peptide (LAP) domain. A 17-year-old carrier had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic manifestations.

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ASP263HIS [p.D263H]

In family 3 with Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous asp263-to-his (D263H) mutation in exon 5 of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation alters a highly conserved residue of the LAP domain and disrupts the RGD motif needed for integrin binding. SIFT predicted it to be deleterious and MutationTaster predicted it to be disease causing. One patient with this mutation had short stature and received growth hormone therapy. Dissected aortic wall from a patient with the mutation showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition. Immunohistochemistry showed paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling, with increased pSMAD2 and pERK and elevated TGFB1 mRNA.

**.0004 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300TRP [p.R300W]

In 4 families (families 2, 4, 5, and 11) with Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous arg300-to-trp (R300W) mutation in exon 5 of the TGFB3 gene. Arg300 is the last amino acid of the LAP domain and the last residue of the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif. Modeling predicted loss of ionic interactions and steric rearrangements. Mutations affecting arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

**.0005 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ILE322THR [p.I322T]

In family 10 with Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous ile322-to-thr (I322T) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the active cytokine region. Modeling predicted that it alters the position of Arg325, a residue important for binding TGFBR2, and disrupts hydrophobic contacts with the LAP domain.

**.0006 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, LEU401PRO [p.L401P]

In family 8 with Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous leu401-to-pro (L401P) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the active cytokine region. Modeling predicted changed hydrophobic interactions with residues of both the LAP and TGFB3 domains.

**.0007 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, TYR365TER [p.Y365*]

In the proband of family 6 with Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a de novo heterozygous tyr365-to-ter (Y365X) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T), inherited from a mother with variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement. The authors considered the contribution of the SMAD3 variant to pathogenesis unclear.

**.0010 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLY [c.898C>G; p.R300G]

In a father and his 2 children with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, Kuechler et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing of 2 affected members, and Sanger sequencing confirmed it and showed that it segregated with the phenotype. The mutation affected the same codon as a previously reported arg300-to-gln mutation in a child with overgrowth. The authors suggested that arg300 mutations presumably increase TGF-beta signaling.
