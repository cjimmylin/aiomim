### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also known as Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (190230) on chromosome 14q24. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is a connective tissue disorder whose clinical features overlap with those of Marfan syndrome and other forms of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Craniofacial features include hypertelorism, bifid uvula and cleft palate, and skeletal features include arachnodactyly, joint hypermobility, pectus deformity and scoliosis. Stature may be short or tall (Rienhoff et al., 2013; Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015; Kuechler et al., 2015). Cardiovascular involvement is highly variable. It may include aneurysm and dissection of the thoracic and abdominal aorta and mitral valve disease, but some affected individuals have no cardiovascular findings (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015; Kuechler et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Kuechler et al. (2015) referred to the emerging connective tissue disorder associated with TGFB3 mutations as 'Rienhoff syndrome,' a name they described as tentative.

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European American girl born to nonconsanguineous, healthy parents. At birth she had contractures of the right hand, which were most severe in the third and fourth fingers, and contractures of all toes, together with a midline facial nevus flammeus and mild hypotonia. Her birth weight was 2.9 kg (5th centile). She was evaluated for failure to thrive at 3 months of age. At 17 months she weighed 7.5 kg (less than the 1st centile), could not crawl or roll, and had several other findings. These were bilateral pes planus, mild pectus excavatum, hyperextensibility of multiple large joints, mild retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, blue sclerae, a small metopic ridge, and a bifid uvula with an intact hard palate. Muscle bulk was decreased in all appendicular and axial muscles, with strength 1/5, low tone, diminished reflexes and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. She stood independently with a positive Gowers sign at 20 months and walked at 24 months with a 'hip waddle.' Neurocognitive development was appropriate for age. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at age 7 years showed type 1 fiber predominance with mild, focal type 1 fiber disproportion, which was consistent with disuse, and no dystrophic or inflammatory changes. Yearly echocardiograms from 18 months showed no cardiac defect or dysfunction. At 6.5 years the aortic annulus and aortic root measured 1.34 cm (z score 0.59) and 1.72 cm (z score 0.39), respectively. Slit lamp examination was normal. The authors noted that the patient had the hypertelorism and bifid uvula of Loeys-Dietz syndrome and skeletal findings typical of Marfan syndrome. However, she did not meet the diagnostic criteria for Marfan, Beals-Hecht or Loeys-Dietz syndrome, mainly because of her significant growth retardation, the absence of cardiovascular findings and her distinct muscle histopathology.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with clinical features overlapping Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome and an inheritance pattern consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. Seven family members aged between 40 and 68 years had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three died from aortic dissection and rupture, which was confirmed at autopsy in 2 of them (ages 57 and 56 years). Four members had mitral valve abnormalities, ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation that required surgery. Craniofacial abnormalities were subtle and included a long face, high-arched palate and retrognathia, and pectus deformity and scoliosis were frequent. Other recurrent findings were velvety skin, varices and hiatal hernia. Several family members had autoimmune features, including HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease and celiac disease. Pathology reports of the dissected aorta in 2 patients described extensive elastic fiber fragmentation with 'pseudo cyst formation' in the media.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized the findings in 43 affected individuals (23 males and 20 females) from 11 families with TGFB3 mutations. The median age was 34 years (range, 3 to 74 years). Vascular involvement ranged from no cardiovascular abnormalities at age 64 years in 1 patient to type A aortic dissection (4 patients; median age 51 years, range 40 to 80) or type B dissection (6 patients; median age 44.5 years, range 30 to 57). Six patients had aortic aneurysm, and 1 patient died at age 55 years from dissection of a cerebral aneurysm. The authors observed no early arterial dissection, no dissection at small aortic dimensions and no striking aortic or arterial tortuosity. Other cardiovascular features were mitral valve disease, persistent foramen ovale and atrial or ventricular septal defects. Systemic features included hypertelorism (14 patients), bifid uvula (11), cleft palate (5), tall stature (12), arachnodactyly (16), pectus deformity (8), kyphoscoliosis (11) and joint hypermobility (9). Cervical spine instability, clubfoot, dolichocephaly, high-arched palate, retrognathia and inguinal hernia were also observed. Two individuals had early-onset osteoarthritis. No ectopia lentis was found in the medical records. The authors noted striking intrafamilial and interfamilial variability: some patients had typical Loeys-Dietz features and others had none. Aortic wall tissue from a patient with the asp263-to-his mutation showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition. This patient also had short stature and had received growth hormone therapy during puberty.

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a family in which a father and his 2 children had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism and bifid uvula. None had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

Rienhoff et al. (2013) and Matyas et al. (2014) found de novo TGFB3 mutations in sporadic patients. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found autosomal dominant inheritance in a large Dutch family, in which a heterozygous TGFB3 mutation segregated with the phenotype. Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a heterozygous TGFB3 mutation that segregated with the disorder in an affected father and his 2 affected children.

### Mapping

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed genomewide linkage analysis with SNP genotypes from 6 patients of a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms. They identified 2 large genomic regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15, that were shared by all affected patients. The TGFB3 gene on chromosome 14q24 was the most prominent candidate in these regions.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) found no mutation in the TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1 or FBN2 genes in a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, low muscle mass and growth retardation. Exome sequencing then identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene (190230), resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. The affected cysteine is conserved in all TGF-beta family ligands and is part of the cysteine knot. When expressed in cultured cells, the mutant ligand did not activate a TGF-beta reporter. When coinjected with wildtype TGFB3 into Xenopus embryos, it reduced SMAD2 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation. The patient also carried a de novo nonsense mutation in the CDH2 gene (Q70X), but N-cadherin levels in her fibroblasts did not differ from those of controls. The authors concluded that the CDH2 mutation was unlikely to drive the phenotype, although they could not exclude a modifying effect.

Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Kuechler et al. (2015) stated that this mutation was c.899G-A, predicting arg300-to-gln (R300Q).

In a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms and dissections, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found no causal mutation in known thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection genes. They then identified a heterozygous splice site mutation in the TGFB3 gene (c.754+2T-C) by linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing. cDNA sequencing in 2 patients confirmed skipping of exon 4, which resulted in an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids (glu216_lys251del). The mutation segregated with the phenotype. It was also present in a 17-year-old family member who had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic manifestations. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) then screened TGFB3 in 470 probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic thoracic aortic aneurysms and dissections, using Sanger sequencing in 350 and exome sequencing in 120. They identified heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 further probands. These comprised 4 missense mutations: D263H, R300W (in 4 families), I322T and L401P. There was also 1 nonsense mutation, Y365X, and 2 single-base deletions leading to frameshifts (L386Rfs*21 and N235Mfs*11). All of the mutations were absent from control databases. The Y365X mutation occurred de novo; the same proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T), inherited from his or her mother, whose contribution to pathogenesis was unclear. The D263H mutation disrupts the RGD integrin-binding motif of the latency-associated peptide (LAP), and R300W affects the last residue of the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif. On the basis of the truncating and in-frame splice site mutations, the authors hypothesized that the TGFB3 mutations cause loss of function.

In a family in which a father and his 2 children had features overlapping Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Kuechler et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 of the 3 affected members. They identified a novel heterozygous c.898C-G transversion in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and showed that it segregated with the phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found that TGFB3 mutations affecting residue arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula, both in their patients and in the patient reported by Matyas et al. (2014). They also noted that TGFB3 mutations appeared to have opposing effects on height. Their patient with the D263H mutation and the patient reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013) had short stature, whereas several of their own patients and the patient of Matyas et al. (2014) had tall stature.

Kuechler et al. (2015) noted that the R300G mutation in their family affected the same codon as the R300Q mutation in the child with overgrowth reported by Matyas et al. (2014), whereas a loss-of-function mutation (C409Y) had been found in an individual with growth retardation. They suggested that mutations at codon arg300 presumably lead to increased TGF-beta signaling. They further suggested that the short or tall stature of patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of the mutations on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) showed in Xenopus embryos that the TGFB3 C409Y mutant, when coexpressed with wildtype TGFB3, had a dominant-negative effect on TGF-beta signaling. At a 1:1 ratio of mutant to wildtype RNA, pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals were reduced to approximately 40% and 60%, respectively, while BMP-dependent SMAD1 phosphorylation was unaffected. The authors concluded that the phenotype resulted from a hypomorphic allele. They proposed that TGFB3 deficiency during early myogenesis might impair epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition of the dermomyotome and so reduce the number of muscle precursor cells. This would cause hypomyoplasia with normal muscle architecture, distinct from the myopathy caused by excess TGF-beta signaling in Marfan syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed immunohistochemistry on aortic wall tissue from a patient with the TGFB3 D263H mutation. They found evidence of paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling, with increased pSMAD2 (canonical signaling), increased pERK (noncanonical signaling) and elevated TGFB1 mRNA, similar to findings in TGFB2-deficient aortic walls. They noted that this contrasted with the decreased signaling found by Rienhoff et al. (2013) in a Xenopus model. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported that in their patient a 3-year trial of losartan, at doses of up to 2.0 mg/kg/day starting at age 3 years, produced no change in muscle strength or mass.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers, together with at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches. They suggested that, depending on family history, additional imaging of the brain vessels might be indicated.
