### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also known as Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (190230) on chromosome 14q24. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is a connective tissue disorder whose features overlap with Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Reported features include aortic aneurysm and dissection, mitral valve disease, hypertelorism, bifid uvula or cleft palate, joint hypermobility, arachnodactyly, pectus deformity and kyphoscoliosis. Clinical variability is striking both within and between families (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015). Some patients have tall stature (Kuechler et al., 2015). The first reported patient instead had growth retardation, low muscle mass and distal arthrogryposis (Rienhoff et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European American girl born to nonconsanguineous healthy parents. At birth she had distal arthrogryposis, with contractures of the right hand (most severe in the 3rd and 4th fingers) and of all toes. She also had a midline facial nevus flammeus and mild hypotonia. She failed to thrive, and her gross motor development was delayed. At 17 months she had hypertelorism, prominent eyes, blue sclerae, a bifid uvula with an intact hard palate, retrognathia, mild pectus excavatum and pes planus. Her large joints were hyperextensible. She had reduced muscle bulk with low tone and diminished reflexes, and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. She walked at 24 months with a waddling gait and a positive Gowers sign, and her neurocognitive development was normal. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. Growth retardation persisted, and her weight was below the 1st centile at age 7. Yearly echocardiograms from 18 months of age showed no cardiac defect, and her aortic root dimensions were normal (z score 0.39 at age 6.5 years). A quadriceps biopsy at age 7 showed only mild focal type 1 fiber disproportion consistent with disuse, with no dystrophic or inflammatory changes. ⚠︎

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with an autosomal dominant disorder whose features overlapped with Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. Seven members aged 40 to 68 years had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three died of aortic dissection and rupture. Four had mitral valve abnormalities ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation. Other features included subtle craniofacial changes (long face, high-arched palate, retrognathia), pectus deformity, scoliosis, velvety skin, varices and hiatal hernia. Autoimmune features included HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease and celiac disease.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized 43 patients (23 male, 20 female) from 11 families with TGFB3 mutations, whose features overlapped with Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Four patients had type A dissection (median age 51 years) and 6 had type B dissection (median age 44.5 years). Other findings included aortic aneurysm, mitral valve disease, septal defects, hypertelorism, bifid uvula, cleft palate, cervical spine instability, clubfoot, tall stature, arachnodactyly, pectus deformity, kyphoscoliosis, joint hypermobility and hernia. The authors observed no early dissection, no dissection at small aortic size, no striking arterial tortuosity and no ectopia lentis. Clinical variability was striking both within and between families.

Kuechler et al. (2015) described a family in which a father and his 2 children had features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. These features included tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism and bifid uvula. None of the 3 had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) observed autosomal dominant transmission in a large Dutch family, in which a heterozygous TGFB3 mutation segregated with the phenotype. Kuechler et al. (2015) reported transmission of a heterozygous TGFB3 mutation from a father to his 2 affected children. Rienhoff et al. (2013) and Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) each reported a patient with a de novo heterozygous TGFB3 mutation.

### Mapping

All known TAAD genes had been excluded by sequencing in the Dutch family 1. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) then performed SNP-based linkage analysis in 6 patients from this family. All affected patients shared 2 large genomic regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15. Within these regions, the TGFB3 gene on 14q24 was the leading candidate.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) studied a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, hypertelorism, low muscle mass and growth retardation. Exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variant was de novo. The variant was not present in dbSNP130, the EVS database or 1000 Genomes. Cys409 is the penultimate cysteine of the cysteine knot and is conserved across TGF-beta family ligands. No mutations were found in TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1 or FBN2. In cultured cells, secreted TGFB3 C409Y failed to activate a TGF-beta reporter. In Xenopus embryos, coinjection of mutant and wildtype RNA at a 1:1 ratio reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals to about 40% and 60% of wildtype levels, respectively, consistent with a dominant-negative effect. The mutant had no effect on BMP/SMAD1 signaling. The authors concluded that the mutation is hypomorphic and that reduced TGF-beta signaling likely accounts for the phenotype.

Rienhoff et al. (2013) also found a de novo heterozygous nonsense mutation in the CDH2 gene (114020) in the same patient (c.208C-T, Q70X). N-cadherin levels in her fibroblasts did not differ from those in controls, and heterozygous Cdh2-null mice are normal. The authors therefore concluded that the CDH2 variant was unlikely to drive the phenotype, although they could not exclude modifying effects.

In a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Matyas et al. (2014) reported a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide (LAP) domain of TGFB3.

In affected members of the Dutch family 1, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found a heterozygous splice donor variant in intron 4 of the TGFB3 gene (c.754+2T-C). The variant was absent from variant databases and segregated with the phenotype. Analysis of cDNA from 2 patients showed skipping of exon 4, which produces an in-frame deletion of 108 nucleotides and a predicted loss of 36 amino acids (p.Glu216_Lys251del). The variant was also present in a 17-year-old who had mild systemic findings but no documented cardiovascular features.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) then screened 470 additional probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic TAAD for TGFB3 mutations, 350 by Sanger sequencing and 120 by exome sequencing. They found heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 more probands: 4 different missense mutations, 1 nonsense mutation and 2 single-base deletions causing frameshifts. None of these mutations was present in controls in EVS, 1000 Genomes or Genome of the Netherlands. The missense mutations were: - D263H (family 3), which disrupts the RGD integrin-binding motif of the LAP domain - R300W (families 2, 4, 5 and 11), which affects the last residue of the LAP domain and the RKKR furin cleavage motif - I322T (family 10) and L401P (family 8), both in the active cytokine region The proband of family 6 had a de novo nonsense mutation (Y365X). The proband also carried a SMAD3 (603109) variant of unknown significance (A250T), inherited from a mother who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement. The authors considered the contribution of the SMAD3 variant unclear. Families 7 and 9 carried frameshift mutations (L386RfsTer21 and N235MfsTer11), which the authors predicted would lead to nonsense-mediated decay or to truncated proteins that probably lose cytokine activity.

Kuechler et al. (2015) studied a family with a disorder overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. In 3 affected members, they identified a heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing in 2 affected individuals, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and segregated with the phenotype. The authors noted that a different mutation at the same codon (R300Q) had been reported previously in a child with overgrowth. They proposed that mutations at Arg300 increase TGF-beta signaling.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that TGFB3 mutations appeared to have opposing effects on height. One patient with the D263H mutation had short stature and received growth hormone, whereas several other patients had tall stature. Mutations affecting Arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

Kuechler et al. (2015) noted that a heterozygous loss-of-function TGFB3 mutation (C409Y) had been reported in an individual with growth retardation. By contrast, mutations at codon Arg300, which presumably increase TGF-beta signaling, were associated with tall stature or overgrowth. The authors suggested that short or tall stature in patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) suggested that TGFB3 deficiency during early myogenesis could cause hypomyoplasia, possibly through incomplete epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition of the dermomyotome. This would clinically mimic, but be distinct from, the myopathy caused by excess TGF-beta signaling in Marfan syndrome. They concluded that normal muscle mass and complete palatal fusion require a minimum level of TGFB3 signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) examined the dissected aortic wall of a patient with the D263H mutation. They found elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition. Immunohistochemistry showed paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling, with increased canonical pSMAD2 and noncanonical pERK, and TGFB1 mRNA was elevated, similar to findings in TGFB2-deficient aortas. Pathology reports from 2 patients in family 1 also described extensive elastic fiber fragmentation and aortic medial degeneration. Because they had found 3 truncating mutations and an in-frame splice site mutation, the authors hypothesized that TGFB3 mutations cause loss of function. They also proposed that the young ages of previously reported patients (8 and 10.5 years) might explain why those patients lacked obvious cardiovascular disease.

### Clinical Management

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 3-year trial of losartan in their patient, started at age 3 at doses up to 2.0 mg/kg/day. The trial produced no change in her muscle strength or mass. The authors took this as further evidence that her disease mechanism differs from that of disorders with enhanced TGF-beta signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers. They also recommended at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches, and noted that imaging of the brain vessels might be indicated.

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ASP263HIS [p.D263H]

In family 3 with LDS5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous asp263-to-his (D263H) mutation in exon 5 of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation changes a highly conserved residue of the latency-associated peptide domain and disrupts the RGD motif required for integrin binding. In a homology model, Asp263 lies in a surface loop that is accessible to integrins. Dissected aortic tissue from a patient with this mutation showed elastic fiber fragmentation and paradoxically increased TGF-beta signaling, with increased pSMAD2 and pERK. One patient with this mutation had short stature and received growth hormone.

**.0004 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300TRP [p.R300W]

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous arg300-to-trp (R300W) mutation in the TGFB3 gene in 4 families with LDS5 (families 2, 4, 5 and 11). The mutation affects the last amino acid of the latency-associated peptide domain and disrupts the last residue of the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif. Homology modeling predicted loss of ionic interactions and induced steric rearrangements. Mutations at Arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

**.0005 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ILE322THR [p.I322T]

In family 10 with LDS5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous ile322-to-thr (I322T) mutation at a highly conserved residue in the active cytokine region of TGFB3. A homology model predicted that the change would alter the position of Arg325, a residue important for binding to TGFBR2. The model also predicted disruption of hydrophobic contacts with the latency-associated peptide domain.

**.0006 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**

The substitution was predicted to change hydrophobic interactions with residues of both the latency-associated peptide and TGFB3 domains.
