### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also known as Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (190230) on chromosome 14q24.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5) is an autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder whose features overlap with those of Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Reported features include aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta, and mitral valve disease. Craniofacial features include hypertelorism, bifid uvula and cleft palate, and skeletal features include arachnodactyly, joint hypermobility, pectus deformity and scoliosis. The phenotype varies: some affected individuals had no cardiovascular involvement, and stature ranged from tall stature and overgrowth to short stature and growth retardation with low muscle mass. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European-American girl born to nonconsanguineous, healthy parents who had 2 older healthy children. At birth, her weight was at the 5th centile, and she had distal arthrogryposis with finger and toe contractures, hypotonia and bifid uvula. She had failure to thrive, growth retardation and delayed gross motor development. Her motor examination showed decreased bulk in all appendicular and axial muscles, strength 1/5, low tone and diminished reflexes throughout, with markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. She had a positive Gower sign and a waddling gait. Other findings included pes planus, mild pectus excavatum, hyperextensibility of the large joints, long narrow hands and feet with arachnodactyly, and bilateral coxa valga. Craniofacial features were retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, blue sclerae, a metopic ridge, a tubular nose, malar hypoplasia and a midline facial nevus flammeus. Muscle biopsy showed type 1 fiber predominance with mild focal fiber disproportion. She had no cardiac defect or aortic root dilatation, no scoliosis, no cleft of the hard palate, no abnormality of skin texture or wound healing, and no lens abnormality on slit-lamp examination. Her neurocognitive development was normal. Rienhoff et al. (2013) noted that she had the hypertelorism and bifid uvula characteristic of Loeys-Dietz syndrome together with Marfan-like skeletal findings. She did not meet diagnostic criteria for Marfan, Beals-Hecht or Loeys-Dietz syndrome because of her significant growth retardation, absence of cardiovascular findings and distinct muscle histopathology.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with clinical features overlapping with Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Seven family members, between 40 and 68 years of age, had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three members died from aortic dissection and rupture. Four members had mitral valve abnormalities, ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation requiring surgery. Craniofacial abnormalities were rather subtle and included a long face, high-arched palate and retrognathia. Pectus deformity and scoliosis were frequent. Other findings in the family included velvety skin, varices, hiatal hernia, iliac and subclavian artery aneurysms (in 2 members), and elastic fiber fragmentation with medial degeneration of the aorta. Some members had HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease or celiac disease. A 17-year-old mutation carrier (1-IV:1) had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic manifestations, including craniofacial features, easy bruising and scoliosis.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized 43 affected individuals (23 males and 20 females) from 11 families with TGFB3 mutations. The median age was 34 years, and the median age at dissection was 47.5 years. Findings included 4 type A and 6 type B dissections, 6 aortic aneurysms, tall stature (12 individuals), arachnodactyly (16), pectus deformity (8), kyphoscoliosis (11), joint hypermobility (9), hypertelorism (14), bifid uvula (11) and cleft palate (5). Not all patients were evaluated for all features. The authors observed no early arterial dissection, no dissection at small aortic dimensions and no striking aortic or arterial tortuosity. Disease beyond the aorta, as iliac and subclavian artery aneurysms, was found in only 2 patients. Other cardiovascular features included mitral valve disease, persistent foramen ovale and atrial or ventricular septal defects. Ectopia lentis was not found. In family 2, 1 member (2-II:1) had abdominal aortic dissection and died from a cerebral aneurysm dissection at age 55. Hypertelorism was present in 3 members of family 2 and in individuals from families 7 and 8. Bifid uvula was present in 2 members of family 2 and in 1 individual from family 7. Individuals from families 1, 5, 7 and 8 had a long face. Arachnodactyly was noted in an individual from family 5, and metatarsus adductus in an individual from family 8. Patient 3-III:1 (family 3) had an aortic dissection requiring surgery and retrognathia that was also treated surgically. This patient had short stature and received growth hormone therapy during puberty. The dissected aortic wall showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition. In families 9 and 10, only mild elastic fiber fragmentation was observed. Early-onset osteoarthritis was reported in only 2 individuals (10-II:1 and 11-II:1). ⚠︎

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a family in which a father and his 2 children had features overlapping with Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes: tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism and bifid uvula. None had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

LDS5 shows autosomal dominant inheritance. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) described a large Dutch family with a pattern consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, in which a heterozygous TGFB3 mutation segregated with the phenotype. Kuechler et al. (2015) found a heterozygous TGFB3 mutation in an affected father and his 2 affected children. De novo heterozygous TGFB3 mutations were reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013), Matyas et al. (2014) and Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015; family 6). ⚠︎

### Mapping

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed linkage analysis using SNP genotypes from 6 patients of a large Dutch family with autosomal dominant syndromic aortic aneurysms. They identified 2 large genomic regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15, that were shared by all affected patients. The TGFB3 gene on chromosome 14q24 was the most prominent candidate.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, low muscle mass and growth retardation. They identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution at a cysteine in the conserved cysteine knot. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutation was de novo, and it was absent from both unaffected parents and the 2 unaffected sibs. Mutation analysis of TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1 and FBN2 found no mutation. The patient also carried a de novo nonsense mutation in CDH2 (Q70X). N-cadherin levels in her fibroblasts did not differ from those of controls, and the authors concluded that the CDH2 mutation was unlikely to drive the phenotype, although they could not exclude a modifying effect. The C409Y mutant TGFB3 ligand, expressed in 293T cells, did not activate a TGF-beta reporter. When the mutant was coinjected with wildtype TGFB3 in Xenopus embryos, it dominantly reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signaling, to approximately 40% and 60%, respectively, at a 1:1 ratio. It did not affect BMP-dependent SMAD1 phosphorylation. The authors suggested that the mutation is hypomorphic and reduces TGF-beta signaling.

In a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3.

In affected members of a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms (family 1), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous splice site mutation in the TGFB3 gene (c.754+2T-C). The mutation segregated with the phenotype and was absent from variant databases. In 2 patients, cDNA sequencing confirmed skipping of exon 4, which results in an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids (glu216_lys251del). Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) then screened TGFB3 in 470 probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection, using Sanger sequencing in 350 and exome sequencing in 120. They found heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 further probands, all absent from controls. These comprised 4 different missense mutations: D263H (family 3), R300W (families 2, 4, 5 and 11), I322T (family 10) and L401P (family 8). There was also 1 nonsense mutation, Y365X (family 6), and 2 single-base deletions causing a frameshift and premature stop codon, leu386-to-arg-fs (family 7) and asn235-to-met-fs (family 9). The Y365X mutation in family 6 occurred de novo. The proband in that family also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T), which was also present in the proband's mother, who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement. The precise contribution of the SMAD3 variant to pathogenesis was unclear. The authors noted that D263H disrupts the RGD integrin-binding motif of the latency-associated peptide and that R300W affects the last residue of the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif. On the basis of the 3 truncating mutations and the in-frame splice mutation, they hypothesized that TGFB3 mutations cause loss of function.

Kuechler et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 of 3 affected members of a family with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. They identified a novel heterozygous c.898C-G transversion in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and showed that it segregated with the phenotype in the affected father and his 2 affected children.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found that TGFB3 mutations affecting residue arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula. They also noted opposing effects on height: 1 patient with the D263H mutation had short stature, whereas several other patients had tall stature.

Kuechler et al. (2015) noted that the R300G mutation affected the same codon as an R300Q mutation previously reported in a child with overgrowth, whereas a loss-of-function mutation (C409Y) had been found in an individual with growth retardation. They suggested that mutations at codon arg300 presumably increase TGF-beta signaling, and that the short or tall stature seen in patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of the mutations on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) proposed that TGFB3 deficiency during early myogenesis might impair epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition of the dermomyotome. This would reduce the number of muscle precursor cells and cause hypomyoplasia with normal muscle architecture, a picture distinct from the myopathy caused by excess TGF-beta signaling in Marfan syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied aortic tissue from a patient with the TGFB3 D263H mutation by immunohistochemistry. They found evidence of paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling, with increased canonical (pSMAD2) and noncanonical (pERK) signaling and elevated TGFB1 mRNA. These findings resembled those in TGFB2-deficient aortic walls.

### Clinical Management

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported that a 3-year trial of losartan (up to 2.0 mg/kg/day), started at age 3 years in their patient, produced no change in muscle strength or mass. They suggested that this pointed to a pathophysiology distinct from that of mutations that enhance TGF-beta signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers, together with at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches. They suggested that additional imaging of the brain vessels might be indicated, depending on the family history.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.C409Y]

In a 9-year-old European-American girl with LDS5, Rienhoff et al. (2013) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution at a cysteine in the conserved cysteine knot. Her features included distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, hypertelorism, low muscle mass and growth retardation. The patient also carried a de novo CDH2 nonsense mutation, which the authors considered unlikely to drive the phenotype.

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, IVS4DS, T-C, +2 [c.754+2T>C; p.E216_K251del]

In affected members of a large Dutch family with LDS5 (family 1), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.754+2T-C splice site mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The family had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta, as well as mitral valve abnormalities and subtle craniofacial features. The mutation was identified after linkage analysis, segregated with the phenotype and was absent from variant databases. In 2 patients, cDNA sequencing showed skipping of exon 4, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids (glu216_lys251del). The mutation was also present in a 17-year-old family member who had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic manifestations.
