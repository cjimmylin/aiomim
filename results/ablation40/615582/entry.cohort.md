### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also called Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (190230) on chromosome 14q24.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is an autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder whose features overlap with those of Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. Affected individuals may have aortic aneurysm and dissection, mitral valve disease, craniofacial findings such as hypertelorism, bifid uvula and cleft palate, and skeletal findings such as tall stature, arachnodactyly and joint hypermobility. Severity varies widely within and between families, and some mutation carriers have no cardiovascular involvement (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015; Kuechler et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European-American girl born to healthy, nonconsanguineous parents who had 2 older healthy children and no previous miscarriages. From birth she had distal arthrogryposis, with contractures of the fingers and toes, as well as hypotonia, failure to thrive, growth retardation and delayed gross motor development. On examination she had decreased bulk in all appendicular and axial muscles, strength of 1/5, low tone and diminished reflexes throughout. She had markedly reduced subcutaneous fat, a positive Gower sign and a waddling gait. Craniofacial findings included bifid uvula, retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, blue sclerae, a metopic ridge, a tubular nose and a midline facial nevus flammeus. Skeletal and connective tissue findings included long narrow hands and feet with arachnodactyly, hyperextensible large joints, mild pectus excavatum, pes planus and coxa valga. Muscle biopsy showed type 1 fiber predominance. She had no cardiac defect or aortic dilatation, no intellectual disability and no scoliosis.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with features overlapping Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome and an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. Seven family members, aged 40 to 68 years, had aneurysms and dissections that mainly involved the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta, and 3 died from aortic dissection and rupture. Two had iliac and subclavian artery aneurysms. Four members had mitral valve abnormalities, ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation that required surgery. Craniofacial abnormalities were subtle and included a long face, high-arched palate and retrognathia. Some members had pectus deformity, scoliosis, velvety skin, varices, hiatal hernia, HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease or celiac disease, and 1 had easy bruising. In 2 individuals, aortic tissue showed elastic fiber fragmentation and medial degeneration. A 17-year-old mutation carrier had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic manifestations.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) also described 10 additional families (families 2 through 11) with TGFB3 mutations. Some patients had type A or type B aortic dissection, and 1 died from dissection of a cerebral aneurysm. Some had mitral valve disease, patent foramen ovale, or atrial or ventricular septal defects. Typical Loeys-Dietz syndrome findings were common, including hypertelorism, bifid uvula and cleft palate, cervical spine instability and clubfoot. Other recurrent features were dolichocephaly, high-arched palate, retrognathia, tall stature, joint hypermobility, arachnodactyly, pectus deformity and inguinal hernia. One patient had short stature, and 2 had early-onset osteoarthritis. The medical records showed no evidence of ectopia lentis. Aortic wall tissue showed elastic fiber fragmentation with deposition of collagen and proteoglycans.

Across the 11 families, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized 43 patients (23 males and 20 females), not all of whom were evaluated for every feature. Type A dissection occurred in 4 patients (median age 51 years), type B dissection in 6 (median age 44.5 years) and aortic aneurysm in 6. Skeletal findings included tall stature (12), arachnodactyly (16), pectus deformity (8), kyphoscoliosis (11) and joint hypermobility (9). Loeys-Dietz syndrome features included hypertelorism (14), bifid uvula (11) and cleft palate (5). The authors observed striking intra- and interfamilial variability. They found no early arterial dissection, no dissection at small aortic dimensions and no striking arterial tortuosity. Vascular involvement ranged from none at age 64 years to type A or B dissection and death from dissection of a cerebral aneurysm.

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a family in which a father and his 2 children were affected with a disorder overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. All 3 had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism and bifid uvula. None had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of LDS5 is autosomal dominant. In the large Dutch family reported by Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015), inheritance was consistent with an autosomal dominant pattern, and in the family reported by Kuechler et al. (2015), the mutation segregated from an affected father to 2 affected children. De novo heterozygous TGFB3 mutations were reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013) and Matyas et al. (2014), and by Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) in 1 family. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed linkage analysis in a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms. They identified 2 large regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15, that were shared by all affected members. The most prominent candidate in these regions was the TGFB3 gene on chromosome 14q24.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) used exome sequencing in a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, hypertelorism, low muscle mass and growth retardation. They identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutation was de novo, and it was absent from the parents and the 2 unaffected sibs. Cys409 is the penultimate cysteine of the cysteine knot; it is conserved in all TGF-beta family ligands and is essential for ligand activity. The mutant ligand did not activate a TGF-beta reporter in cultured cells. When coexpressed with wildtype TGFB3 in Xenopus embryos, the mutant reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signaling to about 40% and 60% of wildtype, respectively, consistent with a dominant-negative effect, but it did not affect BMP-induced SMAD1 phosphorylation. The authors concluded that the allele is hypomorphic. The patient also carried a de novo nonsense mutation in the CDH2 gene (Q70X), which the authors considered unlikely to drive the phenotype.

In a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3.

In the large Dutch family that they had studied by linkage analysis, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous splice site mutation in the TGFB3 gene (c.754+2T-C). The mutation causes skipping of exon 4 and an in-frame deletion (Glu216_Lys251del), and it segregated with the clinical phenotype. The authors then screened TGFB3 in 470 probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection. They identified heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 further probands: 4 different missense mutations, D263H (family 3), R300W (families 2, 4, 5 and 11), I322T (family 10) and L401P (family 8); a nonsense mutation, Y365X (family 6), which occurred de novo; and 2 single-base deletions causing a frameshift and premature stop codon, Leu386ArgfsTer21 (family 7) and Asn235MetfsTer11 (family 9). All of the mutations were absent from control databases.

Kuechler et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 of the 3 affected members of a family with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. They identified a heterozygous c.898C-G transversion in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and its segregation with the phenotype in the father and his 2 affected children. The authors noted that this mutation affected the same codon as a previously reported mutation (R300Q) in a child with overgrowth.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that TGFB3 mutations affecting residue arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

Kuechler et al. (2015) suggested that mutations at codon arg300 of TGFB3 presumably increase TGF-beta signaling. They proposed that the short or tall stature seen in patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of the mutations on TGF-beta signaling. As an illustration, they contrasted the loss-of-function mutation C409Y, found in an individual with growth retardation, with arg300 mutations, found in individuals with overgrowth or tall stature.

### Pathogenesis

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied aortic wall tissue from a patient with the D263H mutation and found increased pSMAD2, pERK and TGFB1 mRNA. This indicated paradoxically enhanced canonical and noncanonical TGF-beta signaling, even though the truncating and splice mutations suggested loss of function of TGFB3.

### Diagnosis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) found that their patient did not meet the diagnostic criteria for Marfan, Loeys-Dietz or Beals-Hecht syndrome. Features that distinguished her included significant growth retardation, the absence of cardiovascular findings and a muscle biopsy without dystrophic changes. No mutations were found in the TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1 or FBN2 genes.

### Clinical Management

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported that a 3-year trial of losartan in their patient produced no change in muscle strength or mass. They took this as further evidence that the pathophysiology differs from that of mutations that enhance TGF-beta signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers, with at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches. They suggested that brain vessel imaging may also be warranted, depending on the family history.

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.C409Y]

In a 9-year-old European-American girl with LDS5, Rienhoff et al. (2013) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution.
