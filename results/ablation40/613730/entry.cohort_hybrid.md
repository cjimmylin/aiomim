### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by congenital cataracts, severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, and subependymal calcification (Mochida et al., 2010). Akawi et al. (2013) described a catastrophic neonatal course, with death in the first weeks of life in most affected children.

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) reported a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates in which some members had a rare autosomal recessive syndrome characterized by severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The clinical presentation overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with patients who have mutations in occludin, another tight-junction component, but massive intracranial hemorrhage distinguished these patients from the others.

Summarizing the original family, Akawi et al. (2013) stated that the affected members were born with congenital cataracts and developed severe brain abnormalities from progressive hemorrhagic destruction of brain tissue, including the cerebral white matter and basal ganglia. Three of the 8 affected individuals died early in infancy. Brain imaging in all of the affected individuals showed calcification in the subependymal region, multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage with associated liquefaction, and massive cystic degeneration resulting in large ventricles.

Akawi et al. (2013) reported affected children from 3 families of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin. Consanguinity was present in 2 of the families. In family 1, the parents were Turkish second cousins. Their first child, a preterm girl, had intracranial bleeding and died in the neonatal period. The second child, a girl born at term with a birth weight of 2,480 g (less than 10th centile), was admitted on day 5 with tonic seizures that were resistant to treatment. Examination showed a head circumference of 30 cm, a sloping forehead, a prominent, bulging anterior fontanel, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Cranial ultrasound showed prominent expansive bleeding, and brain CT showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Serologic tests for toxoplasmosis, rubella, and cytomegalovirus were negative, as was CSF PCR for cytomegalovirus and toxoplasma, and platelet levels were normal. She developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus and died at 2 months of age.

Family 2 of Akawi et al. (2013) had healthy, nonconsanguineous parents of Afghani origin. Patient 1, a boy born at term with a birth weight of 2,480 g (less than 10th centile), had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth. At 2 weeks of age he presented with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. Brain MRI at 15 days showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities in both cerebral hemispheres, and multiple subependymal cysts. Renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys with echogenic cortex and altered corticomedullary differentiation. He had progressive neurologic deterioration and died at 2 weeks of age. Investigations, including coagulation studies with platelets, karyotype, and array analysis, were normal. His brother, patient 2, had bilateral cataracts at birth, with a birth weight of 3,315 g and a head circumference of 35 cm, and normal tone and reflexes. Cranial ultrasound initially showed periventricular echogenicity without bleeding. EEG on the third day of life showed bilateral epileptiform activity without clinical seizures. On day 5 he deteriorated, his hemoglobin fell from 180 g/L, and he required ventilation. Cranial ultrasound then showed extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage, and he died on the fifth day of life. Abdominal ultrasound showed pelvicalyceal dilatation of the right kidney. ⚠︎

In family 3 of Akawi et al. (2013), the parents were Moroccan first cousins. A maternal uncle had died on the seventh day of life without a diagnosis. The couple's first child, a boy born at 36 weeks' gestation, had cerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus and died at 10 days of age. A fourth pregnancy ended in a stillborn child with hydrocephalus whose cerebral tissue showed necrotic degeneration. The index patient, a girl, was delivered by cesarean section because of fetal tachycardia at 37 weeks. She had bilateral central and nuclear cataracts at birth. On the second day of life she presented with irritability, crying, and a bulging anterior fontanel. Brain ultrasound showed dilated lateral ventricles, intraventricular hemorrhage, and frontal periventricular cysts. Head CT showed multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, and diffusely hypodense brain parenchyma. MRI showed intraventricular and intraparenchymal hemorrhage, edema and cystic changes of the brain parenchyma, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. Circulation in the circle of Willis was normal. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus without clinical seizures. Platelet levels and metabolic screening were normal, but she had severe anemia. By day 18 her head circumference was 41 cm (+4 SD), and she died at home at 39 days of age.

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that all of the affected children in their report had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, with seizures in some. Neuroimaging showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with evidence of brain destruction and subependymal, basal ganglia, and white matter calcification. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement, which had been described in the original family, were more variable. Counting the original family, the authors stated that the disorder was lethal in 7 of 10 cases, with death in the first few weeks of life. The 3 survivors were severely retarded, with severe spasticity and seizures. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HDBSCC in the consanguineous family reported by Mochida et al. (2010) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Akawi et al. (2013) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance: affected children in 3 families were homozygous for JAM3 mutations, and their parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

By homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates, Mochida et al. (2010) mapped the disease locus to chromosome 11q25, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with HDBSCC, Mochida et al. (2010) sequenced genes in the candidate interval on chromosome 11q25 and identified a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which resulted in a frameshift with early termination.

In affected children from 3 families of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified 3 different homozygous mutations in the JAM3 gene by sequencing its coding exons: C219Y, E116K, and M1R. The mutations segregated with the disorder in each family. Parents were heterozygous, and the available unaffected sibs were either homozygous for the wildtype allele or heterozygous. None of the mutations was found in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project, and all affected highly conserved residues. In transfected HeLa cells, the C219Y mutant was retained predominantly in the endoplasmic reticulum, indicating a trafficking defect. In contrast, the E116K mutant localized to the plasma membrane like the wildtype protein. Because the affected children had similar phenotypes even though the mutations lay in different domains, the authors concluded that the disorder probably resulted from total loss of JAM3 function. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Mochida et al. (2010) noted that JAM3, a tight-junction protein, is present in vascular endothelium. They concluded that their results suggested JAM3 is essential for maintaining the integrity of the cerebrovascular endothelium and for normal lens development in humans.

### Diagnosis

Akawi et al. (2013) discussed the differential diagnosis. The syndrome of band-like intracranial calcification with simplified gyration and polymicrogyria, caused by mutation in another tight-junction gene, OCLN, lacks intracranial hemorrhage and congenital cataract, and their patients had no cortical malformation or polymicrogyria. COL4A1-related disorders can include antenatal intracranial hemorrhage and porencephaly, but they are autosomal dominant and differ clinically. Fowler syndrome, caused by mutation in FLVCR2, is characterized by proliferative vasculopathy and presents with fetal akinesia deformation sequence.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, IVS5DS, G-T, +1 [c.612+1G>T; c.747+1G>T; p.V205Lfs; p.V250LfsX26]

In affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with HDBSCC, Mochida et al. (2010) identified a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which led to a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) listed this mutation as c.612+1G-T (c.747+1G-T in the nomenclature based on NM_032801.3). It causes a 19-bp insertion that leads to a frameshift and a premature termination codon (p.V205Lfs).

**.0002 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, CYS219TYR [c.656G>A; p.C219Y]

In a Turkish girl, born to parents who were second cousins, with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.656G-A transition in exon 6 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution. Her parents were heterozygous. The mutation alters 1 of 4 highly conserved cysteine residues and disrupts 1 of the 2 disulfide bonds that stabilize the Ig-like folds. In transfected HeLa and COS7 cells, most of the mutant protein was retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and failed to reach the cell membrane.

**.0003 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, GLU116LYS [c.346G>A; p.E116K]

In 2 brothers, born to nonconsanguineous Afghani parents, with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.346G-A transition in exon 4 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution in the Ig-like V-type domain. The parents were heterozygous. In transfected HeLa cells, the mutant protein localized to the plasma membrane like the wildtype protein. The authors suggested that the mutation might interfere with dimerization or with interactions between JAM3 and its partners.

**.0004 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, MET1ARG [c.2T>G; p.M1R]

In a Moroccan girl, born to first-cousin parents, with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.2T-G transversion in the JAM3 gene, resulting in a met1-to-arg (M1R) substitution at the initiator codon. Her parents were heterozygous. The authors predicted that the mutation would interfere with translation and that use of an alternative in-frame ATG would encode a nonfunctional or unstable protein.
