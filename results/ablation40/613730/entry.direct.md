### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Affected individuals have congenital cataracts and severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, with subependymal calcification shown on brain imaging (Mochida et al., 2010). Many patients die in the first weeks of life, and those who survive have severe intellectual disability, spasticity, and seizures (Akawi et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) studied a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. Some members had a rare autosomal recessive syndrome of severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The clinical picture overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with cases caused by mutation in occludin. However, the massive intracranial hemorrhage set these patients apart from the others. Akawi et al. (2013) summarized this original family. The affected members were born with congenital cataracts and developed severe brain abnormalities from progressive hemorrhagic destruction of brain tissue, including the cerebral white matter and basal ganglia. Three of the 8 affected individuals died early in infancy. Brain imaging in all affected individuals showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage, with liquefaction and massive cystic degeneration leading to large ventricles. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement were also described.

Akawi et al. (2013) reported affected children from 3 families. The first family was Turkish; the unaffected parents were second cousins. Their first child was a preterm girl who had intracranial bleeding and died in the neonatal period. Their second child, a girl, was born at term with a birth weight of 2,480 g (less than the 10th centile). On day 5 she was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit with tonic seizures that did not respond to treatment. On examination she had a head circumference of 30 cm, a sloping forehead, a prominent bulging anterior fontanel, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Cranial ultrasound showed prominent expansive bleeding. Brain CT showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Serologic tests for toxoplasmosis, rubella, and cytomegalovirus were negative, as was CSF PCR for cytomegalovirus and toxoplasma. Platelet levels were normal. She developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus and died at 2 months of age.

The second family reported by Akawi et al. (2013) was of Afghani origin, and the healthy parents were not consanguineous. The first child, a boy, was born at term with a birth weight of 2,480 g (less than the 10th centile). He had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth. At 2 weeks of age he presented with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. Brain MRI at 15 days showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities in both cerebral hemispheres, and multiple subependymal cysts. Renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys with an echogenic cortex and altered corticomedullary differentiation. He deteriorated neurologically and died at 2 weeks of age. Extensive investigations were normal, including coagulation studies with platelets. His affected brother, the third child, was born at term with a birth weight of 3,315 g (25th centile) and a head circumference of 35 cm (50th centile). He had bilateral cataracts at birth. Cranial ultrasound first showed periventricular echogenicity without bleeding. He deteriorated on day 5, when his hemoglobin dropped from 180 g/L. EEG on the third day of life showed bilateral epileptiform activity. Cranial ultrasound then revealed extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage, and he died on the fifth day of life. Abdominal ultrasound showed pelvicalyceal dilatation of the right kidney.

The third family reported by Akawi et al. (2013) was of Moroccan origin, and the parents were first cousins. Their first child, a boy born at 36 weeks' gestation, had cerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus and died at 10 days of age. A fourth pregnancy ended in a stillbirth with hydrocephalus and necrotic degeneration of the cerebral tissues. The mother's brother had died on the seventh day of life without a diagnosis. The index patient, a girl, had a birth weight of 2,520 g and a head circumference of 34.5 cm (50th centile), and bilateral central and nuclear cataracts were present at birth. On the second day of life she presented with irritability, crying, and a bulging anterior fontanel. Head CT showed multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, and diffusely hypodense brain parenchyma. MRI showed intraventricular and intraparenchymal hemorrhage, edema and cystic changes of the brain parenchyma, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus, although she had no clinical seizures. Platelet levels were normal, and she had severe anemia. On day 18 her head circumference was 41 cm (+4 SD). She died at 39 days of age.

Akawi et al. (2013) found that the phenotype of their patients closely resembled that of the original family. All of their patients had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, and some had seizures. Neuroimaging in all of them showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with brain destruction, together with subependymal, basal ganglia, and white matter calcification. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement varied more within this group. Across the 10 cases, the disorder was lethal in 7, with death in the first few weeks of life. The 3 survivors had severe mental retardation with severe spasticity and seizures.

### Inheritance

Mochida et al. (2010) established autosomal recessive inheritance of this disorder in a large consanguineous family. Akawi et al. (2013) confirmed it: in 3 more families, affected individuals were homozygous for JAM3 mutations and their parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Mochida et al. (2010) performed homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. They mapped the disease locus to chromosome 11q25, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochida et al. (2010) sequenced genes in the candidate interval on chromosome 11q25 in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. Affected members were homozygous for a mutation in the canonical splice donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which caused a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) sequenced the coding exons of JAM3 in affected members of 3 families of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin. Each family had a different homozygous mutation: C219Y, E116K, and M1R. In each family the mutation segregated with the disease. Parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sibs who were available were either homozygous wildtype or heterozygous. None of the mutations was found in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. In HeLa cells, the C219Y mutant protein was retained mostly in the endoplasmic reticulum, which suggested a trafficking defect. The E116K mutant reached the plasma membrane normally, like the wildtype protein. The M1R mutation alters the initiator codon and was predicted to impair protein synthesis.

### Diagnosis

Akawi et al. (2013) discussed the differential diagnosis. The syndrome of band-like intracranial calcification, simplified gyration, and polymicrogyria, caused by mutation in OCLN, lacks intracranial hemorrhage and congenital cataract. Their patients also had no cortical malformation or polymicrogyria. COL4A1-related disorders can present in overlapping ways, but they are autosomal dominant and their clinical presentation is different. Proliferative vasculopathy and hydranencephaly-hydrocephaly syndrome (Fowler syndrome), caused by mutation in FLVCR2, presents with fetal akinesia deformation sequence, which also distinguishes it.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, IVS5DS, G-T, +1 [c.747+1G>T; c.612+1G>T; p.V250LfsX26; p.V205Lfs]

Mochida et al. (2010) studied affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. They were homozygous for a mutation in the canonical splice donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing that led to a frameshift with early termination. Akawi et al. (2013) listed this mutation as c.747+1G-T (c.612+1G-T in NM_032801.4). They reported that it causes a 19-bp insertion, a frameshift, and a premature termination codon.

**.0002 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, CYS219TYR [c.656G>A; p.C219Y]

Akawi et al. (2013) studied an affected girl born to Turkish parents who were second cousins. She was homozygous for a c.656G-A transition in exon 6 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution in the Ig-like C2-type domain. The mutation was found by sequencing and segregated with the disease in the family. Cys219 is predicted to take part in one of the 2 intrachain disulfide bonds that stabilize the Ig folds. In transfected HeLa cells, the mutant protein was retained mostly in the endoplasmic reticulum and did not reach the plasma membrane.

**.0003 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, GLU116LYS [c.346G>A; p.E116K]

Akawi et al. (2013) studied 2 affected brothers born to nonconsanguineous Afghani parents. Both were homozygous for a c.346G-A transition in exon 4 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution in the Ig-like V-type domain. The mutation segregated with the disease in the family. The mutant protein localized normally to the plasma membrane in HeLa cells. The authors suggested that the mutation might disrupt dimerization or interaction with partner proteins.

**.0004 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, MET1ARG [c.2T>G; p.M1R]

Akawi et al. (2013) studied an affected girl born to Moroccan parents who were first cousins. She was homozygous for a c.2T-G transversion in the JAM3 gene that changes the initiator methionine to arginine (M1R). The mutation segregated with the disease in the family and was predicted to impair translation of the protein.
