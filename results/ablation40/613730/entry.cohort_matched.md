### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a rare autosomal recessive syndrome. Its cardinal features are severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts (Mochida et al., 2010). Akawi et al. (2013) noted that affected children had progressive neurologic impairment, with seizures in some. The disorder was usually lethal within the first weeks of life, and survivors were severely retarded with severe spasticity and seizures.

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) studied a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. Some members of the family had a rare autosomal recessive syndrome characterized by severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The authors noted that the clinical presentation overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with patients who have occludin mutations. They stated that massive intracranial hemorrhage distinguished their patients from these others.

Akawi et al. (2013) reported a Turkish girl (family 1, II2) whose parents were second cousins. Her birth weight was below the 10th centile. On day 5 she developed tonic seizures that did not respond to treatment. Examination showed a head circumference of 30 cm, a sloping forehead, a prominent and bulging anterior fontanelle, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Neuroimaging showed subependymal calcification, multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage, and posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus. She did not have thrombocytopenia. She died at 2 months of age. ⚠︎

Akawi et al. (2013) also reported 2 brothers born to healthy, nonconsanguineous parents of Afghani origin (family 2). The first (II1) was born at term by emergency cesarean section and weighed 2,480 g (below the 10th centile). He had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth, along with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. Brain MRI at 15 days of age showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities in both cerebral hemispheres, multiple subependymal cysts, and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages. Renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys with an echogenic cortex and altered corticomedullary differentiation. Coagulation and platelets were normal. He deteriorated neurologically and died at 2 weeks of age. His brother (II3) had bilateral cataracts at birth. His tone and reflexes were normal and he had no clinical seizures. Cranial ultrasound initially showed periventricular echogenicity without a bleed. EEG on the third day of life showed bilateral epileptiform activity. On day 5 he deteriorated: his hemoglobin dropped from 180 g/L to 96 g/L, he required ventilation, and cranial ultrasound showed extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage. He died on the fifth day of life. Abdominal ultrasound showed pelvicalyceal dilatation of the right kidney.

The third family reported by Akawi et al. (2013) was of Moroccan origin. The parents were first cousins and had had a total of 5 pregnancies. The index patient (II5), a girl, had fetal tachycardia. Bilateral central and nuclear cataracts were found at birth. On the second day of life she presented with irritability, crying, and a bulging anterior fontanelle. Head CT showed multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, and diffusely hypodense brain parenchyma. Brain MRI showed intraventricular and intraparenchymal hemorrhage, edema and cystic changes of the brain parenchyma, frontal periventricular cysts, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus. She had severe anemia without thrombocytopenia. At day 18 her head circumference was 41 cm (+4 SD) and her scalp veins were prominent. She died at 39 days of age. ⚠︎

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that all the affected children had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, and some had seizures. Neuroimaging showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with brain destruction and calcification in the subependymal region, basal ganglia, and white matter. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement were more variable. Counting the original family, the disorder was lethal within the first weeks of life in 7 of 10 cases. The 3 survivors were severely retarded with severe spasticity and seizures. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Mochida et al. (2010) described the syndrome as autosomal recessive in a large consanguineous family in which affected members were homozygous for a JAM3 mutation. Akawi et al. (2013) found the same pattern in 3 further families: affected children were homozygous for JAM3 mutations, their parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were either homozygous wildtype or heterozygous.

### Mapping

Mochida et al. (2010) used homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates to map the disease locus to chromosome 11q25. The maximum multipoint lod score was 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochida et al. (2010) sequenced genes in the candidate interval in affected members of a consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. They found a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice-donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which caused a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) identified 3 novel homozygous JAM3 mutations in affected members of 3 families of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin: C219Y, E116K, and M1R. The mutations segregated with the disease. Parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sibs who were available were homozygous wildtype or heterozygous. None of the mutations were found in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project, and all affected highly conserved residues.

### Pathogenesis

Mochida et al. (2010) concluded that their results suggested JAM3 is essential for maintaining the integrity of the cerebrovascular endothelium and for normal lens development in humans.

Akawi et al. (2013) expressed mutant JAM3 in transfected HeLa cells. The C219Y mutant, which disrupts one of the disulfide bonds that stabilize the Ig-like domains, was largely retained in the endoplasmic reticulum, indicating a trafficking defect. The E116K mutant localized normally to the plasma membrane, and the authors suggested that it impairs dimerization or interaction with partner proteins. They predicted that the M1R mutation, which alters the initiator methionine, would interfere with translation, and that use of an alternative in-frame ATG would yield a nonfunctional or unstable protein. Because the phenotypes were similar even though the mutations lay in different domains, they concluded that the disorder likely reflects total loss of JAM3 function.

### Diagnosis

Akawi et al. (2013) distinguished the disorder from 3 others. OCLN-related band-like intracranial calcification with polymicrogyria lacks intracranial hemorrhage and congenital cataract. COL4A1-related disorders are autosomal dominant. Fowler syndrome (FLVCR2) presents with fetal akinesia and proliferative vasculopathy.
