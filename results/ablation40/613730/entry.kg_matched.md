### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts (Mochida et al., 2010). Affected infants have bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment. Most die within the first few weeks of life, and the few who survive are severely retarded, with severe spasticity and seizures (Akawi et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) described a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates in which some members had a rare autosomal recessive syndrome. Its main features were severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The clinical presentation overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with cases involving occludin mutations. However, massive intracranial hemorrhage set these patients apart from the others.

Akawi et al. (2013) described a Turkish girl (family 1) whose parents were second cousins. She was born at term with a birth weight of 2480 g (below the 10th centile) and was admitted on day 5 with treatment-resistant tonic seizures. On examination she had a head circumference of 30 cm, a sloping forehead, a bulging anterior fontanel, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Brain CT showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. TORCH studies and platelet levels were normal. She developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus and died at 2 months of age. An older sister, born preterm, had intracranial bleeding and died in the neonatal period. ⚠︎

Akawi et al. (2013) described 2 affected brothers (family 2) born to healthy, nonconsanguineous parents of Afghan origin. Patient 1 had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth. At 2 weeks of age he presented with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. MRI showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities, and multiple subependymal cysts, and renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys. He died at 2 weeks of age; an extensive workup, including platelet and coagulation studies, had been normal. Patient 2 had bilateral cataracts at birth. EEG on day 3 showed bilateral epileptiform activity. On day 5 he deteriorated, with a drop in hemoglobin and extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage, and he died that day. Abdominal ultrasound showed right renal pelvicalyceal dilatation. ⚠︎

Akawi et al. (2013) described a Moroccan girl (family 3) whose parents were first cousins. She had bilateral central and nuclear cataracts at birth, and on day 2 she presented with irritability and a bulging anterior fontanel. CT and MRI showed multifocal intraparenchymal and intraventricular hemorrhage, subependymal calcification, edema, cystic changes of the brain parenchyma, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus without clinical seizures, and she had severe anemia. Her head circumference reached 41 cm (+4 SD) by day 18, and she died at 39 days. An older brother had cerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus and died at 10 days. Another pregnancy ended in a stillbirth with hydrocephalus and necrotic degeneration of the cerebral tissue.

Akawi et al. (2013) found that their patients closely resembled the original UAE family. All had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, and some had seizures. Neuroimaging showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with brain destruction, as well as subependymal, basal ganglia, and white matter calcification. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement were more variable. Across both reports the disorder was fatal in 7 of 10 cases, with death in the first few weeks of life. The 3 survivors were severely retarded, with severe spasticity and seizures. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Mochida et al. (2010) reported autosomal recessive inheritance of the syndrome in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates.

In 3 families of Turkish, Afghan, and Moroccan origin, Akawi et al. (2013) found that homozygous JAM3 mutations segregated with the disorder. The parents were heterozygous carriers, and the unaffected sibs who could be tested were either homozygous wildtype or heterozygous. These findings were consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Two of the 3 families were consanguineous.

### Mapping

Mochida et al. (2010) used homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates to map the disease locus to chromosome 11q25 (maximum multipoint lod score of 6.15).

### Molecular Genetics

Mochida et al. (2010) studied affected members of a consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. By sequencing genes in the 11q25 candidate interval, they identified a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice-donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which caused a frameshift and early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) found homozygous mutations in the JAM3 gene in affected members of 3 families. In the Turkish patient (family 1), sequencing identified a homozygous c.656G-A transition that results in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution. In 2 Afghan brothers (family 2), they found a homozygous c.346G-A transition that results in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution. In the Moroccan patient (family 3), they found a homozygous c.2T-G transversion that results in a met1-to-arg (M1R) substitution. None of the variants was present in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project, and each segregated with the disorder in its family. When expressed in HeLa and COS7 cells, the C219Y mutant protein was largely retained in the endoplasmic reticulum. In contrast, the E116K mutant protein reached the plasma membrane normally.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that the JAM3 mutations were spread across the coding sequence and affected different domains, yet the affected children had similar phenotypes. They concluded that the disorder is homogeneous at the clinical and molecular level, probably because the mutations cause total loss of protein function.

### Pathogenesis

Mochida et al. (2010) noted that JAM3, a tight-junction protein, is known to be present in vascular endothelium. They suggested that JAM3 is essential in humans for maintaining the integrity of the cerebrovascular endothelium and for normal lens development.

### Diagnosis

Akawi et al. (2013) distinguished this disorder from 3 others. The first was band-like intracranial calcification with simplified gyration and polymicrogyria due to OCLN mutations, which lacks intracranial hemorrhage and congenital cataract. The second was the group of autosomal dominant COL4A1-related disorders. The third was Fowler syndrome (proliferative vasculopathy and hydranencephaly-hydrocephaly due to FLVCR2 mutations), which presents with fetal akinesia deformation sequence. None of their patients had a cortical malformation or polymicrogyria.

**.0002 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, CYS219TYR [c.656G>A; p.C219Y]

In a Turkish girl with HDBSCC whose parents were second cousins, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.656G-A transition in exon 6 of the JAM3 gene. The mutation results in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution at a conserved cysteine in the Ig-like C2-type domain that is predicted to form a disulfide bond. The mutation segregated with the disorder, the parents were heterozygous, and it was not present in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. Flag-tagged C219Y protein expressed in HeLa and COS7 cells was largely retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and did not reach the plasma membrane, suggesting a trafficking defect.

**.0003 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, GLU116LYS [c.346G>A; p.E116K]

In 2 brothers with HDBSCC born to nonconsanguineous parents of Afghan origin, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.346G-A transition in exon 4 of the JAM3 gene. The mutation results in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution at a conserved residue in the Ig-like V-type domain. It was absent from dbSNP and the 1000 Genomes Project and segregated with the disorder. In HeLa cells the mutant protein reached the plasma membrane normally, like wildtype protein. The authors suggested that the variant may interfere with dimerization or with binding to interacting partners.

**.0004 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, MET1ARG [c.2T>G; p.M1R]

In a Moroccan girl with HDBSCC whose parents were first cousins, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.2T-G transversion in the JAM3 gene. The mutation changes the initiator methionine to arginine (M1R) and was predicted to disrupt translation of the protein. It was absent from dbSNP and the 1000 Genomes Project and segregated with the disorder, and the parents were heterozygous.
