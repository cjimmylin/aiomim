### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its features are congenital cataracts, severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, and subependymal calcification (Mochida et al., 2010). Akawi et al. (2013) described a distinctive, catastrophic neonatal presentation. Most affected children died in the first few weeks of life, and the survivors were severely retarded with spasticity and seizures.

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) described a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates in which some members had a rare autosomal recessive syndrome characterized by severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The clinical presentation overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with cases involving mutations in occludin, another component of the tight-junction complex. Massive intracranial hemorrhage distinguished these patients from the others.

Akawi et al. (2013) described affected children from 3 families of different ethnic backgrounds (Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan); 2 of the families were consanguineous. In family 1, the parents were Turkish second cousins. The proband was a girl born at term after a normal pregnancy, with a birth weight of 2480 g (less than 10th centile). She was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit on day 5 with tonic seizures that were resistant to treatment. Examination showed a head circumference of 30 cm, a sloping forehead, a prominent bulging anterior fontanel, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Cranial ultrasound showed prominent expansive bleeding, and brain CT showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Serologic tests for toxoplasmosis, rubella, and cytomegalovirus were negative, as was cerebrospinal fluid PCR for cytomegalovirus and toxoplasma. Platelet levels were normal. She developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus and died at 2 months of age. Her older sister, born preterm, had intracranial bleeding and died in the neonatal period.

Family 2 reported by Akawi et al. (2013) had healthy, nonconsanguineous parents of Afghani origin and 2 affected boys. Patient 1 was born at term with a birth weight of 2480 g (less than 10th centile). He had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth. At 2 weeks of age he presented with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. Brain MRI at 15 days showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities in both cerebral hemispheres, and multiple subependymal cysts. Renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys with an echogenic cortex and altered corticomedullary differentiation. He deteriorated neurologically and died at 2 weeks of age. Investigations, including coagulation studies and platelets, were normal. His brother (patient 2) was born at term with normal growth parameters and had bilateral cataracts at birth. Cranial ultrasound initially showed periventricular echogenicity without a bleed. On day 5 he deteriorated, and his hemoglobin dropped from 180 g/L to 96 g/L. EEG on the third day of life showed bilateral epileptiform activity, and cranial ultrasound revealed extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage. He died on the fifth day of life. Abdominal ultrasound showed pelvicalyceal dilatation of the right kidney.

In family 3 of Akawi et al. (2013), the parents were Moroccan first cousins. The index patient was a girl delivered by cesarean section at 37 weeks' gestation because of fetal tachycardia. She had bilateral central and nuclear cataracts at birth. On the second day of life she presented with irritability, crying, and a bulging anterior fontanel. Brain ultrasound showed dilated lateral ventricles, intraventricular hemorrhage, and frontal periventricular cysts. Head CT showed multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, and diffusely hypodense brain parenchyma. Brain MRI showed intraventricular and intraparenchymal hemorrhage, edema, cystic changes of the brain parenchyma, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus, although she had no clinical seizures. She had severe anemia, and platelet levels and metabolic screening were normal. By day 18 her head circumference was 41 cm (+4 SD), and she died at 39 days of age. Her older brother had cerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus and died at 10 days of age. A fourth pregnancy ended in a stillborn child with hydrocephalus and necrotic degeneration of the cerebral tissues. A maternal uncle had died on the seventh day of life without a known diagnosis.

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that their patients' phenotype was very similar to that of the original family. All affected children had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, with seizures in some. Neuroimaging in all patients showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with evidence of brain destruction and subependymal, basal ganglia, and white matter calcification. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement were more variable. The disorder was lethal in 7 of the 10 cases, with death in the first few weeks of life, and the 3 survivors were severely retarded with severe spasticity and seizures.

### Inheritance

Mochida et al. (2010) found that the disorder showed autosomal recessive inheritance in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. Akawi et al. (2013) found that homozygous JAM3 mutations segregated with the disorder in 3 additional families, 2 of them consanguineous. The parents were heterozygous carriers, and the available unaffected sibs were either homozygous wildtype or heterozygous.

### Mapping

By homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates, Mochida et al. (2010) mapped the disease locus to chromosome 11q25, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochida et al. (2010) sequenced genes in the 11q25 candidate interval in affected members of a consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. They identified a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice-donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which led to a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) sequenced the JAM3 gene in affected members of 3 families and found 3 different homozygous mutations: C219Y in a Turkish family, E116K in an Afghani family, and M1R in a Moroccan family. The mutations were not reported in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. All 3 affected highly conserved amino acids and were predicted to be disease-causing by PolyPhen-2, SIFT, and MutationTaster. Each mutation segregated with the disorder in its family. When expressed in HeLa cells, the C219Y mutant protein was largely retained in the endoplasmic reticulum, suggesting a trafficking defect. In contrast, the E116K mutant reached the plasma membrane normally, like the wildtype protein.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that the JAM3 mutations were scattered throughout the coding sequence and affected different domains, yet the affected children had similar phenotypes. They concluded that the disorder is homogeneous at the clinical and molecular level, probably because the mutations cause total loss of protein function.

### Pathogenesis

Mochida et al. (2010) noted that JAM3, a tight-junction protein, is known to be present in vascular endothelium. They suggested that JAM3 is essential for maintaining the integrity of the cerebrovascular endothelium and for normal lens development in humans.

### Diagnosis

Akawi et al. (2013) discussed the differential diagnosis. The syndrome of band-like intracranial calcification, simplified gyration, and polymicrogyria, caused by mutations in OCLN, lacks intracranial hemorrhage and congenital cataract, and their patients showed no malformation of the cerebral cortex or polymicrogyria. COL4A1-related disorders can overlap clinically but are inherited as autosomal dominant traits and present differently. Proliferative vasculopathy and hydranencephaly-hydrocephaly syndrome (Fowler syndrome), caused by mutations in FLVCR2, presents with fetal akinesia deformation sequence.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, IVS5DS, G-T, +1 [c.612+1G>T; c.747+1G>T; p.V205Lfs; p.V250LfsX26]

In affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC), Mochida et al. (2010) identified a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice-donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, which led to a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) listed this mutation as c.612+1G-T, or c.747+1G-T in the NM_032801.3-based nomenclature used by Mochida et al. (2010). They described it as causing a 19-bp insertion that leads to a frameshift and a premature termination codon (p.V205Lfs; p.V250LfsX26).

**.0002 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, CYS219TYR [c.656G>A; p.C219Y]

In a Turkish girl with HDBSCC whose parents were second cousins, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.656G-A transition in exon 6 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution. The affected residue is one of 4 highly conserved cysteines and is predicted to form a disulfide bond in the Ig-like C2-type domain. The parents were heterozygous, and the mutation was not reported in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. When expressed in HeLa and COS7 cells, the Flag-tagged C219Y mutant was largely retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and failed to reach the plasma membrane, suggesting a trafficking defect.

**.0003 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, GLU116LYS [c.346G>A; p.E116K]

In a family of Afghani origin with nonconsanguineous parents, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.346G-A transition in exon 4 of the JAM3 gene in a child with HDBSCC. The mutation results in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the Ig-like V-type domain. It segregated with the disorder in the family and was not reported in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. In HeLa cells the mutant protein localized to the plasma membrane, like wildtype. The authors suggested that the mutation may interfere with dimerization or with interactions with partner proteins.

**.0004 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, MET1ARG [c.2T>G; p.M1R]

In a Moroccan girl with HDBSCC whose parents were first cousins, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.2T-G transversion in the JAM3 gene. The mutation changes the initiator methionine to arginine (M1R) and is predicted to impair protein synthesis. The parents were heterozygous, and the mutation was not reported in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project.
