### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its features are congenital cataracts and severe intracranial hemorrhage with destruction of the brain and subependymal calcification (Mochida et al., 2010). Most affected children die in the first weeks of life. Those who survive have severe mental retardation, severe spasticity, and seizures (Akawi et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) described members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates who had a rare autosomal recessive syndrome. Its features were severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The presentation overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with patients who have occludin mutations. However, massive intracranial hemorrhage set these patients apart.

Akawi et al. (2013) described a Turkish girl whose parents were second cousins. She was born at term weighing 2480 g (below the 10th centile). On day 5 she was admitted with tonic seizures that did not respond to treatment. Examination showed a 30-cm head circumference, a sloping forehead, a bulging anterior fontanel, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Brain CT showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Infection serologies and platelet levels were normal. She developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus and died at 2 months of age. An older sister, born preterm, had intracranial bleeding and died in the neonatal period.

Akawi et al. (2013) also described 2 affected brothers of Afghani origin whose parents were healthy and nonconsanguineous. The first brother had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth. At 2 weeks he presented with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. MRI at 15 days showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities, and multiple subependymal cysts. Renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys. Extensive metabolic, genetic, and coagulation investigations were normal, and he died at 2 weeks. The second brother had bilateral cataracts at birth. On day 5 he deteriorated, with a fall in hemoglobin, bilateral epileptiform activity on EEG, and extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage. He died on the fifth day of life. He also had pelvicalyceal dilatation of the right kidney.

Akawi et al. (2013) further described a girl whose parents were Moroccan first cousins. She had central and nuclear bilateral cataracts at birth. On day 2 she presented with irritability and a bulging fontanel. CT and MRI showed multifocal intraparenchymal and intraventricular hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, diffuse brain edema, cystic changes, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus without clinical seizures, and she had severe anemia. Her head circumference reached 41 cm (+4 SD), and she died at 39 days. One sib died at 10 days with cerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus. Another sib was stillborn with hydrocephalus and necrotic cerebral tissue.

Akawi et al. (2013) summarized the phenotype shared across these families. All affected children had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, and some had seizures. In all patients, neuroimaging showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with brain destruction and calcification in the subependymal region, basal ganglia, and white matter. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement were more variable. Including the original family, the disorder was lethal within the first weeks of life in 7 of 10 cases. The 3 survivors were severely retarded, with severe spasticity and seizures. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Mochida et al. (2010) reported that the disorder in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates followed an autosomal recessive pattern. Akawi et al. (2013) found that in 3 further families the mutations segregated with the disorder in a pattern consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Parents were heterozygous carriers, and unaffected sibs were either homozygous wildtype or heterozygous.

### Mapping

Mochida et al. (2010) used homozygosity mapping in the consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates to map the disease locus to chromosome 11q25. The maximum multipoint lod score was 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochida et al. (2010) sequenced genes in the 11q25 candidate interval in the consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. Affected members were homozygous for a mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) at the canonical splice-donor site of intron 5. RT-PCR analysis of a lymphoblast cell line from a patient confirmed abnormal splicing, which leads to a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) used sequencing to identify homozygous mutations in the JAM3 gene in 3 families. Each mutation segregated with the disorder and was absent from dbSNP and the 1000 Genomes Project. The Turkish girl from family 1 was homozygous for a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) mutation in the Ig-like C2-type domain. In transfected HeLa cells, this mutant protein was retained mostly in the endoplasmic reticulum. The 2 Afghani brothers from family 2 were homozygous for a glu116-to-lys (E116K) mutation in the Ig-like V-type domain. This mutant protein reached the plasma membrane normally in HeLa cells. The Moroccan family (family 3) carried a homozygous mutation that changes the initiator methionine to arginine (M1R).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that the missense mutations affected different domains of JAM3, yet the affected children had similar phenotypes. They suggested that this clinical homogeneity is likely due to total loss of protein function.

### Pathogenesis

Mochida et al. (2010) noted that JAM3, a tight-junction protein, is known to be present in vascular endothelium. They suggested that in humans JAM3 is essential for maintaining the integrity of the cerebrovascular endothelium and for normal lens development.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, IVS5DS

Mochida et al. (2010) studied affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC). In these patients they identified a homozygous mutation at the canonical splice-donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. The disease locus had been mapped to chromosome 11q25 by homozygosity mapping, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 6.15. RT-PCR analysis of a lymphoblast cell line from a patient confirmed abnormal splicing, which leads to a frameshift with early termination.

**.0002 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, CYS219TYR [c.656G>A; p.C219Y]

Akawi et al. (2013) studied a Turkish girl with HDBSCC whose parents were second cousins (family 1). By sequencing they found a homozygous c.656G-A transition in exon 6 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution in the Ig-like C2-type domain. The mutation segregated with the disorder, affected a conserved residue, and was absent from dbSNP and the 1000 Genomes Project. In transfected HeLa cells, the C219Y mutant was retained mostly in the endoplasmic reticulum rather than reaching the plasma membrane. The authors suggested that loss of a disulfide bond causes misfolding and a trafficking defect.

**.0003 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, GLU116LYS [c.346G>A; p.E116K]

Akawi et al. (2013) studied 2 Afghani brothers with HDBSCC whose parents were nonconsanguineous (family 2). Both brothers were homozygous for a c.346G-A transition in exon 4 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution in the Ig-like V-type domain. The mutation segregated with the disorder and was absent from dbSNP and the 1000 Genomes Project. The E116K mutant reached the plasma membrane normally in HeLa cells. The authors suggested that it may disrupt dimerization or interaction with partner proteins.

**.0004 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, MET1ARG [c.2T>G; p.M1R]

Akawi et al. (2013) studied a Moroccan family with HDBSCC in which the parents were first cousins (family 3). They identified a homozygous c.2T-G transversion in the JAM3 gene that changes the initiator methionine to arginine (M1R). The mutation segregated with the disorder and was absent from dbSNP and the 1000 Genomes Project. The authors predicted that it impairs protein synthesis.
