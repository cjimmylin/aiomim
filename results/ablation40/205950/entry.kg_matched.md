### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive pyridoxine-refractory sideroblastic anemia-2 (SIDBA2) is caused by mutation in the SLC25A38 gene, which encodes an erythroid-specific mitochondrial carrier family protein. ⚠︎

### Description

Guernsey et al. (2009) noted that the sideroblastic anemias are a heterogeneous group of congenital and acquired blood disorders. Their defining morphologic feature is ringed sideroblasts. These are bone marrow erythroid precursors with abnormal iron deposits inside the mitochondria. The form described in this entry is congenital, does not respond to pyridoxine, and shows autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Clinical Features

Manabe et al. (1982) described a female infant with congenital refractory sideroblastic anemia that did not respond to pyridoxine therapy.

Jardine et al. (1994) described a brother and sister, born to unaffected parents, who had transfusion-dependent, pyridoxine-refractory sideroblastic anemia from birth. Their hemoglobin levels were 4.3 and 6.4 g/dl, respectively.

### Biochemical Features

In erythroblasts from a female infant with congenital sideroblastic anemia, Manabe et al. (1982) found a marked reduction of delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) synthetase activity, both with and without pyridoxal phosphate treatment. Erythroblast mitochondrial neutral protease activity was normal. This protease specifically inactivates the apo form of ALA synthetase. The sensitivity of apo-ALA synthetase to the protease was also normal. The authors speculated that the reduced ALA synthetase activity was due to impaired production rather than accelerated destruction of the enzyme, which could explain why the patient did not respond to pyridoxine.

In contrast, Jardine et al. (1994) found normal delta-aminolevulinate synthase activity in erythroblasts from fractionated marrow of the affected sister in their family. The activity was 135 pmol delta-aminolevulinate formed/10(6) erythroblasts/hour, within the normal range of 110-650 pmol.

### Inheritance

Jardine et al. (1994) found no dimorphic erythrocyte population in the mother of affected brother and sister sibs. Her peripheral leukocytes showed random X inactivation. These findings indicated that she was not a carrier of an X-linked sideroblastic anemia mutation. Together with the exclusion of ALAS2, the authors concluded that the sideroblastic anemia in this family was strongly suggested to be an autosomal recessive trait. Guernsey et al. (2009) identified an autosomal recessive form of nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia.

### Mapping

Jardine et al. (1994) used an intragenic ALAS2 polymorphism, a G-to-A transition 220 nt 3-prime of the AATAAA polyadenylation signal. The polymorphism had a gene frequency of 25% among 148 alleles typed by mismatch PCR and XmnI digestion. The affected sibs had received different maternal alleles at the ALAS2 locus, which excluded ALAS2 mutations as the cause of their sideroblastic anemia.

### Heterogeneity

Jardine et al. (1994) noted that ALAS2 mutations at Xp11.21 had been reported in X-linked sideroblastic anemia. They excluded ALAS2 in their family with presumed autosomal recessive congenital sideroblastic anemia, which indicated genetic heterogeneity. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In the affected son of the family described by Jardine et al. (1994), sequence analysis of the ALAS2 coding region, intron/exon boundaries, and 1-kb 5-prime promoter region found no mutations.

Using positional cloning, Guernsey et al. (2009) identified a previously unknown form of autosomal recessive nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia. It was associated with mutations in SLC25A38, the gene encoding an erythroid-specific mitochondrial carrier family protein.

### Pathogenesis

Guernsey et al. (2009) showed that SLC25A38 is important for heme biosynthesis in eukaryotes.
