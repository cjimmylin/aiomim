### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive congenital sideroblastic anemia-2 (SIDBA2) is caused by mutation in the SLC25A38 gene.

### Description

Guernsey et al. (2009) noted that the sideroblastic anemias are a heterogeneous group of congenital and acquired hematologic disorders. Their morphologic hallmark is ringed sideroblasts, which are bone marrow erythroid precursors containing pathologic iron deposits within mitochondria. Pyridoxine-refractory congenital sideroblastic anemia has been described as transfusion-dependent anemia present from birth that does not respond to pyridoxine (Jardine et al., 1994).

### Clinical Features

Manabe et al. (1982) described a female infant with congenital refractory sideroblastic anemia that did not respond to pyridoxine therapy.

Jardine et al. (1994) described a brother and sister, born to unaffected parents, who had transfusion-dependent, pyridoxine-refractory sideroblastic anemia from birth. Their hemoglobin levels were 4.3 and 6.4 g/dl, respectively.

### Biochemical Features

In the female infant reported by Manabe et al. (1982), erythroblast delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) synthetase activity was markedly reduced, both with and without pyridoxal phosphate treatment. Mitochondrial neutral protease activity of erythroblasts, which specifically inactivates the apo form of ALA synthetase, was normal. The sensitivity of apo-ALA synthetase to the neutral protease was also normal. The authors speculated that the reduced ALA synthetase activity was caused by impaired production of the enzyme rather than rapid destruction, and that this could explain the lack of response to pyridoxine therapy.

Jardine et al. (1994) measured delta-aminolevulinate synthase activity in erythroblasts from fractionated marrow of the affected sister. The value was 135 pmol delta-aminolevulinate formed/10(6) erythroblasts/hour, against a normal range of 110-650 pmol.

### Inheritance

Jardine et al. (1994) reported 2 affected sibs of opposite sex born to unaffected parents. Their mother had no dimorphic erythrocyte population and showed random X inactivation in her peripheral leukocytes, which showed that she did not carry any X-linked sideroblastic anemia mutation. Together with their exclusion of the ALAS2 locus, these findings strongly suggested to the authors that the sideroblastic anemia in this family was an autosomal recessive trait. Guernsey et al. (2009) found that mutations in SLC25A38 cause a form of autosomal recessive nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia.

### Heterogeneity

Jardine et al. (1994) noted that mutations in the erythroid-specific delta-aminolevulinate synthase gene (ALAS2) at Xp11.21 had been reported in patients with X-linked sideroblastic anemia. In the son of their family, sequencing found no mutation in the ALAS2 coding region, the intron/exon boundaries, or the 1-kb 5-prime promoter region. They identified a G-to-A transition located 220 nt 3-prime of the AATAAA polyadenylation signal in the 3-prime region of ALAS2. Genotyping of 148 alleles by mismatch PCR and XmnI restriction analysis gave this polymorphism a gene frequency of 25%. Analysis of how this intragenic polymorphism was inherited showed that the affected sibs had received different maternal alleles at the ALAS2 locus. This excluded ALAS2 mutations as the cause of their sideroblastic anemia.

### Molecular Genetics

Using positional cloning, Guernsey et al. (2009) defined a previously unknown form of autosomal recessive nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia. They associated it with mutations in the SLC25A38 gene, which encodes an erythroid-specific mitochondrial carrier family protein.

### Pathogenesis

Guernsey et al. (2009) demonstrated that SLC25A38 is important for heme biosynthesis in eukaryotes.
