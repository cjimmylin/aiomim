### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene (607429) on chromosome 10p12.

### Description

Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is an autosomal recessive disorder of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) biosynthesis caused by mutation in the PDSS1 gene. Reported features include bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, optic atrophy with reduced vision, mild intellectual disability or developmental delay, and cardiac involvement such as valvulopathy, pulmonary hypertension or heart failure. Disease severity ranged from a progressive multisystem disorder in infancy that ended in death in early childhood to survival into adolescence. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) reported a family (family 1) with CoQ10 deficiency. Quinone-dependent oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activities were in the range of the lowest control values, while OXPHOS enzyme activities were normal.

Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported 2 sisters born to consanguineous Egyptian parents. The proband (II:1), a 16-year-old girl born after an uneventful pregnancy, had severe language delay in early childhood. At age 4 years she was found to have profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, which was treated with an externally powered prosthesis. She also had difficulty with verbal comprehension, mild intellectual disability, and progressive visual loss. Visual acuity was 1/10 in both eyes, and she had red-green dyschromatopsia without a relative afferent pupillary defect. She had optic atrophy, mainly in the inferior temporal sector. Visual evoked potentials showed increased latency and reduced amplitude of P100, and optical coherence tomography showed reduced thickness of the retinal nerve fiber layer. Other findings were diffuse mild muscle weakness and mild postural tremor of the upper limbs. Brain MRI was normal. Cardiac evaluation showed a dysplastic mitral valve with moderate insufficiency, tricuspid insufficiency, an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 40 mmHg, and a moderately dilated left atrium. She did not have lactic acidosis, macrocephaly, peripheral neuropathy, nephrotic syndrome or failure to thrive. Her younger affected sister (II:4) also had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. At age 6 years she developed decreased visual acuity, and ophthalmologic examination showed optic atrophy with bilateral pallor of the optic nerve head in the temporal sector, as well as divergent strabismus. She had mild intellectual disability and muscular involvement. She did not have cardiac valvulopathy, lactic acidosis, macrocephaly, peripheral neuropathy or nephrotic syndrome. Nardecchia et al. (2021) noted that in PDSS1 deficiency, sensorineural deafness appeared to precede optic atrophy, unlike in OPA1 and OPA3 defects. They proposed adding PDSS1 deficiency to the differential diagnosis of optic atrophy with sensorineural deafness.

Bellusci et al. (2021) reported a boy who presented at age 5 months with failure to thrive, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, bilateral hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis and distal phalangeal erythema. Laboratory tests showed anemia, lactic acidosis, 3-methylglutaconic aciduria, tricarboxylic aciduria and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes. Brain imaging at 12 months showed mild cerebral atrophy. He went on to develop a progressive multisystem disorder with severe developmental delay, pyramidal dysfunction, seizures, tremor, optic atrophy, nystagmus, severe visual and hearing impairment, cystic white matter change and chronic heart failure. He died at age 3 years.

### Biochemical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) confirmed CoQ10 deficiency in 2 families, one with a PDSS1 mutation and one with a COQ2 mutation. Quinone-dependent OXPHOS activities were in the range of the lowest control values, and adding quinone restored normal OXPHOS activities.

In the infant reported by Bellusci et al. (2021), CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes were decreased, and urinary organic acid analysis showed 3-methylglutaconic aciduria and tricarboxylic aciduria.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of COQ10D2 in the families reported by Mollet et al. (2007) and Nardecchia et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Mollet et al. (2007) found a homozygous PDSS1 mutation in a family identified by homozygosity mapping. In the consanguineous family reported by Nardecchia et al. (2021), both affected sisters were homozygous for a PDSS1 mutation and both parents were carriers. Bellusci et al. (2021) found biallelic PDSS1 mutations in an affected infant. ⚠︎

### Mapping

In family 1, Mollet et al. (2007) performed a genome-wide search for homozygosity and identified a region on chromosome 10 that contains the PDSS1 gene.

### Heterogeneity

In a second family with CoQ10 deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) found a single-base-pair frameshift deletion in the COQ2 gene (c.1198delT, N401fsX415).

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with CoQ10 deficiency (family 1), Mollet et al. (2007) found a region of homozygosity on chromosome 10. Sequencing of the PDSS1 gene in this region identified a homozygous mutation (D308E) that changed a conserved amino acid. When the mutant yeast COQ1 gene was introduced into a yeast Deltacoq1 strain, the transformed yeast grew poorly on respiratory medium. The authors concluded that the PDSS1 mutation caused the OXPHOS deficiency.

Vasta et al. (2012) performed targeted next-generation sequencing of the exons of 908 known and candidate nuclear genes in 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders. None of the 18 patients with abnormal respiratory chain complex activities had defects in respiratory chain complex subunits or assembly factors. Instead, the authors found variants in known pathogenic genes, including PDSS1. ⚠︎

In 2 sisters from a consanguineous Egyptian family with COQ10D2, Nardecchia et al. (2021) found a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution at a highly conserved position. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and was not reported in the gnomAD database. It segregated with the disorder in the family: both parents were carriers, the affected younger sister was homozygous, the unaffected sister was homozygous wildtype, and the unaffected brother was heterozygous.

In a boy with a progressive multisystem form of COQ10D2, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene: a c.716T-G transversion resulting in a val239-to-gly (V239G) substitution, and a c.1183C-T transition resulting in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nardecchia et al. (2021) noted that, among CoQ10 deficiencies, visual system involvement had been reported only in defects of the very first steps of CoQ10 synthesis. Optic atrophy occurred in PDSS1 defects, optic atrophy combined with retinitis pigmentosa in PDSS2 defects, and retinitis pigmentosa alone in COQ2 defects.

### Pathogenesis

Bellusci et al. (2021) suggested that the combination of distal phalangeal erythema, livedo reticularis and pulmonary hypertension in their patient with PDSS1-related CoQ10 deficiency pointed to microvascular dysfunction as a possible underlying mechanism.

### Clinical Management

Nardecchia et al. (2021) stated that CoQ10 synthesis defects should be considered treatable conditions and included in the differential diagnosis of optic atrophy with sensorineural deafness.

In the infant reported by Bellusci et al. (2021), the dose of ubidecarenone was increased to 30 mg/kg/day once the biallelic PDSS1 mutations were identified. This high-dose oral CoQ10 did not control disease progression, and he died at age 3 years of a progressive multisystem disorder.
