### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene (607429) on chromosome 10p12.

### Description

Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by a defect in the first steps of CoQ10 biosynthesis. Reported patients have had sensorineural hearing loss followed by optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and cardiac valvulopathy. More severe early-onset multisystem forms, with encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and death in early childhood, have also been described (Nardecchia et al., 2021; Bellusci et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) reported a family with an inborn error of CoQ10 biosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) deficiency. In their summary of previously published cases, Nardecchia et al. (2021) described the 2 sibs reported by Mollet et al. (2007), a 22-year-old male and a 14-year-old female. Both had neurosensorial deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscular weakness, and cardiac involvement consisting of mild to moderate valvulopathy. They also had lactic acidosis, macrocephaly, peripheral neuropathy, and obesity/livedo reticularis. In these sibs the neurosensorial hearing loss came before the optic atrophy.

According to Nardecchia et al. (2021), Vasta et al. (2012) described a patient with a much more severe phenotype. This patient had developmental delay, failure to thrive, and leukoencephalopathy with a lactic acid peak on spectroscopy, together with a severe nephrotic syndrome that led to kidney failure and death at age 16 months.

Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported 2 sisters born to consanguineous Egyptian parents. The proband was a 16-year-old girl. Her motor development was normal, and she walked independently at 15 months, but she had severe language delay. Profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss was found at age 4 years. After she was fitted with a prosthesis she could speak simple sentences, but difficulty with verbal comprehension persisted, and mild intellectual disability was later diagnosed. She was referred at age 14 years for progressive visual loss. Her visual acuity had been normal at age 11 years and was 1/10 bilaterally at age 14 years. She had red-green dyschromatopsia, optic atrophy prevalent in the inferior temporal sector, visual evoked potentials with increased P100 latency and reduced amplitude, and bilateral reduction of retinal nerve fiber layer thickness on optical coherence tomography. Neurologic examination showed diffuse mild muscle weakness and mild postural tremor of the upper limbs, and brain MRI was normal. Echocardiography showed a moderately dysplastic mitral valve with moderate insufficiency, medium-grade tricuspid insufficiency with an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 40 mm Hg, and a moderately dilated left atrium. Her 6-year-old sister had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. At age 6 years she developed decreased visual acuity, and examination showed optic atrophy with bilateral temporal pallor of the optic nerve head, as well as divergent strabismus. In both sisters the sensorineural hearing loss came before the optic atrophy.

Bellusci et al. (2021) reported a boy born prematurely after an in vitro fertilization pregnancy complicated by twin-twin transfusion. At age 5 months he had failure to thrive, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, bilateral hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, and distal phalangeal erythema; the erythema persisted as a chronic sign. He went on to develop a progressive multisystem disorder with severe developmental delay, pyramidal dysfunction, seizures, tremor, optic atrophy and nystagmus, severe visual and hearing impairment, white matter cystic change, and chronic heart failure. He died at 3 years of age. Brain MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy at 12 months. At 24 months it showed cystic change in the white matter, with signal abnormalities and small necrotic or porencephalic cysts in the white matter tracts surrounding the lateral ventricles and in the commissural fibers; the U fibers, pyramidal tracts, and gray matter were spared.

### Biochemical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) found that in patients with CoQ10 deficiency, quinone-dependent OXPHOS activities were in the range of the lowest control values, whereas OXPHOS enzyme activities were normal. Adding quinone restored normal OXPHOS activities, which confirmed the CoQ10 deficiency.

Bellusci et al. (2021) found anemia, lactic acidosis, 3-methylglutaconic and tricarboxylic aciduria, and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes in their patient. They stated that this was the first reported patient with PDSS1 mutations to have 3-methylglutaconic aciduria.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of COQ10D2 in the families reported by Mollet et al. (2007) and Nardecchia et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the consanguineous Egyptian family reported by Nardecchia et al. (2021), both affected sisters were homozygous for a PDSS1 mutation, both parents were carriers, and the unaffected sibs were either heterozygous or homozygous wildtype. Bellusci et al. (2021) found biallelic PDSS1 mutations in their patient. ⚠︎

### Mapping

By a genome-wide search for homozygosity in a family with CoQ10 deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) identified a region on chromosome 10 that includes the PDSS1 gene.

### Heterogeneity

In a second family with CoQ10 deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) identified a frameshift mutation in the COQ2 gene, showing that CoQ10 deficiency is genetically heterogeneous.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with CoQ10 deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) found a region of homozygosity on chromosome 10 and then identified a homozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene (D308E) that changes a conserved amino acid. Yeast Deltacoq1 strains transformed with the corresponding mutant yeast COQ1 gene showed defective growth on respiratory medium. The authors concluded that the mutation causes the OXPHOS deficiency.

Vasta et al. (2012) performed targeted next-generation sequencing of the exons of 908 known and candidate nuclear genes in 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders. They identified variants in several known pathogenic genes, including PDSS1. Nardecchia et al. (2021) stated that the PDSS1-deficient patient described by Vasta et al. (2012) was compound heterozygous for a missense and a truncating variant.

In 2 Egyptian sisters with sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, Nardecchia et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution. The variant lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity and segregated with the disorder in the family. Sanger sequencing of OPA1, OPA3, and WFS1 had found no pathogenic variants. ⚠︎

In a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene: c.716T-G (V239G) and c.1183C-T (R395X).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nardecchia et al. (2021) noted that their homozygous patients and the 2 sibs homozygous for the D308E mutation reported by Mollet et al. (2007) shared a phenotype of deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, and muscle weakness, with hearing loss coming before the optic atrophy. In contrast, the patient reported by Vasta et al. (2012), who was compound heterozygous for a missense and a truncating variant, had a much more severe phenotype with leukoencephalopathy and nephrotic syndrome and died at 16 months.

### Pathogenesis

Bellusci et al. (2021) suggested that the coexistence of phalangeal erythema, livedo reticularis, and pulmonary hypertension in their patient pointed to microvascular dysfunction as a possible underlying mechanism.

### Diagnosis

Nardecchia et al. (2021) proposed that PDSS1 deficiency be added to the differential diagnosis of optic atrophy associated with sensorineural deafness. They noted that in PDSS1 deficiency the onset of sensorineural deafness appears to precede the optic atrophy, unlike in OPA1 and OPA3 defects. They also stated that CoQ10 synthesis defects should be considered treatable conditions.

### Clinical Management

Bellusci et al. (2021) started oral ubidecarenone at 15 mg/kg/day in their patient at age 5 months and increased the dose to 30 mg/kg/day after the PDSS1 mutations were identified 3 months later. High-dose CoQ10 supplementation did not control disease progression, and the patient died at age 3 years.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, ASP308GLU [p.D308E; c.924T>G; p.Asp308Glu; D308E]

In a family with CoQ10 deficiency and OXPHOS deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) identified a homozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene that results in an asp308-to-glu (D308E) substitution at a conserved residue. Yeast Deltacoq1 strains transformed with the corresponding mutant yeast COQ1 gene showed defective growth on respiratory medium. Nardecchia et al. (2021) gave the nucleotide change as c.924T-G and stated that the 2 affected sibs had deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and valvulopathy.

**.0002 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, GLN245HIS [c.735G>T; p.Q245H; p.Gln245His]

In 2 sisters, born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, with sensorineural hearing loss followed by optic atrophy, Nardecchia et al. (2021) identified a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution at a highly conserved residue. The variant was found by whole-exome sequencing and was not present in the gnomAD database. It lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity and segregated with the disorder in the family: both parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sibs were heterozygous or homozygous wildtype.

**.0003 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, VAL239GLY [c.716T>G; p.V239G; NM_014317.3:c.716T>G; p.Val239Gly]

Bellusci et al. (2021) reported a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency who had mitochondrial encephalopathy, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, distal phalangeal erythema, and 3-methylglutaconic aciduria, and who died at age 3 years. In this patient they identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene: a c.716T-G transversion, resulting in a val239-to-gly (V239G) substitution, and a c.1183C-T transition, resulting in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution.

**.0004 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**

For a discussion of the c.1183C-T transition in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution, that was found in compound heterozygous state in a patient with primary CoQ10 deficiency by Bellusci et al. (2021), see PDSS1, VAL239GLY.
