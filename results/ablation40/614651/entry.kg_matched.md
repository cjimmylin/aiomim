### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene (607429) on chromosome 10p12.

### Description

Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is an autosomal recessive disorder of CoQ10 biosynthesis. The phenotype varies. Some patients have sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, with mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and valvulopathy. Others have a severe, progressive multisystem disorder that can include leukoencephalopathy, nephrotic syndrome, and death in early childhood (Nardecchia et al., 2021; Bellusci et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) studied a family with inborn errors of CoQ10 biosynthesis and OXPHOS deficiency. Nardecchia et al. (2021) later reviewed the 2 affected sibs in this family. They had deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and valvulopathy, and the neurosensory hearing loss came before the optic atrophy.

Vasta et al. (2012) identified PDSS1 variants in a patient with a suspected mitochondrial disorder. According to Nardecchia et al. (2021), this patient's phenotype was much more severe, with leukoencephalopathy and nephrotic syndrome, and the patient died at age 16 months. ⚠︎

Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported 2 sisters born to consanguineous Egyptian parents. The proband was 16 years old. Her motor development was normal, but her language was severely delayed, and profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss was found at age 4 years. She later had mild intellectual disability. Her vision then declined progressively: visual acuity was normal at age 11 years and 1/10 bilaterally at age 14 years. She also had red-green dyschromatopsia, optic atrophy mainly in the inferior temporal sector, abnormal VEPs, and reduced retinal nerve fiber layer thickness on OCT. Other findings were diffuse mild muscle weakness, mild postural tremor of the upper limbs, and a moderately dysplastic mitral valve with moderate insufficiency and tricuspid insufficiency. Brain MRI was normal. Her younger sister, age 6 years, had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. At age 6 years she also developed decreased visual acuity, and examination showed optic atrophy with bilateral temporal pallor of the optic nerve head and divergent strabismus. In both sisters, as in the sibs reported by Mollet et al. (2007), the hearing loss came before the optic atrophy.

Bellusci et al. (2021) described a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency. He was born prematurely after in vitro fertilization, and the pregnancy was complicated by twin-twin transfusion. At 5 months of age he had failure to thrive, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, bilateral hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, and chronic distal phalangeal erythema. He went on to develop a progressive multisystem disorder with severe developmental delay, pyramidal dysfunction, seizures, tremor, optic atrophy, nystagmus, severe visual and hearing impairment, cystic white matter changes, and chronic heart failure. He died at 3 years of age. Brain MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy at 12 months. At 24 months it showed cystic change in the white matter, with signal abnormalities and small necrotic or porencephalic cysts in the white matter tracts surrounding the lateral ventricles and commissural fibers; the U fibers, pyramidal tracts, and gray matter were spared.

### Biochemical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) performed enzymologic studies in 2 families with CoQ10 deficiency. Quinone-dependent OXPHOS activities were in the range of the lowest control values, whereas OXPHOS enzyme activities themselves were normal. Adding quinone restored normal OXPHOS activities, which confirmed the CoQ10 deficiency.

The patient of Bellusci et al. (2021) had anemia, lactic acidosis, 3-methylglutaconic and tricarboxylic aciduria, and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes. He was the first reported PDSS1 patient with 3-methylglutaconic aciduria.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance of COQ10D2 was supported by the homozygous PDSS1 mutation in affected sibs reported by Mollet et al. (2007) and by the homozygous mutation in sisters born to consanguineous parents, both of whom were heterozygous carriers, reported by Nardecchia et al. (2021). Bellusci et al. (2021) reported a patient with compound heterozygous PDSS1 mutations. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Mollet et al. (2007) ran a genome-wide search for homozygosity in a family with CoQ10 deficiency and identified a region on chromosome 10 that contains the PDSS1 gene. PDSS1 encodes the human ortholog of yeast COQ1, a key enzyme of CoQ10 synthesis.

### Heterogeneity

In a second family with CoQ10 deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) found a mutation in the COQ2 gene instead: a homozygous single base pair frameshift deletion (c.1198delT, N401fsX415) that creates a premature stop codon, identified by direct sequencing. Yeast Deltacoq2 strains transformed with the mutant human COQ2 gene grew poorly on respiratory medium. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Mollet et al. (2007) studied a family with CoQ10 deficiency and OXPHOS deficiency. In the affected members, they found a homozygous nucleotide substitution in the PDSS1 gene that changes a conserved amino acid (D308E). Yeast Deltacoq1 strains transformed with mutant yeast COQ1 grew poorly on respiratory medium, and the authors concluded that the mutation causes the OXPHOS deficiency.

Vasta et al. (2012) performed targeted next-generation sequencing of the exons of 908 known and candidate nuclear genes in 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders. Among the variants they identified were variants in PDSS1. Nardecchia et al. (2021) later described this patient as compound heterozygous for a missense and a truncating variant. ⚠︎

Nardecchia et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 Egyptian sisters with sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy. They identified a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution. The variant affects a highly conserved residue, was absent from gnomAD, and lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity. Both parents carried the variant. Of the unaffected sibs, one sister was homozygous wildtype and one brother was heterozygous. Sanger sequencing of the OPA1, OPA3, and WFS1 genes found no pathogenic variants. ⚠︎

In an infant with primary CoQ10 deficiency, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene (NM_014317.3): c.716T-G (V239G) and c.1183C-T (R395X).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nardecchia et al. (2021) compared their patients with the 3 previously reported patients with PDSS1 deficiency from 2 families. Their homozygous Q245H patients resembled the 2 sibs homozygous for D308E reported by Mollet et al. (2007): all had deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, and muscle weakness, with hearing loss coming before optic atrophy. In contrast, the patient reported by Vasta et al. (2012), who was compound heterozygous for a missense and a truncating variant, had a much more severe phenotype, with leukoencephalopathy and nephrotic syndrome, and died at age 16 months. ✗

### Pathogenesis

Bellusci et al. (2021) noted that their PDSS1 patient had phalangeal erythema, livedo reticularis, and pulmonary hypertension together. They suggested that this combination pointed to microvascular dysfunction as a possible underlying mechanism.

### Diagnosis

Nardecchia et al. (2021) proposed that PDSS1 deficiency, which is clinically heterogeneous, be added to the differential diagnosis of optic atrophy with sensorineural deafness. They noted that in PDSS1 deficiency the deafness appears to begin before the optic atrophy, unlike in OPA1 and OPA3 defects. They also stated that CoQ10 synthesis defects should be considered treatable conditions.

### Clinical Management

Bellusci et al. (2021) treated their patient with oral ubidecarenone (CoQ10) at 15 mg/kg/day from 5 months of age and increased the dose to 30 mg/kg/day after the molecular diagnosis. The high-dose supplementation did not control disease progression, and the patient died at 3 years of age.
