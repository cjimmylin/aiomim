### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes the glucose transporter GLUT1.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects is a rare form of hereditary stomatocytosis. It is marked by a cold-induced red cell cation leak, hemolytic anemia, and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder (Flatt et al., 2011). Flatt et al. (2011) and Bawazir et al. (2012) described it as a cation-leaky form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome. Bawazir et al. (2012) proposed that the multisystem disease probably reflects both deficient glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier and the harmful osmotic effects of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.

### Nomenclature

Bawazir et al. (2012) used the term 'cation-leaky GLUT1-deficiency syndrome' for this disorder, and Flatt et al. (2011) called it a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased permeability of the red cell membrane to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis. Both had high-Na, low-K erythrocytes.

Fricke et al. (2004) reported 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree with stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis, and expanded the description of 1 previously reported case. In all cases, red cells showed marked cation leaks at 37 degrees C and markedly abnormal intracellular Na(+) and K(+) concentrations. The cases differed in how the passive K(+) leak varied with temperature. In 3 cases the temperature dependence was simple and monotonic. In 2 cases there was a minimum at about 20 to 25 degrees C, so that the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, which is the cryohydrocytosis phenotype. These were the only 2 known cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Both had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts, and massive hepatosplenomegaly, which Fricke et al. (2004) suggested probably defined a new hemato-neurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) summarized the features of their 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis: a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, hepatosplenomegaly, cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) described an infant who presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, and microcephaly. Seizures developed later, and developmental milestones were delayed. MRI and CT scans of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. The child had visual impairment due to both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. Red cell cation measurements showed extremely leaky red cells, which accounted for the hemolysis. The red cells also lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia. Bawazir et al. (2012) stated that this was the fourth pedigree described with this syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) separated the band 7 red cell membrane proteins by SDS gel electrophoresis into 3 components of about 30,000, 28,000, and 26,000 Da. The 28,000-Da component was reduced in both of their patients with permeability disorders. Two-dimensional electrophoresis split this component in normal membranes into 2 proteins with different isoelectric points, called 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. Protein 22 x 8 was completely absent in the patients with hereditary stomatocytosis and cryohydrocytosis, whereas 60 x 8 was present as usual. All band 7 proteins were present in normal subjects and in reticulocytosis controls. The authors suggested that loss of this protein might cause the increased Na and K permeability.

Fricke et al. (2004) found by Western blotting that all their cases were deficient in the 32-kD red cell membrane protein stomatin. Immunocytochemistry showed that the deficiency was not complete. On typical blood films, some red cells were stomatin-positive and most were negative, although in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive.

Flatt et al. (2011) studied how stomatin is lost during erythropoiesis. They found that the loss occurs during reticulocyte maturation and involves endocytosis.

### Inheritance

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that the hereditary stomatocytoses are a group of dominantly inherited hemolytic anemias.

### Molecular Genetics

Flatt et al. (2011) identified mutations in the SLC2A1 gene in 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Expression studies in Xenopus oocytes showed that the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Using a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, the authors proposed possible mechanisms for the phenotypes of the 2 mutations.

In an infant with pseudohyperkalemia, hemolysis, and seizures, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deletes isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

SLC2A1, 3-BP DEL, ILE435DEL/ILE436DEL [Ile435del; Ile436del]

In an infant with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deletes isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1. The patient had pseudohyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, microcephaly, seizures, developmental delay, periventricular calcification, cataracts, and retinal dysfunction. The patient's red cells were extremely leaky to cations and lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way.
