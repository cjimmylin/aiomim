### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene (138140) on chromosome 1p34.2.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis (sdCHC) is a rare form of stomatocytosis. Its features include a cold-induced cation leak from red cells, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder (Fricke et al., 2004; Flatt et al., 2011). Flatt et al. (2011) showed that the disorder results from mutations in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes the glucose transporter GLUT1, and described it as a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome. In this disorder the red cell membrane is abnormally permeable to sodium and potassium, which can cause pseudohyperkalemia (Bawazir et al., 2012).

### Nomenclature

Flatt et al. (2011) called stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome. Bawazir et al. (2012) used the term 'cation-leaky GLUT1-deficiency syndrome' for the disorder.

### Clinical Features

Fricke et al. (2004) reported 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree with stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis, and gave further details on 1 previously reported case. Two of these patients had stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis, and the authors stated that they were the only 2 known cases of this variant. Both had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts and massive hepatosplenomegaly. Fricke et al. (2004) suggested that this probably defined a new hemato-neurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) summarized the 2 patients with sdCHC that their group had reported before. They described the disorder as being associated with a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, as well as cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) reported an infant with GLUT1 deficiency and a cation-leaky red cell state. The infant presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction and microcephaly. Seizures developed later, and developmental milestones were delayed. MRI and CT scans of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. Vision was impaired by both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. The authors stated that this was the fourth pedigree described with this syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied erythrocyte membrane proteins in 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased erythrocyte membrane permeability to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis. On 1-dimensional SDS gel electrophoresis, the band 7 proteins separated into components of approximately 30,000, 28,000 and 26,000 daltons, and the 28,000-dalton component was decreased in both patients. Two-dimensional electrophoresis separated the normal 28,000-dalton component into 2 proteins, designated 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. Protein 22 x 8 was completely absent in both patients, whereas 60 x 8 was present as usual. All band 7 proteins, including 22 x 8, were invariably present in normal subjects and in reticulocytosis controls.

In all of their cases of stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis, Fricke et al. (2004) found by Western blotting of red cell membranes that the 32-kD membrane protein stomatin was deficient. All cases had pronounced cation leaks at 37 degrees C, with markedly abnormal intracellular Na+ and K+ concentrations. Immunocytochemistry showed that the stomatin deficiency was not complete. On typical blood films, some red cells were stomatin-positive and most were negative, although in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive. The cases differed markedly in how the passive K+ leak depended on temperature. Three cases showed a simple monotonic temperature dependence. The other 2 showed a minimum at about 20 to 25 degrees C, so that the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, giving the cryohydrocytosis phenotype.

In their patient, Bawazir et al. (2012) measured red cell cation content and found that the red cells were extremely leaky, which caused the hemolysis. The red cells also lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis that their group had reported before, Flatt et al. (2011) found 2 different mutations in the SLC2A1 gene. Expression studies in Xenopus oocytes showed that the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Flatt et al. (2011) also compared the molecular basis of the GLUT1 deficiency syndrome, paroxysmal exercise-induced dyskinesia and sdCHC phenotypes.

In an infant with pseudohyperkalemia, hemolysis and seizures, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that results in deletion of isoleucine 435 or 436 of GLUT1.

### Pathogenesis

Lande et al. (1982) suggested that absence of the band 7 membrane protein 22 x 8 might be responsible for the increased Na and K permeability in hereditary stomatocytosis and cryohydrocytosis.

Using a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, Flatt et al. (2011) proposed possible mechanisms underlying the phenotypes caused by the 2 SLC2A1 mutations they found. They also studied the loss of stomatin during erythropoiesis and found that it occurs during reticulocyte maturation and involves endocytosis.

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the multisystem disease probably reflects 2 combined effects. One is deficient glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The other is the harmful osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.
