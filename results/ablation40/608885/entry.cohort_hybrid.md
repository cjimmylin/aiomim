### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene (138140) on chromosome 1p34.2.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects is a rare form of hereditary stomatocytosis. The red cell membrane leaks sodium and potassium, the leak increases in the cold, and red cell stomatin is deficient. Patients have hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder (Fricke et al., 2004; Flatt et al., 2011). Flatt et al. (2011) considered the disorder a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome.

### Nomenclature

Bawazir et al. (2012) referred to the disorder as 'cation-leaky GLUT1-deficiency syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Fricke et al. (2004) studied patients with congenitally sodium- and potassium-leaky red cells of the hereditary stomatocytosis class. They described 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree, and expanded on 1 previously reported case. In 2 of these cases, the red cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, giving the cryohydrocytosis phenotype. The authors stated that these 2 were the only known cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Both patients had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts, and massive hepatosplenomegaly. Fricke et al. (2004) suggested that this probably defined a new hemato-neurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) restudied the 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis whom their group had reported earlier. They described the disorder as a rare form of stomatocytosis with a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) described an infant who presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, and microcephaly. Later, the infant developed seizures and had delayed developmental milestones. MRI and CT scans of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. Visual impairment resulted from both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. The authors stated that this was the fourth pedigree described with this syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) examined the red cell membrane proteins of 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased red cell permeability to sodium and potassium. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis. On SDS gel electrophoresis, the 28-kD component of the band 7 proteins was decreased in both patients. Two-dimensional electrophoresis separated this component into 2 proteins, 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. In both patients, 22 x 8 was completely absent and 60 x 8 was present as usual. In contrast, 22 x 8 was always present in normal subjects and in reticulocytosis controls. The authors raised the possibility that the absence of this protein was responsible for the increased sodium and potassium permeability.

In all of their cases of stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis, Fricke et al. (2004) found by Western blotting that red cell membranes were deficient in the 32-kD membrane protein stomatin. All cases had marked cation leaks at 37 degrees C. Immunocytochemistry showed that the stomatin deficiency was incomplete: on blood films some red cells were stomatin-positive, and all platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive. The cases differed in how the passive potassium leak depended on temperature. In 3 cases the leak rose steadily with temperature. In the 2 cases with cryohydrocytosis, the leak was lowest at around 20 to 25 degrees C, and the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C. ⚠︎

In their patient, Bawazir et al. (2012) measured red cell cation content and found extremely leaky red cells, which caused the hemolysis. The red cells also lost potassium in a temperature-dependent manner, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia.

### Inheritance

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that the hereditary stomatocytoses, the group to which stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis belongs, are a series of dominantly inherited hemolytic anemias. The abstracts available here do not state the zygosity of the SLC2A1 mutations in patients with this disorder (Flatt et al., 2011; Bawazir et al., 2012).

### Molecular Genetics

In the 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis previously reported by Fricke et al. (2004), Flatt et al. (2011) identified mutations in the SLC2A1 gene. The 2 mutations were expressed in Xenopus oocytes, where they caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. ⚠︎

In an infant with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deleted isoleucine-435 or -436 of GLUT1.

### Pathogenesis

Flatt et al. (2011) used a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1 to propose possible mechanisms behind the phenotypes of the 2 SLC2A1 mutations they found. They also found that stomatin was lost during reticulocyte maturation and that this loss involved endocytosis.

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the multisystem pathology probably reflects 2 effects combined. The first is a deficiency of glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The second is the harmful osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in the cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.

In an infant with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deleted isoleucine-435 or -436 of GLUT1. The infant had pseudohyperkalemia, hemolysis, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, hepatic dysfunction, microcephaly, seizures, developmental delay, periventricular calcification, cataracts, and retinal dysfunction. The infant's red cells were extremely cation-leaky and lost potassium in a temperature-dependent manner.
