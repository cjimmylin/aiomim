### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene (138140) on chromosome 1p34.2.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is a rare form of stomatocytosis. It is associated with a cold-induced red cell cation leak, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder (Flatt et al., 2011). Flatt et al. (2011) showed that the disorder results from mutations in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes the GLUT1 glucose transporter. They considered it a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased permeability of the erythrocyte membrane to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis. Both had high-Na, low-K erythrocytes.

Fricke et al. (2004) described 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis, who were the only 2 such cases known. Both had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts and massive hepatosplenomegaly. Fricke et al. (2004) suggested that this probably defined a new hemato-neurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that their 2 previously reported cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis had a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly. The patients also had cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) described an infant with GLUT1 deficiency and a cation-leaky red cell state. The infant presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction and microcephaly, and later developed seizures and delayed developmental milestones. MRI and CT of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. Vision was impaired because of both cataracts and retinal dysfunction.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) used 1-dimensional SDS gel electrophoresis to separate the erythrocyte membrane band 7 proteins into components of about 30, 28 and 26 kD. The 28-kD component was decreased in both of their patients. Two-dimensional electrophoresis split the normal 28-kD component into 2 proteins, 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. In the patients with hereditary stomatocytosis and cryohydrocytosis, protein 22 x 8 was completely absent, whereas 60 x 8 was present as usual. All band 7 proteins, including 22 x 8, were invariably present in normal subjects and in reticulocytosis controls.

Fricke et al. (2004) studied 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree of hereditary stomatocytosis, and expanded on 1 previously reported case. Western blotting of red cell membranes showed deficiency of the 32-kD membrane protein stomatin in all cases. All cases had pronounced cation leaks at 37 degrees C, with markedly abnormal intracellular Na+ and K+ concentrations. Immunocytochemistry showed that the stomatin deficiency was not complete. On blood films, some red cells were stomatin-positive and most were negative, although in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive.

Bawazir et al. (2012) measured red cell cation content in their patient and found that the red cells were extremely leaky, which caused the hemolysis. The red cells also lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia.

### Heterogeneity

Among their cases of stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis, Fricke et al. (2004) found marked differences in how the passive K+ leak depended on temperature. Three cases showed a simple monotonic temperature dependence. Two cases showed a minimum at about 20 to 25 degrees C, so that the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C; this is the phenotype known as cryohydrocytosis. Fricke et al. (2004) associated this stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis variant with neurologic dysfunction.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis, Flatt et al. (2011) found 2 different mutations in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes the GLUT1 glucose transporter. Expression studies in Xenopus oocytes showed that the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Flatt et al. (2011) concluded that the disorder is a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome.

In an infant with pseudohyperkalemia, hemolysis and seizures, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deletes isoleucine 435 or 436 of GLUT1. The authors stated that this was the fourth pedigree described with this syndrome. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Flatt et al. (2011) compared the molecular basis of 3 phenotypes that all result from SLC2A1 mutations: GLUT1 deficiency syndrome, paroxysmal exercise-induced dyskinesia and stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Lande et al. (1982) suggested that absence of the band 7 membrane protein 22 x 8 might be responsible for the increased erythrocyte Na and K permeability in hereditary stomatocytosis and cryohydrocytosis.

Flatt et al. (2011) used a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1 to propose possible mechanisms for the phenotypes caused by their 2 SLC2A1 mutations. They also found that loss of stomatin from erythrocytes occurs during reticulocyte maturation and involves endocytosis.

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the multisystem disease probably reflects 2 combined effects. The first is deficient glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The second is the harmful osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.
