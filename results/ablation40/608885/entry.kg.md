### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene (138140) on chromosome 1p34.2.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis (sdCHC) is a rare form of stomatocytosis. It is associated with a cold-induced cation leak in red cells, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder (Flatt et al., 2011). Red cell membranes are deficient in stomatin (Fricke et al., 2004). Flatt et al. (2011) and Bawazir et al. (2012) found that the disorder is caused by mutations in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes GLUT1. In the patient reported by Bawazir et al. (2012), the extremely cation-leaky red cells caused hemolysis and pseudohyperkalemia. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Fricke et al. (2004) described 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. In both patients, the passive red cell K+ leak was lowest at around 20 to 25 degrees C, so the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C. Both patients had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts and massive hepatosplenomegaly. Fricke et al. (2004) suggested that this probably defined a new haemato-neurological syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that they had previously reported 2 cases of sdCHC. They characterized it as a rare form of stomatocytosis associated with a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, as well as cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) described an infant with GLUT1 deficiency and a cation-leaky red cell state. The infant presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction and microcephaly. Seizures developed later, and developmental milestones were delayed. Brain MRI and CT showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. Visual impairment resulted from cataracts and retinal dysfunction.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied erythrocyte membrane proteins from 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased erythrocyte membrane permeability to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis. On one-dimensional SDS gel electrophoresis, the 28,000-dalton band 7 component was reduced in both patients. On two-dimensional electrophoresis, this component resolved into 2 proteins, 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. Protein 22 x 8 was completely absent in both patients, whereas 60 x 8 was present.

Fricke et al. (2004) described 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree of hereditary stomatocytosis, and gave further details on 1 previously reported case. In every case, Western blotting of red cell membranes showed a deficiency of stomatin, the 32-kD membrane protein. All cases had pronounced cation leaks at 37 degrees C, with markedly abnormal intracellular Na+ and K+ concentrations. Immunocytochemistry showed that the stomatin deficiency was not complete: some red cells were stomatin positive, and all platelets and neutrophils were stomatin positive.

Bawazir et al. (2012) found that their patient's red cells were extremely leaky to cations, which caused the hemolysis. The red cells lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which showed that the hyperkalemia was pseudohyperkalemia.

### Heterogeneity

Fricke et al. (2004) found that stomatin-deficient cases of hereditary stomatocytosis differed markedly in how the passive K+ leak depended on temperature. Three cases showed a simple monotonic temperature dependence. The other 2 cases had a minimum at 20 to 25 degrees C, which is the cryohydrocytosis phenotype. Only these 2 cryohydrocytosis cases had the associated neurologic syndrome. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In the 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis, Flatt et al. (2011) identified 2 different mutations in the SLC2A1 gene. Expression studies in Xenopus oocytes showed that the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Using a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, Flatt et al. (2011) proposed potential mechanisms underlying the phenotypes of the 2 mutations.

In an infant with pseudohyperkalemia, hemolysis and seizures, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deletes isoleucine 435 or 436 of GLUT1. Bawazir et al. (2012) noted that this was the fourth pedigree reported with this syndrome.

### Pathogenesis

Lande et al. (1982) found that all band 7 proteins, including 22 x 8, were present in every normal subject and reticulocytosis control they surveyed. Because the missing protein was unique to the patients, Lande et al. (1982) raised the possibility that absence of this band 7 protein is responsible for the increased Na and K permeability in hereditary stomatocytosis and cryohydrocytosis.

Flatt et al. (2011) found that in sdCHC, stomatin is lost during reticulocyte maturation in erythropoiesis and that the loss involves endocytosis.

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the multisystem pathology probably reflects 2 effects. The first is deficient glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The second is the harmful osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably red cells.
