### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene (602242) on chromosome 19q13.32.

### Description

Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (FHH3) is an autosomal dominant disorder of extracellular calcium homeostasis involving the parathyroids, kidneys, and bone. Like FHH1 and FHH2, it is characterized by lifelong elevation of serum calcium with inappropriately low urinary calcium excretion. Unlike FHH1 and FHH2, which usually have normal PTH, FHH3 features increased serum PTH, hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia (Nesbit et al., 2013). Howles et al. (2016) described FHH3 as the most clinically severe of the 3 FHH types. It is caused by heterozygous AP2S1 mutations affecting arg15, and the mutant AP2-sigma proteins impair calcium-sensing receptor-mediated signal transduction.

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) called the form of familial benign hypercalcemia in a kindred from Oklahoma the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok). Lloyd et al. (1999) used the designation FBHOk, Hannan et al. (2010) used FBHH3 or FBHH-Ok, and Nesbit et al. (2010, 2013) used FHH3.

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) described a large kindred with familial benign hypercalcemia (FBH). They surveyed 46 members across 5 generations, aged 1.5 to 91 years, by biochemical testing and/or chart review, and found 19 affected. In the 15 hypercalcemic subjects tested biochemically, serum immunoreactive PTH rose with age, became supranormal by about age 30 years, and appeared to increase further after that. Radiographic changes suggestive of osteomalacia were present in 3 of 5 affected adults older than 40 years who were studied. Two hypercalcemic adults had tetracycline-labeled iliac crest biopsies. In one of them, a 51-year-old woman, the biopsy confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. The authors suggested that the disorder may not be entirely benign and that osteomalacia, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia, can develop in adulthood.

Trump et al. (1995) studied a 5-generation kindred from Oklahoma, USA, with 19 affected members, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected members. In this family, familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia was associated with a developmental elevation in serum PTH levels.

Hannan et al. (2010) studied 40 members from 5 generations of the Oklahoma kindred. In affected members, the diagnosis was based on hypercalcemia with a ratio of calcium clearance to creatinine clearance below 0.01. Five affected individuals older than 30 years had elevated serum PTH, and 3 adults older than 40 years had evidence of osteomalacia.

Nesbit et al. (2010) studied a second kindred. The proband was a 43-year-old woman with FHH and no CASR mutation. Her corrected serum calcium was 2.74 mmol/liter (normal 2.15-2.55), her serum PTH was 47 pg/ml (normal 10-65), and her ratio of urinary calcium clearance to creatinine clearance was 0.006. Of 24 members from 3 generations of the kindred, 16 were hypercalcemic with normal or elevated serum PTH and mild hypophosphatemia. The authors considered these features consistent with FHH3. ⚠︎

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese female infant whose hypercalcemia was found on a routine laboratory test done for poor weight gain while she was breastfed. At 49 days of age, adjusted serum calcium was 13.1 mg/dL, intact PTH was 27 pg/mL, and the urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg. There was no evidence of hyperparathyroidism, a PTHrP-producing neoplasm, or vitamin D excess. The authors noted that, apart from the hypercalciuria, these data appeared consistent with defective calcium-sensing receptor-mediated signaling.

Howles et al. (2016) described 3 previously unreported unrelated probands with FHH3. The first was a 34-year-old woman with a 12-year history of hypercalcemia who had fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. The second was a 22-year-old man with hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. The third was a 52-year-old woman with about 20 years of hypercalcemia who had headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy.

### Biochemical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) found that affected members of their FBH kindred had normal serum alkaline phosphatase activity and normal creatinine. Their serum inorganic phosphate levels were lower than those of unaffected members of the kindred.

Nesbit et al. (2013) summarized biochemical findings in 13 FHH3 families or probands with AP2S1 mutations, whose ages ranged from 8 days to 78 years. Affected members of the Oklahoma kindred (13 females, 3 males) had a mean serum calcium of 2.88 mmol/l and a urinary calcium:creatinine clearance ratio of 0.003. Affected members of the Northern Ireland kindred (11 females, 5 males) had a mean serum calcium of 2.72 mmol/l. Across mutations, patients had similarly elevated serum calcium and PTH and reduced urinary calcium excretion.

### Inheritance

Trump et al. (1995) studied a 5-generation Oklahoma kindred that included affected members and obligate carriers. In the same kindred, Hannan et al. (2010) found that the disorder segregated with a single chromosome 19q13 haplotype, and they calculated LOD scores under an autosomal dominant model with 90% penetrance. Nesbit et al. (2013) described FHH3 as an autosomal dominant disorder and found heterozygous AP2S1 mutations that cosegregated with the disorder in 2 kindreds.

### Mapping

In the Oklahoma kindred, Trump et al. (1995) tested 17 polymorphic markers from the candidate regions 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3 (FBH), 11q13 (MEN1), and 11p15 (PTH). Linkage to all of these regions was excluded (lod scores less than -2.00 at theta = 0.05-0.25), and analysis of multipoint crossovers and the LINKMAP program confirmed the exclusion.

Lloyd et al. (1999) performed a genomewide search with chromosome-specific sets of microsatellite polymorphisms in the Oklahoma family. They found linkage between FBHOk and 8 chromosome 19q13 loci, with a maximum lod score of 6.67 at theta = 0.00 with D19S606. Recombinants further mapped the locus to an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866.

Hannan et al. (2010) performed linkage analysis in 34 informative members of the Oklahoma kindred, 19 of them affected, using 24 polymorphic loci (17 microsatellites and 7 SNPs). They found linkage to chromosome 19q13, with a peak 2-point LOD score of 6.92 at theta = 0 at D19S606. Recombinants in individuals II.2, III.3, and III.4 refined the FBHH3 locus to a 4.1-Mb interval (less than 10 cM) flanked by D19S112 and rs245111. This interval contains 135 protein-coding genes and 24 predicted genes. The syntenic region on mouse chromosome 7 contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs1, -rs2, -rs3, and -rs5), but Hannan et al. (2010) found no human or primate homologs of these sequences in the 19q13 region. Comparative analysis showed that 2 conserved segments are inverted in human, and that an 11.6-Mb mouse region containing 35 genes, including the 4 Gprc2a-rs genes, is absent from the human genome. The authors concluded that FBHH3 cannot be attributed to abnormalities of the Gprc2a-rs genes.

In a second kindred, Nesbit et al. (2010) found that FHH cosegregated with microsatellite and SNP markers on chromosome 19q13.3, with a peak lod score of 5.98 at theta = 0 with D19S412. Analysis of recombinants placed the locus in a 3.46-Mb interval flanked centromerically by rs1990932 and telomerically by D19S604. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that this interval contains about 110 genes.

### Heterogeneity

Because the disorder in the Oklahoma kindred was not linked to markers that segregate with FBH, MEN1, or PTH, Trump et al. (1995) concluded that it indicated further genetic heterogeneity and a third locus for FBH. Lloyd et al. (1999) noted that not all patients with familial benign hypercalcemia have CASR mutations and that another FBH locus maps to 19p13.3. After mapping the Oklahoma disorder to 19q13, they suggested that this locus may encode another calcium-sensing receptor or a mediator of calcium homeostasis.

Hannan et al. (2010) and Nesbit et al. (2010) noted 3 designated types of FHH: FHH1 on 3q21.1, caused by CASR mutations; FHH2 on 19p; and FHH3 on 19q13. Nesbit et al. (2010) stated that FHH1 accounts for more than 65% of patients and suggested that FHH3 may explain the disorder in FHH patients without CASR mutations. Nesbit et al. (2013) found AP2S1 arg15 missense mutations in more than 20% of FHH patients without CASR mutations. Howles et al. (2016) noted that FHH type 1 is caused by CASR mutations, type 2 by GNA11 mutations, and type 3 by AP2S1 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Lloyd et al. (1999) sequenced the coding region, 5-prime UTR, and part of the promoter of the calmodulin III gene, which lies within the 19q13 interval, in an affected member of the Oklahoma family. They found no abnormalities, indicating that this gene is unlikely to cause the disorder.

Hannan et al. (2010) selected 12 candidate genes from the FBHH3 interval (PTH2, PTGIR, GNG8, EMP3, SPHK2, TULP2, NUCB1, HRC, RCN3, CYTH2, RRAS, and ATF5) because they are expressed in the kidney and/or parathyroid and may affect GPCR signaling. Sequencing of the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries in an affected individual and an unrelated normal individual found no abnormalities, and RT-PCR excluded splicing abnormalities. The authors concluded that these genes are unlikely to cause FBHH3.

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on 1 affected person from each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, from Oklahoma and from Northern Ireland. Within the 3.46-Mb FHH3 interval on 19q13.3, the only shared variant was a C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene predicting an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. Dideoxynucleotide sequencing confirmed the variant, and restriction enzyme analysis showed that it cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members spanning 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. The authors then sequenced all 5 exons of AP2S1 in 50 unrelated FHH patients (23 males and 27 females) in whom CASR mutations had been excluded. They found 11 heterozygous missense mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 R15C, 3 R15H, and 4 R15L. Together with the 2 original kindreds, this gave 13 unrelated probands with arg15 mutations. Arg15 was significantly conserved in 5,400 human exomes (p less than 10(-10)), which the authors took to indicate a likely mutational hotspot. Functional studies showed that the mutants reduced the sensitivity of CaSR-expressing cells to extracellular calcium and reduced CaSR endocytosis (see PATHOGENESIS). ⚠︎

In a Japanese infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria, Fujisawa et al. (2013) found no pathologic mutation in CASR or GNA11. They identified a heterozygous R15L mutation in AP2S1, which they presumed to be de novo.

Howles et al. (2016) found that each of 3 unrelated FHH3 probands carried a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation causing an R15C, R15H, or R15L substitution in AP2-sigma. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that patients with the R15C, R15H, and R15L AP2S1 mutations had similar elevations in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium. They concluded that the specific mutation did not correlate with the clinical phenotype.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) concluded that a lack of hypocalciuria does not necessarily argue against an AP2S1 mutation. They suggested that their patient's early infantile age may have contributed to her hypercalciuria.

### Pathogenesis

Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that arg15 of AP2-sigma-2 is conserved in all orthologs and in all human AP sigma paralogs. In the AP2 crystal structure, arg15 forms key contacts with the glutamine at position -4 of cargo dileucine motifs. Modeling predicted that the cys15 and leu15 substitutions reduce affinity for the dileucine motif; the effect of his15 was harder to predict. The authors proposed that AP2-sigma-2 recognizes a C-terminal dileucine motif in CaSR (RHQPLL, residues 1009-1014). In HEK293 cells, replacing this CaSR dileucine motif (leu-leu1013-1014) with ala-ala shifted the concentration-response curve to the right and raised the EC50 (3.63 mM vs 3.41 mM for wildtype; p less than 0.0001), indicating that the motif is functionally significant.

Nesbit et al. (2013) expressed the R15C, R15H, and R15L AP2S1 mutants in HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR. All 3 raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium (2.90, 2.73, and 2.86 mM vs 2.53 mM for wildtype; p less than 0.0001), indicating reduced sensitivity to extracellular calcium, as seen with FHH1 CASR mutations. The cys15 mutant gave an EC50 significantly higher than vector alone, which the authors suggested indicates a possible dominant-negative effect. Intact-cell ELISA showed that, after stimulation with extracellular calcium, cells transfected with wildtype AP2S1 lost more surface CaSR than cells transfected with the R15C mutant or vector alone. The authors concluded that the AP2S1 arg15 mutants reduce CaSR endocytosis and disrupt CaSR signaling, resulting in FHH3. They noted that AP2-sigma-2 is ubiquitously expressed, yet its arg15 mutations cause a specific disorder of calcium homeostasis rather than a multisystem disease. They suggested that arg15 has a specific role, such as recognizing the CaSR dileucine motif, and that mutations at other AP2S1 residues might cause different diseases.

Howles et al. (2016) measured intracellular calcium and MAPK-serum response element reporter activity in cells expressing the calcium-sensing receptor and FHH3-associated AP2-sigma mutants. Cinacalcet at 10 nM corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in cells expressing the cys15, his15, or leu15 mutants.

### Diagnosis

McMurtry et al. (1992) concluded that in their kindred, FBH in adults can be especially hard to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism because serum immunoreactive PTH may be elevated. They proposed reviewing other kindreds for age-related rises in immunoreactive PTH and for skeletal disease in late adulthood, to clarify whether this kindred represents an unusual variant of FBH.

### Clinical Management

Fujisawa et al. (2013) treated a Japanese infant with FHH3 with a low-calcium formula containing 2.6 mg/dL calcium. She showed catch-up growth, and both serum calcium and the urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio decreased. Feeding a 2:1 mixture of low-calcium and standard formula kept serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without a marked rise in serum PTH. The authors concluded that low-calcium formula is worth trying in infants with FHH.

Howles et al. (2016) treated 3 FHH3 probands with oral cinacalcet, 30 to 60 mg daily, for 33 to 45 months. Serum calcium fell by more than 20% and symptoms abated. Serum phosphate rose and PTH fell, but both stayed within the normal range. No proband developed nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia. The authors concluded that cinacalcet can reduce FHH3 symptoms in the short term with all 3 AP2S1 mutations, but said that long-term surveillance is needed.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15CYS [p.C15; p.R15C]

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on affected members of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, from Oklahoma and from Northern Ireland. In both, they identified a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene that results in an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. Restriction enzyme analysis showed that the mutation cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members spanning 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. They also found R15C in 4 of 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, the R15C mutant raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium (2.90 mM vs 2.53 mM for wildtype), and the EC50 was significantly higher than with vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. After stimulation with extracellular calcium, cells transfected with the R15C mutant lost less surface CaSR than cells transfected with wildtype AP2S1, indicating reduced CaSR endocytosis.

Howles et al. (2016) identified a heterozygous R15C mutation in 1 of 3 unrelated FHH3 probands. Cinacalcet at 10 nM corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in cells expressing the cys15 mutant.

**.0002 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15HIS [p.H15; p.R15H]

Nesbit et al. (2013) found a heterozygous CGC-to-CAC change in the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-his (R15H) substitution, in 3 unrelated FHH probands who lacked CASR mutations: a woman diagnosed at age 19, a man diagnosed at age 73, and a woman diagnosed at age 49. All 3 had hypercalcemia (serum calcium 2.62 to 2.85 mmol/l). In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, the R15H mutant raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium (2.73 mM vs 2.53 mM for wildtype; p less than 0.0001).

Howles et al. (2016) identified a heterozygous R15H mutation in 1 of 3 unrelated FHH3 probands. Cinacalcet at 10 nM corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in cells expressing the his15 mutant.

**.0003 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15LEU [p.L15; p.R15L]

Nesbit et al. (2013) found a heterozygous CGC-to-CTC change in the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-leu (R15L) substitution, in 4 unrelated FHH probands or families who lacked CASR mutations. Ages at diagnosis ranged from 0.02 years to 52 years, and serum calcium levels were 3.03 to 3.3 mmol/l. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, the R15L mutant raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium (2.86 mM vs 2.53 mM for wildtype; p less than 0.0001).

In a Japanese female infant with hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, and poor weight gain, Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous R15L mutation in AP2S1, which they presumed to be de novo. No pathologic mutation was found in CASR or GNA11.
