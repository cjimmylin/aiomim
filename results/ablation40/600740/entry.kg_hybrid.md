### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene (602242) on chromosome 19q13.

### Description

Nesbit et al. (2013) described familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type 3 (FHH3) as an autosomal dominant disorder of extracellular calcium homeostasis that involves the parathyroids, kidneys, and bone. Like FHH1 and FHH2, it features lifelong elevation of serum calcium with inappropriately low urinary calcium excretion. FHH1 and FHH2 are usually associated with normal circulating PTH, but FHH3 is distinguished by increased serum PTH, hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia. Howles et al. (2016) stated that type 3 is clinically the most severe of the 3 types of familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia.

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) called the form of familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia (FBH) in a kindred from Oklahoma the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok). Hannan et al. (2010) noted that the 3 genetically distinct types of familial benign hypocalciuric hypercalcemia had been designated FBHH1, FBHH2, and FBHH3, and that FBHH3 is also referred to as the Oklahoma variant (FBHH-Ok).

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) described a large kindred with familial benign hypercalcemia. After the proposita was identified, they surveyed 46 kindred members from 5 generations, aged 1.5 to 91 years, by biochemical testing and/or chart review, and found 19 to be affected. Serum immunoreactive PTH (iPTH) was measured in 3 separate immunoassays in 15 hypercalcemic subjects. In this group, iPTH became supranormal by about age 30 years and appeared to rise further after that. Radiographic changes suggestive of osteomalacia were present in 3 of 5 affected adults older than 40 years who were studied. Two hypercalcemic adults underwent iliac crest biopsy after tetracycline labeling. In one of them, a 51-year-old woman, the biopsy confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. The authors concluded that the disorder may not be totally benign and that osteomalacia, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia, can develop in adulthood.

Trump et al. (1995) studied a 5-generation kindred from Oklahoma with 19 affected members, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected members. In this kindred, familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia was associated with a developmental elevation in serum PTH levels.

Hannan et al. (2010) investigated 40 members from 5 generations of the previously reported Oklahoma kindred. Affected status was established by hypercalcemia together with a low ratio (less than 0.01) of calcium clearance to creatinine clearance. Elevated serum PTH had been observed in some affected individuals older than 30 years. Three adults older than 40 years showed evidence of osteomalacia.

Nesbit et al. (2010) studied the kindred of a 43-year-old woman with familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia who had no CASR mutation. Her corrected serum calcium was 2.74 mmol/liter (normal, 2.15-2.55), her serum PTH was 47 pg/ml (normal, 10-65), and her ratio of urinary calcium clearance to creatinine clearance was 0.006. Of 24 members from 3 generations of the kindred, 16 had hypercalcemia with normal or elevated serum PTH concentrations and mild hypophosphatemia. The authors considered these features consistent with FHH3.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese female infant whose hypercalcemia was found on a routine laboratory test done because of poor weight gain on breastfeeding. At 49 days of age, her adjusted serum calcium was 13.1 mg/dL, intact PTH was 27 pg/mL, and urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg. There was no evidence of hyperparathyroidism, a PTHrP-producing neoplasm, or vitamin D excess. The authors noted that, apart from the hypercalciuria, these findings appeared consistent with defective calcium-sensing receptor-mediated signaling.

Howles et al. (2016) described 3 previously unreported unrelated probands with FHH3. A 34-year-old woman had a 12-year history of hypercalcemia and presented with fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. A 22-year-old man presented with hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. A 52-year-old woman had an approximately 20-year history of hypercalcemia and presented with headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy.

### Biochemical Features

In the kindred studied by McMurtry et al. (1992), affected individuals had normal serum alkaline phosphatase activity and creatinine levels. Their serum inorganic phosphate levels were lower than those of unaffected kindred members.

Nesbit et al. (2013) tabulated biochemical findings in 13 families or patients with AP2S1 mutations, whose ages ranged from 8 days to 78 years. Affected members of the Oklahoma kindred (13 females and 3 males; mean age at diagnosis 36 years) had a mean serum calcium of 2.88 mmol/l, mean serum phosphate of 0.77 mmol/l, mean PTH of 65 pg/ml, and a urinary calcium:creatinine clearance ratio of 0.003. Affected members of the Northern Ireland kindred (11 females and 5 males; mean age at diagnosis 32 years) had a mean serum calcium of 2.72 mmol/l, mean serum phosphate of 0.95 mmol/l, mean PTH of 60 pg/ml, and a urinary calcium:creatinine clearance ratio of 0.006. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Hannan et al. (2010) stated that familial benign hypocalciuric hypercalcemia is transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait with a high degree of penetrance. In the Oklahoma kindred, the disorder segregated with a single chromosome 19q13 haplotype. Nesbit et al. (2013) found heterozygous AP2S1 missense mutations in patients with FHH3, which they considered consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a presumably de novo heterozygous AP2S1 mutation in an affected infant.

### Mapping

In the Oklahoma kindred, Trump et al. (1995) used 17 polymorphic markers to test the candidate regions 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3 (FBH loci), 11q13 (MEN1), and 11p15 (PTH). Linkage to all of these regions was excluded (lod scores less than -2.00 at theta = 0.05-0.25), and analysis of multipoint crossovers with the LINKMAP program confirmed the exclusion. ⚠︎

Lloyd et al. (1999) carried out a genomewide search in the Oklahoma family using chromosome-specific sets of microsatellite polymorphisms. They established linkage between the disorder and 8 loci on chromosome 19q13, with a maximum lod score of 6.67 at theta = 0.00 with D19S606. Recombinants placed the locus in an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866.

Hannan et al. (2010) studied 34 informative members of the Oklahoma kindred, 19 of them affected, using 24 polymorphic loci (17 microsatellites and 7 SNPs). The peak 2-point lod score was 6.92 at theta = 0 with D19S606. Recombinants in 3 individuals refined the FBHH3 locus to a 4.1-Mb interval flanked centromerically by D19S112 and telomerically by rs245111. The syntenic region on mouse chromosome 7 contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs genes), but the authors found no human or primate homologs of these genes in the human 19q13 region. Comparative analysis showed that 2 conserved segments are inverted in man and that an 11.6-Mb mouse region containing 35 genes, including the 4 Gprc2a-rs genes, is absent from the human genome. The authors therefore concluded that FBHH3 cannot be attributed to abnormalities of these genes. ⚠︎

In a second kindred with FHH3, Nesbit et al. (2010) found that the disorder cosegregated with microsatellite and SNP loci on chromosome 19q13.3, with a peak lod score of 5.98 at theta = 0 with D19S412. Analysis of recombinants mapped the locus to a 3.46-Mb interval flanked centromerically by rs1990932 and telomerically by D19S604.

### Heterogeneity

Trump et al. (1995) found that the disorder in the Oklahoma kindred was not linked to markers that segregate with FBH, MEN1, or PTH, and concluded that there was further genetic heterogeneity and a third locus for FBH. Nesbit et al. (2010) noted that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia has 3 defined types: FHH1 on 3q21.1, caused by CASR mutations and accounting for more than 65% of patients; FHH2 on 19p; and FHH3 on 19q13. They suggested that FHH3 may account for the disorder in FHH patients who lack CASR mutations. Nesbit et al. (2013) found AP2S1 Arg15 mutations in more than 20% of FHH patients without CASR mutations. Howles et al. (2016) stated that type 1 is caused by CASR mutations, type 2 by GNA11 mutations, and type 3 by AP2S1 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Lloyd et al. (1999) sequenced the coding region, 5-prime UTR, and part of the promoter of the calmodulin III gene, which lies within the 19q13 interval, in an affected member of the Oklahoma family. They found no abnormalities and concluded that this gene is unlikely to be involved.

Hannan et al. (2010) sequenced the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries of 12 candidate genes from the FBHH3 interval in an affected member of the Oklahoma kindred and in an unrelated normal individual. These genes were PTH2, PTGIR, GNG8, EMP3, SPHK2, TULP2, NUCB1, HRC, RCN3, CYTH2, RRAS, and ATF5. No abnormalities were found, and RT-PCR excluded splicing abnormalities.

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on an affected individual from each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, from Oklahoma and from Northern Ireland. Within the 3.46-Mb FHH3 interval on 19q13.3, they found a single shared variant: a C-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, predicting an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. Dideoxynucleotide sequencing confirmed the variant, and restriction endonuclease analysis showed that it cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. The authors then sequenced AP2S1 in 50 unrelated FHH patients in whom CASR mutations had been excluded. They found 11 heterozygous missense mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 R15C, 3 R15H, and 4 R15L. The authors considered Arg15 a likely mutational hotspot because 3 different mutations at this residue were found in 13 unrelated probands and the residue was significantly conserved in 5,400 human exomes (p less than 10(-10)). In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, all 3 mutants raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium, indicating reduced sensitivity to extracellular calcium. The R15C mutant also reduced CaSR endocytosis.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) studied a Japanese infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria. They found no pathologic mutation in CASR or GNA11 and identified a presumably de novo heterozygous R15L mutation in AP2S1.

Howles et al. (2016) found that each of 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic FHH3 carried a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation causing an R15C, R15H, or R15L substitution.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that patients with the R15C, R15H, and R15L AP2S1 mutations had similar elevations in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium excretion. They concluded that there was no correlation between the specific mutation and the clinical phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that Arg15 of AP2-sigma-2 is conserved in all orthologs and in all human AP sigma subunit paralogs. In the AP2 crystal structure, Arg15 forms key contacts with the glutamine at position -4 of cargo dileucine motifs. Modeling predicted that the Cys15 and Leu15 substitutions would reduce affinity for the dileucine motif; the effect of His15 was harder to predict. CaSR has a C-terminal dileucine motif (RHQPLL, residues 1009-1014). In HEK293 cells, replacing Leu-Leu1013-1014 with Ala-Ala raised the EC50 of CaSR (3.63 vs 3.41 mM for wildtype; p less than 0.0001), indicating that the motif is functionally significant. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, the R15C, R15H, and R15L mutants raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium to 2.90, 2.73, and 2.86 mM, respectively, compared with 2.53 mM for wildtype (all p less than 0.0001). Cells with the Cys15 mutant had an EC50 significantly higher than cells with vector alone, which the authors suggested indicates a possible dominant-negative effect. After stimulation with extracellular calcium, cells transfected with wildtype AP2S1 showed a greater reduction in cell-surface CaSR than cells transfected with the R15C mutant or with vector alone. The authors concluded that AP2-sigma-2 has a role in CaSR endocytosis and in CaSR signaling, and that disruption by Arg15 mutants results in FHH3. Because AP2-sigma-2 is ubiquitously expressed, they suggested that Arg15 has a specific role, such as recognizing the CaSR dileucine motif, which would explain why the mutations cause a specific disorder of calcium homeostasis rather than a multisystem disease.

### Diagnosis

McMurtry et al. (1992) concluded that in their kindred, familial benign hypercalcemia in adults can be especially difficult to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism because serum iPTH may be elevated.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) concluded that the absence of hypocalciuria does not necessarily argue against an AP2S1 mutation. They suggested that their patient's early infantile age may have contributed to her hypercalciuria.

### Clinical Management

Fujisawa et al. (2013) fed their infant patient a low-calcium formula containing 2.6 mg/dL of calcium. She showed catch-up growth, and her serum calcium and urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio both decreased. Feeding a 2:1 mixture of low-calcium and standard formula maintained serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without a marked rise in serum PTH. The authors concluded that low-calcium formula is worth trying in infants with FHH.

Howles et al. (2016) found that cinacalcet at 10 nM corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in cells expressing CaSR and the Cys15, His15, or Leu15 AP2-sigma mutants. They then treated 3 FHH3 probands with oral cinacalcet, 30 to 60 mg daily, for 33 to 45 months. Serum calcium fell by more than 20% and symptoms abated. Serum phosphate rose and PTH fell, but both remained within the normal range. None of the probands developed nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia, but the authors stated that long-term surveillance would be needed.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15CYS [p.C15; p.R15C; p.Arg15Cys]

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on affected members of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, from Oklahoma and from Northern Ireland. Both carried a heterozygous C-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members of the 2 kindreds. The authors also found the R15C mutation in 4 of 50 unrelated FHH patients in whom CASR mutations had been excluded. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, the mutant raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium to 2.90 mM, compared with 2.53 mM for wildtype. This EC50 was also significantly higher than with vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. The mutant also reduced calcium-stimulated CaSR endocytosis.

Howles et al. (2016) identified a heterozygous R15C mutation in 1 of 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic FHH3. In vitro, cinacalcet corrected the abnormal signaling in cells expressing the Cys15 mutant.

**.0002 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15LEU [p.L15; p.R15L; p.Arg15Leu]

Nesbit et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous CGC-to-CTC change in the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-leu (R15L) substitution, in 4 of 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. Age at diagnosis ranged from 0.02 to 52 years, and serum calcium was 3.03 to 3.3 mmol/l. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, the mutant raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium to 2.86 mM, compared with 2.53 mM for wildtype.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a presumably de novo heterozygous R15L mutation in a Japanese female infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria. She had no pathologic mutation in CASR or GNA11.

Howles et al. (2016) identified a heterozygous R15L mutation in 1 of 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic FHH3.

**.0003 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15HIS [p.H15; p.R15H; p.Arg15His]

Nesbit et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous CGC-to-CAC change in the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-his (R15H) substitution, in 3 of 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. The carriers were a woman diagnosed at age 19 years, a man diagnosed at 73 years, and a woman diagnosed at 49 years. Their serum calcium ranged from 2.62 to 2.85 mmol/l. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, the mutant raised the EC50 for extracellular calcium to 2.73 mM, compared with 2.53 mM for wildtype.

Howles et al. (2016) identified a heterozygous R15H mutation in 1 of 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic FHH3.
