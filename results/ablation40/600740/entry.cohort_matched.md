### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene (602242) on chromosome 19q13.

### Description

Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (FHH3) is an autosomal dominant disorder of extracellular calcium homeostasis. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that FHH1 and FHH2 usually have normal circulating PTH and are generally asymptomatic. In contrast, FHH3 is characterized by increased serum PTH concentrations, hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia. Howles et al. (2016) described FHH3 as the most clinically severe of the 3 types of familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia. They stated that it is caused by heterozygous AP2S1 mutations resulting in Arg15Cys, Arg15His, or Arg15Leu substitutions, and that the mutant AP2-sigma proteins impair signal transduction mediated by the calcium-sensing receptor.

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) named this form of familial benign hypercalcemia (FBH) the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok), after the kindred in which it was first described. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) studied a large kindred with familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia. After the proposita was identified, they surveyed 46 kindred members from 5 generations, aged 1.5 to 91 years, biochemically and/or by chart review. Of these, 19 were affected. Fifteen hypercalcemic subjects were examined biochemically, and their serum immunoreactive PTH levels, measured in 3 separate immunoassays, became supranormal by about age 30 years and appeared to rise further thereafter. Affected members had lower serum inorganic phosphate than unaffected members, with normal serum alkaline phosphatase activity and normal serum creatinine. Five affected adults older than 40 years were studied radiographically, and 3 of them had changes suggestive of osteomalacia. In one of these, a 51-year-old woman, iliac crest biopsy confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. McMurtry et al. (1992) concluded that the disorder in this kindred may not be totally benign, because osteomalacia can develop in adulthood, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia. They suggested that it might be an unusual variant of familial benign hypercalcemia. ⚠︎

Trump et al. (1995) described a 5-generation kindred from Oklahoma, USA, with 19 affected members, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected members. In this kindred, familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia was associated with a developmental elevation in serum PTH levels.

Hannan et al. (2010) investigated 40 members from 5 generations of the Oklahoma kindred. The biochemical phenotype was established by hypercalcemia together with a low ratio (less than 0.01) of calcium clearance to creatinine clearance. Elevated serum PTH concentrations had been observed in some affected members older than 30 years, and 3 adults older than 40 years showed evidence of osteomalacia. The 34 family members included in the genetic analysis comprised 19 affected members, 13 unaffected members, and 2 unaffected spouses.

Nesbit et al. (2010) identified a second FHH3 kindred, spanning 3 generations. The proband was a 43-year-old woman with a corrected serum calcium of 2.74 mmol/liter (normal, 2.15-2.55 mmol/liter), a serum PTH of 47 pg/ml (normal, 10-65 pg/ml), and a urinary calcium clearance:creatinine clearance ratio of 0.006. Sixteen family members were hypercalcemic with normal or elevated serum PTH concentrations and mild hypophosphatemia.

Nesbit et al. (2013) investigated 32 members (17 affected and 15 unaffected) from 4 generations of the previously reported Oklahoma kindred (FHHOk) and 23 members (16 affected and 7 unaffected) from 3 generations of a second reported kindred (FHHNI). Affected members had hypercalcemia and hypocalciuria. The mean urinary calcium clearance:creatinine clearance ratio was 0.003 in the FHHOk kindred and 0.006 in the FHHNI kindred. The authors also studied 11 unrelated probands or families with AP2S1 mutations who had hypercalcemia, most with low urinary calcium excretion. Patients with AP2S1 mutations ranged in age from 8 days to 78 years. ⚠︎

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese female infant whose hypercalcemia was found on a routine laboratory test performed for poor weight gain on breast feeding. At 49 days of age, her serum calcium (adjusted by Payne's formula) was 13.1 mg/dL, intact PTH was 27 pg/mL, and the urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg, indicating hypercalciuria. There was no evidence of hyperparathyroidism, a PTHrP-producing neoplasm, or vitamin D excess.

Howles et al. (2016) described 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic hypercalcemia. A 34-year-old woman with a 12-year history of hypercalcemia had fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. A 22-year-old man had hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. A 52-year-old woman with an approximately 20-year history of hypercalcemia had headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy.

### Inheritance

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that heterozygous AP2S1 mutations cosegregated with the disorder over 4 generations in 2 kindreds and occurred in further unrelated patients, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a presumably de novo heterozygous AP2S1 mutation in an affected infant. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Trump et al. (1995) tested 17 polymorphic markers in the 5-generation Oklahoma kindred. They excluded linkage to the FBH regions on 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3, to the MEN1 region on 11q13, and to the PTH region on 11p15 (lod scores less than -2.00 at theta = 0.05-0.25). Multipoint crossover analysis and the LINKMAP program confirmed the exclusion.

Lloyd et al. (1999) performed a genomewide search in the Oklahoma family and found linkage to 8 loci on chromosome 19q13. The maximum lod score was 6.67 at theta = 0.00 for D19S606. Recombinants placed the locus in an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866.

In the Oklahoma kindred, Hannan et al. (2010) found linkage to 10 of 14 informative 19q13 microsatellite loci, with a maximum 2-point lod score of 6.92 at theta = 0 for D19S606. Recombinants in 3 individuals, together with SNP analysis, refined the locus to a 4.1-Mb interval flanked by D19S112 and rs245111. This interval contained 135 protein-coding genes and 24 predicted genes. The syntenic region on mouse chromosome 7 contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs1, -rs2, -rs3, and -rs5), but no human or primate homologs of these were present in the human interval. Comparative analysis showed that 2 conserved segments were inverted between mouse and human, and that an 11.6-Mb mouse region containing 35 genes, including the 4 Gprc2a-rs genes, was absent from the human genome. The authors concluded that FBHH3 could not be attributed to Gprc2a-rs defects.

Nesbit et al. (2010) studied a 3-generation FHH kindred in which the proband had no CASR mutation. FHH cosegregated with chromosome 19q13.3 markers, with a peak lod score of 5.98 at 0% recombination with D19S412. Analysis of recombinants mapped the locus to a 3.46-Mb interval flanked by SNP rs1990932 and D19S604.

### Heterogeneity

Trump et al. (1995) found that the disorder in the Oklahoma kindred was not linked to markers that segregate with FBH, MEN1, or PTH. They concluded that FBH shows further genetic heterogeneity, with a third locus. Lloyd et al. (1999) likewise noted that excluding linkage to the CASR locus on 3q and to the FBH locus on 19p13.3 in this family indicated genetic heterogeneity of familial benign hypercalcemia. ⚠︎

Nesbit et al. (2010) noted that FHH is genetically heterogeneous. FHH1 (3q21.1, CASR) accounts for more than 65% of patients, and FHH2 (19p) and FHH3 (19q13) had each previously been described in single North American kindreds. They suggested that FHH3 may explain the disorder in FHH patients without CASR mutations. Nesbit et al. (2013) similarly noted that FHH1, FHH2, and FHH3 are autosomal dominant disorders mapping to 3q21.1, 19p, and 19q13.3, respectively.

### Molecular Genetics

Lloyd et al. (1999) sequenced the coding region, 5-prime UTR, and part of the promoter of the calmodulin III gene, which lies within the 19q13 interval, in an affected member of the Oklahoma family. They found no abnormalities and concluded that this gene was unlikely to be involved.

Hannan et al. (2010) sequenced the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries of 12 candidate genes in the FBHH3 interval that are expressed in kidney and/or parathyroid in an affected member of the Oklahoma kindred. The genes were PTH2, PTGIR, GNG8, EMP3, SPHK2, TULP2, NUCB1, HRC, RCN3, CYTH2, RRAS, and ATF5. They found no abnormalities, and RT-PCR ruled out splicing defects.

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in an affected individual from each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, the Oklahoma kindred (FHHOk) and the FHHNI kindred. The only novel variant they found within the 3.46-Mb FHH3 interval on 19q13.3 was a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, which encodes AP2-sigma-2, resulting in an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. Restriction enzyme analysis showed that the mutation cosegregated with the disorder in members (32 affected and 23 unaffected) from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. The authors then analyzed 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations and identified 11 heterozygous missense AP2S1 mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 were R15C, 3 were R15H, and 4 were R15L. They concluded that Arg15 mutations may underlie FHH in more than 20% of patients without CaSR mutations. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, each of the R15C, R15H, and R15L mutants shifted the calcium concentration-response curve to the right, with significantly higher EC50 values than wildtype, indicating decreased sensitivity to extracellular calcium. With the R15C mutant, the EC50 was higher than with vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect.

In a Japanese female infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria, Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a presumably de novo heterozygous R15L mutation in the AP2S1 gene. No pathologic mutation was found in the CASR or GNA11 genes.

Howles et al. (2016) reported 3 unrelated symptomatic probands, each of whom had a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation causing an Arg15Cys, Arg15His, or Arg15Leu substitution. In cells expressing the calcium-sensing receptor together with the Cys15, His15, or Leu15 AP2-sigma mutants, cinacalcet (10 nM) corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and the MAPK-serum response element reporter responses to extracellular calcium. This indicated that allosteric modulation of CASR can correct the loss of function caused by AP2S1 mutations.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that patients with the 3 different AP2S1 Arg15 mutations, aged 8 days to 78 years, had similar elevations in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium excretion. They concluded that there was no correlation between mutation and clinical phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

From the AP2 crystal structure, Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that Arg15 of AP2-sigma-2 is conserved in all orthologs and in the human AP sigma paralogs. Together with Arg21 of AP2-alpha-1, it forms key contacts with the glutamine at position -4 of the dileucine motif of cargo proteins, and substitution by Cys or Leu was predicted to reduce affinity for this motif. Mutating the C-terminal dileucine motif of CaSR (Leu-Leu1013-1014 to Ala-Ala) in transfected HEK293 cells shifted the calcium concentration-response curve to the right with a significantly higher EC50, indicating that the motif is functionally significant. In an intact-cell ELISA, cells transfected with wildtype AP2S1 showed a further reduction in cell-surface CaSR after stimulation with high extracellular calcium, compared with cells transfected with the R15C mutant or with vector. This indicated that AP2-sigma-2 has a role in CaSR endocytosis that is disrupted by the Arg15 mutants. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that although AP2-sigma-2 is ubiquitously expressed, Arg15 mutations cause a specific disorder of extracellular calcium homeostasis rather than a multisystem disorder. They suggested that this may be because Arg15 has a specific role, such as recognizing the CaSR dileucine motif.

### Diagnosis

McMurtry et al. (1992) noted that in their kindred, familial benign hypercalcemia in adults could be especially difficult to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism, because serum immunoreactive PTH levels may be elevated.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) concluded that the absence of hypocalciuria does not necessarily rule out an AP2S1 mutation. They suggested that the early infantile age of their patient may have contributed to her hypercalciuria.

### Clinical Management

In a Japanese infant with FHH3, Fujisawa et al. (2013) found that a low calcium formula (2.6 mg/dL calcium) led to catch-up growth and lowered both serum calcium and the urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio. A 2:1 mixture of low calcium and standard formula kept serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without a marked rise in PTH. The authors concluded that low calcium formula is worth trying in infants with FHH.

Howles et al. (2016) treated 3 unrelated probands with FHH3, who had heterozygous AP2S1 Arg15Cys, Arg15His, or Arg15Leu mutations, with oral cinacalcet (30 to 60 mg daily) for 33 to 45 months. Treatment reduced serum calcium by more than 20% and relieved symptoms. Serum phosphate rose and PTH fell, but both stayed within normal ranges. None of the patients developed nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia, although the authors noted that long-term surveillance would be required.

### Population Genetics

Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that 3 different mutations, all altering only the Arg15 residue, occurred in 13 unrelated probands. Arg15 was significantly conserved in 5,400 human exomes, indicating that this residue is likely a mutational hotspot.
