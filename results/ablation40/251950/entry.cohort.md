## MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS; MMLA

### Phenotype-Gene Relationships

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Disease caused by biallelic PNPLA8 variants has been described with a range of presentations. Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) grouped them into 3 forms: a congenital form with congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early death; childhood neurodegeneration with progressive microcephaly; and adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with peripheral neuropathy. Shukla et al. (2018) considered the disorder a progressive neurodegenerative mitochondrial disease. Nakamura et al. (2024) concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum, ranging from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly to childhood-onset neurodegeneration.

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) reported a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described a 23-year-old man with mitochondrial myopathy. He had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that had caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy presented. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and severe lactic acidosis developed at one stage of the illness. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids, which was confirmed by histochemistry and thin-layer chromatography.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a girl (CMH193) with progressive muscle weakness. Toe-walking was noted in her first year. At age 2 years she had a positive Gowers sign, and by age 3 years she could no longer walk. Other features included hypotonia, complex partial seizures, poor weight gain, a lean body habitus, dystonia, dysarthria, dysmetria, spasticity, and tight heel cords. Laboratory studies showed lactic acidosis with an elevated anion gap, elevated blood pyruvate, and CSF 5-methyltetrahydrofolate deficiency. Creatine phosphokinase was normal. Brain MRI showed increased signal in the medial globus pallidus. Muscle biopsy showed variable fiber size with atrophic fibers but no ragged red fibers. Electron microscopy showed abnormal mitochondrial cristae, osmiophilic inclusions, and subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria. Respiratory chain function was normal. She was last reported at age 7 years.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls with variable but similar features: microcephaly, severe global developmental delay, spasticity, lactic acidosis, and progressive cerebellar atrophy. Patient 1, the second child of a consanguineous Indian couple, was evaluated on the sixth postnatal day for congenital microcephaly and seizures. She had low birth weight, multifocal clonic seizures in the neonatal period, spasticity of all 4 limbs, and overriding cranial sutures. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. Patient 2, of Indian/Fijian ancestry and born to nonconsanguineous parents, developed normally until 13 months of age and then regressed. Her features included epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures, severe global developmental delay, absent speech, gastrostomy tube feeding, central hypotonia, acquired microcephaly, choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, spasticity, and joint contractures. Brain MRI showed delayed myelination, progressive cerebellar atrophy, brainstem and cerebral atrophy, and a thin corpus callosum, and MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. EEG showed multifocal epileptiform discharges with background slowing, and visual evoked potentials were abnormal. Echocardiography detected hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis. Blood lactate was elevated on several occasions, and GDF15 was elevated. She also had obstructive sleep apnea and restrictive lung disease. Muscle electron microscopy showed enlarged, irregular mitochondria, but respiratory chain complex I-IV activities were normal. She was last reported at age 15 years.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset, severe, progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) reported 2 Lithuanian adult sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy. The disease advanced markedly in their teens and progressed only slightly in adulthood. Cognition was preserved, and there was no visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver dysfunction. The brother, aged 49 years at last report, first developed lower limb weakness and distal lower limb muscle atrophy at age 14 years and became nonambulant at age 44. Examination at 45 years showed mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus. Other findings were axial ataxia, tetraparesis, decreased muscle tone, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, dysmetria, length-dependent hypoesthesia in the lower limbs, and sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. Brain imaging showed mild cerebellar atrophy, and laboratory studies were normal. His sister, aged 21 years at last report, developed difficulty walking and weakness of both lower limbs at age 14 years. She had distal upper and lower limb muscle atrophy, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia, and appendicular dysmetria that was more pronounced in the lower limbs. She was unable to maintain an upright posture and had an axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy. Cranial nerve examination and cognitive evaluation were normal, and EMG showed no myopathic changes. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 (family I) had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 (family II) had infantile-onset seizures, progressive microcephaly, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 new patients with biallelic PNPLA8 variants. In the 12 families with neurologic disease, they found central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). The 8 severely affected patients (patients 1 to 8) were from Japan (2), Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Turkey, and Lebanon, and all but the 2 Japanese patients were born to consanguineous parents. Families 7 and 8 had been reported previously. These patients had congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), no developmental milestones, early-onset seizures (beginning between day 1 and 7 months of age), a simplified gyral pattern, pontocerebellar hypoplasia, and progressive cerebral atrophy. Patient 1 died at 4 years, patient 2 at 10 months, and patient 8 at 8 months of age. The patients from families 9 and 10 had intermediate severity, with cerebellar atrophy that was very mild in patient 10. Four patients had a relatively mild phenotype: 3 Iranian sibs born to consanguineous parents (family 11) and an Egyptian woman born to consanguineous parents (family 12). All 4 attained walking (4 of 4) and then had regression of milestones (4 of 4). They also had ataxia (3 of 4), mild or no intellectual disability (3 of 4), and isolated or absent seizures (4 of 4), together with hypotonia, dystonia, a neuromuscular disorder, and cerebellar atrophy. One of the sibs in family 11 had seizures from age 31 years. Nakamura et al. (2024) also reviewed 25 affected individuals from 20 families with biallelic PNPLA8 variants.

### Biochemical Features

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin levels appeared lower in patient skin fibroblasts than in control fibroblasts, with no significant correlation with phenotypic severity. Fibroblasts from 1 severely affected patient had reduced maximal mitochondrial respiration but normal mitochondrial morphology.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MMLA is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Saunders et al. (2015) showed that each of the 2 PNPLA8 variants in their patient had been inherited from a carrier parent, and Shukla et al. (2018) likewise found that the variants in their patients were inherited from carrier parents.

### Molecular Genetics

In a girl with MMLA, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: p.Asn112HisfsTer29 and p.Leu659AlafsTer4. Segregation analysis showed that each variant had been inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither variant. Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle showed a dramatic decrease of the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 (iPLA2-gamma) bands compared with control muscle, consistent with a null genotype. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project or in an internal database of 1,913 samples.

In an Indian girl born to consanguineous parents, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in PNPLA8 (c.517G-T, Glu173Ter; NM_001256007.1); both parents were heterozygous. In a second, unrelated girl, they identified compound heterozygous mutations in PNPLA8: a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT; Leu759AlafsTer4) and a nonsense mutation (c.419C-A; Ser140Ter). Each variant had been inherited from a carrier parent.

By whole-exome sequencing in a patient with prenatal-onset, severe, progressive neurodegeneration, Masih et al. (2021) identified a novel homozygous missense variant in the patatin domain of PNPLA8 (c.1874A-G).

By whole-exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing in a Lithuanian man with adolescent-onset ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in PNPLA8: c.2269del (Thr757GlnfsTer10) and the previously described c.2275_2276del (Leu759AlafsTer4). The c.2275_2276del variant was maternally inherited. Paternal DNA was not available, but the sequencing data showed that the 2 variants were in trans. Sanger sequencing confirmed both variants in his affected sister.

By exome sequencing, Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) identified a loss-of-function variant in PNPLA8 (c.1231C-T; Arg411Ter) in family I and a missense variant (c.1559T-A; Val520Asp) in family II.

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified biallelic PNPLA8 variants in 12 families with neurologic disease. All 14 variants they identified had frequencies below 0.001% in population databases. In patient-derived lymphoblastoid cell lines, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant from patient 1 caused skipping of exons 6 to 8. On immunoblotting, skin fibroblasts from patient 1 showed a marked reduction in the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, indicating a null effect. The splicing effects of c.2075-2A-G (patient 2) and c.1684-2A-G (patient 5) could not be tested. The frameshift variant p.Glu296LysfsTer6 in severely affected patient 6 also had a null effect. In contrast, patient 12, who had a mild phenotype and the p.Leu759AlafsTer4 variant, showed weak expression of the 77-kDa protein, suggesting residual protein function. The missense variant from a previously reported severely affected patient (homozygous p.Val520Asp) gave normal protein levels.

Nakamura et al. (2024) also identified homozygous N-terminal frameshift variants in PNPLA8, affecting only the 88-kDa isoform, in 2 patients from 2 unrelated Iranian families in a non-neurologic cohort. One patient had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss. In the optic atrophy family, an affected sib of the proband was heterozygous for the variant, and the authors considered the significance of these variants undetermined.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that all PNPLA8 variants reported to date introduced premature stop codons. Because so few patients were known, they concluded that no genotype-phenotype correlation could be drawn.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that both truncating variants in their sibs lay in the last coding exon of PNPLA8. They suggested that such transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and possibly retain partial enzyme activity. This would explain a less severe phenotype than the severe early-childhood neurodegeneration reported when c.2275_2276del occurs in trans with another truncating variant.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype of PNPLA8-related disease. They suggested that the site of the variant, rather than its type, correlated strongly with age at onset, and that the PNPLA8-related conditions form a spectrum of overlapping features that line up with different ages of onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were associated with the severe end of the spectrum. All patients with the mild, late-onset movement disorder phenotype carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10 or p.Leu759AlafsTer4), which were thought to escape nonsense-mediated decay and leave residual protein.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) did not define the metabolic defect in their patient. They proposed that an abnormality in the Krebs cycle caused the overproduction of lactate and the lipid accumulation in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) found normal SDH and COX activity and unaffected electron transport chain function in patient muscle. They suggested that altered homeostasis of the inner mitochondrial membrane, rather than a primary respiratory chain defect, underlies PNPLA8-related disease.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that cerebral organoids from PNPLA8-knockout and patient-derived iPSCs had a smaller subventricular zone, fewer PAX6+HOPX+ basal radial glial cells, and fewer SATB2+ upper-layer neurons. Deep-layer neurons and basal intermediate progenitors were unchanged, and the authors suggested an altered S phase in apical radial glia. PNPLA8-knockout neural progenitor cells had reduced lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid, but mitochondrial function and cardiolipin were normal. Adding lysophosphatidic acid partly restored the number of basal radial glial cells in patient-derived organoids. Spatial transcriptomics of the ventricular zone in patient-derived organoids showed enrichment of gene sets for the cell cycle, neuronal differentiation, and lysophospholipid pathways, and depletion of basal radial glia gene sets. The authors suggested that loss of PNPLA8 alters the fate specification of apical radial glia.

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) observed marked improvement in their patient's myopathy after he started prednisone and thiamine, and the improvement persisted after these drugs were withdrawn.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found profound alterations in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism in iPLA2-gamma-null mice. Hippocampal cardiolipin was markedly elevated, with a shift to shorter-chain molecular species. Plasmenylethanolamine content was decreased, and oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species and ceramides were increased. The hippocampus contained enlarged heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. Purification and proteomic analysis identified these structures as degenerating mitochondria. The authors concluded that loss of iPLA2-gamma causes a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder with autophagy and cognitive dysfunction.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that the clinical features and mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities in their patient closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice have mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 334AATT [c.334_337delaatt; p.N112Hfs; p.N112fs]

In a girl with MMLA, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT) causing a frameshift and premature termination (Asn112HisfsTer29). The other was a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG; Leu659AlafsTer4). Each variant had been inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither. Western blot analysis of patient muscle showed a dramatic decrease of the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 bands. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project or in an internal database of 1,913 samples.

**.0002 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 1975AG [c.1975_1976delag; p.L659Afs; p.L659fs]

In a girl with MMLA, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG) causing a frameshift and premature termination (Leu659AlafsTer4). The other was a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT; Asn112HisfsTer29). The variants had been inherited from carrier parents, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither. PNPLA8 bands were dramatically decreased in patient muscle.

**.0003 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, GLU173TER [c.517G>T; p.E173*]

In an Indian girl born to consanguineous parents, who had congenital microcephaly, neonatal seizures, spasticity, and an elevated lipid-lactate peak on brain MR spectroscopy, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous c.517G-T transversion in the PNPLA8 gene (NM_001256007.1), resulting in a glu173-to-ter (E173X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation.

**.0004 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 2275CT [c.2269del; c.2275_2276del; c.2275_2276delct; p.L759Afs; p.L759fs; p.T757Qfs; p.T757fs]

In a girl of Indian/Fijian ancestry who had regression from 13 months of age, epilepsia partialis continua, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and elevated blood lactate, Shukla et al. (2018) identified compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT) causing a frameshift and premature termination (Leu759AlafsTer4). The other was a c.419C-A transversion resulting in a ser140-to-ter (S140X) substitution. The variants had been inherited from carrier parents.

In 2 Lithuanian sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified compound heterozygosity for the c.2275_2276del mutation (Leu759AlafsTer4), which was maternally inherited, and a 1-bp deletion (c.2269del) resulting in a frameshift (Thr757GlnfsTer10). Sequencing data showed that the 2 variants were in trans, but paternal DNA was not available. Both variants lie in the last coding exon. ⚠︎

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified homozygosity for the p.Leu759AlafsTer4 mutation in an Egyptian woman born to consanguineous parents (patient 12). She had a mild phenotype with developmental regression after walking, mild intellectual disability, hypotonia, dystonia, and cerebellar atrophy, and her cells showed weak expression of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, suggesting residual function. Three Iranian sibs born to consanguineous parents (family 11), who had a similarly mild phenotype, carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10 or p.Leu759AlafsTer4).

**.0008 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**

**.0009 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**

The patient's skin fibroblasts showed a marked reduction in the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, indicating a null effect, and reduced maximal mitochondrial respiration.
