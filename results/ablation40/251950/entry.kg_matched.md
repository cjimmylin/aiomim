## MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS; MMLA

### Phenotype-Gene Relationships

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. Saunders et al. (2015) described a girl with a progressive mitochondrial myopathy, neurologic decline and lactic acidosis, whose muscle showed degenerating mitochondria. Later reports widened the phenotype. Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) recognized congenital, infantile, childhood and adult-onset forms. Nakamura et al. (2024) concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease is a continuum, ranging from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy to childhood-onset neurodegeneration.

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described a 23-year-old man with a mitochondrial myopathy. He had a high-output circulatory state and a cardiomyopathy, which caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy presented. His venous lactate levels were elevated, and at one stage of the illness he developed severe lactic acidosis. Electron microscopy of his muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids. Histochemistry and thin-layer chromatography confirmed the lipid accumulation.

Saunders et al. (2015) described a 7-year-old girl (CMH193) who was evaluated for suspected mitochondrial myopathy. She had no symptoms and met her developmental milestones in the first year, apart from toe-walking. She had a positive Gowers sign at age 2 years and had lost the ability to walk by age 3. Her disease progressed to include proximal muscle weakness, dysarthria, a decline in fine motor skills, dysmetria, and hypotonia that evolved into spasticity. She also developed dystonia and complex partial seizures, with left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. She had a very lean body habitus and did not gain weight, and her lactic acidosis required bicarbonate supplementation. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle increased signal in the medial globus pallidus on both sides. This was no longer seen 2 years later, which the authors suggested reflected maturation of myelination. MR spectroscopy and head CT were normal. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at age 4 showed variable fiber size, with isolated small atrophic fibers of all subtypes and no ragged red fibers. On electron microscopy, the mitochondria showed marked disarray of their cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions. There were also subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggesting degenerating mitochondria. Secondary lysosomes containing mitochondria-derived residual bodies were increased, but no pathogenic variants were found in lipofuscinosis genes.

Shukla et al. (2018) described a female infant (patient 1) born to consanguineous Indian parents. She was evaluated on day 6 of life for congenital microcephaly (head circumference 29 cm, -4 SD at birth) and for seizures that had begun on day 2. She had multifocal clonic seizures and spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. TORCH testing was negative.

Shukla et al. (2018) also described a girl (patient 2) of Indian/Fijian origin born to nonconsanguineous parents. She developed normally until about 13 months of age and then regressed, with episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures that were difficult to control. She became nonverbal and required gastrostomy feeding. She had acquired microcephaly (49 cm, -4.7 SD at 15 years), central hypotonia with muscle weakness, choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal weakness, absent reflexes and severe contractures. Brain MRI showed progressive atrophy of the cerebellum, brainstem, and parietal and occipital lobes, with thinning of the corpus callosum, and MRS showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. Her muscle biopsy showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria with membrane whorls and disrupted cristae, with normal complex I-IV activities. She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, elevated blood lactate, elevated GDF15, reduced visual evoked potentials and obstructive sleep apnea.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with severe, progressive neurodegeneration of prenatal onset who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) reported 2 adult sibs, a brother and a sister, with slowly progressive ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy beginning at age 14 years. Their cognition stayed normal over time. At age 14 the brother developed weakness and distal atrophy of the lower limbs, with ataxia and a gait disturbance. By age 30 he had marked axial ataxia, tetraparesis, hypotonia, wasting of the calves and feet, absent lower-limb reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, dysmetria and mild length-dependent hypoesthesia. Nerve conduction studies showed a sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. He could no longer walk from age 44. At age 45 he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus. Brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy, and his MMSE score was 30/30. His sister had difficulty walking and weakness of both lower limbs from age 14 years. At age 21 she had distal atrophy of the upper and lower limbs, absent lower-limb reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia and appendicular dysmetria, and she could not keep an upright posture. Her cranial nerve and cognitive examinations were normal. Nerve conduction studies showed an axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, and EMG showed no myopathic changes. Neither sib had cognitive decline or visual, auditory, cardiac or liver dysfunction. The disease advanced markedly in the teens but progressed only slightly through adulthood. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families with PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathy. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 had progressive microcephaly, seizures beginning in infancy, progressive cortical atrophy, loss of white matter, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype. They also reviewed the phenotypes of 6 previously reported patients with biallelic PNPLA8 variants. These were a congenital-onset form with congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy and early death; childhood neurodegeneration with progressive microcephaly; and an adult-onset form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy. ⚠︎

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals (6 male, 8 female) with neurologic manifestations from 12 unrelated families with biallelic PNPLA8 variants. The families were of Japanese, Saudi Arabian, Iraqi, Afghan, Pakistani, Turkish, Lebanese, Egyptian, Iranian and Indian/Fijian origin. Ages at last assessment ranged from 3 days to 33 years. Common features were central hypotonia (13/13), dystonia (8/12), seizures (10/14) and cerebellar atrophy (12/12). A severe group of 8 patients (Families 1-8) had congenital microcephaly (5/7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7/7), peripheral spasticity (7/8) and seizures beginning within the first 7 months of life (8/8). In this group, neuroimaging showed a simplified gyral pattern (6/6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7/7) and severe progressive atrophy of the cerebral cortex (5/5). Four patients (Families 11-12) had a relatively mild phenotype. All 4 learned to walk and later lost milestones; 3 of 4 had ataxia, 3 of 4 had mild or no intellectual disability, and seizures were isolated or absent. Brain MRI in this group showed little or no loss of cerebral cortical volume but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Patients in Families 9 and 10 were of intermediate severity and also had cerebellar atrophy. Combining these patients with 11 previously reported individuals (25 individuals from 20 families), the authors concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease is a continuum, from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy to childhood-onset neurodegeneration. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

In the girl they reported, Saunders et al. (2015) found elevated blood lactate (3.8-5.6 mmol/L) and pyruvate and a consistently elevated anion gap, but the lactate:pyruvate ratio was not elevated. Muscle SDH and COX activities and electron transport chain function were normal. The plasma acylcarnitine profile and creatine phosphokinase were also normal. CSF showed a deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. The authors suggested that these results pointed to mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect.

Shukla et al. (2018) found that their patient 2 had repeatedly elevated blood lactate. On MRS she had a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak in the brain parenchyma and CSF. At age 14 years her growth and differentiation factor-15 was elevated (1293 pg/ml; normal below 750 pg/ml). The activities of electron transport chain complexes I-IV in her muscle were normal.

In skin fibroblasts from their Patient 1, Nakamura et al. (2024) found normal mitochondrial morphology on electron microscopy. However, extracellular flux analysis showed significantly lower maximal respiration than in controls. Cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin levels appeared lower in patient fibroblasts than in controls, but did not correlate significantly with phenotypic severity.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of PNPLA8-related mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is autosomal recessive. Saunders et al. (2015) found that both PNPLA8 variants in their patient were inherited from healthy carrier parents. Shukla et al. (2018) found that the biallelic PNPLA8 variants in 2 patients were inherited from heterozygous carrier parents. Nakamura et al. (2024) showed autosomal recessive inheritance in 12 unrelated families with biallelic PNPLA8 variants, several of which had first-cousin consanguinity.

### Molecular Genetics

In a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) used trio exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4). Both were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and each was inherited from a carrier parent. Two healthy sibs carried neither variant. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle showed a dramatic decrease in the 88-, 63- and 52-kD iPLA2-gamma bands compared with 3 control muscle samples, consistent with a null genotype. A 110-kD immunoreactive band was present in controls but absent in the patient. Mass spectrometry identified it as HSPA4, which the authors attributed to nonspecific antibody binding.

In patient 1, born to consanguineous Indian parents, Shukla et al. (2018) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsense mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (c.517G-T, E173X). Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant and showed that both parents were heterozygous. In patient 2, trio whole-exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous PNPLA8 mutations: a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT) causing a frameshift (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), and a nonsense mutation (c.419C-A, S140X). Sanger sequencing confirmed the variants, and each was inherited from a carrier parent.

In a patient with severe, progressive neurodegeneration of prenatal onset who died in infancy, Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous missense mutation (c.1874A-G) in the patatin domain of the PNPLA8 gene.

In 2 sibs with ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy beginning in adolescence, Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in exon 11 of the PNPLA8 gene. One was a novel 1-bp deletion (c.2269del, p.Thr757GlnfsTer10), absent from ExAC, 1000 Genomes and gnomAD, which the authors classified as pathogenic under ACMG criteria. The other was the previously reported 2-bp deletion c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), inherited from the heterozygous mother. Paternal DNA was unavailable, but exome and Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 variants were in trans. The variants were confirmed in the affected sister.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing to study 2 families with PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathy. In Family I they identified a loss-of-function mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (c.1231C-T, R411X). In Family II they identified a missense mutation (c.1559T-A, V520D).

Nakamura et al. (2024) used exome or genome sequencing, with segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing, to study 14 patients from 12 families. They identified 14 PNPLA8 variants, 12 of them novel: 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries and 2 missense variants. Each variant had a frequency below 0.001% in population databases including gnomAD. Twelve of the 14 individuals carried combinations of nonsense, frameshift and splice-site variants, suggesting loss of function. In Patient 1, a Japanese boy from a nonconsanguineous family in the severe group who died at age 4 years, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant caused aberrant splicing with skipping of exons 6-8 in lymphoblastoid cells. His skin fibroblasts showed a significant reduction of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, indicating a null effect. The frameshift mutation p.Glu296Lysfs*6 in Patient 6, also in the severe group, likewise had a null effect on PNPLA8 protein expression in skin fibroblasts. SpliceAI predicted loss of the splice acceptor site for the intronic variants c.2075-2A-G in Patient 2 (delta score 0.97) and c.1684-2A-G in Patient 5 (delta score 0.93), both in the severe group. Their effect on splicing was not tested experimentally. Fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for the previously reported missense mutation p.Val520Asp, who had a severe phenotype, showed protein expression similar to that of controls. The authors also identified 2 individuals from 2 unrelated Iranian families in a non-neurologic cohort who were homozygous for distinct N-terminal frameshift variants in PNPLA8 affecting only the largest (88-kDa) isoform. One had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss. An affected sib in the optic atrophy family was heterozygous, and the authors considered these variants of undetermined significance. Fibroblasts from Patient 14, who carried p.Val7*, showed weak expression of the 77-kDa protein.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) observed that the 3 known patients with PNPLA8 variants had similar but variable phenotypes, ranging from congenital microcephaly with neonatal seizures to regression after normal early development. They stated that a genotype-phenotype correlation could not yet be drawn because so few individuals had been reported. ⚠︎

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that the c.2275_2276del mutation had previously been found in compound heterozygosity with another truncating variant in patients with severe neurodegeneration beginning in early childhood. In their sibs, combined with c.2269del, it caused a less severe neurologic phenotype. Because both truncating variants lie in the last coding exon of PNPLA8, the authors suggested that the transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and perhaps keep partial enzyme activity.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) suggested that the age at onset of PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathies correlates strongly with the site of the variant rather than its type. They also suggested that the congenital, infantile, childhood and adult-onset forms share some features and form a spectrum in which the clinical features track the age at onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) compared their patients with 11 previously reported patients (25 individuals from 20 families). Biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms, including at least the 77-kDa isoform, were associated with the severe end of the spectrum: early-onset epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. Patients of intermediate severity carried a loss-of-function variant, missense variants or the C-terminal p.Leu759Alafs*4. Biallelic C-terminal frameshifts were associated with a milder phenotype with late-onset movement disorder. All patients with the relatively mild neurodegenerative phenotype (Families 11-12 and 2 previously reported adults) carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants, either p.Thr757Glnfs*10 or p.Leu759Alafs*4. Fibroblasts from Patient 12, who had the mild phenotype, showed weak expression of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, suggesting residual function. The authors proposed that p.Leu759Alafs*4 escapes nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. They suggested that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting the 77- and 74-kDa isoforms are sufficient to cause the severe phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) did not define the metabolic defect in their patient. They postulated an abnormality in the Krebs cycle that led to overproduction of lactate and to lipid accumulation in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) suggested that, as in PLA2G6-related disease, the primary mechanism of PNPLA8-related disease may be degeneration of the mitochondrial inner membrane. In this model, electron transport and ATP synthesis proteins would be affected only secondarily, through altered membrane homeostasis. They also speculated that loss of the 110-kD band might contribute to pathogenesis.

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that all PNPLA8 variants reported up to that time introduced premature stop codons and were likely to cause a truncated protein or nonsense-mediated decay. The abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes in their patient 2 resembled the findings in Pnpla8-null mice. The authors suggested that the human phenotype closely resembled the progressive neurodegeneration seen in the null mice.

Nakamura et al. (2024) made PNPLA8 knockout cerebral organoids from CRISPR/Cas9-edited human iPSCs. At 12 weeks, the organoids had smaller subventricular zone-like regions, fewer PAX6+HOPX+ basal radial glial cells and fewer SATB2+ upper-layer neurons. Ventricular zone size, deep-layer neurons, intermediate progenitors, mitosis and apoptosis were unchanged, and increased BrdU labeling suggested an altered S-phase length in the ventricular zone. Organoids derived from iPSCs of Patient 1 reproduced the knockout phenotype compared with organoids from his father, confirming the loss-of-function nature of his variants. Spatial transcriptomics of the ventricular zone of the patient's organoids showed enrichment of gene sets for neuron differentiation, cell cycle and lysophospholipid pathways, with reduced basal radial glia gene signatures. The authors suggested that loss of PNPLA8 alters the fate specification of apical radial glial cells. Mitochondrial morphology and OXPHOS activity were essentially unaffected in PNPLA8 knockout neural progenitor cells (NPCs). The authors suggested that NPCs depend mainly on glycolysis, whereas mitochondrial dysfunction in OXPHOS-dependent neurons might contribute to progressive cerebral and cerebellar neurodegeneration. Knockout NPCs had lower amounts of lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidic acid, while cardiolipin was unaffected. Adding lysophosphatidic acid for 7 days increased the number of basal radial glial cells in organoids from Patient 1. The authors concluded that PNPLA8-dependent phospholipid synthesis is crucial for the abundance of basal radial glia, and that biallelic null variants cause microcephaly through this mechanism. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) reported that their patient's myopathy improved markedly after he started prednisone and thiamine. The improvement continued after both drugs were withdrawn.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that mice lacking calcium-independent phospholipase A2-gamma (iPLA2-gamma-null mice) had major changes in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism. Shotgun lipidomics showed a markedly raised hippocampal cardiolipin content, with a shift toward shorter-chain molecular species. The mice also had changes in choline and ethanolamine glycerophospholipids, including less plasmenylethanolamine, more oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species and more ceramides. Electron microscopy of the hippocampus showed enlarged heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. These structures were purified by buoyant density centrifugation and identified as degenerating mitochondria by SDS-PAGE and proteomics. The authors concluded that loss of iPLA2-gamma function causes a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder marked by degenerating mitochondria, autophagy and cognitive dysfunction. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that their patient's phenotype resembled that of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice show mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, enhanced insulin sensitivity, a thin body habitus and cold intolerance, and they have dysmorphic mitochondria similar to those in the patient's muscle. The authors concluded that this resemblance supported a causal link between the PNPLA8 variants and the phenotype.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 334AATT [c.334_337delaatt; p.N112Hfs; p.N112fs]

Saunders et al. (2015) studied a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis. By trio exome sequencing they identified compound heterozygosity for 2 frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT) resulting in p.Asn112HisfsX29. The second was a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG). The c.334_337delAATT variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and was inherited from a carrier parent. Two healthy sibs carried neither variant. The variant was absent from the Exome Sequencing Project database and from an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle showed a dramatic decrease in the iPLA2-gamma bands, consistent with a null genotype.

**.0002 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 1975AG [c.1975_1976delag; p.L659Afs; p.L659fs]

In a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) identified a 2-bp deletion in the PNPLA8 gene (c.1975_1976delAG, NM_001256011.1), resulting in a frameshift (p.Leu659AlafsX4). It was found in compound heterozygosity with the c.334_337delAATT frameshift. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and inherited from a carrier parent. It was absent from the Exome Sequencing Project database and from an internal database of 1,913 samples. Muscle from the patient showed a dramatic decrease in the iPLA2-gamma protein bands.

**.0003 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, GLU173TER [c.517G>T; p.E173*]

In a female infant (patient 1) born to consanguineous Indian parents, who had congenital microcephaly, neonatal seizures, a simplified gyral pattern, cerebellar hypoplasia and brainstem atrophy, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous c.517G-T transversion in the PNPLA8 gene (NM_001256007.1) by whole-exome sequencing. The mutation results in a glu173-to-ter (E173X) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant and showed that both parents were heterozygous.

**.0004 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 2275CT [c.2275_2276del; c.2275_2276delct; p.L759Afs; p.L759fs]

In a girl of Indian/Fijian origin (patient 2) who regressed after normal development until about 13 months of age, Shukla et al. (2018) used trio whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT) causing a frameshift (p.Leu759AlafsTer4). The other was a nonsense mutation (c.419C-A, S140X). Each mutation was inherited from a carrier parent.

In 2 sibs with ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy beginning in adolescence, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified the c.2275_2276del mutation (p.Leu759AlafsTer4; NM_001256007.3), inherited from the heterozygous mother. It was in compound heterozygosity (in trans) with a 1-bp deletion (c.2269del). The authors noted that this genotype caused a less severe neurologic phenotype than that of earlier patients carrying c.2275_2276del with another truncating variant. Because both variants lie in the last coding exon, they suggested that the transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported that p.Leu759Alafs*4 was a recurrent C-terminal frameshift mutation. In the homozygous state, or together with another C-terminal frameshift, it was associated with a mild neurodegenerative phenotype with late-onset movement disorder. It was also found in patients of intermediate severity. Fibroblasts from Patient 12, who had the mild phenotype, showed weak expression of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, suggesting residual function. The authors proposed that the variant escapes nonsense-mediated mRNA decay.

**.0005 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, SER140TER [c.419C>A; p.S140*]

In a girl of Indian/Fijian origin (patient 2) with developmental regression after 13 months of age, epilepsia partialis continua, acquired microcephaly and progressive cerebellar and brainstem atrophy, Shukla et al. It was found in compound heterozygosity with a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT). Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant, and it was inherited from a carrier parent.
