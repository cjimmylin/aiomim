### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in PNPLA8, which encodes calcium-independent phospholipase A2-gamma. The first patient reported with PNPLA8 variants had progressive muscle weakness, loss of ambulation, lactic acidosis, dystonia, seizures, and abnormal mitochondrial ultrastructure in muscle (Saunders et al., 2015). Later reports showed that PNPLA8-related disease is a phenotypic continuum. At the severe end are a developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia; at the mild end is a childhood- or later-onset neurodegeneration with movement disorder and cerebellar atrophy (Abdel-Hamid et al., 2024; Nakamura et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described a 23-year-old man with a mitochondrial myopathy. He had had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy, which caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy appeared. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and severe lactic acidosis developed at one stage of the illness. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids, which was confirmed histochemically and by thin-layer chromatography.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a girl (CMH193) who began toe-walking in her first year. She had a positive Gowers sign at age 2 years and could no longer walk by age 3 years. She had fatigue, declining fine motor skills, dysarthria, progressive proximal muscle weakness, tight heel cords, rightward deviation of the tongue, dysmetria, hypotonia, spasticity, and dystonia, including oral motor dystonia. She had complex partial seizures with left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. Her body habitus was very lean and she did not gain weight. She had lactic acidosis that required bicarbonate, an elevated anion gap, elevated blood pyruvate, and cerebrospinal fluid 5-methyltetrahydrofolate deficiency. Brain MRI showed increased signal intensity of the medial globus pallidus. Muscle biopsy showed variable fiber size with atrophic fibers but no ragged red fibers. Electron microscopy showed disarray of mitochondrial cristae, osmiophilic mitochondrial inclusions, subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria, and increased secondary lysosomes. Creatine phosphokinase was not elevated, the lactate:pyruvate ratio was not elevated, and no electron transport chain defect was found. She was last reported at age 7 years.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls and observed a variable phenotype. Patient 1, of Indian origin, was the second child of consanguineous parents and had a healthy older sister. She presented at birth with microcephaly, overriding cranial sutures, low birth weight (2410 g, -2 SD), multifocal clonic seizures, and spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. She was last reported at 10 days of age. ⚠︎

Patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018), of Indian/Fijian origin and born to nonconsanguineous parents, developed normally until about 1 year of age. Regression began at 13 months, with epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures. At last report, at age 15 years, she had severe global developmental delay, was nonverbal, and was fed by gastrostomy. She had central hypotonia, acquired microcephaly, choreiform movements, dystonia, progressive proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, spasticity, and evolving severe contractures of the hamstrings, biceps, and ankles. Brain imaging showed prominent ventricles, delayed myelination, progressive cerebellar atrophy, brainstem atrophy, parietal and occipital cortical volume loss, and thinning of the corpus callosum. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak in the parenchyma and cerebrospinal fluid. EEG showed multifocal epileptiform discharges with background slowing, and visual evoked potentials were of reduced amplitude with prolonged latency. She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, subaortic stenosis, elevated blood lactate, elevated growth and differentiation factor-15, obstructive sleep apnea, and restrictive lung disease. Electron microscopy of muscle showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria surrounded by membrane whorls, with disrupted cristae, electron-dense deposits, lipid droplets, pools of glycogen, and empty vacuoles. Activities of electron transport chain complexes I through IV were within normal ranges. The authors noted that her presentation resembled that of the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015). ⚠︎

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset, severe, progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) described a milder, adolescence-onset form of PNPLA8-associated disease in 2 sibs. The brother (sibling A), age 49 years at last report, developed lower limb weakness and distal muscle atrophy at age 14 years. He had ataxia with axial ataxia and dysmetria, tetraparesis, decreased muscle tone, wasting of the calf and foot muscles, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, and length-dependent hypoesthesia of the lower limbs. Studies showed a sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. He became nonambulant at age 44 years. At age 45 years he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, and brain imaging showed mild cerebellar atrophy. His sister (sibling B) developed difficulty walking and lower limb weakness at age 14 years. At age 21 years she had muscle atrophy in the distal upper and lower limbs, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia, and appendicular dysmetria that was more marked in the lower limbs, and she could not maintain an upright posture. She had an axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, and EMG showed abnormal spontaneous activity in distal lower limb muscles without myopathic changes. Cranial nerve examination and cognition were normal in both sibs. Cerebellar ataxia and polyneuropathy progressed markedly in the teens but only slightly in adulthood. Neither sib had visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver involvement. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 (family I) had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 (family II) had progressive microcephaly, infantile-onset seizures, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors concluded that PNPLA8-related conditions share some overlapping features and form a spectrum, with clinical features that consistently track the age of onset (congenital, infantile, childhood, or adult).

Nakamura et al. (2024) analyzed 14 new and 11 previously reported individuals with biallelic PNPLA8 variants. Eight patients from families 1 through 8 formed a severe group. Their ancestries were Japanese (families 1 and 2, both nonconsanguineous), Saudi Arabian, Iraqi, Afghan, Pakistani, Turkish, and Lebanese (families 3-8, all consanguineous); families 7 and 8 had been reported previously. Common features in this group were congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7 of 7), peripheral spasticity (7 of 8), and seizures beginning by 7 months of age (8 of 8), with onset as early as day 1. Most of these patients also had progressive microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, and hypotonia. Dystonia was present in 2 of the 6 patients with data (patients 1 and 2). Imaging showed a simplified gyral pattern, pontocerebellar hypoplasia, and cerebral and/or cerebellar atrophy. Patient 1 died at age 4 years, patient 2 at age 10 months, and patient 8 at age 8 months.

Nakamura et al. (2024) described 2 patients with intermediate severity. Patient 9, a Turkish boy from a consanguineous family, had congenital and progressive microcephaly, global developmental delay, severe intellectual disability, hypotonia, dystonia, a neuromuscular disorder, seizures from age 2 years, pontocerebellar hypoplasia, and cerebellar atrophy. Patient 10, a 9-year-old Egyptian girl from a consanguineous family, had developmental regression, hypotonia, spasticity, dystonia, ataxia, and a neuromuscular disorder. She had no microcephaly, intellectual disability, or seizures, and brain imaging showed only very mild cerebellar atrophy.

Nakamura et al. (2024) described a milder group of 4 patients: 3 Iranian sibs from a consanguineous family (family 11; patients 11a, 11b, and 11c, ages 33 and 29 years for 11a and 11c) and a 21-year-old Egyptian woman from a consanguineous family (patient 12). All 4 attained walking (4 of 4) and later had regression of milestones (4 of 4). Three had ataxia (3 of 4), 3 had mild or no intellectual disability (3 of 4), and seizures were single or absent (4 of 4); patient 11b had a seizure at age 31 years. They had a late-onset movement disorder with dystonia, along with hypotonia and a neuromuscular disorder. Brain MRI showed little or no cerebral cortical volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. The authors concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum, from a variable developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia to childhood- or later-onset neurodegeneration with movement disorder and cerebellar atrophy. ⚠︎

Nakamura et al. (2024) also identified 2 individuals from 2 unrelated Iranian families in a non-neurologic cohort (families 13 and 14). One had optic atrophy, and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis in the family reported by Saunders et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The compound heterozygous PNPLA8 variants in their patient were each inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither variant. Shukla et al. (2018) found that both parents of a patient born to consanguineous parents were heterozygous for the homozygous variant in their child, and that the compound heterozygous variants in a second patient were inherited from carrier parents.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that the pedigrees of 12 unrelated families with biallelic PNPLA8 variants were consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

In a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous frameshift variants in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsTer29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsTer4). Each variant was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither variant. Neither variant was present in the Exome Variant Server or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot analysis of patient muscle showed a dramatic decrease in the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD iPLA2-gamma immunoreactive bands compared with control muscle, consistent with loss of function.

By whole-exome sequencing in 2 unrelated girls with a progressive neurodegenerative mitochondrial phenotype, Shukla et al. (2018) identified biallelic loss-of-function variants in PNPLA8. Patient 1, born to consanguineous parents, was homozygous for a nonsense variant, c.517G-T (p.Glu173Ter); both parents were heterozygous. Patient 2 was compound heterozygous for a frameshift variant, c.2275_2276delCT (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), and a nonsense variant, c.419C-A (p.Ser140Ter). The variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with autosomal recessive inheritance. All of the variants introduced a premature stop codon, and the authors stated that they were very likely to produce a truncated protein or undergo nonsense-mediated decay.

By whole-exome sequencing in a patient with prenatal-onset severe progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy, Masih et al. (2021) identified a novel homozygous missense variant, c.1874A-G, in the patatin domain of PNPLA8.

In 2 sibs with adolescence-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified compound heterozygous frameshift variants in PNPLA8: a novel c.2269del (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10) variant and the previously reported c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4) variant. The c.2275_2276del variant was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but exome and Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 variants were in trans. The variants were confirmed in the affected sister by Sanger sequencing. The c.2269del variant was not present in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes Project, or gnomAD databases, and the authors classified it as pathogenic under ACMG guidelines.

By exome sequencing, Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) identified a loss-of-function variant in PNPLA8, c.1231C-T (p.Arg411Ter), in family I and a missense variant, c.1559T-A (p.Val520Asp), in family II.

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified 14 PNPLA8 variants in their patients, 12 of them novel: 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense. Each variant had a population frequency below 0.001%. Twelve of the 14 individuals carried combinations of nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants, which suggested loss of function. In lymphoblastoid cells from patient 1, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant caused skipping of exons 6 through 8. Immunoblotting of his skin fibroblasts showed a significant reduction in the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, indicating a null effect, and a similar null effect was found for the p.Glu296LysfsTer6 variant in patient 6. Patient-derived cells were not available to test c.2075-2A-G (patient 2) and c.1684-2A-G (patient 5) experimentally, but SpliceAI predicted that each causes loss of a splice acceptor site (delta scores 0.97 and 0.93, respectively). Patient 12, who was homozygous for p.Leu759AlafsTer4, showed weak expression of the 77-kDa protein, which suggested residual function. Skin fibroblasts from a previously reported patient with a severe phenotype who was homozygous for the missense variant p.Val520Asp expressed PNPLA8 protein at levels similar to controls. In patient fibroblasts, cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin levels appeared lower than in controls but did not correlate significantly with phenotypic severity.

In 2 individuals from unrelated Iranian families in a non-neurologic cohort, 1 with optic atrophy and 1 with nonsyndromic hearing loss, Nakamura et al. (2024) found homozygosity for distinct N-terminal frameshift variants in PNPLA8, including p.Val7Ter, which affect only the largest (88-kDa) protein isoform. In the family with optic atrophy, an affected sib of the proband was heterozygous for the variant. The authors therefore considered these N-terminal variants to be of undetermined significance.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that the 3 known patients with PNPLA8 variants had similar but variable clinical pictures. They concluded that, with so few patients, no genotype-phenotype correlation could yet be drawn.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that the c.2275_2276del variant had previously been reported in compound heterozygous state with another truncating variant in a patient with severe early-childhood neurodegeneration. Both truncating variants in their mildly affected sibs lay in the last coding exon of PNPLA8. The authors suggested that such transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and retain partial enzyme activity, resulting in a milder phenotype.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype of PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathy. They suggested that disease onset correlated strongly with the site of the variant rather than its type.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms, including at least the 77-kDa isoform, were associated with the severe end of the spectrum, with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. Biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10 or p.Leu759AlafsTer4) were associated with a milder neurodegenerative phenotype with late-onset movement disorder. The 4 patients of intermediate severity carried combinations of a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent C-terminal frameshift variant p.Leu759AlafsTer4. The authors proposed that C-terminal truncations escape nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and leave residual protein.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) did not define the metabolic defect in their patient. They postulated an abnormality in the Krebs cycle that led to overproduction of lactate and lipid accumulation in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) found normal SDH and COX activity and normal electron transport chain function in patient muscle, and the lactate:pyruvate ratio was not elevated. They suggested that PNPLA8-related disease may result mainly from degeneration of the mitochondrial inner membrane, with electron transport proteins affected only secondarily. A 110-kD band present in control muscle but absent in the patient was identified by mass spectrometry as HSPA4, which had reacted nonspecifically with the antibody. The authors speculated that loss of this chaperone might also contribute to the disease.

Shukla et al. (2018) found that the abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes and inclusions seen in muscle from one patient matched findings in Pnpla8-null mice. They noted that the human phenotype was strikingly similar to the progressive neurodegeneration seen in the null mice.

Nakamura et al. (2024) generated cerebral organoids from CRISPR/Cas9 PNPLA8-knockout iPSCs. Compared with wildtype organoids, the knockout organoids had a smaller subventricular zone-like region, fewer PAX6+/HOPX+ basal radial glial cells, and fewer SATB2+ upper-layer neurons. Ventricular zone size, deep-layer neurons, and basal intermediate progenitors were unchanged, and increased BrdU incorporation suggested an altered S-phase length in apical progenitors. Organoids derived from patient 1's iPSCs reproduced this phenotype, with fewer basal radial glial cells and upper-layer neurons than organoids from his father's cells. Spatial transcriptomics of patient apical radial glia showed reduced expression of basal radial glia gene sets and enrichment of gene sets for cell cycle, neuronal differentiation, and lysophospholipid signaling.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found decreased lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid in PNPLA8-knockout neural progenitor cells; other phospholipids and free fatty acids were unchanged. Treating patient 1-derived cerebral organoids with lysophosphatidic acid increased the number of basal radial glial cells. The authors concluded that PNPLA8-dependent phospholipid synthesis is crucial for basal radial glia abundance. Maximal mitochondrial respiration was reduced in patient 1's skin fibroblasts, whose mitochondrial morphology was normal. Mitochondrial morphology and oxidative phosphorylation were barely affected in PNPLA8-knockout neural progenitor cells, and the authors suggested that mitochondria-related fate specification is unlikely to be dysregulated in PNPLA8-deficient progenitors.

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) observed marked improvement in their patient's myopathy after prednisone and thiamine were started. The improvement was maintained after these drugs were withdrawn.

### Population Genetics

Saunders et al. (2015) found that truncating PNPLA8 variants were rare in the Exome Variant Server, which contained no homozygous truncating variants.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that iPLA2-gamma-null mice had profound alterations in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism. Hippocampal cardiolipin content was markedly elevated, with a shift to shorter chain length molecular species. Plasmenylethanolamine content was decreased, and oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species and ceramides were increased. Electron microscopy of the hippocampus showed enlarged heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, along with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. Purification and proteomics identified these structures as degenerating mitochondria. The mice showed autophagy and cognitive dysfunction, and the authors concluded that iPLA2-gamma loss of function results in a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that the clinical presentation and mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities of their patient closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice show mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, enhanced insulin sensitivity, a thin body habitus, cold intolerance, and dysmorphic mitochondria.

**.0010 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 1625+3 [c.1625+3_1625+6del]

In a Japanese boy (patient 1) from a nonconsanguineous family, Nakamura et al. (2024) identified a c.1625+3_1625+6del variant in the PNPLA8 gene.
