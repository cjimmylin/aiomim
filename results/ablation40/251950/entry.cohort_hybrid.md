### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is a mitochondrial disorder. In the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015), it presented as progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia, seizures, poor weight gain, and lactic acidosis. Muscle from this patient showed degenerating mitochondria with abnormal cristae. Later reports of patients with biallelic PNPLA8 mutations described a broader neurologic spectrum. Nakamura et al. (2024) characterized it as a continuum. At the severe end were developmental and degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. At the mild end was later-onset neurodegeneration with movement disorder and cerebellar atrophy (Burnyte et al., 2023; Nakamura et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) reported a 23-year-old man with a mitochondrial myopathy. He had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that led to cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy appeared. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and at one stage of the illness he developed severe lactic acidosis. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids, which was confirmed histochemically and by thin-layer chromatography.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl (CMH193) who was evaluated for a suspected mitochondrial myopathy with dystonia, abnormal gait, and an abnormal muscle biopsy. She had no symptoms in her first year and met all developmental milestones, although her parents noticed toe-walking. A Gowers sign was present at age 2 years, and by age 3 she could no longer walk. She could sit independently for only 10 minutes because of fatigue. Her fine motor skills declined, and her speech became dysarthric. Muscle weakness was more pronounced proximally. Other findings included tight heel cords, subtle rightward deviation of the tongue, and generalized mildly increased tone. Her symptoms progressed, with increasing proximal weakness and dysmetria. Hypotonicity progressed to spasticity of different opposing muscle groups. She later developed complex partial seizures with recurrent left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. Despite CoQ10 supplementation she did not gain weight and remained very lean. She developed significant lactic acidosis that required bicarbonate supplementation. Creatine phosphokinase was normal, but the anion gap had been consistently elevated since age 5. At 33 months, brain MRI showed subtle increased signal in the medial globus pallidus bilaterally; this was no longer visible 2 years later. Muscle biopsy at 4 years showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic fibers of all subtypes and no ragged-red fibers. On electron microscopy, mitochondria showed marked disarray of cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions. There were also subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggesting degenerating mitochondria. Secondary lysosomes containing residual bodies that appeared to be derived from mitochondria were increased.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls with biallelic PNPLA8 mutations. Patient 1 was born to consanguineous Indian parents. At birth she had microcephaly (head circumference 29 cm, -4 SD) and a weight of 2,410 g (-2 SD). Multifocal clonic seizures began on the second day of life, and she had spasticity of all 4 limbs and overriding cranial sutures. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. Patient 2 was born to nonconsanguineous parents of Indian/Fijian origin. She developed normally until about 13 months of age. She then regressed, with episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures that were difficult to control. Her development stagnated at an 8-month level, she was nonverbal, and she needed a gastrostomy tube. She developed acquired microcephaly, with a head circumference at 15 years of 49 cm (-4.7 SD). Other findings included central hypotonia, choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, and severe contractures. Serial MRI showed progressive volume loss in the cerebellum, brainstem, and parietal and occipital lobes, along with thinning of the corpus callosum. MR spectroscopy showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. On electron microscopy, muscle showed enlarged and irregularly shaped mitochondria with surrounding membrane whorls and disrupted cristae, as well as lipid droplets and pools of glycogen. Activities of respiratory chain complexes I through IV were normal. Blood lactate was elevated on several occasions. She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, visual evoked potentials with severely reduced amplitudes, elevated growth and differentiation factor-15, and obstructive sleep apnea. The authors noted that Patient 2's presentation was similar to that of the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015). ⚠︎

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset, severe, progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) reported 2 adult sibs with slowly progressive ataxia that began in adolescence. In the brother, symptoms began at age 14 years with weakness and distal muscle atrophy of the lower limbs, accompanied by ataxia and gait disturbance. He needed assistance to walk from age 17 years and became nonambulant at age 44. On examination he had tetraparesis, decreased tone, distal muscle wasting, absent lower limb reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, marked dysmetria, and length-dependent hypoesthesia. At age 45 years he also had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus. His Mini-Mental State Examination score was 30/30. Nerve conduction studies showed a sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, and brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. His sister developed walking difficulty and lower limb weakness at age 14 years. At age 21 she had distal limb atrophy, absent lower limb reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia, and appendicular dysmetria. Her cognition was normal, and she had an axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy. Neither sib had visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver involvement.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3, from the second family, had progressive microcephaly, infantile-onset seizures, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype. They concluded that PNPLA8-related conditions form a spectrum of overlapping features, with clinical features consistently tracking the age of onset. ⚠︎

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals from 12 unrelated families with biallelic PNPLA8 variants. Ages at last assessment ranged from 3 days to 33 years. Common features were central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). Eight patients (families 1-8) had a severe phenotype. Their features included congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7 of 7), peripheral spasticity (7 of 8), seizures beginning within 7 months of age (8 of 8), a simplified gyral pattern (6 of 6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7 of 7), and severe progressive cerebral cortical atrophy (5 of 5). Four patients (families 11 and 12) had a milder phenotype. All 4 attained walking and had regression of milestones. Three of the 4 had ataxia and mild or no intellectual disability, and seizures were isolated or absent. MRI in this group showed little or no cerebral cortical volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Two patients (families 9 and 10) were of intermediate severity. Together with 11 previously reported individuals, the authors concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum. It ranges from variable developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy to childhood-onset neurodegeneration.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient of Saunders et al. (2015), blood lactic and pyruvic acid were elevated on several occasions at age 7 years. Lactic acid was 3.8 to 5.6 mmol/L (normal, 0.7-2.1) and pyruvic acid 0.18 to 0.32 mmol/L (normal, 0.08-0.16), with lactate:pyruvate ratios of 17.5 to 21.1 (normal, less than 20). The plasma acylcarnitine profile was normal. At age 6, CSF neurotransmitter studies showed a deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. Muscle SDH and COX activities were normal, and electron transport chain function was unaffected. The authors suggested that these results pointed to mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect.

### Inheritance

Saunders et al. (2015) found that the 2 PNPLA8 mutations in their patient were each inherited from a carrier parent, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Shukla et al. (2018) likewise found that the mutations were inherited from carrier parents. Nakamura et al. (2024) found that the pedigrees of 12 unrelated families with biallelic PNPLA8 variants showed autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Saunders et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing on a girl with mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis and on her parents. The patient was compound heterozygous for 2 frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsTer29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsTer4), numbered on NM_001256011.1. Sanger sequencing confirmed both mutations, and each was inherited from a carrier parent. Two healthy sibs carried neither mutation. Neither mutation was present in the Exome Variant Server or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. On Western blot analysis of patient muscle, the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD iPLA2-gamma bands were dramatically decreased compared with control muscle.

Shukla et al. (2018) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 unrelated girls. In the girl from a consanguineous family, they found a homozygous nonsense mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (c.517G-T; Glu173Ter). The second girl was compound heterozygous for c.2275_2276delCT (Leu759AlafsTer4) and c.419C-A (Ser140Ter). In both families the mutations were inherited from carrier parents. All of the mutations introduced a premature stop codon. The authors considered them very likely to produce a truncated protein or to undergo nonsense-mediated decay.

In a patient with prenatal-onset severe progressive neurodegeneration, Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous missense mutation, c.1874A-G, in the patatin domain of PNPLA8.

In 2 sibs with adolescent-onset ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a novel c.2269del (Thr757GlnfsTer10) and the other was the previously reported c.2275_2276del (Leu759AlafsTer4). The c.2275_2276del mutation was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but sequencing showed that the 2 mutations were in trans. The c.2269del variant was absent from ExAC, 1000 Genomes, and gnomAD.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing in 2 families. In family I they identified a loss-of-function mutation in PNPLA8 (c.1231C-T; Arg411Ter), and in family II a missense mutation (c.1559T-A; Val520Asp).

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified biallelic PNPLA8 variants in 14 affected individuals from 12 unrelated families using exome or genome sequencing. There were 14 variants, of which 12 were novel: 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense. All had a population frequency of less than 0.001%. Twelve of the 14 individuals carried combinations of nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants, which suggested loss of function. In Patient 1, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant caused skipping of exons 6 through 8, and his fibroblasts showed a significant reduction in the 77-kD PNPLA8 isoform. The frameshift variant p.Glu296LysfsTer6 in Patient 6, who had a severe phenotype, also had a null effect. Two individuals from a separate non-neurologic cohort were homozygous for N-terminal frameshift variants that affect only the 88-kD isoform; one had optic atrophy and the other nonsyndromic hearing loss. In the family with optic atrophy, an affected sib of the proband was heterozygous, and the authors considered the significance of these N-terminal variants undetermined.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that the 3 patients then known had similar but variable clinical pictures. With so few patients, no genotype-phenotype correlation could be drawn.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that the c.2275_2276del mutation had previously been reported in compound heterozygous state with another truncating variant in a patient with severe neurodegeneration in early childhood. Both truncating mutations in their sibs were in the last coding exon of PNPLA8. The authors suggested that such transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and keep partial enzyme activity, resulting in a milder phenotype.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) suggested that disease onset correlated strongly with the site of the variant rather than its type.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were associated with the severe end of the spectrum, which typically included congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. The 4 patients of intermediate severity carried combinations of a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent C-terminal frameshift p.Leu759AlafsTer4. All patients with the mild phenotype of late-onset movement disorder had biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants, p.Thr757GlnfsTer10 or p.Leu759AlafsTer4. Fibroblasts from a mildly affected patient homozygous for p.Leu759AlafsTer4 showed weak expression of the 77-kD PNPLA8 protein. The authors proposed that C-terminal truncations escape nonsense-mediated decay and leave residual protein. In contrast, fibroblasts from a severely affected, previously reported patient homozygous for p.Val520Asp expressed PNPLA8 protein at levels similar to controls.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) did not define the metabolic defect in their patient. They postulated an abnormality in the Krebs cycle that led to overproduction of lactate and accumulation of lipid in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) suggested that PNPLA8-related disease may result mainly from altered homeostasis of the mitochondrial inner membrane, with electron transport and ATP synthesis proteins affected only secondarily. In control muscle they found a 110-kD immunoreactive band that was absent in their patient. Mass spectrometry identified it as HSPA4, which had reacted nonspecifically with the antibody. The authors speculated that loss of this chaperone might also contribute to the disease.

Nakamura et al. (2024) generated cerebral organoids from PNPLA8-knockout iPSCs. Compared with wildtype, the knockout organoids had a smaller subventricular zone-like region, fewer basal radial glial cells, and fewer upper-layer neurons. Organoids derived from the iPSCs of a patient with congenital microcephaly reproduced these findings. In the patient-derived organoids, spatial transcriptomics of apical radial glia showed reduced basal radial glia gene sets and enrichment of cell cycle, neuronal differentiation, and lysophospholipid pathway gene sets. Knockout neural progenitor cells had reduced amounts of lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid. Adding lysophosphatidic acid partly restored the number of basal radial glial cells in patient-derived organoids. Maximal mitochondrial respiration was reduced in the patient's fibroblasts. In knockout neural progenitor cells, however, mitochondrial morphology and oxidative phosphorylation were barely affected.

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) observed marked improvement in their patient's myopathy after prednisone and thiamine were started. The improvement persisted after these drugs were withdrawn.

In the patient of Saunders et al. (2015), a multivitamin containing iron, carbidopa-levodopa, and leucovorin initially improved symptoms. CoQ10 increased her appetite but did not lead to weight gain, and her lactic acidosis required bicarbonate supplementation.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found profound alterations in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism in iPLA2-gamma-null mice. Hippocampal cardiolipin content was markedly elevated and shifted toward shorter chain length molecular species. Plasmenylethanolamine content was decreased, and oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species and ceramides were increased. Electron microscopy showed enlarged heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, which proteomics identified as degenerating mitochondria, along with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. The mice showed autophagy and cognitive dysfunction.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that their patient's clinical presentation, muscle histology, and mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice show mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, enhanced insulin sensitivity, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance.
