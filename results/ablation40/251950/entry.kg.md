## MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS; MMLA

### Phenotype-Gene Relationships

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive mitochondriopathy. Its cardinal features include muscle weakness, lactic acidosis and neurodegeneration, with abnormal mitochondrial ultrastructure in muscle (Saunders et al., 2015). PNPLA8-related disease covers a range of overlapping phenotypes. These include a congenital form with congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy and early death; a childhood neurodegeneration form with progressive microcephaly; and an adult-onset form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy (Abdel-Hamid et al., 2024). Frequent features include central hypotonia, dystonia, seizures and cerebellar atrophy (Nakamura et al., 2024). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described a 23-year-old man with mitochondrial myopathy. He had a high-output circulatory state and a cardiomyopathy that caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy appeared. His venous lactate levels were elevated, and at one stage of the illness he developed severe lactic acidosis. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids. Histochemistry and thin-layer chromatography confirmed the lipid accumulation.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl (CMH193) with a suspected mitochondrial myopathy. She had no symptoms in her first year and met her developmental milestones, although toe-walking was noted. She had a positive Gowers sign at age 2 years and could no longer walk by age 3. Her course was progressive. Features included proximal muscle weakness, dysmetria, dysarthria, a decline in fine motor skills, oral motor dystonia, and hypotonia that progressed to spasticity. She had complex partial seizures with left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. She had a very lean body habitus without weight gain and significant lactic acidosis that required bicarbonate supplementation. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at age 4 years showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic type 1 and type 2 fibers, and no ragged red fibers. On electron microscopy, the mitochondria showed marked disarray of the cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions. There were also subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggestive of degenerating mitochondria. Secondary lysosomes containing residual bodies were increased. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle bilateral increased signal in the medial globus pallidus, which was no longer seen 2 years later.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl (patient 1) born to consanguineous parents. She had congenital microcephaly (head circumference -4 SD at birth), multifocal clonic seizures beginning on day 2 of life, and spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. TORCH testing was negative.

Shukla et al. (2018) also reported a girl (patient 2) of Indian/Fijian origin born to nonconsanguineous parents. She developed normally until about 13 months of age and then regressed, with epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures. Her development then stagnated severely. She had acquired microcephaly (-4.7 SD at age 15 years), was nonverbal, and was fed by gastrostomy tube. Other features were hypotonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent reflexes, choreiform movements, dystonia and contractures. She developed progressive atrophy of the cerebrum, cerebellum and brainstem, and MR spectroscopy showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, abnormal visual evoked potentials, obstructive sleep apnea, elevated blood lactate and elevated GDF15.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with severe neurodegeneration that began before birth, progressed, and led to death in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) described 2 adult Lithuanian sibs, a brother and a sister. Both had slowly progressive cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy beginning at age 14 years, with preserved cognition. The brother was 49 years old at the time of the report. He had lower-limb weakness, distal muscle atrophy, ataxia and gait disturbance, tetraparesis, absent lower-limb tendon reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, dysmetria, and mild distal hypoesthesia. He became nonambulant at age 44 years. At age 45 he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, his Mini-Mental State Examination score was normal, and brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. At age 21, his sister had severe axial ataxia, appendicular dysmetria, distal muscle atrophy, areflexia, and bilateral Babinski signs. EMG showed no myopathic changes. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families with PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathy. Patient 1 (family I) had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 (family II) had progressive microcephaly, seizures beginning in infancy, progressive cortical atrophy, loss of white matter, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors noted that only 6 patients with biallelic PNPLA8 variants had been reported before. Those patients had a congenital form (congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early death), a childhood neurodegeneration form with progressive microcephaly, or an adult-onset form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals (6 male, 8 female) from 12 unrelated families with neurological disease and biallelic PNPLA8 variants. Many of the families were consanguineous, and they came from countries including Japan, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Turkey, Lebanon, Egypt and Iran. Age at last assessment ranged from 3 days to 33 years. Frequent features were central hypotonia (13/13), dystonia (8/12), seizures (10/14) and cerebellar atrophy (12/12). Eight patients (families 1-8) were at the severe end of the spectrum. They had congenital microcephaly (5/7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7/7), peripheral spasticity (7/8) and seizure onset within 7 months of age (8/8). Their neuroimaging showed a simplified gyral pattern (6/6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7/7) and severe progressive cerebral cortical atrophy (5/5). Four patients (families 11-12) had a relatively mild phenotype. All 4 learned to walk and then lost milestones. Three of the 4 had ataxia, 3 of the 4 had mild or no intellectual disability, and seizures were either a single event or absent. Their brain MRI showed little or no cerebral cortical volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Two further patients (families 9 and 10) were of intermediate severity. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Saunders et al. (2015) found that their patient had a persistently elevated anion gap from age 5 years. At age 7 she had elevated blood lactate (3.8-5.6 mmol/L) and pyruvate, but the lactate:pyruvate ratio was not elevated, and her plasma acylcarnitine profile was normal. CSF studies at age 6 showed 5-methyltetrahydrofolate deficiency. Creatine kinase and liver function tests were normal, as were mtDNA tRNA sequencing and mtDNA depletion studies. Muscle SDH and COX activity and electron transport chain function were unaffected. The authors suggested that this pattern indicated mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect.

In patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018), the muscle biopsy showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria with surrounding membrane whorls, disrupted cristae, electron-dense deposits, lipid droplets and glycogen pools. Activities of electron transport chain complexes I through IV were normal. GDF15 was elevated at 1293 pg/ml at age 14 years.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that skin fibroblasts from their patient 1 had significantly lower maximal mitochondrial respiration than fibroblasts from healthy controls, although mitochondrial morphology was normal on electron microscopy. Cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin appeared to be decreased in patient fibroblasts compared with controls but did not correlate with phenotypic severity.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MMLA is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Saunders et al. (2015) found that each of 2 compound heterozygous PNPLA8 variants in their patient was inherited from a carrier parent. Shukla et al. (2018) likewise found that the biallelic PNPLA8 variants in their 2 patients were inherited from carrier parents.

### Molecular Genetics

Saunders et al. (2015) performed trio exome sequencing in a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis. They found that she was compound heterozygous for 2 frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4). Both mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Each was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither. Neither mutation was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. On Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle, the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 (iPLA2-gamma) protein bands were markedly decreased compared with 3 control muscles. A 110-kD band seen in controls was also absent. Mass spectrometry identified this band as HSPA4, which the authors concluded had reacted nonspecifically with the antibody.

Using whole-exome sequencing, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in PNPLA8, c.517G-T (p.Glu173Ter; NM_001256007.1), in patient 1, the daughter of consanguineous Indian parents. Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Using trio whole-exome sequencing in patient 2, they identified compound heterozygous PNPLA8 mutations: a frameshift, c.2275_2276delCT (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), and a nonsense mutation, c.419C-A (p.Ser140Ter). Both mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and each was inherited from a carrier parent. ⚠︎

In a patient with severe neurodegeneration that began before birth, Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous missense mutation, c.1874A-G, in the patatin domain of the PNPLA8 gene.

In 2 Lithuanian sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in exon 11 of the PNPLA8 gene. One was a novel c.2269del (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10), and the other was the previously reported c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4). The c.2269del mutation was not found in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, or gnomAD databases. The c.2275_2276del mutation was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but sequencing showed that the 2 mutations were on different alleles (in trans). Sanger sequencing confirmed both mutations in the affected sister. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing in 2 families. In patient 1 (family I), who had the congenital form of the disease, they identified a loss-of-function mutation in the PNPLA8 gene, c.1231C-T (p.Arg411Ter). In patients 2 and 3 (family II), who had infantile-onset disease, they identified a missense mutation, c.1559T-A (p.Val520Asp).

Nakamura et al. (2024) studied 14 individuals from 12 families by exome or genome sequencing, with Sanger segregation analysis. They identified 14 PNPLA8 variants, 12 of them novel: 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense variants. Every variant had a frequency below 0.001% in population databases (dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, jMorp and gnomAD). Twelve of the 14 individuals carried combinations of nonsense, frameshift and splice-site variants, suggesting loss of function. In patient 1, a Japanese patient, the intronic variant c.1625+3_1625+6del caused skipping of exons 6-8 through aberrant splicing in patient lymphoblastoid cells. The 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein was significantly reduced in the patient's fibroblasts, indicating a null effect. Patients 2 and 5 carried the splice-acceptor variants c.2075-2A-G and c.1684-2A-G, respectively. No patient cells were available, but SpliceAI predicted loss of the splice acceptor site (delta scores 0.97 and 0.93, respectively). In patient 6, a Pakistani patient with a severe phenotype, the frameshift variant p.Glu296Lysfs*6 also had a null effect on PNPLA8 protein in skin fibroblasts.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that the 3 known patients with PNPLA8 loss-of-function variants had similar but variable clinical features. With so few patients known, they could not establish a genotype-phenotype correlation.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that c.2275_2276del had previously been reported in compound heterozygous state with another truncating variant in a patient with severe early-childhood neurodegeneration, whereas their patients had a milder phenotype. Both truncating variants in their patients lie in the last coding exon of PNPLA8. The authors therefore suggested that these transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and possibly retain partial enzyme activity.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype of PNPLA8-related disease. They suggested that age at onset correlates strongly with the location of the variant rather than with its type, and they described the PNPLA8-related conditions as a spectrum of overlapping features that correspond to different ages of onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) combined their patients with 11 previously reported individuals from 8 families. Biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were associated with the severe phenotype. All patients with a relatively mild neurodegenerative phenotype and late-onset movement disorders carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757Glnfs*10 or p.Leu759Alafs*4). Intermediate cases carried a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent p.Leu759Alafs*4. In skin fibroblasts, weak residual expression of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein was found in patient 12 (mild phenotype, p.Leu759Alafs*4) and in patient 14 (non-neuronal phenotype, p.Val7*). In contrast, the homozygous missense variant p.Val520Asp from a previously reported patient with a severe phenotype gave protein levels similar to controls. The authors suggested that C-terminal truncating variants escape nonsense-mediated decay and lead to less severe disease.

Nakamura et al. (2024) also studied 2 unrelated Iranian families from a non-neurological cohort. In these families they identified individuals homozygous for distinct N-terminal PNPLA8 frameshift variants that affect only the largest (88-kDa) isoform. One proband had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss. In the optic atrophy family, an affected sib was only heterozygous for the variant, so the authors considered these variants to be of undetermined significance.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) did not define the metabolic defect in their patient. They postulated an abnormality involving the Krebs cycle that resulted in overproduction of lactate and lipid accumulation in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) suggested that, as in PLA2G6-related disease, PNPLA8-related disease may result primarily from altered homeostasis of the mitochondrial inner membrane, with effects on electron transport and ATP synthesis being secondary.

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that all PNPLA8 variants reported to that point introduce premature stop codons and are likely to result in truncated protein or nonsense-mediated decay. They also observed that the abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes in their patient 2 resembled findings in Pnpla8-null mice, and that the human phenotype resembled the progressive neurodegeneration seen in those mice.

Nakamura et al. (2024) generated cerebral organoids from CRISPR/Cas9 PNPLA8-knockout human iPSCs and from iPSCs of their patient 1. Compared with controls, these organoids had a smaller subventricular zone-like region, fewer PAX6+HOPX+ basal radial glial cells, and fewer SATB2+ upper-layer neurons. Deep-layer neurons and basal intermediate progenitors were unchanged. Knockout neural progenitors had normal mitochondrial morphology and respiration but reduced levels of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), lysophosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidic acid. Adding LPA partly restored the number of basal radial glia in patient organoids.

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) observed a marked improvement in their patient's myopathy after he started prednisone and thiamine. The improvement continued after both drugs were withdrawn.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported that their patient's symptoms initially improved with a multivitamin containing iron, carbidopa-levodopa, and leucovorin. CoQ10 supplementation increased her appetite but did not lead to weight gain.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that mice lacking iPLA2-gamma had major changes in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism and mitochondrial phospholipid homeostasis. Shotgun lipidomics showed a markedly raised hippocampal cardiolipin content, with the molecular species shifted toward shorter chains. Choline and ethanolamine glycerophospholipids were altered, with less plasmenylethanolamine, more oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species, and more ceramides. On electron microscopy, the authors saw enlarged, heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. Purification and proteomics identified these structures as degenerating mitochondria. The mice showed autophagy and cognitive dysfunction. The authors concluded that iPLA2-gamma has an obligatory role in neuronal mitochondrial lipid metabolism and membrane structure, and that its loss of function causes a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that their patient's phenotype and mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice have mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance, as well as dysmorphic mitochondria in the myocardium and cerebellum. The authors suggested that this resemblance supported a causal link between the PNPLA8 variants and the patient's disease. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 334AATT [c.334_337delaatt; p.N112Hfs; p.N112fs]

In a 7-year-old girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PNPLA8 gene by trio exome sequencing. One was a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT) causing a frameshift (p.Asn112HisfsX29); the other was a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG; p.Leu659AlafsX4). Both mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Each was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither. Neither was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. On Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle, the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 protein bands were markedly decreased.

**.0002 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 1975AG [c.1975_1976delag; p.L659Afs; p.L659fs]

For discussion of the 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG) in the PNPLA8 gene, which causes a frameshift (p.Leu659AlafsX4), see the 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT). Saunders et al. (2015) found the 2 mutations in compound heterozygous state in a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis. Each mutation was inherited from a carrier parent, and PNPLA8 protein in the patient's muscle was markedly decreased.

**.0003 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, GLU173TER [c.517G>T; p.E173*]

In an Indian girl (patient 1) born to consanguineous parents, Shukla et al. (2018) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.517G-T transversion (NM_001256007.1) in the PNPLA8 gene, resulting in a glu173-to-ter (E173X) substitution. The girl had congenital microcephaly, neonatal-onset seizures, spasticity, and an elevated lipid-lactate peak on MR spectroscopy. Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents were heterozygous for the mutation.
