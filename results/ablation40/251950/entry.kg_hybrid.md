### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder. Saunders et al. (2015) first described it in a girl with progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia, seizures, poor weight gain, and lactic acidosis who had biallelic loss-of-function PNPLA8 mutations. Later reports widened the phenotype. Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) recognized congenital-onset, infantile-onset, childhood-onset, and adult-onset forms. Nakamura et al. (2024) reviewed 25 affected individuals and concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum. At one end is developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. At the other is childhood- or later-onset neurodegeneration with ataxia and progressive cerebellar atrophy.

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described a 23-year-old man with a mitochondrial myopathy. Twenty-two years before the skeletal myopathy appeared, he had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that caused cardiac insufficiency. His venous lactate levels were elevated, and at one stage of the illness he developed severe lactic acidosis. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids, which histochemistry and thin-layer chromatography confirmed. The metabolic defect was not defined.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl who was evaluated for a suspected mitochondrial myopathy with dystonia, abnormal gait, and an abnormal muscle biopsy. She was born at term weighing 3.1 kg. She had no symptoms in the first year and met all developmental milestones, although her parents noticed toe-walking. At age 2 she had a positive Gowers sign, and by age 3 years she could no longer walk. She could sit independently for only 10 minutes because of fatigue. Her fine motor skills declined and her speech became dysarthric. She had proximal muscle weakness, tight heel cords, and generalized mildly increased tone. The weakness and dysmetria progressed, and hypotonicity progressed to spasticity of opposing muscle groups. She later developed complex partial seizures with recurrent left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. She had a very lean body habitus and significant lactic acidosis that required bicarbonate supplementation. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle increased signal in the medial globus pallidus bilaterally. This finding was no longer seen 2 years later, which the authors suggested reflected maturation of myelination. MR spectroscopy and head CT were normal. A right quadriceps biopsy at age 4 years showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic fibers of all subtypes and no ragged red fibers. Electron microscopy showed mitochondria with marked disarray of cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions. It also showed subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggestive of degenerating mitochondria, and increased secondary lysosomes containing residual bodies that appeared to derive from mitochondria.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls. Patient 1 was the second child of consanguineous parents from India. At birth she weighed 2,410 g (-2 SD), measured 48 cm (-1 SD), and had a head circumference of 29 cm (-4 SD). Seizures began on the second day of life, and she was evaluated on day 6 for congenital microcephaly and seizures. Examination showed overriding cranial sutures, multifocal clonic seizures, and spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. TORCH testing was negative.

Patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018), of Indian/Fijian origin and born to nonconsanguineous parents, developed normally until about 13 months of age. She then regressed, with episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and difficult-to-control focal seizures. At 2 years she was nonverbal and needed a gastrostomy tube. Her head growth stopped after age 3 years, and at 15 years her head circumference was 49 cm (-4.7 SD). She had central hypotonia with muscle weakness, choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, and severe contractures. Over a decade, brain MRI showed volume loss in the cerebellum, brainstem, and parietal and occipital lobes, with thinning of the corpus callosum. MR spectroscopy showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. On electron microscopy, muscle biopsy showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria with surrounding membrane whorls and disrupted cristae, together with electron-dense deposits, lipid droplets, and glycogen pools. Other findings were reduced visual evoked potentials, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, obstructive sleep apnea, and restrictive lung disease. ⚠︎

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset severe and progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) reported 2 adult sibs, a brother and a sister, whose symptoms began at age 14 years. Both had slowly progressive ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, and their cognition was preserved. The brother, now 49 years old, first developed weakness and distal muscle atrophy of the lower limbs, with ataxia and gait disturbance. He could not walk without assistance from age 17 years. At 30 years he had marked axial ataxia, tetraparesis, decreased muscle tone, wasting of the calves and feet, absent lower-limb reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, dysmetria, and mild length-dependent hypoesthesia. Nerve conduction studies showed sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. He became nonambulant at age 44. At 45 years he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, and his Mini-Mental State Examination score was 30/30. Brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. His sister developed difficulty walking and weakness of both lower limbs at age 14 years. At 21 years she had distal atrophy of all limbs, absent lower-limb reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia, and appendicular dysmetria, and she could not maintain an upright posture. Her cognition was normal, and EMG showed no myopathic changes. Neither sib had visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver dysfunction. Their disease advanced markedly in the teens but progressed only slightly through adulthood. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 had progressive microcephaly, infantile-onset seizures, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals (6 male and 8 female) from 12 unrelated families of various origins, many of them consanguineous. Ages at last assessment ranged from 3 days to 33 years. Frequent features were central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). Eight patients (Families 1-8) had severe disease. In this group, features included congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7 of 7), peripheral spasticity (7 of 8), and seizure onset within 7 months of age (8 of 8). Neuroimaging in this group showed a simplified gyral pattern (6 of 6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7 of 7), and severe progressive cerebral cortical atrophy (5 of 5). Four patients (Families 11-12) had relatively mild disease. All 4 learned to walk and later had regression of milestones. Three of the 4 had ataxia and 3 had mild or no intellectual disability, and seizures were isolated or absent. Their brain MRI showed little or no cerebral cortical volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Patients from Families 9 and 10 were of intermediate severity. Nakamura et al. (2024) also identified 2 individuals with biallelic PNPLA8 variants in a non-neurologic cohort. One had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015), blood lactate (3.8-5.6 mmol/L; normal 0.7-2.1) and pyruvate (0.18-0.32 mmol/L; normal 0.08-0.16) were elevated on multiple occasions, with lactate:pyruvate ratios of 17.5 to 21.1. Her anion gap had been consistently elevated (17 mmol/L) since age 5. The plasma acylcarnitine profile and creatine phosphokinase were normal. CSF showed deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. Muscle SDH and COX reactions and electron transport chain function were normal. The authors suggested that these results indicated mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect.

Patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018) had elevated blood lactate on several occasions. At age 14 years her growth and differentiation factor-15 was elevated (1,293 pg/ml; normal less than 750 pg/ml). Activities of electron transport chain complexes I to IV in muscle were within normal ranges.

In skin fibroblasts from their Patient 1, Nakamura et al. (2024) found normal mitochondrial morphology on electron microscopy. However, maximal respiration was significantly lower than in controls. Cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin levels appeared lower in patient fibroblasts than in controls but did not correlate significantly with phenotypic severity.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MMLA in the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Shukla et al. (2018) and Nakamura et al. (2024) confirmed this pattern in further families.

### Molecular Genetics

Saunders et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing on a girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis and on her healthy parents. They identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4). Both mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and were inherited from carrier parents. Two healthy sibs carried neither variant. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. On Western blot of patient muscle, the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 bands were markedly reduced compared with 3 control muscles.

Shukla et al. (2018) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 unrelated girls. In patient 1, born to consanguineous parents, they identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in PNPLA8 (c.517G-T; E173X), and both parents were heterozygous. In patient 2 they identified compound heterozygous mutations: a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT) causing a frameshift (Leu759AlafsTer4), and a nonsense mutation (c.419C-A; S140X). Segregation analysis showed that the mutations were inherited from carrier parents.

Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing in a patient with prenatal-onset severe neurodegeneration. They identified a homozygous missense mutation (c.1874A-G) in the patatin domain of PNPLA8.

Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 sibs with adolescence-onset ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy. They identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in exon 11 of PNPLA8: a novel 1-bp deletion (c.2269del; Thr757GlnfsTer10) and the previously reported c.2275_2276del (Leu759AlafsTer4). The c.2275_2276del mutation was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was unavailable, but exome and Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 mutations were in trans. Both mutations were confirmed in the affected sister.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing and identified a PNPLA8 loss-of-function mutation (c.1231C-T; R411X) in one family and a missense mutation (c.1559T-A; V520D) in another.

Nakamura et al. (2024) studied 14 patients from 12 families by exome or genome sequencing with Sanger segregation analysis. They identified 14 PNPLA8 variants, 12 of them novel. These comprised 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense variants, each with a population frequency below 0.001%. Twelve of the 14 patients carried combinations of nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants. In patient lymphoblastoid cells, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant in Patient 1 caused skipping of exons 6 to 8, and his fibroblasts showed a significant reduction in the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein. Fibroblasts from Patient 6, who carried p.Glu296Lysfs*6, also showed a null effect. SpliceAI predicted that the c.2075-2A-G and c.1684-2A-G variants would abolish splice acceptor sites. Two Iranian individuals from a non-neurologic cohort, one with optic atrophy and one with hearing loss, were homozygous for N-terminal frameshift variants affecting only the 88-kDa isoform. In the optic atrophy family, an affected sib was heterozygous, and the authors considered these variants of undetermined significance.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that the 3 patients reported to that time had similar but variable phenotypes. They stated that the number of individuals was too small to deduce a genotype-phenotype correlation.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that the c.2275_2276del mutation had previously been reported, in compound heterozygosity with another truncating variant, in severe neurodegeneration of early childhood. In their sibs, the same mutation combined with c.2269del caused a less severe neurologic phenotype. Because both truncating mutations lie in the last coding exon, the authors suggested that the transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and may retain partial enzyme activity.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) suggested that the site of the variant, rather than its type, correlates strongly with age at disease onset. They also suggested that the congenital, infantile, childhood, and adult-onset forms share some features and form a spectrum.

Nakamura et al. (2024) analyzed 25 affected individuals from 20 families. Biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were associated with the severe end of the spectrum, typically with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. Patients of intermediate severity carried a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent C-terminal frameshift p.Leu759Alafs*4. Patients with a relatively mild phenotype and late-onset movement disorders all had biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757Glnfs*10 or p.Leu759Alafs*4). Fibroblasts from a mildly affected patient with p.Leu759Alafs*4 showed weak expression of the 77-kDa protein, suggesting residual function. In contrast, fibroblasts from a severely affected patient homozygous for the missense variant p.Val520Asp showed protein expression similar to controls. The authors concluded that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting the 77- and 74-kDa isoforms are sufficient for the severe phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) postulated that their patient had an abnormality of the Krebs cycle that caused overproduction of lactate and lipid accumulation in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) suggested that, as in PLA2G6-related disease, degeneration of the mitochondrial inner membrane may be the primary pathogenic mechanism of PNPLA8-related disease. In this model, electron transport and ATP synthesis proteins would be affected only secondarily through altered membrane homeostasis.

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that the PNPLA8 variants reported to that time introduce premature stop codons and are likely to cause a truncated protein or nonsense-mediated decay.

Nakamura et al. (2024) generated cerebral organoids from CRISPR/Cas9-edited PNPLA8-knockout iPSCs. These organoids had smaller subventricular zone-like regions, fewer basal radial glial cells, and fewer SATB2-positive upper-layer neurons. The number of intermediate progenitors and deep-layer neurons was unchanged. Organoids derived from Patient 1 reproduced these findings. In neural progenitor cells, loss of PNPLA8 barely affected mitochondrial morphology or OXPHOS activity. Knockout neural progenitor cells had less lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid. Adding lysophosphatidic acid partly restored the number of basal radial glial cells in patient-derived organoids. The authors concluded that biallelic null PNPLA8 variants cause microcephaly by reducing the number of basal radial glial cells.

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) reported marked improvement in their patient's myopathy after he started prednisone and thiamine. The improvement continued after the drugs were withdrawn.

In the patient of Saunders et al. (2015), a multivitamin containing iron, together with carbidopa-levodopa and leucovorin, initially improved symptoms. CoQ10 supplementation increased her appetite, but she did not gain weight. Her lactic acidosis required bicarbonate supplementation.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that iPLA2-gamma-null mice had markedly increased hippocampal cardiolipin content, with a shift toward shorter-chain molecular species. Other lipid changes included decreased plasmenylethanolamine, increased oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine, and increased ceramides. Electron microscopy showed enlarged, degenerating heteromorphic lamellar structures, which were identified as degenerating mitochondria, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. The authors concluded that loss of iPLA2-gamma causes a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder characterized by autophagy and cognitive dysfunction.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that their patient's phenotype resembled that of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice have mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, enhanced insulin sensitivity, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance. Their mitochondrial morphologic changes closely matched those in the patient's muscle.
