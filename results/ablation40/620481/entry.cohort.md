### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the THPO gene (600044) on chromosome 3q27.1.

### Description

Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is an autosomal recessive disorder. It presents in infancy or childhood with severe thrombocytopenia and reduced or absent bone marrow megakaryocytes, and it can progress to trilineage bone marrow failure. Serum thrombopoietin is absent or inappropriately normal. THPO is produced outside hematopoietic cells, so the marrow failure does not respond to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, but it does respond to thrombopoietin receptor agonists (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (2013) reported 2 sibs from a Micronesian family who had idiopathic autosomal recessive aplastic anemia.

Seo et al. (2017) reported 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families who had severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to trilineage bone marrow failure. Thrombopoietin was absent from the patients' serum.

Pecci et al. (2018) reported 3 affected sibs born to first-cousin parents of Egyptian origin. The proband was a boy who presented in July 2009, at age 3.5 years, with ecchymotic patches. On examination he had preauricular skin tags and a left divergent squint. Blood counts showed very severe thrombocytopenia with normal platelet size, hyporegenerative anemia (reticulocyte count 22.8 x 10(9)/l), and severe neutropenia. The bone marrow was hypocellular, with very rare immature megakaryocytes. He had recurrent epistaxis, gum bleeding, and rectal bleeding, as well as recurrent febrile neutropenia and otitis media. Chromosome breakage testing excluded Fanconi anemia. His serum THPO was 10.9 pg/ml, which was not increased. His brother had severe isolated thrombocytopenia at age 7 months, with normal peripheral blood cell morphology and a markedly decreased number of bone marrow megakaryocytes. From age 1.6 years the brother had recurrent spontaneous bleeding that required platelet transfusions; his serum THPO was 14.1 pg/ml. Their sister developed a hematoma at a vaccination site at age 2 months and was found to have anemia and severe thrombocytopenia with normal blood cell morphology. The parents had normal blood counts. Pecci et al. (2018) noted that the oldest sib had trilineage marrow hypoplasia, whereas the younger sibs had isolated thrombocytopenia or thrombocytopenia with anemia. They suggested that the hematologic picture worsens with age and is indistinguishable from CAMT caused by MPL mutations.

Capaci et al. (2023) reported a Muslim Palestinian boy (II-3) born to consanguineous parents. He presented at age 3.5 years with easy bruising and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 21 x 10(9)/L) with a normal mean platelet volume. Bone marrow examination of 2 separate biopsies showed normocellular marrow with severe megakaryocyte hypoplasia, a few small immature forms, and mild dyserythropoiesis, consistent with amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia. He also had mild, occasional neutropenia and urethral stenosis, but no pancytopenia. His serum thrombopoietin was 126 pg/mL, which was inappropriately normal to slightly elevated.

### Inheritance

Dasouki et al. (2013), Seo et al. (2017), Pecci et al. (2018), and Capaci et al. (2023) each found that CAMT2 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Affected individuals were homozygous for THPO mutations, and some of the families were consanguineous. Heterozygous carriers in the families reported by Dasouki et al. (2013) and Capaci et al. (2023) had mild thrombocytopenia or reduced platelet counts.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 Micronesian sibs with autosomal recessive aplastic anemia, Dasouki et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.112C-T missense mutation in the THPO gene, which they called R17C. Both parents and 1 heterozygous sib had reduced platelet counts, whereas a sib with the wildtype sequence had a normal platelet count. Media containing the mutant THPO were 2- to 3-fold less able to sustain proliferation of THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cells. In silico modeling predicted that adding a fifth cysteine to the receptor-binding domain, which contains 4 conserved cysteines forming 2 disulfide bonds, might disrupt normal disulfide bonding and impair receptor binding. Dasouki et al. (2013) concluded that this partial loss-of-function allele caused aplastic anemia in the homozygous state and mild thrombocytopenia in the heterozygous state.

In affected children from 3 unrelated families with severe thrombocytopenia progressing to trilineage bone marrow failure, Seo et al. (2017) identified homozygous mutations in the THPO gene. Affected children in 2 families were homozygous for an R99W mutation, and the affected child in the third family was homozygous for an R157X mutation. Both mutations led to a lack of THPO in the patients' serum. Seo et al. (2017) concluded that biallelic loss-of-function mutations in THPO cause bone marrow failure.

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with CAMT2, Pecci et al. (2018) found no variants in MPL and identified a homozygous c.355C-T mutation in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg119-to-cys (R119C) substitution. The parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Residue R119 lies in helix C of the MPL-binding domain and is conserved across species and in EPO. In transfected HEK293T cells, the mutant protein was expressed and stable, but its concentration in culture supernatants was about 23-fold lower than wildtype, suggesting impaired secretion. The previously reported R38C mutant behaved similarly. At equal concentrations, R119C THPO retained about 50% of wildtype activity in stimulating proliferation of UT7-TPO cells, and it caused defective activation of STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT downstream of MPL. ⚠︎

In a Muslim Palestinian boy with CAMT2 born to consanguineous parents, Capaci et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.-323C-T variant (NM_000460.2; chr3:g.184096040G-A) in the promoter region of the THPO gene. The parents and a younger sib were heterozygous, and 2 healthy sibs were homozygous for the wildtype allele. The heterozygous mother and sib had asymptomatic mild thrombocytopenia. In HEK293T cells, the variant reduced promoter reporter activity by almost 80%. In chromatin immunoprecipitation assays, it impaired binding of the transcription factors ETS1 and STAT4 to the THPO promoter. Knockdown of ETS1 and STAT4 in HepG2 cells reduced THPO mRNA and protein, and the 2 factors were shown to interact by proximity ligation and coimmunoprecipitation.

### Pathogenesis

Seo et al. (2017) stated that bone marrow failure caused by THPO mutations does not respond to transplantation because the defect lies outside the hematopoietic cells.

### Diagnosis

Pecci et al. (2018) found that serum THPO levels in 2 affected sibs were within the normal range, which was inappropriately low for their megakaryocyte-platelet mass. By contrast, levels are markedly increased in CAMT caused by MPL mutations. They suggested that measuring serum THPO could distinguish THPO-mutant CAMT from MPL-related CAMT and from other marrow failure syndromes.

Capaci et al. (2023) suggested that serum THPO measurement could serve as a screening tool, because a normal serum THPO level in a patient with amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia should strongly suggest a THPO defect. They recommended that the routine workup for suspected CAMT include analysis of THPO, including its regulatory regions.

### Clinical Management

Seo et al. (2017) reported that 4 children with THPO-related bone marrow failure who received hematopoietic stem cell transplants all had poor graft function. Their severe cytopenias persisted even after repeated transplants from different donors. In the 2 surviving patients, treatment with a THPO receptor agonist improved trilineage hematopoiesis.

Pecci et al. (2018) treated all 3 affected sibs with the THPO-mimetic romiplostim. All 3 had trilineage hematologic responses, their bleeding and infections remitted, and transfusion independence was maintained for up to 6.5 years without side effects. The authors noted that HSCT is not expected to be effective because THPO is produced outside hematopoietic cells.

Capaci et al. (2023) treated a boy with CAMT2 due to a homozygous THPO promoter variant with eltrombopag. At 2.8 mg/kg/day, his platelet count rose gradually to 125 x 10(9)/L and his bruising resolved. This was the first report of eltrombopag therapy in a patient with a THPO mutation.
