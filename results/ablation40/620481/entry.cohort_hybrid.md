### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the THPO gene (600044) on chromosome 3q27.1.

### Description

Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is an autosomal recessive disorder of thrombopoietin (THPO) deficiency. Patients have severe thrombocytopenia with reduced or absent bone marrow megakaryocytes, which can progress in childhood to trilineage bone marrow failure. Serum THPO levels are absent, normal, or only slightly raised, which is inappropriately low for the reduced megakaryocyte-platelet mass. Because the defect lies outside hematopoietic cells, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation does not correct the disorder, whereas THPO receptor agonists improve blood counts (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018; Capaci et al., 2023). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (2013) reported 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with idiopathic, autosomal recessive aplastic anemia. In a later discussion of this family, Pecci et al. (2018) noted that bone marrow aplasia had been recognized at ages 16 and 28 years in the 2 sibs, and that 2 other sibs had died of aplastic anemia in adolescence. Dasouki et al. (2013) did not report whether congenital thrombocytopenia had preceded the marrow aplasia, so Pecci et al. (2018) could not exclude a CAMT-like course in these patients.

Seo et al. (2017) reported 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families who had severe thrombocytopenia progressing to trilineage bone marrow failure. Four of the children received hematopoietic stem cell transplants. All 4 had poor graft function and persistent severe cytopenias, even after repeated transplants from different donors. Serum THPO was absent in the patients.

Pecci et al. (2018) reported 3 sibs with CAMT, born to first-cousin parents of Egyptian origin. The male proband presented at 3.5 years of age with ecchymotic patches. On examination he had preauricular skin tags and a left divergent squint. He had very severe thrombocytopenia (platelets 3 x 10(9)/l), hyporegenerative anemia (hemoglobin 7.8 g/dl, reticulocytes 22.8 x 10(9)/l), and severe neutropenia (0.3 x 10(9)/l). Platelet size was normal. The bone marrow was hypocellular with very rare immature megakaryocytes, and the karyotype was normal. Fanconi anemia and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria were excluded, and he was first diagnosed with idiopathic aplastic anemia. He went on to have recurrent major hemorrhages (epistaxis and gum and rectal bleeding) and recurrent severe infections (febrile neutropenia or otitis media) every 1 to 2 months, which required frequent admissions and transfusions. His brother had severe isolated thrombocytopenia (26 x 10(9)/l) at 7 months of age, with normal peripheral blood morphology and a markedly reduced number of marrow megakaryocytes. He developed recurrent spontaneous bleeding requiring platelet transfusions at 1.6 years. Their sister developed a hematoma at a vaccination site at 2 months of age and was found to have anemia and severe thrombocytopenia. The parents had normal blood counts. Pecci et al. (2018) noted that thrombocytopenia was the only feature present in all 3 patients. They concluded that the trilineage aplasia in the oldest sib suggested worsening with age, and that the presentation was indistinguishable from CAMT due to MPL mutations.

Capaci et al. (2023) reported a boy born to first-cousin Muslim Palestinian parents. He presented at 3.5 years of age with easy bruising and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 21 x 10(9)/L) with a normal mean platelet volume, and he had a lifelong history of frequent bruising. His platelet count shortly after birth had been 70 x 10(9)/L. At 2 years of age he was diagnosed with urethral stenosis; his platelet count was then 20 x 10(9)/L, and he needed a platelet transfusion before meatotomy. Apart from mild occasional neutropenia, he had no other cytopenias. Two bone marrow biopsies showed normocellular marrow with severe megakaryocyte hypoplasia, a few small immature forms, and mild dyserythropoiesis. Chromosome breakage with diepoxybutane was not increased, and the marrow karyotype was 46,XY. Capaci et al. (2023) described his picture as CAMT with severe congenital thrombocytopenia and marked megakaryocyte hypoplasia but without pancytopenia. His mother had mild thrombocytopenia that worsened in each pregnancy and was stable at about 80 x 10(9)/L, with no bleeding. His younger sib had a platelet count of 130 x 10(9)/L around birth and 160 x 10(9)/L several weeks later.

### Biochemical Features

Seo et al. (2017) found that the THPO mutations in their patients resulted in a lack of THPO in serum.

Pecci et al. (2018) measured serum THPO in 2 affected sibs before treatment and found 10.9 pg/ml in the proband and 14.1 pg/ml in his brother. These values fell within the range seen in 50 healthy subjects (6.9 to 54.4 pg/ml). Because serum THPO is normally markedly increased in bone marrow aplasia or hypoplasia, including CAMT due to MPL mutations, the authors considered these levels inappropriately low for the patients' megakaryocyte-platelet mass.

In the patient reported by Capaci et al. (2023), serum THPO was 126 pg/mL (laboratory reference range 15-80 pg/mL for children under 15 years of age). The authors regarded this as only slightly raised despite the severely reduced megakaryocyte and platelet mass.

### Inheritance

Several reports showed autosomal recessive transmission of CAMT2. Dasouki et al. (2013) found the mutation in the homozygous state in 2 affected sibs, and Seo et al. (2017) found homozygous mutations in 3 families. Pecci et al. (2018) found a homozygous mutation in 3 affected sibs born to consanguineous parents, both of whom were heterozygous. Capaci et al. (2023) found a homozygous mutation in a child of first-cousin parents who were both heterozygous. In 2 families, heterozygous relatives had mild thrombocytopenia (Dasouki et al., 2013; Capaci et al., 2023).

### Molecular Genetics

Dasouki et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to study 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with autosomal recessive aplastic anemia. Both sibs were homozygous for a c.112C-T transition in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg38-to-cys substitution (R38C; R17C when the 21-residue signal peptide is excluded) in the receptor-binding domain. Both parents and a sib were heterozygous, and another sib had the wildtype sequence. In silico modeling suggested that the extra cysteine could disrupt the 2 disulfide bonds of the receptor-binding domain and weaken receptor binding. Medium containing the mutant protein was 2- to 3-fold less able to sustain proliferation of THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cells, and the authors described R17C as a partial loss-of-function allele.

Seo et al. (2017) used exome and targeted sequencing to study 5 patients from 3 unrelated families with severe thrombocytopenia progressing to bone marrow failure. Affected children in 2 families were homozygous for a THPO R99W mutation, and the affected child in the third family was homozygous for an R157X mutation. Both mutations resulted in a lack of THPO in patient serum. The authors concluded that biallelic loss-of-function THPO mutations cause bone marrow failure that does not respond to transplant because the mechanism is extrinsic to hematopoietic cells.

Pecci et al. (2018) studied 3 sibs with CAMT from a consanguineous Egyptian family. Sanger sequencing of MPL and THPO showed no MPL variants in the proband, but he was homozygous for a c.355C-T transition in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg119-to-cys (R119C) substitution. His 2 affected sibs were also homozygous, and both parents were heterozygous. The variant was not found in the SNP, 1000 Genomes, or ExAC databases. R119 lies in helix C of the receptor-binding domain and is conserved across species and in erythropoietin. In transfected HEK293T cells, the R119C and R38C mutant proteins were expressed and as stable as wildtype. However, their concentrations in culture supernatants were about 23-fold lower than wildtype, suggesting impaired secretion. At equal concentrations, R119C THPO stimulated UT7-TPO cell proliferation at about 45 to 53% of wildtype levels, and it caused defective activation of STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT downstream of MPL. The R38C mutant gave similar results.

Capaci et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to study a Palestinian boy with CAMT born to first-cousin parents. He was homozygous for a c.-323C-T substitution in the THPO promoter region (transcript variant 1), 45 nucleotides upstream of the transcription start site. The parents and the younger sib were heterozygous, and 2 healthy sibs were homozygous for the wildtype allele. The variant was in dbSNP but not in gnomAD. No variants were found in MPL, MECOM, HOXA11, RBM8A, Fanconi anemia genes, or other bone marrow failure genes. The variant lay within predicted binding sites for ETS1 and STAT4 and lowered THPO promoter reporter activity by almost 80% in HEK293T cells. Chromatin immunoprecipitation showed that ETS1 and STAT4 bound the wildtype promoter, with reduced binding to the mutant promoter, at least for STAT4. In HepG2 hepatocyte cells, knockdown of ETS1 and/or STAT4 strongly reduced THPO mRNA and protein. The 2 factors interacted, suggesting that they form a transcriptional complex on THPO regulatory sequences.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dasouki et al. (2013) found that both parents and a sib who were heterozygous for the R17C THPO mutation had reduced platelet counts, whereas a wildtype sib had a normal count. They concluded that this partial loss-of-function allele caused aplastic anemia when homozygous and mild thrombocytopenia when heterozygous. Capaci et al. (2023) likewise found asymptomatic mild thrombocytopenia in the mother and a younger sib, both heterozygous for the c.-323C-T variant. By contrast, Pecci et al. (2018) found normal blood counts, with platelet counts of 197 and 193 x 10(9)/l, in the parents heterozygous for R119C.

### Diagnosis

Pecci et al. (2018) suggested that measuring serum THPO could help distinguish CAMT patients with THPO mutations from those with MPL variants or other bone marrow failure syndromes. They recommended considering the disorder in children or young adults with hypomegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia, with or without other cytopenias, including those with apparently acquired aplastic anemia.

Capaci et al. (2023) proposed serum THPO measurement as a screening tool, stating that a normal THPO level in amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia should strongly suggest a THPO alteration. They recommended that molecular analysis of THPO, including its regulatory regions, be part of the routine diagnostic workup for suspected CAMT.

### Clinical Management

Seo et al. (2017) reported that the 4 patients who received hematopoietic stem cell transplants had poor graft function and persistent severe cytopenias, even after repeated transplants with different donors. In the 2 surviving patients, trilineage hematopoiesis improved after treatment with a THPO receptor agonist. Reviewing these cases, Pecci et al. (2018) noted that 3 of the transplanted children died early after transplantation.

The proband reported by Pecci et al. (2018) did not respond to cyclosporine A or oxymetholone. At 4.4 years of age he started romiplostim, at 1 microgram/kg/week for 3 months and then 4 microgram/kg monthly, with rapid trilineage improvement. He had no further spontaneous bleeding or infections and needed no further transfusions, and the response was maintained after 6.5 years. His 2 affected sibs were also treated with romiplostim, with good platelet responses, and the sister also had a hemoglobin response. Their brother remained free of bleeding and transfusions after 6.3 years. No side effects were observed. The authors noted that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is not expected to be effective for cytopenias caused by THPO defects.

Capaci et al. (2023) treated their patient with oral eltrombopag at 1.4 mg/kg/day, and his platelet count stabilized at about 40 x 10(9)/L. After the dose was increased to 2.8 mg/kg/day, his platelet count rose gradually to 125 x 10(9)/L and his easy bruising resolved. This was the first report of eltrombopag therapy in a patient with a THPO mutation.
