### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the THPO gene (600044) on chromosome 3q27.1.

### Description

Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the gene for thrombopoietin (THPO). It presents in infancy or early childhood with severe thrombocytopenia and a marked reduction of bone marrow megakaryocytes. In some patients it progresses to trilineage bone marrow failure or aplastic anemia. Unlike CAMT caused by MPL mutations, serum THPO is not markedly increased. The bone marrow failure does not respond to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, but patients have responded to THPO receptor agonists.

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (2013) described 2 sibs from a Micronesian family who had idiopathic, autosomal recessive aplastic anemia.

Seo et al. (2017) reported 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families who had severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to trilineage bone marrow failure. THPO was absent from the patients' serum.

Pecci et al. (2018) reported 3 sibs born to Egyptian parents who were first cousins. The proband was a boy who presented at 3.5 years of age with ecchymotic patches. On examination he had preauricular skin tags and a divergent squint of the left eye. His blood counts showed very severe thrombocytopenia, hyporegenerative anemia (reticulocyte count 22.8 x 10(9)/l), and severe neutropenia. His platelets were of normal size. The bone marrow was hypocellular with very rare immature megakaryocytes. He had recurrent epistaxis, gum bleeding, rectal bleeding, recurrent otitis media, and recurrent episodes of febrile neutropenia. There was no spontaneous or diepoxybutane-induced chromosome breakage. His brother had severe isolated thrombocytopenia at 7 months of age, with normal peripheral blood cell morphology and a markedly reduced number of bone marrow megakaryocytes without other significant abnormalities. From 1.6 years of age, the brother had recurrent spontaneous bleeding that required platelet transfusions. A third sib, a girl, appeared normal at birth. At 2 months of age she developed a hematoma at a vaccination site, and her blood counts showed anemia and severe thrombocytopenia with normal blood cell morphology. Serum THPO was 10.9 pg/ml in the proband and 14.1 pg/ml in his brother. The parents had normal blood counts. Thrombocytopenia was the only feature present in all 3 affected sibs. Because the oldest sib had trilineage marrow hypoplasia, the authors suggested that the hematologic picture worsens with age. They noted that the clinical presentation could not be distinguished from CAMT due to MPL mutations.

Capaci et al. (2023) reported a Muslim Palestinian boy born to consanguineous parents. He presented at 3.5 years of age with easy bruising and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 21 x 10(9)/L) with a normal mean platelet volume. He also had urethral stenosis and mild, occasional neutropenia. Two bone marrow biopsies showed a normocellular marrow with severe hypoplasia of megakaryocytes, a few small immature forms, and mild dyserythropoiesis, consistent with amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia. He did not have pancytopenia or increased chromosomal breakage. His serum THPO concentration was 126 pg/mL, only slightly elevated.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMT2 is autosomal recessive. Dasouki et al. (2013) described the aplastic anemia in their Micronesian family as autosomal recessive, and both affected sibs were homozygous for a THPO mutation. In the consanguineous Egyptian family reported by Pecci et al. (2018), the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for a THPO mutation and the parents were heterozygous, consistent with recessive inheritance. In the consanguineous Palestinian family reported by Capaci et al. (2023), the proband was homozygous, and his parents and a younger sib were heterozygous.

Some heterozygous carriers had mild thrombocytopenia. Dasouki et al. (2013) found reduced platelet counts in both heterozygous parents and in a heterozygous sib, whereas a wildtype sib had a normal platelet count. Capaci et al. (2023) found asymptomatic mild thrombocytopenia in the heterozygous mother and younger sib of their proband. In contrast, the heterozygous parents in the family of Pecci et al. (2018) had normal blood counts.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with autosomal recessive aplastic anemia, Dasouki et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.112C-T transition in the THPO gene. The mutation results in an arg38-to-cys (R38C) substitution in the receptor-binding domain, designated R17C when the 21-amino acid signal peptide is excluded. Both parents and 1 sib were heterozygous for the mutation, and another sib was wildtype. The THPO receptor-binding domain contains 4 conserved cysteines that form 2 disulfide bonds. In silico modeling predicted that the fifth cysteine introduced by the mutation could disrupt normal disulfide bonding and cause poor receptor binding. Medium containing the mutant THPO was 2- to 3-fold less able to sustain proliferation of THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cells, consistent with a partial loss-of-function allele.

In 5 affected individuals from 3 unrelated families with severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to trilineage bone marrow failure, Seo et al. (2017) identified homozygous mutations in the THPO gene. The affected children in 2 families were homozygous for an R99W mutation, and the affected child in the third family was homozygous for an R157X mutation. Both mutations resulted in a lack of THPO in the patients' serum. The authors concluded that biallelic loss-of-function mutations in THPO cause bone marrow failure.

In 3 sibs with CAMT from a consanguineous Egyptian family, Pecci et al. (2018) identified a homozygous c.355C-T transition in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg119-to-cys (R119C) substitution. No MPL variants were found in the proband. The parents were heterozygous for the mutation. It was not present in the SNP, 1000 Genomes, or ExAC databases. In transfected HEK293T cells, the R119C mutant and the previously reported R38C mutant were expressed and stable. However, their concentrations in culture supernatants were about 23-fold lower than that of wildtype THPO, suggesting that both mutations impair THPO secretion. At equal concentrations, R119C THPO induced about 50% of the wildtype proliferation of THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cells and caused defective activation of STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT downstream of MPL. The authors predicted that R119, located in helix C of the receptor-binding domain, contributes to the low-affinity binding site for MPL.

In a Palestinian boy born to first-cousin parents who had congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia without pancytopenia, Capaci et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.-323C-T transition in the promoter region of THPO (transcript variant 1). The variant lies 45 nucleotides upstream of the transcription start site. It was present in dbSNP but not in gnomAD. No variants were found in MPL, MECOM, HOXA11, RBM8A, or in Fanconi anemia or other bone marrow failure genes. The parents and a younger sib were heterozygous for the variant, and 2 healthy sibs were homozygous for the wildtype allele. The variant lies within predicted ETS1 and STAT4 binding sites and reduced THPO promoter reporter activity by almost 80% in HEK293T cells. Chromatin immunoprecipitation showed that ETS1 and STAT4 bound the wildtype promoter, with reduced binding to the mutant promoter, at least for STAT4.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dasouki et al. (2013) concluded that the partial loss-of-function R17C (R38C) THPO allele caused aplastic anemia in the homozygous state and mild thrombocytopenia in the heterozygous state.

### Pathogenesis

Seo et al. (2017) concluded that the bone marrow failure caused by biallelic THPO mutations does not respond to transplantation because the mechanism lies outside the hematopoietic cells. They considered this support for the critical role of the MPL-THPO pathway in hematopoiesis.

In HepG2 hepatocyte cells, Capaci et al. (2023) showed that knockdown of ETS1 and/or STAT4 strongly reduced THPO mRNA and protein levels. Proximity ligation and coimmunoprecipitation showed that STAT4 and ETS1 interacted, suggesting that they form a transcriptional complex on THPO regulatory sequences. Knockdown of STAT3 also reduced THPO expression, although no STAT3 binding site was predicted in the promoter. The authors proposed defective binding of ETS1 and STAT4 to the THPO promoter as a novel mechanism of CAMT.

### Diagnosis

Pecci et al. (2018) found that serum THPO levels in 2 affected sibs were within the normal range, which was inappropriately low for their megakaryocyte-platelet mass. In contrast, THPO levels are markedly increased in CAMT due to MPL mutations. They suggested that measuring serum THPO could help distinguish CAMT patients with THPO mutations from those with MPL variants or other bone marrow failure syndromes.

Capaci et al. (2023) noted that their patient's serum THPO (126 pg/mL) was only slightly above the normal range despite severely reduced megakaryocyte and platelet mass, whereas THPO is markedly increased in other forms of amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia. They suggested serum THPO measurement as a screening tool, with a normal level in amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia strongly suggesting a THPO alteration. They recommended that analysis of THPO, including its regulatory regions, be part of the diagnostic workup for CAMT.

### Clinical Management

Seo et al. (2017) reported that 4 of 5 affected children with THPO mutations received hematopoietic stem cell transplants. All 4 had poor graft function with persistent severe cytopenias, even after repeated transplants from different donors. The 2 surviving patients showed improved trilineage hematopoiesis after treatment with a THPO receptor agonist.

Pecci et al. (2018) treated all 3 affected sibs with the THPO mimetic romiplostim. Treatment produced trilineage hematologic responses, remission of bleeding and infections, and transfusion independence, maintained for up to 6.5 years without side effects. The proband had previously not responded to cyclosporine A or oxymetholone. The authors noted that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is not expected to be effective in cytopenias caused by THPO defects. They emphasized that recognizing these patients avoids invasive and unnecessary treatments such as HSCT and immunosuppression.

Capaci et al. (2023) treated their patient with eltrombopag, starting at 1.4 mg/kg/day and increasing to 2.8 mg/kg/day. His platelet count rose to 125 x 10(9)/L and his easy bruising resolved. This was the first report of eltrombopag treatment in a patient with a THPO mutation. The authors noted that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is expected to be ineffective in CAMT due to THPO defects.
