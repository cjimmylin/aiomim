### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the THPO gene (600044) on chromosome 3q27.1.

### Description

Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is an autosomal recessive disorder. Affected children have severe thrombocytopenia with reduced or absent bone marrow megakaryocytes. Some also have anemia and/or neutropenia, and the disease can progress to trilineage bone marrow aplasia in childhood (Pecci et al., 2018; Capaci et al., 2023). Because the defect lies in the cytokine thrombopoietin and not in hematopoietic cells, serum THPO levels are not raised as expected. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is ineffective, but patients respond to THPO-receptor agonists (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018; Capaci et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (2013) reported 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with idiopathic aplastic anemia that showed autosomal recessive inheritance. In reviewing this family, Pecci et al. (2018) noted that bone marrow aplasia had been recognized at ages 16 and 28 years in the 2 patients and that 2 other sibs had died of aplastic anemia in adolescence. The original report did not state whether the aplasia had been preceded by congenital thrombocytopenia.

Seo et al. (2017) reported 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families with severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to trilineage bone marrow failure. Four of the children received hematopoietic stem cell transplants. All 4 had poor graft function and persistent severe cytopenias, even after repeated transplants from different donors.

Pecci et al. (2018) reported 3 affected sibs born to Egyptian parents who were first cousins. The proband, a boy, presented at age 3.5 years with ecchymotic patches. He had very severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count 3 x 10(9)/l), hyporegenerative anemia (reticulocyte count 22.8 x 10(9)/l) and severe neutropenia (0.3 x 10(9)/l). Platelet size was normal and there were no cytomorphologic abnormalities. Bone marrow was hypocellular with very rare immature megakaryocytes, and the marrow karyotype was normal. Fanconi anemia and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria were excluded, and he was diagnosed with idiopathic aplastic anemia. He then had recurrent major hemorrhages (epistaxis, gum bleeding and rectal bleeding) and recurrent severe infections, which required multiple platelet and red blood cell transfusions. On examination he also had preauricular skin tags and a left divergent squint. His brother had severe isolated thrombocytopenia (26 x 10(9)/l) at age 7 months, with a markedly decreased number of bone marrow megakaryocytes. From age 1.6 years he had recurrent spontaneous bleeding that required platelet transfusions. A younger sister developed a hematoma at a vaccination site at age 2 months and was found to have anemia and severe thrombocytopenia. The parents had normal blood counts. Thrombocytopenia was the only feature present in all 3 patients. The authors suggested that the trilineage aplasia in the oldest sib meant that the hematologic picture worsens with age. They considered the presentation indistinguishable from that of CAMT due to MPL mutations.

Capaci et al. (2023) reported a boy born to Muslim Palestinian parents who were first cousins. He presented at age 3.5 years with easy bruising and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 21 x 10(9)/L), with a normal mean platelet volume. His platelet count shortly after birth had been 70 x 10(9)/L. At age 2 years it was 20 x 10(9)/L, when he was diagnosed with urethral stenosis and received a platelet transfusion before meatotomy. Apart from occasional mild neutropenia he had no other cytopenias. Two bone marrow biopsies showed normocellular marrow with severe megakaryocyte hypoplasia, a few small immature forms, and mild dyserythropoiesis. Chromosome breakage analysis ruled out Fanconi anemia, and the bone marrow karyotype was normal (46,XY). He did not have pancytopenia. The authors hypothesized that, without treatment, he would have progressed to multilineage marrow aplasia.

### Biochemical Features

Seo et al. (2017) found no THPO in the serum of patients homozygous for either of the THPO mutations they identified.

Pecci et al. (2018) measured serum THPO in the 2 older affected sibs before romiplostim treatment. Levels were within the normal range: 10.9 pg/ml in the proband and 14.1 pg/ml in his brother, compared with 6.9 to 54.4 pg/ml in 50 healthy controls. Serum THPO is usually markedly increased in bone marrow aplasia or hypoplasia, including CAMT due to MPL mutations. The authors therefore considered these levels inappropriately low, consistent with defective THPO production.

Capaci et al. (2023) found a serum THPO concentration of 126 pg/mL in their proband (laboratory reference range 15 to 80 pg/mL for children under 15 years). By comparison, a series of patients with CAMT due to MPL mutations had a median of 1,493 pg/mL. The authors interpreted the level as lower than expected for the reduced megakaryocyte and platelet mass, indicating defective THPO production.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMT2 in the families reported by Dasouki et al. (2013), Seo et al. (2017), Pecci et al. (2018), and Capaci et al. (2023) was autosomal recessive. Some heterozygous carriers had mild thrombocytopenia. In the family of Dasouki et al. (2013), both parents and a sib who were heterozygous had reduced platelet counts. In the family of Capaci et al. (2023), the heterozygous mother had mild thrombocytopenia. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Pecci et al. (2018) noted that most cases of CAMT are caused by biallelic mutations in MPL, the gene encoding the thrombopoietin receptor. Some patients with the clinical picture of CAMT do not carry MPL mutations, which indicates genetic heterogeneity. Capaci et al. (2023) used the terms CAMT-MPL and CAMT-THPO to distinguish CAMT caused by mutations in MPL from CAMT caused by mutations in THPO.

### Molecular Genetics

Dasouki et al. (2013) studied 2 Micronesian sibs with autosomal recessive aplastic anemia. By whole-exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous c.112C-T missense mutation in the THPO gene that results in an arg38-to-cys substitution (R17C when the 21-residue signal peptide is excluded) in the receptor-binding domain. In silico modeling predicted that this fifth cysteine could disrupt normal disulfide bonding and impair receptor binding. In functional assays, medium containing mutant THPO was 2- to 3-fold less able to sustain proliferation of THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cells. Both parents and a sib were heterozygous and had reduced platelet counts, and a sib with wildtype sequence had a normal platelet count. The authors concluded that this partial loss-of-function allele causes aplastic anemia in the homozygous state and mild thrombocytopenia in the heterozygous state.

Seo et al. (2017) studied 5 patients from 3 unrelated families with thrombocytopenia progressing to bone marrow failure. Using exome and targeted sequencing, they identified homozygous mutations in the THPO gene: R99W in affected children from 2 families, and R157X in the affected child from the third family. Both mutations resulted in a lack of THPO in the patients' serum.

In the proband of an Egyptian family with CAMT, Pecci et al. (2018) sequenced MPL and THPO. They found no MPL variant but identified a homozygous c.355C-T transition in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg119-to-cys (R119C) substitution. The 2 affected sibs were also homozygous and the parents were heterozygous. The variant was absent from the SNP, 1000 Genomes, and ExAC databases. The residue lies in helix C of the MPL-binding domain and is highly conserved, including in EPO. In HEK293T cells, the mutant protein was expressed and stable, but its concentration in culture supernatants was about 23-fold lower than wildtype. At equal THPO concentrations, the mutant retained about 50% of wildtype activity in stimulating UT7-TPO cell proliferation and showed defective activation of STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT. The previously reported R38C variant behaved similarly.

Capaci et al. (2023) studied a Palestinian boy with CAMT. By whole-exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous c.-323C-T substitution in the promoter region of the THPO gene, 45 nucleotides upstream of the transcription start site of transcript variant 1. Both parents and a younger sib were heterozygous, and 2 healthy sibs were homozygous for the wildtype allele. The variant was listed in dbSNP but was absent from gnomAD, and it lies in a region conserved across species. No variants were found in MPL, MECOM, HOXA11, RBM8A, or genes associated with Fanconi anemia or other inherited bone marrow failure syndromes. The variant was predicted to abolish consensus binding sites for ETS1 and STAT4. In HEK293T cells it reduced THPO promoter-driven luciferase reporter activity by almost 80%, and in chromatin immunoprecipitation assays binding to the promoter was impaired, at least for STAT4.

### Pathogenesis

Seo et al. (2017) concluded that biallelic loss-of-function mutations in THPO cause bone marrow failure that does not respond to transplantation because the mechanism lies outside the hematopoietic cells.

Pecci et al. (2018) found that the R119C and R38C THPO variants were stable in cells but secreted poorly. At equal concentrations, the R119C protein had reduced ability to stimulate MPL. The authors concluded that both reduced THPO secretion and reduced THPO activity contribute to the disease.

In HepG2 hepatocyte cells, Capaci et al. (2023) found that knocking down ETS1 and STAT4, alone or together, strongly reduced endogenous THPO mRNA and protein. Proximity ligation and coimmunoprecipitation assays showed that ETS1 and STAT4 interact, suggesting that they form a transcriptional complex on THPO regulatory sequences. Knocking down STAT3 also reduced THPO expression, although no STAT3 binding site was predicted in the promoter.

### Diagnosis

Pecci et al. (2018) suggested that THPO-related CAMT should be suspected in children or young adults with hypomegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia, with or without other cytopenias, including those with apparently acquired aplastic anemia. They proposed that measuring serum THPO could help distinguish patients with THPO mutations from those with MPL variants or other bone marrow failure syndromes.

Capaci et al. (2023) recommended that molecular analysis of THPO, including its regulatory regions, be routinely included in the diagnostic workup of suspected CAMT. They also suggested that a normal serum THPO level in a patient with amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia should strongly suggest an alteration of THPO.

### Clinical Management

Seo et al. (2017) reported that the 2 surviving patients in their series had improved trilineage hematopoiesis after treatment with a THPO receptor agonist.

Pecci et al. (2018) treated all 3 affected sibs with romiplostim. The proband had not responded to cyclosporine A or oxymetholone. At age 4.4 years he started romiplostim at 1 microgram/kg/week for 3 months, followed by 4 micrograms/kg monthly, and had a rapid trilineage response. His mean neutrophil count rose from 0.7 to 1.7 x 10(9)/l. He had no further spontaneous bleeding, infections, or transfusions during 6.5 years of follow-up. His brother achieved a platelet response, remission of bleeding, and transfusion independence over 6.3 years of follow-up. His sister had platelet and hemoglobin responses. No side effects attributable to romiplostim were observed.

Capaci et al. (2023) treated their proband with eltrombopag at 1.4 mg/kg/day. His platelet count stabilized at about 40 x 10(9)/L after 1 month, and the dose was increased to 2.8 mg/kg/day. After 6 months at that dose his platelet count had reached 125 x 10(9)/L and his easy bruising had resolved. The authors described this as the first report of eltrombopag therapy in a patient with a THPO mutation. ⚠︎
