### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the THPO gene (600044) on chromosome 3q27.1.

### Description

Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is an autosomal recessive disorder of thrombopoietin (THPO) production. It is characterized by severe thrombocytopenia with markedly reduced bone marrow megakaryocytes, which may progress to trilineage bone marrow failure resembling aplastic anemia (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018). Because the defect lies in a cytokine produced outside the hematopoietic system, the marrow failure does not respond to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, whereas affected individuals have responded to THPO receptor agonists (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018; Capaci et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (2013) described 2 sibs from a Micronesian family who had idiopathic aplastic anemia with autosomal recessive inheritance.

Seo et al. (2017) reported 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families who had severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to trilineage bone marrow failure. Four of the children received hematopoietic stem cell transplants. All 4 had poor graft function and persistent severe cytopenias, even after repeated transplants from different donors.

Pecci et al. (2018) reported a boy born to Egyptian first-cousin parents who presented at 3.5 years of age with ecchymotic patches. He had very severe thrombocytopenia (platelets 3 x 10(9)/l), hyporegenerative anemia, and severe neutropenia, with normal platelet size and no cytomorphologic abnormalities. Bone marrow was hypocellular with very rare immature megakaryocytes and a normal karyotype. After Fanconi anemia and PNH were excluded, he was diagnosed with idiopathic aplastic anemia. He went on to have recurrent major hemorrhages (epistaxis, gum and rectal bleeding) and severe infections that required repeated transfusions. Examination also showed preauricular skin tags and a left divergent squint. His 2 younger sibs were also affected. A brother had severe isolated thrombocytopenia (26 x 10(9)/l) at 7 months, with markedly fewer bone marrow megakaryocytes, and developed recurrent spontaneous bleeding requiring platelet transfusions at 1.6 years. A sister developed a hematoma at a vaccination site at 2 months and had anemia and severe thrombocytopenia. The parents had normal blood counts. Pecci et al. (2018) noted that thrombocytopenia was the only feature present in all patients and suggested that the trilineage aplasia in the oldest sib indicated that the disease worsens with age, making the presentation indistinguishable from CAMT due to MPL mutations.

Capaci et al. (2023) reported a boy born to first-cousin Muslim Palestinian parents who presented at age 3.5 years with easy bruising and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 21 x 10(9)/L) with a normal mean platelet volume. His platelet count shortly after birth had been 70 x 10(9)/L, and at age 2 years it was 20 x 10(9)/L, when he was diagnosed with urethral stenosis. Bone marrow biopsies showed normocellular marrow with severe megakaryocyte hypoplasia, a few small immature forms, and mild dyserythropoiesis. Apart from occasional mild neutropenia, he had no other cytopenias and no pancytopenia. Chromosome breakage analysis ruled out Fanconi anemia, and the bone marrow karyotype was normal.

### Biochemical Features

Seo et al. (2017) found that THPO was absent from the serum of patients carrying either the homozygous R99W or the homozygous R157X THPO mutation.

Pecci et al. (2018) found that serum THPO levels measured before romiplostim treatment in the 2 older affected sibs were within the normal range: 10.9 pg/ml in the proband and 14.1 pg/ml in his brother, compared with 6.9 to 54.4 pg/ml in 50 healthy controls. Because serum THPO is usually markedly raised in bone marrow aplasia, including MPL-related CAMT, the authors considered these levels inappropriately low, supporting defective THPO production.

Capaci et al. (2023) found a serum THPO concentration of 126 pg/mL in their proband (reference range 15-80 pg/mL for children). They noted that THPO is expected to be very much higher in amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia (e.g., a median of 1,493 pg/mL in CAMT due to MPL mutations) and interpreted the inappropriately normal-range level as indicating defective THPO production, similar to patients with THPO coding mutations that prevent secretion.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMT2 in the families reported by Dasouki et al. (2013), Seo et al. (2017), Pecci et al. (2018), and Capaci et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance; affected individuals were homozygous for THPO mutations, and in the families studied by Pecci et al. (2018) and Capaci et al. (2023) the parents were heterozygous.

Heterozygous carriers may have mild thrombocytopenia. Dasouki et al. (2013) found reduced platelet counts in both parents and a sib heterozygous for the THPO R17C variant, and concluded that this partial loss-of-function allele causes aplastic anemia in the homozygous state and mild thrombocytopenia in the heterozygous state. Capaci et al. (2023) found that the heterozygous mother of their proband had mild thrombocytopenia (platelet count about 80 x 10(9)/L) that worsened during each pregnancy, without bleeding symptoms; a heterozygous younger sib had a platelet count of 130 x 10(9)/L around birth and 160 x 10(9)/L several weeks later. In contrast, the heterozygous parents in the family reported by Pecci et al. (2018) had normal blood counts.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 Micronesian sibs with autosomal recessive aplastic anemia, Dasouki et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.112C-T missense mutation in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg38-to-cys (R38C) substitution (R17C when the 21-residue signal peptide is excluded) in the receptor-binding domain. In silico modeling predicted that a fifth cysteine, in addition to the 4 conserved cysteines in the receptor-binding domain, might disrupt normal disulfide bonding and impair receptor binding. Medium containing the mutant THPO was 2- to 3-fold less able to sustain proliferation of THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cells, and the authors concluded that this was a partial loss-of-function allele. Heterozygous family members had reduced platelet counts, whereas a sib with wildtype sequence had a normal platelet count.

In affected children from 2 of 3 families with thrombocytopenia progressing to bone marrow failure, Seo et al. (2017) used exome and targeted sequencing to identify a homozygous R99W mutation in the THPO gene. In the affected child from the third family, they identified a homozygous R157X mutation in THPO. Both mutations resulted in a lack of THPO in the patients' serum. Seo et al. (2017) concluded that biallelic loss-of-function mutations in THPO cause bone marrow failure that does not respond to transplant because the mechanism lies outside the hematopoietic cells.

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with CAMT, Pecci et al. (2018) found no MPL variant but identified a homozygous c.355C-T transition in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg119-to-cys (R119C) substitution. The parents were heterozygous. The variant was absent from the SNP, 1000 Genomes, and ExAC databases. Residue R119 (R98 in the mature protein) lies in helix C of the MPL-binding domain and is highly conserved, including in EPO. In HEK293T cells, wildtype and R119C THPO were expressed and equally stable, but the THPO concentration in R119C supernatants was about 23-fold lower than wildtype, indicating defective secretion. At equal THPO concentrations, R119C retained about 50% of wildtype activity in stimulating proliferation of MPL-dependent UT7-TPO cells and caused defective phosphorylation of STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT. The previously reported R38C variant gave similar results. ⚠︎

In a boy from a consanguineous Palestinian family with CAMT, Capaci et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.-323C-T substitution in the promoter region of the THPO gene, 45 nucleotides upstream of the transcription start site of transcript variant 1. Both parents and a younger sib were heterozygous, and 2 healthy sibs were homozygous wildtype. The variant was listed in dbSNP but absent from gnomAD, and it lies in a region conserved across species. No variants were found in MPL, MECOM, HOXA11, RBM8A, or Fanconi anemia and other bone marrow failure genes. The c.-323C nucleotide was predicted to lie within consensus binding sites for ETS1 and STAT4 that are lost with the variant. In HEK293T cells, a luciferase reporter driven by the mutant THPO promoter showed almost 80% less activity than the wildtype reporter; silencing ETS1 and/or STAT4 reduced activity from the wildtype promoter, whereas the mutant promoter was almost inactive regardless of silencing. Chromatin immunoprecipitation showed that both factors bound the wildtype promoter strongly and the mutant promoter less, with binding significantly impaired at least for STAT4.

### Pathogenesis

Seo et al. (2017) concluded that bone marrow failure due to biallelic loss-of-function THPO mutations does not respond to transplant because the mechanism lies outside the hematopoietic cells, and that the findings support the critical role of the MPL-THPO pathway in hematopoiesis.

Pecci et al. (2018) showed that the R119C and R38C THPO variants caused defective THPO secretion from HEK293T cells and reduced activity of the secreted protein in stimulating MPL-dependent cell proliferation and downstream STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT phosphorylation.

In HepG2 hepatocyte cells, Capaci et al. (2023) found that knocking down ETS1 and STAT4, alone or together, strongly reduced endogenous THPO mRNA and protein. Proximity ligation and coimmunoprecipitation showed that ETS1 and STAT4 interact, suggesting that they form a transcriptional complex on THPO regulatory sequences. Knockdown of STAT3 also reduced THPO expression, although no STAT3 binding site was predicted in the promoter. These findings indicated that loss of ETS1 and STAT4 binding due to a promoter mutation is a mechanism of CAMT.

### Diagnosis

Pecci et al. (2018) suggested that THPO-related CAMT should be suspected in children or young adults with hypomegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia, with or without other cytopenias, including apparently acquired aplastic anemia. They proposed that serum THPO measurement could help distinguish patients with THPO mutations from those with MPL variants or other bone marrow failure syndromes, and stressed that recognizing these patients avoids unnecessary hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and immunosuppression.

Capaci et al. (2023) recommended that molecular analysis of THPO, including its regulatory regions, be part of the diagnostic workup for suspected CAMT. They suggested serum THPO measurement as a screening tool, noting that a normal serum THPO level in the setting of amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia should strongly suggest an alteration of THPO.

### Clinical Management

Seo et al. (2017) reported that the 2 surviving patients in their study showed improved trilineage hematopoiesis after treatment with a THPO receptor agonist, and stressed the importance of accurate genetic diagnosis for treatment decisions in bone marrow failure.

Pecci et al. (2018) treated all 3 affected sibs with the THPO mimetic romiplostim. The proband, who had not responded to cyclosporine A or oxymetholone, started romiplostim at 4.4 years (1 microgram/kg/week for 3 months, then 4 micrograms/kg monthly) and had a rapid trilineage response, with neutrophils rising from a mean of 0.7 to 1.7 x 10(9)/l; he had no further spontaneous bleeding, infections, or transfusions over 6.5 years of follow-up. His brother achieved a platelet response, remission of bleeding, and transfusion independence over 6.3 years, and his sister had platelet and hemoglobin responses. No side effects were observed.

Capaci et al. (2023) treated their proband with the THPO-receptor agonist eltrombopag, starting at 1.4 mg/kg/day. His platelet count stabilized at about 40 x 10(9)/L after 1 month, and the dose was increased to 2.8 mg/kg/day. After 6 months at that dose his platelet count had risen to 125 x 10(9)/L and his easy bruising had resolved. This was the first report of eltrombopag therapy in a patient with a THPO mutation. The authors noted that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is expected to be ineffective in CAMT due to THPO mutations.
