### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding the Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1 (SLC5A1; 182380) on chromosome 22q12.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disease that appears within the first weeks of life. It is characterized by a selective failure to absorb dietary glucose and galactose from the intestine. The resulting severe diarrhea and dehydration are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991; Martin et al., 1996). The disorder is caused by a defect in SGLT1, the Na+/glucose cotransporter of the intestinal brush border (Martin et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

ANDERSON et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption, together with her family. Her jejunal mucosa could not concentrate glucose in vitro, and she had a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption. Her 5-year-old brother was asymptomatic.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported remission of glucose intolerance in familial glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 Arab children with glucose-galactose malabsorption from 8 different families. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. The mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. The children first came to attention because of chronic watery diarrhea present since birth and failure to thrive. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another child was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All affected individuals presented with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration in the newborn period. Other features of the initial presentation were increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite illness, irritability, and apathy. When the disease was not recognized, a prolonged clinical course followed. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) studied glucose transport by jejunal mucosa in vitro and by kidney in vivo in an affected girl, her family, and 16 normal volunteers. In normal human jejunal mucosa, glucose transport was concentrative, saturable, sodium and energy dependent, and showed competitive inhibition. Biopsy specimens from 6 normal controls and from the proband's asymptomatic brother accumulated glucose to 16 times the concentration in the incubation medium. In contrast, the proband's mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. Both parents and a half sister had impaired glucose transport, with values between those of normal subjects and the proband. In these 3 heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired but glucose efflux was the same as in normal controls. Kinetic analysis showed a reduced capacity (Vmax) with a normal affinity (Km) for glucose transport. All subjects accumulated fructose similarly. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption in the proband, whereas her brother's titration curve was similar to those of 7 normal volunteers.

Meeuwisse (1970) reported studies of renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) found intermediate impairment of intestinal glucose transport in both parents and a half sister of an affected girl. They concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Xin and Wang (2011) found homozygous SLC5A1 mutations in affected members of a large Amish pedigree, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1991) noted that the SGLT1 gene is on the distal q arm of chromosome 22. Turk et al. (1993) used fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe to refine the localization of SGLT1 to chromosome 22q13.1. The gene had previously been placed at 22q11.2-qter.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 (SLC5A1) cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family with GGM. Sequencing showed a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. Xenopus oocytes injected with the mutant cRNA completely lost Na(+)-dependent glucose transport.

Turk et al. (1993) stated that the cause of GGM in 2 patients was a missense mutation in SGLT1, asp28-to-asn (D28N), and that this change abolished SGLT1 transport activity in vitro.

Martin et al. (1996) screened the SGLT1 gene in 30 new patients with GGM and used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype. They concluded that defects in both the trafficking and the function of SGLT1 cause GGM.

Xin and Wang (2011) sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons and the adjacent exon-intron boundaries of SLC5A1 in 33 Old Order Amish patients with GGM. They found 4 homozygous missense mutations that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype in all affected individuals: N51S, A411T, R558H, and H615Q.

### Pathogenesis

Turk et al. (1991) noted that intestinal biopsies from GGM patients had shown a specific defect in Na(+)-dependent glucose absorption at the brush border. Normally, glucose absorption is mediated by the Na+/glucose cotransporter in the brush border membrane of the intestinal epithelium. Cellular influx is driven by the transmembrane Na+ electrochemical gradient, and the sugar then crosses the basolateral membrane into the blood via the facilitated glucose carrier.

### Diagnosis

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that heterozygotes for glucose-galactose malabsorption can be detected, because in vitro studies of their jejunal mucosa showed a reduced capacity for glucose transport.

Martin et al. (1996) performed prenatal screening for GGM in 2 pregnancies in a large consanguineous family in which 2 proband sibs carried the D28N mutation in SGLT1. They PCR-amplified the first exon of SGLT1 from genomic DNA and tested for D28N by EcoRV restriction digestion. The proband's sib was heterozygous for the mutation and a cousin was not a carrier. At 2 years of age, both children were healthy and had had no diarrheal symptoms.

In 8 Arab children, Abdullah et al. (1996) confirmed the diagnosis of glucose-galactose malabsorption by laboratory investigations that included small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay.

### Clinical Management

Turk et al. (1991) noted that the severe diarrhea and dehydration of GGM are usually fatal unless glucose and galactose are eliminated from the diet.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported that all 8 Arab children with glucose-galactose malabsorption improved clinically on a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) observed that Amish patients with GGM who were correctly diagnosed and adequately managed improved dramatically, with immediate cessation of diarrhea and rapid rehydration, and then grew and developed normally. When the disease was not recognized, a prolonged clinical course followed.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested a founder effect for the SLC5A1 mutations associated with GGM in the Old Order Amish. They proposed population-specific genetic testing to achieve early diagnosis, which they considered critical for a favorable outcome.
