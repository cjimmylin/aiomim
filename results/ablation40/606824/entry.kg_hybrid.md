### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SLC5A1 gene (182380) on chromosome 22q12.3.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disorder that appears within the first weeks of life. Affected infants cannot absorb dietary glucose and galactose from the intestine. The resulting severe diarrhea and dehydration are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991). The disorder is caused by a defect in the Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1 of the intestinal brush border (Martín et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

ANDERSON et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported remission of glucose intolerance in familial glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 children with glucose-galactose malabsorption from 8 different families. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. Their mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. All of the children had chronic watery diarrhea from birth and failure to thrive. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All of them presented with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. Other features at initial presentation were increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite illness, and irritability and apathy.

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) studied glucose transport by jejunal mucosa in vitro and by the kidney in vivo in a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption, in her family, and in 16 normal volunteers. Biopsy specimens from 6 normal controls and from the proband's asymptomatic 5-year-old brother accumulated glucose to 16 times the concentration in the incubation medium. The proband's mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. All subjects accumulated fructose to a similar degree. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption in the proband, whereas her brother's titration curve was similar to those of 7 normal volunteers. The authors concluded that absent intestinal glucose transport is accompanied by partial impairment of renal glucose transport.

Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) found impaired jejunal glucose transport in both parents and a half sister of a patient with glucose-galactose malabsorption. Their values fell between those of normal controls and that of the proband. In these heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired but glucose efflux was the same as in normal controls. Kinetic analysis showed a reduced capacity (Vmax) with normal affinity (Km) for glucose transport. The authors concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait and that heterozygotes can be detected.

In a large Old Order Amish pedigree, Xin and Wang (2011) found that homozygous SLC5A1 mutations cosegregated with GGM, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1991) stated that SGLT1, the Na+/glucose cotransporter gene they had previously cloned from normal human ileum, resides on the distal long arm of chromosome 22.

The SGLT1 gene had previously been assigned to 22q11.2-qter. Using fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe, Turk et al. (1993) refined its location to chromosome 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 (SLC5A1) cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family with GGM. Sequencing identified a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. Xenopus oocytes injected with cRNA carrying the mutation showed complete loss of Na(+)-dependent glucose transport.

Turk et al. (1993) stated that in 2 patients with GGM, the underlying cause was a missense mutation in SGLT1, asp28-to-asn (D28N). This change had been shown in vitro to eliminate SGLT1 transport activity.

Martín et al. (1996) noted that they had previously found a missense mutation in the SGLT1 gene in 2 sisters with GGM. They screened the gene in 30 new patients with GGM and used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype. According to the title of their report, the mutations caused defects in SGLT1 trafficking and function.

Xin and Wang (2011) studied 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. They sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons and exon-intron boundaries of the SLC5A1 gene. They identified 4 homozygous missense mutations, c.152A-G (N51S), c.1231G-A (A411T), c.1673G-A (R558H), and c.1845C-G (H615Q), which cosegregated with the GGM phenotype in all affected individuals.

### Pathogenesis

Turk et al. (1991) noted that intestinal biopsies from GGM patients had shown a specific defect in Na(+)-dependent glucose absorption in the brush border. Normal glucose absorption is mediated by the Na+/glucose cotransporter in the brush border membrane of the intestinal epithelium. Cellular influx is driven by the transmembrane Na+ electrochemical gradient, and the sugar then moves to the blood across the basolateral membrane via the facilitated glucose carrier.

Martín et al. (1996) concluded that defects in the trafficking and function of the Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1 cause GGM. SGLT1 couples sugar transport to Na+ gradients across the intestinal brush border.

### Diagnosis

Abdullah et al. (1996) confirmed the diagnosis of glucose-galactose malabsorption in 8 Arab children with laboratory investigations that included small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay.

Martín et al. (1996) performed prenatal screening for GGM in 2 subsequent pregnancies in a large consanguineous family. In this family, 2 proband sibs with GGM had been shown to carry the D28N mutation in SGLT1. The first exon of SGLT1 was PCR-amplified from genomic DNA and screened for D28N by EcoRV restriction digestion. The proband's sib was heterozygous for the mutation, and a cousin was not a carrier. Both children were healthy without diarrheal symptoms at 2 years of age.

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested that population-specific genetic testing is needed to pursue early diagnosis of GGM in the Amish, since early diagnosis is critical for a favorable outcome.

### Clinical Management

Abdullah et al. (1996) found that all 8 children with glucose-galactose malabsorption in their series responded clinically to a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) observed that Amish patients with GGM who were correctly diagnosed and adequately managed improved dramatically. Their diarrhea stopped immediately, they rehydrated quickly, and their growth and development were normal afterward. When the disease was not recognized, a prolonged clinical course followed.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested a founder effect for the SLC5A1 mutations associated with GGM in the Old Order Amish.
