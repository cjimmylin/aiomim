### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SLC5A1 gene (182380) on chromosome 22q12.3.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disease. It appears within the first weeks of life and is marked by a selective failure to absorb dietary glucose and galactose from the intestine. The resulting severe diarrhea and dehydration are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991). The disorder is caused by a defect in the intestinal brush border Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1 (Martín et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

Anderson et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption and her family. In vitro, her jejunal mucosa was unable to concentrate glucose throughout a 60-minute incubation period. She also had a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption. Both of her parents and a half sister showed impaired intestinal glucose transport. Her asymptomatic brother had normal transport.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported familial glucose-galactose malabsorption in which the glucose intolerance went into remission.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 Arab children with glucose-galactose malabsorption from 8 different families. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. They were first seen for chronic watery diarrhea, present since birth, and failure to thrive. The mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All affected individuals presented as newborns with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. Part of the initial presentation was increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing regardless of illness, and irritability and apathy. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) found that jejunal mucosa from both parents of an affected girl (obligate heterozygotes) and from her half sister had glucose transport values between normal and those of the proband. Influx was impaired but efflux was normal. Kinetic analysis showed a reduced Vmax with a normal Km for glucose transport. Fructose accumulation was similar in all subjects. Renal glucose titration showed that the proband's absent intestinal glucose transport was accompanied by a partial impairment of renal glucose reabsorption. Her unaffected brother had a normal titration curve.

Meeuwisse (1970) reported studies on renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Their conclusion rested on the intermediate impairment of intestinal glucose transport in both parents and a half sister of an affected girl. Turk et al. (1991) and Martín et al. (1996) also described GGM as autosomal recessive. In a large Old Order Amish pedigree, Xin and Wang (2011) found homozygous SLC5A1 mutations in all affected individuals. ⚠︎

### Mapping

The SGLT1 gene had previously been placed at 22q11.2-qter. Turk et al. (1993) used fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe to refine its location to chromosome 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 (SLC5A1) cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family affected with GGM. Sequencing identified a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. Mutant cRNA injected into Xenopus oocytes completely abolished Na(+)-dependent glucose transport. ⚠︎

Martín et al. (1996) screened the SGLT1 gene in 30 new patients with glucose-galactose malabsorption. They used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype. They concluded that defects in SGLT1 trafficking and function cause the disorder.

In 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree, Xin and Wang (2011) found 4 homozygous missense mutations in the SLC5A1 gene: c.152A-G (N51S), c.1231G-A (A411T), c.1673G-A (R558H), and c.1845C-G (H615Q). All 4 cosegregated with the GGM phenotype in every affected individual.

### Pathogenesis

Turk et al. (1991) noted that intestinal biopsies from GGM patients had shown a specific defect in Na(+)-dependent glucose absorption at the brush border. In normal intestine, this absorption is carried out by the Na+/glucose cotransporter in the brush border membrane.

### Diagnosis

Elsas et al. (1970) reported that heterozygotes for glucose-galactose malabsorption could be detected by in vitro measurement of glucose transport in jejunal biopsy specimens, which showed a reduced capacity for glucose transport.

Abdullah et al. (1996) confirmed the diagnosis of glucose-galactose malabsorption with laboratory investigations, including small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay.

Martín et al. (1996) performed prenatal screening for GGM in 2 pregnancies in a large consanguineous family in which 2 affected sibs were known to carry a D28N missense mutation in SGLT1. They PCR-amplified the first exon of SGLT1 from genomic DNA and tested for D28N by EcoRV restriction digestion. The proband's sib was heterozygous, and a cousin was not a carrier. At 2 years of age, both children were healthy with no diarrheal symptoms.

### Clinical Management

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported that all 8 Arab children with glucose-galactose malabsorption improved clinically on a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) observed that Amish patients with GGM who were correctly diagnosed and adequately managed showed immediate cessation of diarrhea and rapid rehydration, followed by normal growth and development. When the disease was not recognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested a founder effect for the SLC5A1 mutations associated with GGM in the Old Order Amish. Because early diagnosis is critical for a favorable outcome, they proposed population-specific genetic testing.
