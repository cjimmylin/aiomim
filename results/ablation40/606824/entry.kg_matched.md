### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SLC5A1 gene (182380) on chromosome 22q12.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disorder that appears within the first weeks of life. The intestine selectively fails to absorb dietary glucose and galactose. The resulting severe osmotic diarrhea and dehydration are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991; Martín et al., 1996). The disorder is caused by a defect in the intestinal brush border Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1 (Martín et al., 1996). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Anderson et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported familial glucose-galactose malabsorption in which the glucose intolerance went into remission.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 children with glucose-galactose malabsorption from 8 different families. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. Mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. All had chronic watery diarrhea from birth and failure to thrive. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with glucose-galactose malabsorption from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All affected individuals presented with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. Other features at initial presentation included increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite illness, irritability, and apathy.

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) studied glucose transport in vitro by jejunal mucosa from a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption. Mucosa from 6 normal controls and from her asymptomatic 5-year-old brother accumulated glucose to 16 times the concentration in the incubation medium. The proband's mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. Fructose accumulation was similar in all subjects. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption in the proband. Her unaffected brother had a titration curve similar to those of 7 normal volunteers. The authors concluded that absent intestinal glucose transport is accompanied by partial impairment of renal glucose transport.

Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) found impaired jejunal glucose transport in both parents and a half sister of a patient with glucose-galactose malabsorption. Their values lay between those of normal controls and the proband. In these heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired but efflux was normal. Kinetic analysis showed reduced capacity (Vmax) with normal affinity (Km). The authors concluded that the disorder is autosomal recessive and that heterozygotes can be detected. Autosomal recessive inheritance was also supported by the finding of homozygous SLC5A1 mutations in affected members of an Old Order Amish pedigree (Xin and Wang, 2011).

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1991) reported that SGLT1, the Na+/glucose cotransporter gene they had previously cloned from normal human ileum, maps to the distal long arm of chromosome 22. Turk et al. (1993) used fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe to refine its location to chromosome 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 (SLC5A1) cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family with GGM. Sequencing identified a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. Xenopus oocytes injected with cRNA carrying the mutation completely lost Na(+)-dependent glucose transport.

Turk et al. (1993) noted that in 2 patients with autosomal recessive GGM, the cause was an asp28-to-asn (D28N) missense mutation in SGLT1. In vitro, this change eliminated SGLT1 transport activity. Martín et al. (1996) noted that these 2 patients were proband sibs from a large consanguineous family.

Martín et al. (1996) had previously found a missense mutation in SGLT1 in 2 sisters with GGM. They then screened the SGLT1 gene in 30 new patients with GGM and used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations they identified to the phenotype. According to the title of their report, the mutations affected SGLT1 trafficking and function.

Xin and Wang (2011) studied 33 Old Order Amish patients with GGM from a large pedigree. They sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons and exon-intron boundaries of SLC5A1 and identified 4 homozygous missense mutations: c.152A-G (N51S), c.1231G-A (A411T), c.1673G-A (R558H), and c.1845C-G (H615Q). All 4 cosegregated with the disease phenotype in all affected individuals.

### Pathogenesis

Turk et al. (1991) noted that intestinal biopsies from GGM patients show a specific defect in Na(+)-dependent glucose absorption in the brush border. This absorption is normally carried out by the Na+/glucose cotransporter in the brush border membrane of the intestinal epithelium. Turk et al. (1993) stated that SGLT1 is the main carrier protein for intestinal uptake of dietary glucose and galactose. It is a 75-kDa glycoprotein located in the intestinal brush border and is predicted to have 12 membrane spans. SGLT1 couples sugar transport to Na+ gradients across the intestinal brush border, and Martín et al. (1996) concluded that defects in its trafficking and function cause GGM.

### Diagnosis

Abdullah et al. (1996) confirmed the diagnosis of GGM in their 8 Arab patients by laboratory investigations that included small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay.

Martín et al. (1996) performed prenatal screening for GGM in 2 subsequent pregnancies in a large consanguineous family in which 2 proband sibs with GGM carried the D28N mutation in SGLT1. Exon 1 of SGLT1 was PCR-amplified from fetal genomic DNA and screened for D28N by EcoRV restriction digestion. The proband's sib was heterozygous, and a cousin was not a carrier. Both children remained healthy without diarrheal symptoms at 2 years of age.

### Clinical Management

Abdullah et al. (1996) found that all 8 of their children with GGM responded clinically to a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) observed that Amish patients with GGM who were correctly diagnosed and adequately managed had immediate cessation of diarrhea and rapid rehydration, followed by normal growth and development. When the disease went unrecognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested a founder effect for the SLC5A1 mutations associated with GGM in the Amish. They also suggested that population-specific genetic testing is needed to allow early diagnosis, which is critical for a good outcome.
