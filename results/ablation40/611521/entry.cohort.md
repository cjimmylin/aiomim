### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), also called tyrosine kinase-2 (TYK2) deficiency, is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), or TYK2 deficiency, is an autosomal recessive immunodeficiency. Its core feature is susceptibility to mycobacterial infections (including BCG disease and tuberculosis) and/or viral infections. The first patient reported had been diagnosed clinically with hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES), with atopic dermatitis, elevated serum IgE, and infections with viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria. Most patients reported later had no features of HIES, i.e., no atopy, no high serum IgE, and no staphylococcal disease (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been diagnosed clinically with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient was unusually susceptible to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, born in 1992, who was the third child of healthy first-cousin parents. He received BCG vaccine at 2 months of age, and left axillary lymphadenopathy was found at 8 months. He developed disseminated BCG infection with generalized fistulous lymphadenopathy and fever. His later illnesses included neurobrucellosis, a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, pneumonia, and herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve. He also had severe sensorineural hearing loss and severe cognitive impairment, and MRI showed lacunar infarcts. Serum IgE was normal or slightly high when measured at ages 2, 11, 16, and 18 years (80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml, respectively), and serum IgG, IgM, and IgA were normal. The PPD skin test was negative, and the antibody response to tetanus toxoid was low. He had no atopy or eczema, staphylococcal skin infections, skeletal or dental abnormalities, or candidiasis. Kilic et al. (2012) noted that, unlike the first Japanese patient, their patient had none of the cardinal features of HIES. They suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in patients with BCG disease, particularly those with severe herpesvirus infections, even in the absence of HIES features.

Kreins et al. (2015) restudied the Japanese patient of Minegishi et al. (2006) (P1), a man born to consanguineous parents. He had had atopic dermatitis since 1 month of age and recurrent bacterial and viral infections since 1 year of age. His infections included recurrent Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, BCGitis, nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis with sepsis, parainfluenza virus-3 pneumonia, recurrent oral herpes simplex virus infections, molluscum contagiosum, mild oral candidiasis, pneumonia without pneumatocele, otitis, and sinusitis. He also had ocular sarcoidosis. Repeated testing showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE concentrations (1,430-2,100 IU/ml).

Kreins et al. (2015) also studied 7 TYK2-deficient patients from 5 kindreds originating from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. P2 was the Turkish man reported by Kilic et al. (2012), whose parents were first cousins. Kreins et al. (2015) stated that he had disseminated BCG lymphadenitis at 21 months of age, severe bacterial meningitis with CSF cultures positive for Brucella at 8 years, and shingles at 11 years. P3, a girl from a consanguineous Moroccan kindred, was hospitalized at age 13 years with suspected abdominal tuberculosis. Her abdominal symptoms persisted despite antimycobacterial treatment, and she developed ascites and a psoas abscess. She was readmitted with bacterial meningitis and died 15 months after symptom onset. Her younger brother (P4) was hospitalized at 8 months of age for meningitis of unknown etiology. He had recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels, and at 15 years of age he was receiving no treatment. P5 was a 5-year-old boy born to consanguineous Iranian parents. He received BCG at birth and developed right axillary lymphadenitis at 2 months and multiple cervical lymphadenitis at 4 months. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with fistulization and pulmonary involvement. His 2-year-old sister (P6) had BCG lymphadenopathy lasting 1 month after vaccination at birth and was hospitalized at 1 year of age for an unknown viral infection of the skin. P7 was a 9-year-old girl from Iran born to consanguineous parents. She had miliary tuberculosis, and lung CT showed diffuse bilateral micronodular infiltrations with hilar and mediastinal adenopathies and cavitary consolidation in the right upper lobe. She had also had pneumonia at 8 months of age. P8 was an Argentinian boy who had herpes gingivostomatitis at 18 months of age, then aseptic meningitis and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions at 24 months, as well as exanthema subitum. He had mild mental disability, and it had not been established whether this was congenital or followed the meningitis. None of the 7 patients had features of HIES (atopy, high serum IgE, or staphylococcal disease), and none had fungal infections or mucocutaneous candidiasis. Four of them had a pure phenotype of mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (P4 and P5) or tuberculosis (P3 and P7). Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infection, and they suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in children with severe tuberculosis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient who had eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels greater than 1,000 U/mL, but no viral or mycobacterial infections. The patient's NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, were normal.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy, aged 1 year and 11 months, who was the second child of young nonconsanguineous parents. He was born in 2016 and received BCG vaccine on the third day after birth. He developed abscess and ulceration at the BCG vaccination site and BCG-associated tuberculosis of an axillary lymph node. He also had recurrent respiratory tract infections, bacterial pneumonia, diarrhea, Salmonella infection, Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, and influenza virus infection. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal illness, or lymphopenia. The percentage of B cells was increased and the percentage of NK cells was decreased. He began to say simple words at 3 years and 4 months of age, with normal motor development, and he had a small head circumference. Reviewing 12 patients with TYK2 deficiency, Wu et al. (2020) found that 58% had intracellular bacterial infections, 58% had recurrent viral infections, and 25% had markedly increased serum IgE. None had the facial, skeletal, dental, or vascular features of STAT3-related hyper-IgE syndrome.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 more TYK2-deficient patients who presented with or without high IgE levels and atopy and who had distinct profiles of infecting pathogens.

Zhang et al. (2022) reported 3 unrelated Turkish children with TYK2 deficiency, each born to a different set of consanguineous parents, who were hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. P2, a 2-year-old girl, had moderate COVID-19 pneumonia and did not need oxygen therapy. She had been hospitalized for sepsis as a neonate, admitted to the ICU twice for fever and dyspnea before 5 months of age, and diagnosed with Kawasaki disease at 11 months. At 2 years she was hospitalized for infection with the VZV vaccine. P3, a 4-year-old boy, and P4, a 9-year-old boy, were both admitted to the ICU for critical COVID-19 pneumonia requiring high-flow oxygen therapy. P3 had been hospitalized for sepsis as a neonate and admitted to the ICU for influenza pneumonia at 3 years of age. P4 had recurrent bronchitis requiring hospital admission and inhaler therapy. Neither P3 nor P4 had an adverse reaction to MMR vaccination. P3's 3-year-old younger sister had the same genotype. She had a history of critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and ventilation and of disseminated varicella, but she had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and remained seronegative. Zhang et al. (2022) considered critical COVID-19 pneumonia in unrelated TYK2-deficient patients surprising, because TYK2 deficiency impairs but does not abolish cellular responses to type I IFNs. They noted that more infected TYK2-deficient patients were needed to estimate penetrance, which may be incomplete.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 is autosomal recessive. Kilic et al. (2012) and Kreins et al. (2015) found homozygous TYK2 mutations in patients born to consanguineous parents. In the families studied by Kreins et al. (2015), clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. In the patient reported by Wu et al. (2020), both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance in 3 families by segregation analysis.

### Molecular Genetics

Minegishi et al. (2006) found a homozygous TYK2 mutation in a patient who had been diagnosed clinically with HIES. The patient's cells had defects in signaling by several cytokines, including type I interferon, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23, and transduction of intact TYK2 restored the signaling defects. Kreins et al. (2015) later found that this patient carried a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 of TYK2 (C70HfsX21).

In a Turkish boy with disseminated BCG infection and neurobrucellosis, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del). The deletion created a premature termination codon at position 767. Western blotting detected no full-length TYK2 protein in the patient's EBV-B cells.

In 7 patients from 5 kindreds originating from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina, Kreins et al. (2015) identified 5 different homozygous null mutations in the TYK2 gene. The mutations were a 9-bp deletion in exon 16 (c.2303_2311del; L767X) in the patient previously reported by Kilic et al. (2012), a 1-bp insertion (c.3318_3319insC; T1106HfsX4), a nonsense mutation (c.462G-T; E154X), a 1-bp deletion (c.149delC; S50HfsX1), and a nonsense mutation (c.1912C-T; R638X). All 5 mutations were predicted to cause premature stop codons and were absent from public databases. TYK2 protein was undetectable in the patients' cells.

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent infections, Wu et al. (2020) identified a homozygous missense mutation in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene (c.2395G-A; G799R). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. In the patient's cells, the mutation decreased TYK2 mRNA expression, and Western blot detected no TYK2 protein. In transfected HEK293T cells, the mutant decreased mRNA and protein expression of FLAG- and GFP-tagged TYK2. The patient also carried heterozygous variants in the CFH (134370; c.2089C-T, L697F), LRRC8A (608360; c.1250G-A, R417Q), and NIPBL (608667; c.5575-27_c.5575-26delCT) genes.

Lv et al. (2022) found novel TYK2 mutations in 5 TYK2-deficient patients. They confirmed the pathogenic effects of the mutations by qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays.

In an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated Turkish children, each born to consanguineous parents, who were homozygous for the same TYK2 frameshift variant (P216Rfs*14). This variant had previously been shown to be loss-of-expression, and the authors confirmed that it was loss of function. ACE2-transduced SV40 fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for the variant did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally, were not protected by pretreatment with IFN-alpha-2b, and failed to induce interferon-stimulated genes in response to infection or IFN-alpha-2b. The authors implicated autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency as a new genetic cause of COVID-19 pneumonia.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Lv et al. (2022) noted that TYK2-deficient patients do not necessarily present with features of hyper-IgE syndrome; their patients presented with or without elevated IgE and atopy. The patients' PBMCs responded heterogeneously to IFN-alpha, IFN-beta, IFN-gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. The authors proposed that TYK2 orchestrates many cytokine signaling pathways and that different combinations of defects in these pathways account for the clinical manifestations.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that TYK2 deficiency likely accounted for their patient's complex clinical picture, which included impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation. They noted that TYK2 has obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals in human innate and acquired immunity and that this differs substantially from its function in mice.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that absence of TYK2 led to defective IL-12 signaling and impaired IFN-gamma production, causing susceptibility to BCG and Brucella. They suggested that the viral illnesses were probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that cells from all 8 TYK2-deficient patients had impaired, but not abolished, responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta, and IL-10. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda, and LIF were normal. Surface expression of IFNAR1, IL10R2, and IL12RB1 was reduced, reflecting the scaffolding role of TYK2, and expression of wildtype TYK2 restored it. Cells from the 7 newly studied patients responded normally to IL-6, whereas cells from the original patient with HIES (P1) were hyporesponsive. Neither expression of wildtype TYK2 nor silencing of TYK2 rescued the IL-6 hyporesponsiveness in P1 cells. The authors suggested that impaired IL-6 responses and HIES are not intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency and that P1 may carry a variant in another, as yet unknown, component of the IL-6 pathway. Ex vivo, patients had normal proportions of IL-17-positive T cells, although IL-17 production by naive CD4 T cells was profoundly impaired in vitro. Central and effector memory T-cell numbers were also normal. Kreins et al. (2015) suggested that residual IL-23 responses explain the absence of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) found that a TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in a monocyte assay. It partly reduced IL-10 signaling, together with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not reduce IL-6 signaling. A TYK2-deficient human cell line had almost normal type III IFN signaling and only minimal impairment of virus control. The authors suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may help protect against viruses in TYK2 deficiency, resulting in a surprisingly mild human phenotype.

### Diagnosis

Fuchs et al. (2016) established a simple diagnostic monocyte assay that detects loss of the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in TYK2 deficiency.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) found no individuals homozygous for TYK2 stop mutations in any database, and only 84 heterozygous individuals.

Wu et al. (2020) noted that the c.2395G-A (G799R) variant was present in the gnomAD database, which suggested that it was not a private mutation.
