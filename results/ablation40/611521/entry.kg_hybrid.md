### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency caused by deficiency of tyrosine kinase-2 (TYK2), a Janus kinase that mediates signaling by several cytokines. The first reported patient had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES). He had atopic dermatitis and elevated serum IgE, together with unusual susceptibility to viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria (Minegishi et al., 2006). Most patients reported later had mycobacterial and/or viral infections without features of HIES. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infection, caused by impaired responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta, and that HIES does not appear to be an intrinsic feature of the disorder.

### Nomenclature

Wu et al. (2020) noted that TYK2 deficiency was originally defined in patients with autosomal recessive hyper-IgE syndrome, but had since been proposed as a distinct primary immunodeficiency. They also noted that the International Union of Immunological Societies classified TYK2 deficiency under Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD).

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient was unusually susceptible to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, the third child of healthy first-cousin parents, who was vaccinated with BCG at 2 months of age. He developed left axillary lymphadenopathy at 8 months and generalized lymphadenopathy and fatigue at 21 months. On admission he had fever and fistulous, enlarged, bilateral cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymphadenopathy, and M. bovis BCG was isolated from the draining cervical sinuses. His condition improved with antimycobacterial treatment, without relapse. At 8 years of age he had fever, a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, headache, vomiting, and neck stiffness, and blood and CSF cultures were positive for Brucella. After treatment he had hearing loss (92 dB in the right ear and 82 dB in the left), severe cognitive impairment, and pneumonia, and MRI showed lacunar infarcts in the left temporal and occipital lobes and in both parietal lobes. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the right maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve, which was treated with intravenous acyclovir. He had no skeletal or dental manifestations of HIES and no history of atopy, asthma, candidiasis, boils, folliculitis, or cold abscess. His serum IgE was normal or only slightly high (highest value 218 IU/ml, at 16 years of age). At 2 years of age his serum IgG, IgM, and IgA, his lymphocyte subsets, and his nitroblue tetrazolium test were normal, but his antibody response to tetanus toxoid was low (0.69 IU/ml; normal, 2-20). Kilic et al. (2012) noted that, unlike the previously reported Japanese patient, their patient had none of the cardinal features of HIES.

Kreins et al. (2015) updated the clinical history of the original Japanese patient (P1), who was born to consanguineous parents. He had had atopic dermatitis since 1 month of age and recurrent bacterial and viral infections since 1 year. He developed BCGitis at 22 months, 6 months after vaccination. He also had repeated Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, pneumonia without pneumatocele formation, otitis, and sinusitis. At 15 years of age he had sepsis following severe nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis. Other infections included parainfluenza virus-3 pneumonia, recurrent oral herpes simplex virus (HSV) infections associated with tonsillitis, molluscum contagiosum, and mild oral candidiasis. He repeatedly had hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and serum IgE concentrations of 1,430 to 2,100 IU/ml, and his NIH HIES score was 48 points. At 30 years of age he had ocular sarcoidosis.

Kreins et al. (2015) described 7 further TYK2-deficient patients (P2-P8) from 5 unrelated families from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. All had received BCG vaccine. None had atopy, high serum IgE, staphylococcal disease, other documented pyogenic infections, or fungal infections, including mucocutaneous candidiasis. P2 was the Turkish patient reported by Kilic et al. (2012). P3 and P4 were Moroccan sibs born to first-cousin parents. P3, a girl, was hospitalized at 13 years with abdominal tuberculosis and developed ascites, a psoas abscess from which M. tuberculosis was isolated, and disseminated extrapulmonary tuberculosis; she died after bacterial meningitis, 15 months after onset. Her brother, P4, was hospitalized at 8 months with meningitis of unknown etiology, and had recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels. P5 and P6 were Iranian sibs born to consanguineous parents. P5, a boy, developed right axillary and multiple cervical lymphadenitis at 2 and 4 months of age, and paravertebral and psoas abscesses with fistulization and pulmonary involvement at 4.5 years. He was treated with antimycobacterial drugs, surgery, and IFN-gamma. His sister, P6, developed BCG lymphadenopathy after vaccination and was hospitalized at 1 year for an unknown viral skin infection. P7, a 9-year-old Iranian girl born to consanguineous parents, had miliary tuberculosis with diffuse bilateral micronodular lung infiltrates, hilar and mediastinal adenopathy, and cavitary consolidation; M. tuberculosis was identified in gastric washes. P8, an 11-year-old adopted Argentinian boy, had herpes gingivostomatitis at 18 months and at almost 6 years, aseptic meningitis and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions at 24 months, and aseptic herpes meningitis with disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions at 10 years. He also had mild mental disability of uncertain origin and was maintained on acyclovir prophylaxis. Kreins et al. (2015) noted that P4 and P5 had a pure MSMD phenotype and P3 and P7 a pure tuberculosis phenotype, whereas P2, P6, and P8 resembled patients with partial autosomal recessive STAT1 deficiency. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) described a TYK2-deficient patient who had eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels greater than 1,000 U/mL, but no viral or mycobacterial infections.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy born to young nonconsanguineous parents. After BCG vaccination on day 3 of life, he had repeated abscess formation and ulceration at the injection site, which healed by 10 months of age. At 14 months he developed BCG-associated tuberculosis of a left axillary lymph node; acid-fast bacilli were seen and M. bovis BCG was isolated, and there was no lung involvement. From 6 months of age he had recurrent respiratory tract infections (pneumonia or upper respiratory tract infection every 1 to 2 months) and diarrhea. Pathogens identified included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and BCG, and IgM serology was positive for influenza A and B viruses. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal disease, or lymphopenia. He had a small head circumference and spoke his first simple words at 3 years 4 months, with normal motor development. At 1 year 11 months his serum immunoglobulins, including IgE (27.4 IU/ml), were normal. The percentage of CD19+ B cells was increased (26.6%) and the percentage of CD3-CD16/CD56+ NK cells was decreased (3.6%). In a review of 12 reported TYK2-deficient patients, including their own, Wu et al. (2020) found that 7 (58%) had intracellular bacterial infections and 7 (58%) had recurrent viral infections, 3 (25%) had markedly increased serum IgE, and 2 (17%) had rash or atopic dermatitis/eczema. None had the special facial features, abnormal bone development or pathologic fractures, delayed loss of deciduous teeth, or aneurysms of STAT3-related hyper-IgE syndrome.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 TYK2-deficient patients who presented with or without hyper-IgE levels and atopy, and with distinct profiles of pathogen infection. Lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted.

Zhang et al. (2022) reported 3 unrelated Turkish children with TYK2 deficiency and COVID-19 pneumonia, each born to a different set of consanguineous parents: a 2-year-old girl (P2) and boys aged 4 (P3) and 9 (P4) years. P2 was hospitalized with moderate pneumonia that did not require oxygen therapy. P3 and P4 were admitted to the intensive care unit with critical pneumonia requiring high-flow oxygen. All 3 survived. P2 had been hospitalized for sepsis in the neonatal period, had been admitted to intensive care twice before 5 months of age for fever and dyspnea, was diagnosed with Kawasaki disease at 11 months, and was hospitalized for infection with the varicella-zoster virus vaccine at 2 years. P3 had neonatal sepsis and was admitted to intensive care for influenza pneumonia at 3 years. P4 had recurrent bronchitis requiring hospital admission and inhaler therapy. The 3-year-old sister of P3, who had the same genotype, had had critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and ventilation, as well as disseminated varicella; she had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 in the families reported by Kreins et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance: in all kindreds, clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes for TYK2 mutations, and only in homozygotes. Wu et al. (2020) found that the parents of their homozygous patient were heterozygous for the mutation. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis in 3 families.

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells had defects in multiple cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I interferon, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23, and these signals were restored by transducing the intact TYK2 gene.

In a Turkish boy with disseminated BCG disease, neurobrucellosis, and herpes zoster, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del), creating a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein was detected by Western blotting in the patient's EBV-B cells.

Kreins et al. (2015) reinvestigated the original Japanese patient (P1), who carried a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 of TYK2 (C70HfsX21). In 7 patients (P2-P8) from 5 unrelated families, they identified homozygous TYK2 mutations by linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing (P2), whole-exome sequencing (P3-P7), or targeted next-generation sequencing (P8). The 9-bp deletion in P2, which Kreins et al. (2015) designated c.2303_2311del (L767X), was the same as that reported by Kilic et al. (2012). The other mutations were c.3318_3319insC (T1106HfsX4) in P3 and P4; c.462G-T (E154X) in P5 and P6; c.149delC (S50HfsX1) in P7; and c.1912C-T (R638X) in P8. All were predicted to result in a premature stop codon. Using 4 antibodies against N- or C-terminal epitopes, the authors found that the 130-kD TYK2 protein was undetectable in EBV-B cells from all patients tested and in HVS-T cells and fibroblasts from P2. No abnormal splicing was detected, indicating complete TYK2 deficiency. ⚠︎

In a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels greater than 1,000 U/mL, Fuchs et al. (2016) used a monocyte assay to show that the TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response. The mutation partly reduced IL-10-mediated signaling, in association with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not reduce IL-6-mediated signaling. In their review, Wu et al. (2020) listed this patient as a Kurdish boy carrying the TYK2 mutation c.647delC (P216RfsX14).

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent respiratory infections, Wu et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2395G-A transition in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The patient also had heterozygous variants in the CFH, LRRC8A, and NIPBL genes, but the authors considered the evidence for their pathogenicity insufficient. In the patient's cells, TYK2 mRNA was decreased and no TYK2 protein was detected by Western blotting. When the mutant was expressed in HEK293T cells, mRNA and protein expression of tagged TYK2 constructs were both decreased.

In 5 TYK2-deficient patients, Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations by high-throughput sequencing and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. They confirmed the pathogenic effects by qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays.

In an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) used whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing to identify recessive, biochemically complete inborn errors of type I IFN immunity in 12 (10.7%). Three unrelated Turkish children from consanguineous families were homozygous for the same TYK2 frameshift mutation (P216Rfs*14). The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with an autosomal recessive pattern in all 3 families. None of the 15 recessive defects studied was found in 1,224 controls with benign SARS-CoV-2 infection without pneumonia. SV40-transformed fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for the same TYK2 mutation did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally, were not protected by IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment, and did not induce interferon-stimulated genes.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kreins et al. (2015) found that, unlike P1, none of the 7 newly identified TYK2-deficient patients had features of HIES, even though all 8 had complete TYK2 deficiency. EBV-B cells and fibroblasts from P2-P8 responded normally to IL-6, whereas cells from P1 did not. Expression of wildtype TYK2 in P1 cells did not restore the IL-6 response, and silencing TYK2 in control cells did not affect it. The authors therefore concluded that the impaired IL-6 response in P1 was independent of his TYK2 mutation. Whole-exome sequencing found no mutation in known components of the IL-6 pathway, and the authors suggested that P1 may carry a variant in another, as yet unknown, component. They proposed that his poor IL-6 responses probably underlie at least his staphylococcal disease and perhaps other HIES features.

Zhang et al. (2022) observed complete penetrance of COVID-19 pneumonia in the families with TYK2 deficiency that they reported. However, they considered critical pneumonia in 3 unrelated TYK2-deficient patients surprising, because TYK2 deficiency impairs but does not abolish cellular responses to type I IFNs. They suggested that penetrance may be incomplete and that more infected patients are needed to estimate it.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) found that the TYK2-deficient patient's cells had defects in signaling by type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. They linked these defects to impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation, and concluded that TYK2 function in humans differs substantially from that in mice.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that the absence of TYK2 caused defective IL-12 signaling and impaired IFN-gamma production, leading to susceptibility to BCG and Brucella. They suggested that the herpesvirus infection was probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found low surface expression of IFN-alpha-R1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1 on TYK2-deficient EBV-B cells, which was rescued by expression of wildtype TYK2. Cells from all 8 patients had impaired but not abolished responses to IL-12, with reduced IFN-gamma production by T and NK cells, in contrast to IL-12R-beta-1 deficiency. The authors suggested that this explained both the mycobacterial disease and its relatively favorable response to antibiotics. Responses to IL-23 were also impaired but not abolished. Naive CD4 T cells produced little IL-17A and IL-17F in vitro, but patients had normal proportions of circulating IL-17-positive T cells, which may account for the absence of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis. Responses to IFN-alpha/beta were impaired but not abolished. STAT1 phosphorylation was reduced and STAT3 phosphorylation was absent, both restored by wildtype TYK2, and IFN-alpha failed to control vesicular stomatitis virus replication in EBV-B and HVS-T cells, whereas fibroblasts controlled the virus partially. Responses to IL-10 were impaired but not abolished; the authors suggested that the residual responses probably account for the absence of early-onset colitis. Because P1 and the nonatopic patients had equally impaired IL-10 responses, IL-10 defects were considered unlikely to cause HIES. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda, and LIF were normal. Numbers of central and effector memory T cells were normal in patients P1-P5.

In a TYK2-deficient human cell line, Fuchs et al. (2016) found almost normal type III IFN signaling, with only minimal impairment of virus control. NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, were normal in their patient, which differs from observations in TYK2-deficient mice. The authors suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may contribute to antiviral protection and to the surprisingly mild human phenotype.

Lv et al. (2022) found heterogeneous responses of patient peripheral blood mononuclear cells to IFN-alpha, IFN-beta, IFN-gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. They proposed that TYK2 orchestrates multiple cytokine signaling pathways and that different combinations of defects account for the divergent clinical manifestations.

### Diagnosis

Kilic et al. (2012) concluded that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in patients with BCG disease, particularly those with unusually severe herpesvirus infections, even when cardinal features of HIES are absent. They suggested that this may also apply to patients with other mycobacterial diseases or with infections caused by other intramacrophage pathogens, such as Salmonella and Brucella.

Fuchs et al. (2016) established a simple diagnostic monocyte assay to assess the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in TYK2 deficiency.

Wu et al. (2020) summarized the main features of TYK2 deficiency as a TYK2 mutation, autosomal recessive inheritance, normal lymphocyte numbers with defects in signaling by multiple cytokines, susceptibility to viruses or intracellular bacteria, and IgE that may or may not be elevated. They suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in patients with repeated intracellular bacterial infections (including tuberculosis), repeated viral infections, or eczema, especially when there is a family history.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) found that the 5 TYK2 mutations they newly identified were private, being absent from their in-house exome database and from public databases. No individuals homozygous for TYK2 stop mutations were present in any database, and only 84 heterozygous individuals were found.

Wu et al. (2020) noted that the G799R mutation is listed in the gnomAD database, suggesting that it is not a private mutation.
