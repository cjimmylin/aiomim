### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.2.

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency caused by complete TYK2 deficiency. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype is mycobacterial and/or viral infections, caused by impaired cellular responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta. Affected individuals may have bacille Calmette-Guerin (BCG) disease, tuberculosis, or other intracellular bacterial infections such as Salmonella and Brucella, as well as herpesvirus and other viral infections.

The first reported patient had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES), with atopic dermatitis and elevated serum IgE (Minegishi et al., 2006). Most patients reported since then have not had features of HIES, and Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that impaired IL-6 responses and HIES did not appear to be intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency in humans.

### Nomenclature

Wu et al. (2020) noted that TYK2 deficiency was originally defined in patients with autosomal recessive HIES but had since been proposed to be a clinically distinct primary immunodeficiency. They stated that the International Union of Immunological Societies had classified TYK2 deficiency as a form of mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD).

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient was unusually susceptible to several types of microorganism, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, the third child of healthy first-cousin parents, who was vaccinated with BCG at 2 months of age. Left axillary lymphadenopathy appeared at 8 months, and generalized lymphadenopathy and fatigue followed at 21 months. On admission at 2 years he had fever and fistulous, enlarged bilateral cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymph nodes. The PPD skin test was negative, and M. bovis BCG was isolated from the draining cervical sinuses. He responded to antimycobacterial treatment without relapse. At 8 years he developed Brucella meningitis with a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, after a history of eating unpasteurized cheese. This was followed by severe hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, and lacunar infarcts on MRI. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve, which was treated with acyclovir. Serum IgE at 2, 11, 16, and 18 years of age was 80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml, respectively, which was normal or slightly elevated. Serum IgG, IgM, and IgA were normal, but the antibody response to tetanus toxoid was low. He had no atopy, asthma, candidiasis, boils, folliculitis, or cold abscesses, and investigations for skeletal and dental manifestations of HIES were negative. Kilic et al. (2012) contrasted this patient with the Japanese patient of Minegishi et al. (2006), who had severe atopic dermatitis, staphylococcal disease, and a serum IgE of 2,100 IU/ml, but who also lacked the bone fractures and dental and facial features of autosomal dominant HIES. ⚠︎

Kreins et al. (2015) gave further clinical details on the Japanese patient (P1) of Minegishi et al. (2006), a man born to consanguineous parents. He had atopic dermatitis from 1 month of age and recurrent bacterial and viral infections from 1 year of age. He developed BCG lymphadenitis at 22 months of age and repeated Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, pneumonia without pneumatocele, otitis, and sinusitis. At 15 years he had sepsis following severe nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis. His viral infections included parainfluenza virus-3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV infections with tonsillitis, and molluscum contagiosum, and he also had mild oral candidiasis. Testing repeatedly showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and serum IgE of 1,430 to 2,100 IU/ml. At 30 years of age he had ocular sarcoidosis.

Kreins et al. (2015) also reported 7 TYK2-deficient patients (P2-P8) from 5 unrelated families from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. These patients had mycobacterial and/or viral infections but no features of HIES. P2 was the Turkish patient described by Kilic et al. (2012). P3, a Moroccan girl born to first-cousin parents, was hospitalized at 13 years with suspected abdominal tuberculosis. She went on to develop ascites and a psoas abscess from which M. tuberculosis was isolated, and she died of bacterial meningitis 15 months after symptom onset. Her brother (P4) was hospitalized at 8 months for meningitis of unknown etiology. He had recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels. P5, an Iranian boy born to consanguineous parents, developed axillary and cervical lymphadenitis at 2 and 4 months of age after BCG vaccination at birth. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with fistulization and pulmonary involvement. His younger sister (P6) had BCG lymphadenopathy after vaccination and was hospitalized at 1 year for an unknown viral infection of the skin. P7, a 9-year-old Iranian girl born to consanguineous parents, had miliary tuberculosis with bilateral micronodular lung infiltrates, cavitary consolidation, and hilar, mediastinal, and paraaortic adenopathy. P8, an 11-year-old adopted boy from Argentina, had herpes gingivostomatitis, aseptic herpes meningitis, and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions, as well as mild mental disability that may have been congenital or a consequence of meningitis. None of P2 through P8 had documented pyogenic or staphylococcal infections or fungal infections, and serum IgE was never found to be high. Candidiasis in P1 was limited to a few episodes of mild oral candidiasis. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient who had eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels above 1,000 U/mL, without viral or mycobacterial infections. NK-cell phenotype and function were normal.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy, aged 1 year 11 months, born to nonconsanguineous parents. Abscess and ulceration recurred at the site of his BCG vaccination, and at 14 months he developed BCG-associated tuberculosis of an axillary lymph node, with M. bovis BCG isolated from the draining sinus. From 6 months of age he had pneumonia or upper respiratory tract infections every 1 to 2 months and diarrhea. Pathogens identified included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and M. bovis BCG, and IgM antibodies to influenza A and B viruses were positive. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal illness, or lymphopenia. He began speaking simple words at 3 years 4 months of age, had normal motor development, and had a small head circumference. The percentage of CD19+ B cells was increased and that of NK cells was decreased. In a review of 12 reported TYK2-deficient patients, including theirs, Wu et al. (2020) found that 7 had intracellular bacterial infections, 7 had recurrent viral infections, 2 had rash or atopic dermatitis/eczema, and 3 had markedly increased serum IgE. None had the special facial features, abnormal bone development, pathologic fractures, delayed loss of deciduous teeth, or aneurysms seen in HIES caused by STAT3 mutation.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 TYK2-deficient patients who presented with or without hyper-IgE levels and atopy and who had distinct profiles of pathogen infection.

Zhang et al. (2022) studied an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. Among them were 3 unrelated Turkish children with TYK2 deficiency, each born to a different set of consanguineous parents. P2, a 2-year-old girl, had moderate COVID-19 pneumonia that did not require oxygen. She had been hospitalized for neonatal sepsis, had twice been admitted to intensive care for fever and dyspnea before 5 months of age, had been diagnosed with Kawasaki disease at 11 months, and had been hospitalized for infection with VZV vaccine at 2 years. P3, a 4-year-old boy, and P4, a 9-year-old boy, had critical COVID-19 pneumonia requiring high-flow oxygen in the intensive care unit. P3 had previously had neonatal sepsis and influenza pneumonia requiring intensive care at 3 years of age. P4 had recurrent bronchitis requiring hospital admission and inhaler therapy. The 3-year-old younger sister of P3 had the same genotype and had had critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and ventilation, as well as disseminated varicella. She had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 is autosomal recessive. Kreins et al. (2015) found that, in all of their kindreds, clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. Most of the reported patients were born to consanguineous parents (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2022). In the patient of Wu et al. (2020), both parents were heterozygous for the TYK2 mutation that the patient carried in homozygous state. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis in all 3 of their TYK2-deficient patients.

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with HIES, Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells had defects in several cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. Transducing the intact TYK2 gene restored these cytokine signals.

In a Turkish boy with disseminated BCG infection, neurobrucellosis, and herpes zoster, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del), which created a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein was detected by Western blotting in the patient's EBV-B cells.

Kreins et al. (2015) reported that the patient of Minegishi et al. (2006) carried a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 of TYK2. In 7 patients from 5 kindreds, they identified homozygous TYK2 mutations by linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing (P2, the patient of Kilic et al., 2012), whole-exome sequencing (P3 to P7), or targeted next-generation sequencing (P8). The 5 different mutations included a 9-bp deletion that they designated c.2303_2311del (L767X), a 1-bp insertion (c.3318_3319insC), a nonsense mutation (E154X), a 1-bp deletion (c.149delC), and a second nonsense mutation (R638X). All 5 were predicted to result in a premature stop codon, and all were absent from public databases. TYK2 protein was undetectable with antibodies against N- and C-terminal epitopes in cells from all patients tested, indicating complete TYK2 deficiency. Expressing wildtype TYK2 in patient EBV-B cells restored surface expression of IFN-alpha-R1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1 and STAT phosphorylation in response to IL-23, IFN-alpha, and IL-10.

In a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE above 1,000 U/mL, Fuchs et al. (2016) used a diagnostic monocyte assay to show that the TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response. The mutation partly reduced IL-10 signaling, which was associated with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not reduce IL-6 signaling.

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent respiratory infections, Wu et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2395G-A transition in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution. The mutation was heterozygous in both parents. TYK2 mRNA expression was reduced in the patient's cells, and no TYK2 protein was detected by Western blot. When the mutation was introduced into tagged TYK2 constructs in HEK293T cells, mRNA and protein expression were both reduced. The patient was also heterozygous for variants in the CFH, LRRC8A, and NIPBL genes. The authors considered the evidence for pathogenicity of these variants insufficient but stated that a pathogenic role could not be excluded.

In 5 TYK2-deficient patients, Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations by high-throughput sequencing and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. They confirmed the pathogenic effects of the mutations with qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays.

Zhang et al. (2022) analyzed exome or genome data from 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. They identified 3 unrelated Turkish children, born to consanguineous parents, who were homozygous for the same TYK2 mutation (P216Rfs*14), and confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis in all three. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed that this variant was loss-of-function. ACE2-transduced SV40 fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for this mutation did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally, were not protected by pretreatment with IFN-alpha-2b, and failed to induce IFN-stimulated genes.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kilic et al. (2012) noted that their Turkish patient, unlike the Japanese TYK2-deficient patient, had none of the cardinal features of HIES: atopic dermatitis, staphylococcal infections, and high serum IgE. They found this discrepancy difficult to explain and suggested that it might reflect a difference in the immunologic impact of TYK2 in the two patients.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that all 8 TYK2-deficient patients, including P1, had equally impaired but not abolished responses to IFN-alpha, IL-12, IL-23, and IL-10. They therefore concluded that defects in these responses were unlikely to explain HIES in P1. Only P1 had an impaired response to IL-6, and this defect was not corrected by expressing wildtype TYK2. The authors suggested that the poor IL-6 response in P1 probably underlay at least his staphylococcal disease and perhaps his other HIES features. Because whole-exome sequencing detected no mutation in known components of the IL-6 pathway, they suggested that P1 might carry a variant in another, as yet unknown, component of the pathway.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that TYK2 deficiency probably accounted for their patient's complex clinical manifestations, including impaired T helper-1 differentiation and accelerated T helper-2 differentiation. They also concluded that TYK2 plays obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals involved in innate and acquired immunity in humans, and that this differs substantially from its function in mice.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that absence of TYK2 caused defective IL-12 signaling, which impaired IFN-gamma production and led to susceptibility to BCG and Brucella. They suggested that the viral illnesses were probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that TYK2-deficient cells had reduced surface expression of IFN-alpha-R1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1, and that wildtype TYK2 rescued this defect. Cells from all 8 patients had impaired but not abolished responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta, and IL-10, while responses to IFN-gamma, IL-27, IL-21, IFN-lambda, and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) were normal. The authors proposed that residual IL-12-dependent IFN-gamma production by T and NK cells explained both the mycobacterial disease and its favorable response to antibiotics. Despite impaired IL-23 responses, proportions of IL-17-positive T cells ex vivo were normal, which probably accounted for the lack of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis. Residual IL-10 responses probably accounted for the lack of early-onset colitis. Numbers of central and effector memory T cells were normal, and the authors suggested that reactivation of chronic viral infections resulted mainly from impaired IFN-alpha/beta responses.

In a TYK2-deficient human cell line, Fuchs et al. (2016) found almost normal type III IFN signaling, with minimal impairment of virus control. In contrast to observations in TYK2-deficient mice, NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, were normal in their patient. They suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may contribute to antiviral protection in TYK2 deficiency, resulting in a surprisingly mild human phenotype.

Lv et al. (2022) found that cells from their patients responded heterogeneously to IFN-alpha/beta/gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23, and that lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted. They proposed that TYK2 orchestrates several cytokine signaling pathways and that different combinations of defects in these pathways account for the variable clinical manifestations.

Zhang et al. (2022) found it surprising that critical COVID-19 pneumonia occurred in 2 unrelated TYK2-deficient children, because TYK2 deficiency impairs but does not abolish cellular responses to type I IFNs. They stated that more TYK2-deficient patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 would need to be diagnosed to estimate penetrance. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Kilic et al. (2012) recommended considering TYK2 deficiency in patients with BCG disease, particularly when herpesvirus infections are unusually severe, even in the absence of the cardinal features of HIES. They suggested that this might also apply to other mycobacterial diseases and to infections with other intramacrophagic pathogens such as Salmonella and Brucella. Kreins et al. (2015) suggested that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in children with severe tuberculosis.

Wu et al. (2020) summarized the main features of TYK2 deficiency as autosomal recessive inheritance, a normal lymphocyte count with defects in signaling by multiple cytokines, susceptibility to viruses or intracellular bacteria, and IgE that may or may not be elevated. They suggested considering TYK2 deficiency in patients with repeated intracellular bacterial infections (including tuberculosis), repeated viral infections, or eczema, especially when there is a family history.

### Clinical Management

Zhang et al. (2022) suggested that inborn errors of type I IFN immunity be considered in children hospitalized for COVID-19. They also suggested that personalized treatment in the first days of infection be considered, with therapeutic type I IFN for defects upstream of the type I IFN receptors and monoclonal antibody therapy for defects downstream of them.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) found that their 5 TYK2 mutations were absent from public databases. No individuals homozygous for TYK2 stop mutations were found in any database, and only 84 heterozygous individuals were found. In contrast, Wu et al. (2020) noted that the G799R variant was present in the gnomAD database, suggesting that it was not a private mutation.

Zhang et al. (2022) found recessive complete inborn errors of type I IFN immunity, involving IFNAR1, STAT2, TYK2, or TLR7, in 12 of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. They found none in 1,224 individuals with benign SARS-CoV-2 infection (P = 1.2 x 10(-11)). Based on predicted loss-of-function variants in gnomAD, they estimated the cumulative frequency of recessive deficiency for these 4 genes at 4 x 10(-5).

### History

Kreins et al. (2015) noted that the finding of IL-6 hyporesponsiveness in the TYK2-deficient patient with HIES reported by Minegishi et al. (2006) had led to the identification of STAT3 mutations in patients with autosomal dominant HIES. With hindsight, they called this serendipitous, because their own data indicated that the IL-6 defect in that patient was independent of TYK2. ⚠︎
