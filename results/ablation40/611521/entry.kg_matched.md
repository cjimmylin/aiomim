### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), also known as autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency, is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency caused by complete deficiency of tyrosine kinase-2 (TYK2). Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that its core clinical phenotype is mycobacterial and/or viral infections, caused by impaired responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta. They also concluded that impaired IL-6 responses and hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES) do not appear to be intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency in humans. Wu et al. (2020) summarized the disorder as autosomal recessive and marked by normal lymphocyte counts with defects in signaling by multiple cytokines. Patients are susceptible to viruses or intracellular bacteria, and serum IgE may or may not be elevated.

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient was unusually susceptible to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy with TYK2 deficiency born to healthy first-cousin parents. He received BCG vaccine at 2 months and developed left axillary lymphadenopathy at 8 months. By 21 months this had progressed to disseminated BCG disease with fistulous cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymphadenitis. M. bovis BCG was isolated, and he responded to antimycobacterial treatment. At age 8 years he developed neurobrucellosis, with Brucella in blood and CSF cultures. This left him with severe hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, and lacunar infarcts on MRI. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve, which was cured with acyclovir. The patient had none of the cardinal features of hyper-IgE syndrome: he had no skeletal or dental abnormalities, no coarse facies, no atopy or asthma, and no skin problems such as candidiasis, boils, folliculitis, or cold abscess. He also had no staphylococcal infections. The authors noted that this contrasted with the previously reported Japanese patient with TYK2 deficiency. ⚠︎

Kreins et al. (2015) described 7 TYK2-deficient patients (P2-P8) from 5 unrelated families from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. All had mycobacterial and/or viral infections but none of the features of HIES. In particular, they did not have atopy, high serum IgE, or staphylococcal disease, and none had documented pyogenic or fungal infections, including mucocutaneous candidiasis. All had been BCG vaccinated. Patients P1 through P5 had normal numbers of central and effector memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, unlike patients with autosomal dominant HIES. The authors suggested that reactivation of chronic viral infections results from impaired IFN-alpha/beta responses rather than from a memory T-cell defect.

Kreins et al. (2015) updated the clinical course of the original Japanese TYK2-deficient patient (P1), who was born to consanguineous parents. He had atopic dermatitis from 1 month of age, BCGitis, recurrent S. aureus skin abscesses, Salmonella sepsis, PIV3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV, molluscum contagiosum, mild oral candidiasis, and hypereosinophilia. His IgE was 1,430 to 2,100 IU/ml, and his NIH HIES score was 48. At age 30 years he had ocular sarcoidosis.

Kreins et al. (2015) described a Turkish man (P2), born to consanguineous first-cousin parents, who had disseminated BCG lymphadenitis at 21 months, severe Brucella meningitis at 8 years, and shingles of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve at 11 years. He was 23 years old at the time of the report.

Kreins et al. (2015) described Moroccan sibs born to consanguineous first-cousin parents. The girl (P3) was hospitalized at 13 years with abdominal tuberculosis. She developed ascites, a psoas abscess with M. tuberculosis, and disseminated extrapulmonary tuberculosis, and she died after bacterial meningitis 15 months after onset. Her brother (P4) was hospitalized at 8 months with meningitis of unknown etiology. He had recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels. ⚠︎

Kreins et al. (2015) described Iranian sibs born to consanguineous parents. The boy (P5) developed axillary and cervical BCG lymphadenitis at 2 and 4 months. At 4.5 years he had paravertebral and psoas abscesses with pulmonary involvement, which were treated with antimycobacterial drugs, surgery, and IFN-gamma. His sister (P6) developed BCG lymphadenopathy after vaccination and was hospitalized at 1 year for an unknown viral skin infection.

Kreins et al. (2015) described a 9-year-old Iranian girl (P7), born to consanguineous parents, who had miliary tuberculosis. She had diffuse bilateral micronodular lung infiltrations, hilar and mediastinal adenopathy, and cavitary consolidation, and M. tuberculosis was identified on gastric washes. She had had pneumonia at 8 months.

Kreins et al. (2015) described an 11-year-old adopted Argentinian boy (P8) with recurrent HSV disease. He had herpes gingivostomatitis at 18 months and at almost 6 years, aseptic herpes meningitis, and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions at 24 months and 10 years. He had mild mental disability of uncertain origin and responded to acyclovir prophylaxis. He had no intracellular bacterial infection.

Fuchs et al. (2016) described a TYK2-deficient patient who had eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels greater than 1000 U/mL. The patient had no viral or mycobacterial infections.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy with BCG disease, born in 2016 to young nonconsanguineous parents. After BCG vaccination on day 3 of life, he had repeated abscess and ulceration at the injection site, which had healed by 10 months. At 14 months he developed BCG-associated left axillary lymph node tuberculosis without lung involvement; acid-fast bacilli and Mycobacterium bovis BCG were isolated from the draining sinuses. From 6 months of age he had recurrent respiratory tract infections, with pneumonia or upper respiratory infection every 1 to 2 months, as well as diarrhea. Pathogens found included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and BCG, and IgM serology was positive for influenza A and B. He had no significant viral or fungal infections and no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal disease, or lymphopenia. He also had a small head circumference and delayed speech, saying his first simple words at 3 years 4 months, but his motor development was normal. ⚠︎

Wu et al. (2020) reviewed 12 reported patients with TYK2 deficiency, including their own. The male:female ratio was 8:4, and the mean age at diagnosis was 10.8 years. Seven (58%) had intracellular bacterial infections, 2 (17%) had M. tuberculosis infection, and 7 (58%) had recurrent viral infections. Two (17%) had rash or atopic dermatitis/eczema, 3 (25%) had markedly elevated serum IgE, and 2 (17%) had EBV-related B-cell lymphoma with T-cell lymphopenia. None had the facial features, skeletal abnormalities or fractures, delayed shedding of primary teeth, or aneurysms seen in STAT3-related hyper-IgE syndrome.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 patients with TYK2 deficiency who had distinct profiles of pathogen infection and who presented with or without elevated IgE levels and atopy. The authors noted that TYK2-deficient patients do not necessarily have features of hyper-IgE syndrome. Flow cytometric analysis of T- and B-cell differentiation showed that lymphocyte homeostasis was also disrupted.

Zhang et al. (2022) described 3 unrelated Turkish children with COVID-19 pneumonia, each born to a different set of consanguineous parents: a 2-year-old girl (P2), a 4-year-old boy (P3), and a 9-year-old boy (P4). P2 was hospitalized with moderate pneumonia that did not need oxygen. P3 and P4 were admitted to intensive care with critical pneumonia needing high-flow oxygen. All 3 survived. P2 had a history of neonatal sepsis, 2 ICU admissions for fever and dyspnea before 5 months of age, and Kawasaki disease at 11 months. At 2 years she was hospitalized for infection from the varicella-zoster virus (VZV) vaccine. P3 had neonatal sepsis and an ICU admission for influenza pneumonia at 3 years. P4 had recurrent bronchitis that needed hospital admission and inhaler therapy. P3 and P4 had no adverse reaction to the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine. The 3-year-old younger sister of P3 was homozygous for the same TYK2 variant. She had critical influenza pneumonia that needed intubation and ventilation, as well as disseminated varicella. She had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and remained seronegative.

### Biochemical Features

Kilic et al. (2012) measured serum IgE in their Turkish patient at ages 2, 11, 16, and 18 years and found it to be normal or only slightly high (80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml, respectively). These values were well below the 2,100 IU/ml reported in the Japanese patient with TYK2 deficiency. At age 2 years his serum IgG, IgM, and IgA, lymphocyte subsets, and NBT test were normal, but his antibody response to tetanus toxoid was low (0.69 IU/ml).

Wu et al. (2020) found that at age 1 year 11 months their patient had normal serum IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE (27.4 IU/ml), normal eosinophil counts, and normal percentages of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells. The percentage of CD19+ B cells was increased (26.6%), and the percentage of CD3-CD16/CD56+ NK cells was decreased (3.6%).

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 in the families reported by Kreins et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In all kindreds, clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes for the TYK2 mutations and only in homozygotes. Most of the families were consanguineous. Wu et al. (2020) found both parents of their patient to be heterozygous for the mutation. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis in 3 families.

### Heterogeneity

Kreins et al. (2015) found that EBV-B cells and fibroblasts from patients P2 through P8 responded normally to IL-6, whereas cells from the original Japanese patient (P1) did not. Restoring wildtype TYK2 in P1 cells did not rescue the IL-6 hyporesponsiveness, and silencing TYK2 in control cells did not impair IL-6 responses. This indicated that the IL-6 defect in P1 is independent of the TYK2 mutation. Whole-exome sequencing found no mutation in known components of the IL-6 pathway, and the authors suggested that P1 may carry a variant in another, unknown component. They suggested that the impaired IL-6 response may underlie P1's staphylococcal disease and other HIES features. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Minegishi et al. (2006) found a homozygous TYK2 mutation in a patient with a clinical diagnosis of hyper-IgE syndrome and susceptibility to viral, fungal, and mycobacterial infections. Transducing the intact TYK2 gene into the patient's cells restored the defective cytokine signals. The authors concluded that TYK2 deficiency likely accounted for the patient's complex clinical manifestations.

Woellner et al. (2007) discussed the relationship between hyper-IgE syndrome and mutations in the TYK2 gene.

In a Turkish boy with TYK2 deficiency, born to first-cousin parents, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del) that creates a premature termination codon at position 767. Western blotting detected no full-length TYK2 protein in the patient's EBV-B cells. He was diagnosed with autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency at age 17 years.

In 7 patients from 5 unrelated families, Kreins et al. (2015) identified homozygous mutations in the TYK2 gene, found by linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, or targeted next-generation sequencing. The patients were a Turkish patient (P2), Moroccan sibs (P3 and P4), Iranian sibs (P5 and P6), an Iranian girl (P7), and an Argentinian boy (P8). The authors also reinvestigated the original Japanese patient (P1), who carries a homozygous 4-bp deletion in TYK2. Western blotting with 4 antibodies against N- or C-terminal TYK2 epitopes showed that the 130-kD TYK2 protein was undetectable in EBV-B cells from all patients tested and in HVS-T cells and fibroblasts from P2. No abnormal splicing was detected, which indicated complete TYK2 deficiency. ⚠︎

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent respiratory infections, Wu et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene (c.2395G-A, G799R). Both parents were heterozygous for the variant, which was listed in gnomAD. The authors also found heterozygous variants in the CFH, LRRC8A, and NIPBL genes but considered the evidence for their pathogenicity insufficient. In the patient's cells, TYK2 mRNA was decreased and no TYK2 protein was detected on Western blot. When the mutant was expressed in HEK293T cells, mRNA and protein expression of FLAG- and GFP-tagged TYK2 were both decreased.

In 5 patients with TYK2 deficiency, Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations by high-throughput sequencing and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays confirmed that the mutations were pathogenic.

Zhang et al. (2022) studied an international cohort of 112 children younger than 16 years who were hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. They found recessive, biochemically complete inborn errors of type I IFN immunity in 12 of the children (10.7%), including autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency in 3. In each of these 3 unrelated consanguineous Turkish children, whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing followed by Sanger confirmation identified the same homozygous frameshift mutation in TYK2 (P216Rfs*14). Family segregation analysis confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance in all 3 families. The variant had previously been shown to cause loss of expression in patient cells, and Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed that it was loss-of-function. None of the 15 recessive defects found in the cohort was present in 1,224 controls with benign SARS-CoV-2 infection (P = 1.2 x 10(-11)). The authors identified autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency as a new genetic cause of COVID-19 pneumonia. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kreins et al. (2015) noted that 4 of their 7 new patients had a pure phenotype of either Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (P4 and P5) or tuberculosis (P3 and P7). The other 3 patients (P2, P6, and P8) resembled patients with partial autosomal recessive STAT1 deficiency.

Zhang et al. (2022) observed complete penetrance for COVID-19 pneumonia in their families with autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency. They found critical pneumonia in 3 unrelated TYK2-deficient patients surprising, because TYK2 deficiency impairs, but does not abolish, cellular responses to type I IFNs. They suggested that TYK2 deficiency may have incomplete penetrance and that more infected patients are needed to estimate it. They also speculated that the moderate pneumonia seen in children with TYK2 deficiency might be severe or critical in adults with the same disorder.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) showed that cells from their TYK2-deficient patient had defects in several cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I interferon, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. The authors linked these defects to impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation. They concluded that TYK2 has obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals involved in innate and acquired immunity in humans, and that this role differs substantially from its function in mice.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that loss of TYK2 caused defective IL-12 signaling and impaired IFN-gamma production, making their patient susceptible to BCG and Brucella. They suggested that his herpes virus disease was probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that TYK2-deficient EBV-B cells had low surface expression of IFN-alpha-R1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1. Ectopic expression of wildtype TYK2 rescued this expression, which highlighted the scaffolding role of TYK2 in receptor expression.

Kreins et al. (2015) showed that cells from TYK2-deficient patients had impaired, but not abolished, responses to IL-12. After stimulation with BCG plus IL-12, T and NK cells produced less IFN-gamma. This contrasted with the abolished responses seen in IL-12R-beta-1 deficiency, and the authors suggested that it explains both the mycobacterial susceptibility and the relatively favorable response to antibiotics. Responses to IL-23 were also impaired but not abolished. In vitro production of IL-17A and IL-17F by naive CD4 T cells was profoundly impaired, yet patients had normal proportions of circulating IL-17+ T cells. Candidiasis was very mild in P1 and absent in P2 through P8, which the authors attributed to residual IL-23 responses in vivo.

Kreins et al. (2015) also showed that TYK2-deficient cells had impaired, but not abolished, responses to IFN-alpha/beta. STAT1 phosphorylation was reduced and STAT3 phosphorylation was absent, and both were restored by wildtype TYK2. The cells also showed reduced GAF and ISGF3 DNA binding and low basal ISG expression. IFN-alpha failed to control VSV replication in EBV-B and HVS-T cells and only partly controlled it in fibroblasts. The authors suggested that these defects account for the viral infections and their mild course, similar to partial STAT1 deficiency. TYK2-deficient cells responded normally to IFN-lambda-1 and IFN-lambda-3 in terms of IFIT1 induction, although basal IFIT1 mRNA was extremely low. Responses to IL-27, IFN-gamma, IL-21, and LIF were normal, indicating that TYK2 is redundant for these pathways. All TYK2-deficient patients had impaired, but not abolished, responses to IL-10. STAT3 phosphorylation was reduced and was rescued by wildtype TYK2, SOCS3 induction was impaired, and suppression of LPS-induced TNF in P2 macrophages was intermediate. The residual IL-10 responses probably account for the lack of early-onset colitis. Because P1, who was atopic, and the other, nonatopic patients had equally impaired IL-10 responses, the authors considered IL-10 defects unlikely to cause HIES. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) found that the patient's TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response. It partly reduced IL-10-mediated signaling, which was associated with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but it did not reduce IL-6-mediated signaling. In a TYK2-deficient human cell line, type III IFN signaling was nearly normal, and virus control was only minimally impaired. NK-cell phenotype and function in the patient were normal, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, unlike observations in TYK2-deficient mice. The authors suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may contribute to antiviral protection and explain the surprisingly mild human phenotype of TYK2 deficiency.

Wu et al. (2020) proposed that mycobacterial susceptibility in TYK2 deficiency results from weakened NK- and T-cell responses to IL-12 and IL-23, which lead to insufficient IFN-gamma production. They proposed that viral susceptibility results from impaired IFN-alpha/beta responses and that type III IFN signaling may compensate.

Lv et al. (2022) found that peripheral blood mononuclear cells from their patients responded heterogeneously to IFN-alpha, IFN-beta, IFN-gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. They proposed that TYK2 orchestrates several cytokine signaling pathways, and that different combinations of signaling defects explain the divergent clinical presentations.

Zhang et al. (2022) infected ACE2-transduced SV40 fibroblasts with SARS-CoV-2. The fibroblasts came from a TYK2-deficient patient homozygous for P216fs*14, the same genotype as their patients P2 through P4. These cells did not control viral replication normally. Pretreatment with IFN-alpha-2b blocked infection in cells from healthy donors but not in the patient cells. The patient cells also failed to induce ISGs (IFIT1, MX1, and IFI27) after infection or after IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment. The authors suggested that the deficiency was causal for COVID-19 pneumonia.

### Diagnosis

Kilic et al. (2012) concluded that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in patients with BCG disease, especially those with unusually severe herpes virus infections, even when atopy, high serum IgE, and staphylococcal disease are absent. They suggested that this may also apply to patients with other mycobacterial diseases or with infections by other intramacrophage pathogens, such as Salmonella and Brucella.

Kreins et al. (2015) proposed that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in children with severe tuberculosis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) established a simple monocyte-based diagnostic assay for TYK2 deficiency. Using it, they showed that their patient's mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response.

Wu et al. (2020) suggested that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in patients with repeated intracellular bacterial infections (including tuberculosis), repeated viral infections, or eczema, especially when there is a family history.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) found that the 5 novel TYK2 mutations in their patients were private. The mutations were absent from their in-house whole-exome sequencing database and from public databases (NCBI, Ensembl, 1000 Genomes, and ExAC). No homozygotes for TYK2 stop mutations were present in any database, and only 84 heterozygous individuals were found.

**.0002 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 4-BP DEL, CYS70HISFSTER21 [p.C70Hfs; p.C70fs]

They reported that he carries a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 of the TYK2 gene (C70HfsX21), which results in a premature stop codon. The patient had atopic dermatitis, BCGitis, recurrent S. aureus skin abscesses, Salmonella sepsis, viral infections, and IgE of 1,430 to 2,100 IU/ml.

**.0003 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 9-BP DEL, NT2303 [c.2303_2311del; p.L767*]

In a Turkish man (P2) with IMD35, born to first-cousin parents, Kreins et al. (2015) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2303_2311del) by linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing. The deletion results in a premature stop codon at position 767 (L767X). The patient had disseminated BCG lymphadenitis, Brucella meningitis, and shingles. TYK2 protein was undetectable in his cells.

**.0004 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 1-BP INS, 3318C [c.3318_3319insc; p.T1106Hfs; p.T1106fs]

(2015) identified by whole-exome sequencing a homozygous 1-bp insertion in exon 23 of the TYK2 gene (c.3318_3319insC). The insertion causes a frameshift that generates a premature stop codon at position 1110 (T1106HfsX4). The girl had disseminated tuberculosis, and her brother had meningitis, recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema, and high eosinophil levels.

**.0005 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**

**.0006 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 1-BP DEL, 149C [c.149delc; p.S50Hfs; p.S50fs]

**.0007 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**
