### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.2.

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), or TYK2 deficiency, is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency. Its core feature is susceptibility to mycobacterial infections (including BCG disease and tuberculosis), viral infections, or both. Infections with other intracellular bacteria, such as Salmonella and Brucella, also occur. TYK2 deficiency was first described in a patient with features of hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES), including atopic dermatitis and elevated serum IgE. Most later-reported patients have not had HIES features, and serum IgE elevation varies (Kreins et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2020; Lv et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) described a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient had unusual susceptibility to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, born in 1992, who was the third child of healthy first-cousin parents. He was vaccinated with BCG at 2 months of age. At 8 months, left axillary lymphadenopathy was detected and isoniazid treatment was started. His course included disseminated BCG infection with generalized fistulous lymphadenopathy, fever, fatigue, and a negative PPD skin test. He later had pneumonia and herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve. He also had neurobrucellosis, with a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, severe sensorineural hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, aggressive behavior, and lacunar infarcts in the temporal, occipital, and parietal lobes. Serum IgG, IgM, and IgA were normal, and serum IgE was mildly elevated. His antibody response to tetanus toxoid was low. His medical history included no atopy, asthma, or skin problems such as candidiasis, boils, folliculitis, or cold abscesses. He had no staphylococcal skin or lung infections and no skeletal or dental features of HIES. Kilic et al. (2012) noted that, unlike the Japanese TYK2-deficient patient, their patient lacked the cardinal features of HIES.

Kreins et al. (2015) restudied the Japanese patient (P1) originally reported by Minegishi et al. (2006). P1 was a man born to consanguineous parents. He had atopic dermatitis from 1 month of age and recurrent bacterial and viral infections from 1 year of age. His infections included BCGitis, nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis with sepsis, parainfluenza virus 3 pneumonia, recurrent oral herpes simplex virus infections, molluscum contagiosum, mild oral candidiasis, pneumonia without pneumatocele, otitis, and sinusitis. He also had recurrent staphylococcal skin abscesses and ocular sarcoidosis. Repeated immunologic studies showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE concentrations (1,430-2,100 IU/ml).

Kreins et al. (2015) also reported 7 patients from 5 families in Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. These patients had mycobacterial and/or viral infections but no features of HIES. None had documented pyogenic or staphylococcal infections, fungal infections, or high serum IgE. P2 was the Turkish man born to first-cousin parents previously reported by Kilic et al. (2012). He had disseminated BCG lymphadenitis at 21 months of age, severe bacterial meningitis with CSF cultures positive for Brucella at 8 years, and shingles at 11 years. P3 was a Moroccan girl born to consanguineous parents. She was hospitalized at 13 years with suspected abdominal tuberculosis and developed ascites and a psoas abscess despite antimycobacterial treatment. She died of disseminated tuberculosis at 15 years. Her younger brother (P4) was hospitalized at 8 months for meningitis of unknown etiology. He had recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels; he was 15 years old and untreated at the time of the report. P5 was an Iranian boy born to consanguineous parents and vaccinated with BCG at birth. He developed right axillary lymphadenitis at 2 months and multiple cervical lymphadenitis at 4 months. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with fistulization and pulmonary involvement. His 2-year-old sister (P6) developed BCG lymphadenopathy lasting 1 month after vaccination at birth. At 1 year she was hospitalized for 1 week for an unknown viral infection of the skin. P7 was a 9-year-old Iranian girl born to consanguineous parents. She had pneumonia at 8 months. At 9 years she presented with fever and productive cough, and imaging showed diffuse bilateral micronodular lung infiltrations, hilar and mediastinal adenopathies, and cavitary consolidation in the right upper lobe, consistent with miliary tuberculosis. P8 was an Argentinian boy. He had herpes gingivostomatitis at 18 months of age, then aseptic meningitis and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions at 24 months, as well as exanthema subitum. He also had mild mental disability; it was not clearly established whether this was congenital or followed the meningitis. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infections.

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient who had eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels greater than 1,000 U/mL, without viral or mycobacterial infections.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy, aged 1 year and 11 months, who was the second child of young, nonconsanguineous parents. He was vaccinated with BCG on the third day after birth. He developed BCG disease with abscess and ulceration at the injection site and BCG-associated axillary lymph node tuberculosis. He also had recurrent respiratory tract infections, bacterial pneumonia, diarrhea, Salmonella infection, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, and was positive for influenza A and B IgM antibodies. Other features were delayed speech and a small head circumference. Serum IgG, IgA, IgE, and IgM were normal. The percentage of CD19+ B cells was increased and NK cells were decreased, without lymphopenia. He had no atopy, staphylococcal infection, or fungal infection. Wu et al. (2020) also reviewed 12 reported TYK2-deficient patients, including their own. Seven (58%) had intracellular bacterial infections, 7 (58%) had recurrent viral infections, 2 (17%) had rash or atopic dermatitis/eczema, and 3 (25%) had markedly increased serum IgE. None of the 12 had the facial features, abnormal bone development, pathologic fractures, delayed loss of deciduous teeth, or aneurysms seen in STAT3-related HIES.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 TYK2-deficient patients who had recurrent infections with distinct pathogen profiles, with or without elevated IgE levels and atopy. They noted that TYK2 deficiency was first proposed as a subset of HIES, but that TYK2-deficient patients do not necessarily have HIES features. ⚠︎

Zhang et al. (2022) reported 3 unrelated children of consanguineous Turkish parents with TYK2 deficiency and COVID-19 pneumonia. P2, a 2-year-old girl, was hospitalized for moderate COVID-19 pneumonia but did not need oxygen therapy. Her earlier history included neonatal sepsis, 2 ICU admissions for fever and dyspnea before 5 months of age, Kawasaki disease at 11 months, and hospitalization at 2 years for infection with the VZV vaccine. P3, a 4-year-old boy, and P4, a 9-year-old boy, were both admitted to the ICU for critical COVID-19 pneumonia requiring high-flow oxygen therapy. P3 had been hospitalized for neonatal sepsis and admitted to the ICU for influenza pneumonia at 3 years. P4 had recurrent bronchitis requiring hospital admission and inhaler therapy. Neither P3 nor P4 had an adverse reaction to MMR vaccination. P3's 3-year-old sister had a history of critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and ventilation and of disseminated varicella. She had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and remained seronegative. Zhang et al. (2022) noted that penetrance for COVID-19 pneumonia was complete in the TYK2-deficient families they reported. They found the occurrence of critical pneumonia in 3 unrelated patients surprising, because TYK2 deficiency impairs but does not abolish cellular responses to type I IFNs. They stated that more infected TYK2-deficient patients were needed to estimate penetrance.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 is autosomal recessive. Kreins et al. (2015) found that in all the kindreds they studied, clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. Most reported patients were born to consanguineous parents (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2022). In the nonconsanguineous family reported by Wu et al. (2020), both parents of the homozygous patient were heterozygous carriers.

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells had defects in several cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I interferon, IL6, IL10, IL12, and IL23. Transducing the intact TYK2 gene restored the cytokine signals.

In a Turkish boy with TYK2 deficiency born to first-cousin parents, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del) that created a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein was detectable by Western blotting in the patient's EBV-B cells.

In 8 patients with TYK2 deficiency from 6 kindreds, Kreins et al. (2015) identified homozygous mutations in the TYK2 gene. These included the previously reported Japanese patient (P1), who carried a 4-bp deletion in exon 4 (C70HfsX21), and the previously reported Turkish patient (P2), who carried a 9-bp deletion in exon 16 (c.2303_2311del; L767X). The other mutations were a 1-bp insertion (c.3318_3319insC) in a Moroccan sib pair, a nonsense mutation (c.462G-T; E154X) in an Iranian sib pair, a 1-bp deletion (c.149delC) in an Iranian girl, and a nonsense mutation (c.1912C-T; R638X) in an Argentinian boy. In all kindreds, clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. TYK2 protein was undetectable in cells from all patients with antibodies against N- and C-terminal epitopes, indicating complete TYK2 deficiency with no residual wildtype protein. The 5 novel mutations were absent from public databases.

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient using a simple diagnostic monocyte assay. They showed that the patient's TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response. The mutation also partly reduced IL-10 signaling, which was associated with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not reduce IL-6 signaling.

In a Chinese boy with TYK2 deficiency born to nonconsanguineous parents, Wu et al. (2020) identified a homozygous missense mutation in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene (c.2395G-A; G799R). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The patient also carried heterozygous variants in the CFH, LRRC8A, and NIPBL genes, which the authors regarded as of uncertain significance. TYK2 mRNA expression was reduced in the patient's cells, and no TYK2 protein was detected by Western blot. When the G799R mutation was introduced into FLAG-tagged and GFP-tagged TYK2 constructs and expressed in HEK293T cells, both mRNA and protein expression were reduced compared with wildtype.

In 5 patients with TYK2 deficiency, Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations by high-throughput sequencing and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. They confirmed the pathogenic effects of the mutations with qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays.

In a cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated children born to consanguineous Turkish parents who were homozygous for the same TYK2 frameshift mutation (P216Rfs*14), previously shown to be loss-of-expression. Family segregation analysis confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance in all 3 families. The 3-year-old sister of one patient carried the same genotype and had a history of critical influenza pneumonia and disseminated varicella. ACE2-transduced SV40 fibroblasts from a homozygous patient failed to control SARS-CoV-2 replication, were not protected by IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment, and failed to induce ISGs in response to infection or to IFN-alpha-2b. The authors implicated autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency as a genetic etiology of COVID-19 pneumonia.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kilic et al. (2012) contrasted their Turkish TYK2-deficient patient with the Japanese patient reported by Minegishi et al. (2006). Their patient had no atopic dermatitis, staphylococcal infections, or high serum IgE. They found the discrepancy difficult to explain and suggested it might reflect a difference in the immunologic impact of TYK2 in the 2 patients.

Kreins et al. (2015) found normal IL-6 responses in cells from P2 through P8, unlike cells from the original patient with HIES (P1). Expressing wildtype TYK2 in P1 cells did not rescue the IL-6 hyporesponsiveness, and silencing TYK2 in control cells did not impair IL-6 signaling. The authors concluded that IL-6 hyporesponsiveness and HIES did not appear to be intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency. They suggested these features in P1 might reflect a variant in another, unknown component of the pathway.

Lv et al. (2022) found that cells from their TYK2-deficient patients responded heterogeneously to various cytokines. They proposed that different combinations of defects in the cytokine signaling pathways orchestrated by TYK2 account for the variable clinical manifestations.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that TYK2 deficiency likely accounted for their patient's complex clinical picture, including impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation. They also concluded that TYK2 has obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals involved in innate and acquired immunity in humans.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that the absence of TYK2 caused defective IL-12 signaling, which impaired IFN-gamma production and led to susceptibility to BCG and Brucella. They suggested that the viral illnesses were probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that cells from all 8 of their TYK2-deficient patients had impaired but not abolished responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta, and IL-10. Responses to IFN-gamma, IL-27, IL-21, and LIF were normal. They proposed that residual IL-12-dependent IFN-gamma production explained the mycobacterial disease and its favorable response to antibiotics. Surface expression of IFN-alpha-R1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1 was reduced on TYK2-deficient EBV-B cells, and expression of wildtype TYK2 rescued this defect, consistent with a scaffolding role for TYK2. The proportions of IL-17-positive T cells ex vivo were normal despite impaired IL-23 responses, which probably explained the patients' lack of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis. Residual IL-10 responses probably explained the lack of early-onset colitis. Numbers of central and effector memory T cells were normal, and the authors suggested that reactivation of chronic viral infections resulted mainly from impaired IFN-alpha/beta responses rather than a memory T-cell defect.

In a TYK2-deficient human cell line, Fuchs et al. (2016) found almost normal type III IFN signaling, with minimal impairment of virus control. They suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may contribute to antiviral protection in TYK2 deficiency, leading to a surprisingly mild human phenotype.

Lv et al. (2022) found that PBMCs from TYK2-deficient patients responded heterogeneously to IFN-alpha/beta/gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23, and that lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted.

### Diagnosis

Kilic et al. (2012) recommended considering TYK2 deficiency in patients with BCG disease, particularly when herpesvirus infections are unusually severe, even without the cardinal features of HIES. They suggested that this may also apply to other mycobacterial diseases and to infections with other intramacrophage pathogens such as Salmonella and Brucella.

Kreins et al. (2015) suggested that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in children with severe tuberculosis.

Wu et al. (2020) summarized the main features of TYK2 deficiency as autosomal recessive inheritance, a normal lymphocyte count with defects in signaling by multiple cytokines, susceptibility to viruses or intracellular bacteria, and variable elevation of IgE. They suggested considering TYK2 deficiency in patients with repeated intracellular bacterial infections (including tuberculosis), repeated viral infections, or eczema.

### Clinical Management

Zhang et al. (2022) suggested that inborn errors of type I IFN immunity be considered in children hospitalized for COVID-19. They also suggested considering early personalized treatment, with type I IFN for defects upstream of the type I IFN receptors and with monoclonal antibodies for defects downstream of them.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) found no individuals homozygous for TYK2 stop mutations in any public database, and only 84 heterozygous individuals.

Wu et al. (2020) noted that the TYK2 G799R variant was present in the gnomAD database, which suggested that it was not a private mutation.

Zhang et al. (2022) found recessive complete inborn errors of type I IFN immunity, involving IFNAR1, STAT2, TYK2, or TLR7, in 12 of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. They found none in 1,224 individuals with benign SARS-CoV-2 infection (P = 1.2 x 10(-11)). From pLOF variants in gnomAD, they estimated the cumulative frequency of recessive deficiency of IFNAR1, STAT2, TLR7, and TYK2 at 4 x 10(-5).

### Animal Model

Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that the roles of TYK2 in cytokine signaling in humans differ substantially from its function in mice. Fuchs et al. (2016) noted that NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, were normal in their TYK2-deficient patient, in contrast to observations in TYK2-deficient mice.
