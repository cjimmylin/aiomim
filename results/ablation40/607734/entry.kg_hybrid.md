### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the NEFL gene (162280) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F is a hereditary peripheral neuropathy caused by mutation in the gene that encodes the neurofilament light chain polypeptide. Jordanova et al. (2003) found that patients with NEFL mutations had early onset, often a severe phenotype, and moderately to severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. Abe et al. (2009) found that missense mutations caused a dominant phenotype and that nonsense mutations probably caused a recessive phenotype. Yum et al. (2009) reported a severe, early-onset neuropathy in sibs with a homozygous nonsense mutation. Conduction velocities in these sibs were in the demyelinating range, but nerve pathology showed axonal loss and small myelinated axons that lacked intermediate filaments. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) noted that their patients with a homozygous NEFL mutation approximated the clinical description of Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy. However, their nerve conduction velocities were faster (about 20 m/s) and their nerve biopsy findings were distinct. The authors considered 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) a more fitting name, and stated that under OMIM's labeling of recessive axonal neuropathies their patients should be called CMT2B5. They emphasized that homozygous NEFL mutations should also be considered when evaluating children with Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy. Yum et al. (2009) also noted that the conduction velocities would classify the neuropathy as 'demyelinating' under the conventional 38 m/s criterion.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) studied patients with CMT and NEFL mutations and found that they had an early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. Electrophysiologic examination showed moderately to severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. The authors reported the first nerve biopsy from a CMT patient with a de novo missense mutation in NEFL. It showed axonal pathology with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) reported 6 unrelated Japanese CMT patients with NEFL mutations. All of them had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities, which the authors suggested might be caused by a loss of large-diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) reported a 2-generation Palestinian family in which 4 of 5 children of consanguineous parents (first cousins) had a severe, progressive neuropathy, with onset at about 1.5 years of age. The index patient presented at age 2 years with toe walking. All 4 affected sibs had: - hypotonia during infancy and early childhood - mildly delayed motor milestones - slowly progressive atrophy and weakness of the distal muscles of the feet and hands - moderate sensory impairment in a glove-and-stocking pattern - absent ankle jerks The text described bilateral pes cavus in all 4 sibs, but the table recorded pes cavus in only 3. At the time of the report, 1 patient used a walker and had moderate proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait. The others walked with bilateral molded ankle-foot orthoses. CMT neuropathy scores ranged from 16 to 27. None of the sibs had pathologic reflexes, palpably enlarged nerves, ulcerated feet, cranial neuropathies, or clinically evident hearing loss. One patient had learning difficulties, and brain MRI was normal in the 3 patients who were scanned. The parents and the unaffected sib had normal neurologic examinations.

In the 4 affected sibs, Yum et al. (2009) found that sural, ulnar, median, and radial sensory responses were absent, with 2 exceptions: 1 ulnar response and 1 radial response were present but had low amplitudes and slowed conduction. Median and ulnar motor conduction velocities were severely slowed, ranging from 12 to 25 m/s, and amplitudes fell progressively with age. Peroneal and tibial responses were similarly slowed, declined with age, and eventually became unobtainable in every affected child. EMG showed severe denervation in distal muscles. Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, which the authors took to indicate subclinical involvement of myelinated axons in the central nervous system.

Yum et al. (2009) examined a sural nerve biopsy taken from 1 affected sib at age 16 years. The density of myelinated axons was 1,300/mm, compared with about 6,000/mm in normal controls. All myelinated axons were less than 3 microns in diameter, and many were irregular in shape. There were occasional clusters of regenerated axons but no actively degenerating axons. Myelinated axons contained microtubules but no intermediate filaments. Axons were labeled by an antiserum against the N terminus of NFL but not by one against the C terminus. They were also immunoreactive for NFM, NFH, peripherin, and alpha-internexin. The authors concluded that patients with this mutation have indolent but progressive axonal loss.

### Inheritance

Both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance have been reported for CMT1F. Jordanova et al. (2003) suggested that NEFL mutations should be considered in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that missense NEFL mutations cause a dominant phenotype and that nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype. Yum et al. (2009) reported autosomal recessive inheritance in a consanguineous Palestinian family. In that family, affected sibs were homozygous for a nonsense mutation and both parents were asymptomatic heterozygous carriers. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for mutations in the NEFL gene. They detected 6 disease-associated missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. One patient had a de novo missense mutation.

Abe et al. (2009) screened the NEFL gene in 223 Japanese CMT patients. Patients with the demyelinating form had tested negative for mutations in PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX. Patients with the axonal form had tested negative for mutations in MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS. The authors found 4 heterozygous missense mutations in 5 unrelated patients: P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K. In 1 other patient they found a homozygous nonsense mutation, glu140-to-ter (E140X), which they described as the first homozygous nonsense mutation reported in NEFL.

Yum et al. (2009) sequenced NEFL in a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe, early-onset neuropathy. All 4 affected sibs were homozygous for a c.628G-T transversion, predicted to result in a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation, and the unaffected sib did not carry it. In 2 of the affected sibs, the authors found no mutations in PMP22, GJB1, MPZ, EGR2, PRX, LMNA, GDAP1, or MFN2, and no duplication or deletion of PMP22. The mutation had not been found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at Athena Diagnostics. In transfected cells, the mutant protein did not form filaments and did not interfere with filament formation by wildtype NFL. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jordanova et al. (2003) concluded that their findings provided further evidence that the clinical variation in CMT depends on the gene mutated and on the specific type of mutation. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that nonsense NEFL mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype, in contrast to missense mutations, which cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) expressed the E210X mutant in SW-13 vim- cells, which lack intermediate filament proteins. Unlike wildtype NFL, the mutant did not form filaments; it was found in a diffuse pattern, largely in the nucleus. On immunoblot, it appeared as a truncated band of about 30 kD that accumulated poorly. When coexpressed with wildtype NFL, E210X did not interfere with filament formation, and in SW-13 vim+ cells it did not disrupt the vimentin network. In contrast, the dominant Q333P mutant formed aggregates. The authors concluded that E210X causes a simple loss of function. NEFL is not prominently expressed by myelinating Schwann cells, so the authors concluded that the absence of large myelinated axons was the likely explanation for the slowed conduction velocities. They suggested that neurofilaments are a main determinant of axonal caliber and conduction velocity and are required to maintain myelinated peripheral nervous system axons.
