### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the NEFL gene (162280) on chromosome 8p21.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is a peripheral neuropathy caused by mutation in the NEFL gene. Jordanova et al. (2003) found that patients with NEFL mutations had early onset, often with a severe clinical phenotype, and moderately to severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. Both dominant and recessive forms have been reported. Abe et al. (2009) suggested that heterozygous missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype and nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive one. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) noted that their patients with a homozygous NEFL mutation resembled patients with Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy, but had faster nerve conduction (about 20 m/s) and different biopsy findings. They proposed the term 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) and stated that under the OMIM naming scheme their patients should be called CMT2B5. They emphasized that homozygous NEFL mutations should be considered when evaluating children with Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) found that patients with NEFL mutations had an early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. Electrophysiologic studies showed moderately to severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. They reported the first nerve biopsy from a CMT patient with a de novo missense mutation in NEFL, which showed axonal pathology with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) reported that all 6 Japanese CMT patients with NEFL mutations had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities, and suggested that this was possibly caused by a loss of large-diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) described a 2-generation Palestinian family with first-cousin parents in which 4 of 5 children had a severe, progressive neuropathy starting at about 1.5 years of age. All 4 had hypotonia in infancy and early childhood, mildly delayed motor milestones, slowly progressive distal atrophy and weakness of the feet and hands, and bilateral pes cavus. All had moderate glove-and-stocking sensory impairment and absent ankle jerks, with CMT neuropathy scores of 16 to 27. One patient used a walker; the others walked with molded ankle-foot orthoses. None had pathologic reflexes, enlarged nerves, foot ulcers, cranial neuropathies, or clinically evident hearing loss. One patient had learning difficulties, and brain MRI was normal in the 3 patients scanned. On nerve conduction studies, sensory responses were absent in nearly all nerves tested. Median and ulnar motor conduction velocities were severely slowed (12 to 25 m/s) from early childhood, and amplitudes fell progressively with age. Peroneal and tibial responses eventually became unobtainable, and EMG showed severe distal denervation. Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, which the authors took to indicate subclinical involvement of central nervous system axons. ⚠︎

Yum et al. (2009) examined a sural nerve biopsy from 1 affected member of the Palestinian family at age 16 years. Myelinated axon density was reduced to 1,300/mm, compared with about 6,000/mm in controls. All myelinated axons were less than 3 microns in diameter, many were irregular in shape, and they lacked intermediate filaments while still containing microtubules. There were occasional clusters of regenerated axons but no actively degenerating axons. Axons stained with an N-terminal NFL antiserum but not with a C-terminal one, and they were immunoreactive for NFM, NFH, peripherin, and alpha-internexin. The authors concluded that the patients have indolent but progressive axonal loss.

### Inheritance

Jordanova et al. (2003) suggested that NEFL mutations should be considered in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT, and Abe et al. (2009) found heterozygous missense NEFL mutations in 5 unrelated Japanese patients, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Autosomal recessive inheritance was supported by Abe et al. (2009), who found a homozygous nonsense mutation in 1 patient, and by Yum et al. (2009), who found a homozygous nonsense mutation segregating in a consanguineous Palestinian family in which both parents were heterozygous.

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for mutations in the NEFL gene. They found 6 disease-associated missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. One patient had a de novo missense mutation. The authors suggested that NEFL mutations should be considered in the molecular evaluation of patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT.

Abe et al. (2009) screened the NEFL gene in 223 Japanese CMT patients. The patients with the demyelinating form had tested negative for PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX mutations, and those with the axonal form had tested negative for MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS mutations. They found 4 heterozygous missense mutations in 5 unrelated patients (P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K) and a homozygous nonsense mutation (E140X) in 1 other patient. The authors stated that this was the first report of a homozygous nonsense mutation of NEFL.

In the 4 affected children of a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe early-onset neuropathy, Yum et al. (2009) sequenced NEFL and found a homozygous c.628G-T transversion predicted to cause a nonsense change at codon 210 (E210X). Both parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sib did not carry the mutation. No mutations were found in PMP22, GJB1, MPZ, EGR2, PRX, LMNA, GDAP1, or MFN2, and there was no PMP22 duplication or deletion. The mutation was not found in more than 11,000 individuals tested at Athena Diagnostics. In SW-13 vim- cells, the mutant did not form filaments and did not interfere with filament formation by wildtype NFL, consistent with loss of function. The authors described this as the first recessive NEFL mutation. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jordanova et al. (2003) concluded that their findings provide further evidence that the clinical variation observed in CMT depends on the gene mutated and the specific type of mutation.

Abe et al. (2009) concluded that nonsense mutations in NEFL probably cause a recessive phenotype, whereas missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) expressed the E210X NEFL mutant in SW-13 vim- cells. Unlike wildtype NFL, the mutant did not form filaments and was found in a diffuse, mostly nuclear pattern. It produced a truncated band of about 30 kD that accumulated poorly. When coexpressed, it did not interfere with filament formation by wildtype NFL or with the vimentin network, whereas the dominant Q333P mutant caused aggregates. The authors concluded that E210X causes a simple loss of function.

Yum et al. (2009) noted that the conduction velocities in their patients fell within the 'demyelinating' range. Because NEFL is not prominently expressed by myelinating Schwann cells, they concluded that the absence of large myelinated axons, which lack neurofilaments, was the likely explanation for the slowed conduction. They suggested that neurofilaments determine axonal caliber and conduction velocity and are needed to maintain myelinated peripheral nerve axons.
