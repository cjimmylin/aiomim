### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the NEFL gene (162280) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F caused by NEFL mutations is a peripheral neuropathy. It typically begins early and can be severe. Nerve conduction velocities are slowed moderately to severely (Jordanova et al., 2003). The disorder can be inherited as a dominant trait through heterozygous missense mutations or as a recessive trait through homozygous nonsense mutations (Abe et al., 2009; Yum et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) proposed the term 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) for the disorder in their sibs with a homozygous NEFL mutation. They noted that under the OMIM naming system for recessive axonal neuropathies, their patients would be designated CMT2B5. They also stressed that homozygous NEFL mutations should be considered when evaluating children with Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) studied patients with CMT who carried NEFL mutations. The patients had an early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. On electrophysiologic examination, nerve conduction velocities were moderately to severely slowed. The authors also reported the first nerve biopsy from a CMT patient with a de novo NEFL missense mutation. It showed axonal pathology, with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) studied Japanese CMT patients with NEFL mutations. All of them had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities, which the authors suggested might be caused by a loss of large diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) reported a Palestinian family whose parents were first cousins. Four of their 5 children had a severe, progressive neuropathy, with onset at about 1.5 years of age. The index patient presented at age 2 years with toe walking. All 4 affected children had hypotonia in infancy and early childhood and mildly delayed motor milestones. They also had slowly progressive atrophy and weakness of the distal muscles of the feet and hands, bilateral pes cavus, moderate glove-and-stocking sensory impairment, and absent ankle jerks. One sib used a walker and had moderate proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait. Another sib had mild to moderate weakness of the proximal arm and shoulder girdle. The others walked with molded ankle-foot orthoses. CMT neuropathy scores ranged from 16 to 27. None of the children had pathologic reflexes, palpably enlarged nerves, foot ulcers, cranial neuropathies, or clinically evident hearing loss. One sib had learning difficulties. Brain MRI was normal in the 3 sibs who had it.

Sensory responses were absent in nearly all of these children (Yum et al., 2009). Median and ulnar motor conduction velocities were severely slowed, ranging from 12 to 25 m/s, and amplitudes became progressively smaller with age. Peroneal and tibial responses eventually could not be obtained in any affected child. EMG showed severe denervation in distal muscles. Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, which the authors took as evidence of subclinical involvement of central nervous system axons. A sural nerve biopsy from 1 patient at age 16 years showed a reduced density of myelinated axons (1,300/mm, compared with about 6,000/mm in controls). All myelinated axons were less than 3 microns in diameter, and they contained microtubules but no intermediate filaments. There were occasional clusters of regenerated axons, but no evidence of active axonal degeneration.

### Inheritance

Both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance have been reported. Jordanova et al. (2003) found NEFL mutations in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT, including 1 de novo missense mutation. Abe et al. (2009) found heterozygous missense mutations in some patients and a homozygous nonsense mutation in another. They concluded that nonsense NEFL mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype. In the family reported by Yum et al. (2009), the neuropathy segregated as an autosomal recessive trait. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for NEFL mutations. They found 6 disease-associated missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, which clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. One of the missense mutations had arisen de novo.

Abe et al. (2009) screened NEFL in 223 Japanese CMT patients. Those with the demyelinating form had tested negative for mutations in PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX. Those with the axonal form had tested negative for mutations in MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS. The authors found 4 heterozygous missense mutations (P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K) in 5 unrelated patients, and a homozygous nonsense mutation (E140X) in 1 other patient.

Yum et al. (2009) studied the 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family. All 4 were homozygous for a c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, predicted to result in a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sib did not carry the mutation. Sequencing of PMP22, GJB1, MPZ, EGR2, PRX, LMNA, GDAP1, and MFN2 in 2 of the affected sibs found no additional mutations. In the patient's sural nerve, an antiserum against the N terminus of NFL labeled axons, whereas an antiserum against the C terminus did not. In SW-13 vim- cells, the E210X mutant did not form filaments. It also did not interfere with filament formation by wildtype NFL or with the vimentin network, unlike the dominant Q333P mutant. These findings suggested a simple loss of function.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jordanova et al. (2003) concluded that clinical variation in CMT depends on which gene is mutated and on the specific type of mutation. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that nonsense NEFL mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype, whereas missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) noted that, by the conventional 38 m/s criterion, the conduction velocities in their patients fell in the 'demyelinating' range, even though NEFL is not prominently expressed by myelinating Schwann cells. On the basis of the nerve biopsy findings, they concluded that the slowed conduction was most likely explained by the absence of large myelinated axons. These axons lacked intermediate filaments and probably failed to reach their normal calibers.

### Diagnosis

Jordanova et al. (2003) suggested that NEFL mutations should be considered in the molecular evaluation of patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT.

### Population Genetics

Yum et al. (2009) stated that the E210X mutation had not been found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at a diagnostic laboratory. They noted that its prevalence in the Palestinian population was unknown.

Yum et al. (2009) studied 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe, early-onset neuropathy and severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. All 4 were homozygous for a c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, predicted to cause a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution.
