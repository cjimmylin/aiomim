### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the NEFL gene (162280) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is a peripheral neuropathy. It begins early and is often severe, and nerve conduction velocities are moderately to severely slowed (Jordanova et al., 2003). Both dominant and recessive forms have been reported (Abe et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) proposed the term 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) for the disorder in their patients, who were homozygous for the NEFL E210X mutation. They suggested that in OMIM nomenclature these patients should be designated CMT2B5.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) found that patients with NEFL mutations, identified by screening patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies, had an early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. On electrophysiologic examination, nerve conduction velocities were moderately to severely slowed. Jordanova et al. (2003) also reported the first nerve biopsy from a CMT patient with a de novo NEFL missense mutation. The biopsy showed axonal pathology with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) reported 5 unrelated Japanese CMT patients with heterozygous NEFL missense mutations and 1 Japanese patient with a homozygous NEFL nonsense mutation. All the patients had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities. The authors suggested that this slowing was possibly caused by a loss of large-diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) reported 4 affected children (2 females and 2 males, aged 23, 17, 16, and 13 years) among 5 children of consanguineous Palestinian parents. Symptoms began at about 1.5 years of age. The index patient (II-4) presented at age 2 years with toe walking. The other sibs first showed gait difficulties (II-1 and II-3) or delayed walking (II-5). All 4 affected sibs had infantile hypotonia, distal muscle atrophy and weakness, glove-and-stocking sensory impairment, and absent ankle jerks. Motor milestones were mildly delayed in II-1 and II-3. Pes cavus was present in 3 sibs but not in II-3. Patient II-1 also had moderate proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait, and patient II-4 had mild to moderate proximal arm and shoulder girdle weakness. Patient II-1 had learning difficulties, but the other affected children did not, and 3 of them had normal brain MRIs. ⚠︎

On electrophysiologic study, the patients of Yum et al. (2009) had motor nerve conduction velocities of 12 to 25 m/s, within the demyelinating range, and amplitudes became progressively lower with age. In patient II-5, motor conduction velocities were already severely slowed at 4 months. Sensory responses were absent with 2 exceptions: an ulnar response in patient II-3 at age 4 years and a radial response in patient II-5 at age 10. These responses had low amplitudes (4.5 and 17.0 microV, respectively) and slowed conduction velocities (14 and 9 m/s, respectively). Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of 4 patients, which the authors interpreted as subclinical axonal involvement of the CNS. A sural nerve biopsy from patient II-4 showed fewer myelinated axons than normal, with a density of 1300/mm compared to about 6000/mm in normal controls. All myelinated axons were less than 3 microns in diameter and lacked intermediate filaments.

### Inheritance

Both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance have been reported for CMT1F. Jordanova et al. (2003) found a de novo heterozygous NEFL missense mutation in a CMT patient. Abe et al. (2009) found heterozygous missense mutations in 5 unrelated patients and a homozygous nonsense mutation in 1 patient. They concluded that nonsense NEFL mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype, whereas missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype. Yum et al. (2009) reported autosomal recessive inheritance in a consanguineous Palestinian family: the 4 affected sibs were homozygous for a nonsense mutation, and both parents were heterozygous. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for mutations in the NEFL gene. They detected 6 disease-associated missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. One patient carried a de novo missense mutation.

Abe et al. (2009) screened the NEFL gene in 223 Japanese CMT patients who were negative for mutations in other known CMT genes. They identified 4 heterozygous missense mutations (P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K) in 5 unrelated patients. In 1 other patient, they found a homozygous nonsense mutation, glu140-to-ter (E140X). The authors stated that this was the first report of a homozygous NEFL nonsense mutation.

In 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe early-onset neuropathy, Yum et al. (2009) identified a homozygous c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, resulting in a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation, and the unaffected sib did not carry it. The mutation was not found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at a diagnostic laboratory. Yum et al. (2009) expressed the mutant in SW-13 vim- cells. Unlike the dominant Q333P mutant, E210X did not form filaments, accumulated poorly, and did not disrupt filament assembly by wildtype NFL or the vimentin network. The authors concluded that E210X causes a simple loss of function. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jordanova et al. (2003) concluded that the clinical variation in CMT depends on which gene is mutated and on the specific type of mutation. They suggested that NEFL mutations need to be considered in the molecular evaluation of patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT.

Abe et al. (2009) concluded that nonsense NEFL mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype, in contrast to missense mutations, which cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) studied a sural nerve biopsy from a patient homozygous for the NEFL E210X mutation. Myelinated axons were reduced in number, all were less than 3 microns in diameter, and they lacked intermediate filaments. The authors concluded that the slowed conduction velocities were likely explained by the absence of large myelinated axons rather than by demyelination.
