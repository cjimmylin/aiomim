### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the NEFL gene (162280) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is a peripheral neuropathy caused by mutation in the neurofilament light chain gene (NEFL). Onset is early and the clinical course is often severe. Nerve conduction velocities are slowed, often in the demyelinating range. Both dominantly inherited forms, caused by heterozygous missense mutations, and recessively inherited forms, caused by homozygous nonsense mutations, have been reported.

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) proposed the term 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) for the disorder in their patients, who were homozygous for an NEFL nonsense mutation. They stated that under the OMIM designation for recessive axonal neuropathies, their patients should be called CMT2B5. They also emphasized that homozygous NEFL mutations should be considered when evaluating children with Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies and found NEFL mutations in some of them. Patients with NEFL mutations had an early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. On electrophysiologic examination, nerve conduction velocities were moderately to severely slowed. Jordanova et al. (2003) also reported the first nerve biopsy from a CMT patient with a de novo NEFL missense mutation. It showed axonal pathology with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) studied 6 Japanese patients with NEFL mutations. Five unrelated patients carried heterozygous missense mutations and 1 patient carried a homozygous nonsense mutation. All patients had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities, which the authors suggested was possibly caused by a loss of large diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) reported 4 affected children among the 5 children of first-cousin Palestinian parents. The affected children were 2 females and 2 males, aged 23, 17, 16, and 13 years at the time of the report. All 4 had hypotonia in infancy and early childhood, mildly delayed motor milestones, and slowly progressive atrophy and weakness of the distal muscles of the feet and hands. Bilateral pes cavus was described in all 4. The index patient (II-4) presented at age 2 years with toe walking. In 2 of the sibs the initial neuropathy symptom was a gait abnormality, and in the youngest it was delayed walking. All affected children had glove-and-stocking sensory impairment and absent ankle jerks. Patient II-1 also had moderate proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait, and her ambulation was walker-dependent. Patient II-4 had mild to moderate proximal arm and shoulder girdle weakness. Patient II-1 had learning difficulties, but the other affected children did not. Brain MRI was normal in 3 of the affected children (II-1, II-3, and II-4) and was not done in patient II-5. ⚠︎

In the sibs reported by Yum et al. (2009), motor nerve conduction velocities were severely slowed (12 to 25 m/s), even in early childhood; in patient II-5 slowing was documented from age 4 months. Motor amplitudes became progressively smaller with age. The sural, ulnar, median, and radial sensory responses were all absent, with 2 exceptions: an ulnar response in patient II-3 at age 4 years and a radial response in patient II-5 at age 10, both of low amplitude (4.5 and 17.0 microV, respectively). Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, which the authors took to indicate subclinical CNS axonal involvement. A sural nerve biopsy from patient II-4 showed a reduced number of myelinated axons, with a density of 1300/mm compared to approximately 6000/mm in normal controls. Myelinated axons contained microtubules, but intermediate filaments were not present.

### Inheritance

Both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance of CMT1F have been reported. Jordanova et al. (2003) found heterozygous NEFL mutations in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT, including a de novo missense mutation in one patient. Abe et al. (2009) found heterozygous missense mutations in 5 unrelated Japanese patients and a homozygous nonsense mutation in 1 patient. In the consanguineous Palestinian family reported by Yum et al. (2009), all 4 affected sibs were homozygous for a nonsense mutation, both parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sib did not carry the mutation, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for mutations in the NEFL gene. They detected 6 disease-associated heterozygous missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, which clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. In one patient the missense mutation in NEFL had arisen de novo. ⚠︎

Abe et al. (2009) screened the NEFL gene in 223 Japanese CMT patients. Patients with the demyelinating form had previously tested negative for mutations in PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX. Patients with the axonal form had tested negative for mutations in MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS. The authors found 4 heterozygous missense mutations (P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K) in 5 unrelated patients. In 1 other patient they found a homozygous nonsense mutation (E140X), which they stated was the first report of a homozygous nonsense mutation of NEFL.

In 4 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family with severe, early-onset neuropathy, Yum et al. (2009) identified a homozygous c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, predicted to result in a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation, and the unaffected sib did not carry it. No mutations were found in other genes for demyelinating or recessive axonal CMT. The mutation had not been found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at a diagnostic laboratory. In SW-13 vim- cells, the E210X mutant NFL protein did not form filaments. It showed a diffuse, largely nuclear pattern and accumulated at low levels. Unlike the dominant Q333P mutant, it did not interfere with filament formation by wildtype NFL or with the vimentin network. The authors concluded that these results suggested a simple loss of function.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jordanova et al. (2003) concluded that their findings were further evidence that the clinical variation seen in CMT depends on which gene is mutated and on the specific type of mutation.

Abe et al. (2009) concluded that nonsense mutations in NEFL probably cause a recessive phenotype, whereas missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) noted that conduction velocities in their patients fell in the 'demyelinating' range, even though NEFL is not prominently expressed by myelinating Schwann cells. They concluded that the slowed conduction was likely explained by the absence of large myelinated axons, which lacked intermediate filaments and probably failed to develop their normal calibers.

### Diagnosis

Jordanova et al. (2003) suggested that NEFL mutations should be considered in the molecular evaluation of patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT.

Yum et al. (2009) emphasized that homozygous NEFL mutations should be considered when evaluating children with Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy.

### Population Genetics

Yum et al. (2009) reported that the E210X mutation in NEFL had not been found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at a diagnostic laboratory. They noted that its prevalence in the Palestinian population was unknown.
