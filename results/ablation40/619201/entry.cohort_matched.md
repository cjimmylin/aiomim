### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is caused by homozygous mutation in the KIRREL1 gene (607428) on chromosome 1q23.1.

### Description

Nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is an autosomal recessive kidney disorder characterized by steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) with onset in childhood. Renal biopsy findings in the reported patients included minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and mesangial hypercellularity. One reported patient progressed to chronic kidney disease, and the other kept normal renal function (Solanki et al., 2019).

### Clinical Features

Solanki et al. (2019) reported 2 male patients from 2 unrelated consanguineous families with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome. Patient A666_21 was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at 5 years of age. He had SRNS at initial presentation and was resistant to different immunosuppressive drugs. He never achieved complete remission and continued to have undulating proteinuria with a partial response to treatment. Renal biopsy showed minimal change disease on light microscopy, with mesangial hypercellularity. His renal function stayed normal throughout, and he stopped attending clinical visits after 12.5 years of follow-up. He had no extrarenal manifestations. His younger sister had periorbital edema and proteinuria, and extended relatives had end-stage renal disease or nephrotic syndrome, but none of them were genotyped.

The second patient reported by Solanki et al. (2019), B742_21, was a boy of Italian descent who was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at 14 years of age and had SRNS at initial presentation. He was started on tacrolimus and weaned off corticosteroids. He also received a calcium channel blocker and an ACE inhibitor for high blood pressure and proteinuria. Renal biopsy showed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, podocyte foot process effacement, and low-grade mesangial hypercellularity. Over 4 years of follow-up he developed chronic kidney disease, which continued to progress and had reached CKD stage 2 at the time of the report.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NPHS23 in the families reported by Solanki et al. (2019) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Affected individuals from 2 consanguineous families carried homozygous KIRREL1 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) used whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping to study 596 individuals from 406 families with SRNS. In affected individuals from 2 unrelated consanguineous families, they identified homozygous missense mutations in the KIRREL1 gene: R440C in family A666 and S573L in family B742 (an Italian family). This was a frequency of 0.4% in this preselected cohort. Both residues were conserved down to zebrafish, and both mutations lay within homozygosity peaks on chromosome 1. Flag-tagged KIRREL1 carrying either mutation was stably expressed in human podocytes. The mutant proteins did not localize properly to the cell membrane and instead accumulated in the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes, indicating a trafficking defect. In a transepithelial permeability assay, podocyte monolayers expressing the mutants showed significantly increased albumin flux compared with wildtype.

### Pathogenesis

Solanki et al. (2019) found that KIRREL1 R440C and S573L mutant proteins stably expressed in human podocytes did not localize properly to the cell membrane. Instead they accumulated in the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes, indicating a trafficking defect. Membrane localization of NPHS1 was unaffected. Podocyte monolayers expressing the mutants showed significantly increased albumin flux in a transepithelial permeability assay. The authors suggested that the mutations adversely affect podocyte cell junctions and intercellular integrity.

### Clinical Management

Solanki et al. (2019) reported that one patient with KIRREL1-related SRNS achieved complete remission with tacrolimus, a calcium channel blocker, and an ACE inhibitor but later progressed to chronic kidney disease. The other patient was resistant to immunosuppression. The authors cautioned that no conclusions about genotype and treatment response could be drawn from a single case.

### Population Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) noted that the KIRREL1 S573L variant had an allele frequency of 0.0011 in European populations and was present in the homozygous state in 1 control in gnomAD. They suggested that this could reflect variable expressivity or incomplete penetrance. The R440C variant had a gnomAD minor allele frequency of 3.97 x 10(-6).

### Animal Model

Using immunogold electron microscopy in rodents, Liu et al. (2003) localized Neph1 to the glomerular slit diaphragm. Neph1 bound nephrin through their extracellular segments, and the PDZ domains of ZO-1 bound the cytoplasmic tail of Neph1. The authors gave rodents anti-Neph1 and anti-nephrin antibodies together, each at a dose too low to cause nephritis on its own. Disrupting the Neph1-nephrin interaction in this way caused proteinuria that did not depend on complement or leukocytes, and the foot processes were preserved. Neph1 and nephrin protein in podocytes fell modestly and ZO-1 protein fell sharply, while mRNA levels of all 3 genes were unchanged. Liu et al. (2003) concluded that the nephrin-Neph1 interaction appears to be an important determinant of glomerular permeability. ⚠︎
