### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is caused by homozygous mutation in the KIRREL1 gene (607428) on chromosome 1q23.1.

### Description

Nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is an autosomal recessive kidney disorder. Affected individuals have steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) beginning in childhood or adolescence, with proteinuria and no extrarenal features. Renal biopsy shows low-grade mesangial hypercellularity, a picture of minimal change disease or focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and extensive podocyte foot process effacement. Some patients progress to chronic kidney disease (Solanki et al., 2019).

### Clinical Features

Solanki et al. (2019) reported a boy (A666_21) from a consanguineous family who was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 5 years. His nephrotic syndrome was steroid-resistant from the first presentation and did not respond to several immunosuppressive drugs. He never reached complete remission and had undulating proteinuria that responded only partially to treatment. ACE inhibitors and ARBs did not help, but his renal function remained normal over 12.5 years of follow-up. Renal histology showed minor mesangial hypercellularity consistent with minimal change disease. A younger sister reportedly had periorbital edema and proteinuria. Among extended relatives, 3 children had ESRD and 1 had nephrotic syndrome, but none of these relatives had been genotyped.

Solanki et al. (2019) also reported a boy of Italian descent (B742_21) from a consanguineous family who was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 14 years. His nephrotic syndrome was steroid-resistant from the first presentation, and he also had high blood pressure. Renal biopsy showed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and electron microscopy showed podocyte foot process effacement. He achieved complete remission but developed chronic kidney disease over 4 years of follow-up, reaching CKD stage 2.

Neither of the 2 patients of Solanki et al. (2019) had extrarenal manifestations or syndromic features. Renal histology in both showed low-grade mesangial hypercellularity. Ultrastructural analysis showed extensive foot process effacement, increased mesangial matrix, and mesangial proliferation.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NPHS23 is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Solanki et al. (2019) found homozygous KIRREL1 mutations in affected children from 2 unrelated consanguineous families.

### Molecular Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) used whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping to study 596 individuals from 406 families with SRNS. They found homozygous missense mutations in the KIRREL1 gene in 2 patients from 2 unrelated consanguineous families (A666 and B742): R440C and S573L. Both altered residues are conserved to zebrafish, and each mutation lay within a homozygosity peak on chromosome 1.

In patient A666_21, Solanki et al. (2019) identified a homozygous arg440-to-cys (R440C) mutation. It lay within the maximum nonparametric LOD peak on chromosome 1 identified by homozygosity mapping, and its minor allele frequency in gnomAD was 3.97 x 10(-6).

In patient B742_21, a child of Italian descent, Solanki et al. (2019) identified a homozygous ser573-to-leu (S573L) mutation within the maximum homozygosity peak on chromosome 1. Its minor allele frequency in gnomAD was 7.87 x 10(-4), with an allele frequency of 0.0011 in European populations, and 1 control subject was homozygous for it. The authors raised the possibility of variable expressivity or incomplete penetrance for this variant. They nonetheless considered in silico scores, conservation, and functional data conclusive that it affects protein function.

### Pathogenesis

Solanki et al. (2019) stably expressed Flag-tagged KIRREL1 carrying either R440C or S573L in human podocytes. Compared with wildtype, both mutant proteins were mislocalized and reduced in the membrane fraction, with more protein in the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes. The authors suggested that this pattern reflected trafficking defects. Neither mutation affected the membrane localization of NPHS1. In transepithelial permeability assays, podocyte monolayers expressing mutant KIRREL1 let through significantly more Texas Red-labeled albumin than monolayers expressing wildtype KIRREL1. The authors concluded that the mutations adversely affect podocyte cell junctions and the integrity of contacts between cells.

### Clinical Management

Solanki et al. (2019) reported that patient B742_21, who was homozygous for S573L, reached complete remission after corticosteroids were weaned off. His treatment was tacrolimus, a calcium channel blocker, and gradually increasing doses of an ACE inhibitor, but he later progressed to CKD. Patient A666_21 never reached complete remission. The authors cautioned that no conclusions about genotype and treatment response can be drawn from a single case. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) found KIRREL1 mutations in 2 of 406 families (0.4%) in a highly preselected SRNS cohort. They noted that this showed how rarely KIRREL1 is a monogenic cause of SRNS.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2003) used immunogold electron microscopy in rodents to place Neph1 (Kirrel1) in the glomerular slit diaphragm. Neph1 bound directly to 2 other slit diaphragm components, nephrin and ZO-1. Native and recombinant Neph1 formed dimers and multimers and bound nephrin through their extracellular segments. This interaction was specific: neither protein shared it with P-cadherin, another integral slit diaphragm protein.

To disrupt the Neph1-nephrin interaction in vivo, Liu et al. (2003) injected rodents with anti-Neph1 and anti-nephrin antibodies together, each at a dose that did not cause nephritis on its own. The animals developed proteinuria that did not depend on complement or leukocytes, and their foot processes were preserved. In podocytes, Neph1 and nephrin protein levels fell modestly and ZO-1 protein fell dramatically, while mRNA levels of all 3 genes were unchanged. The fall in ZO-1 depended on the interaction of ZO-1 PDZ domains with the Neph1 cytoplasmic tail. The authors concluded that the Neph1-nephrin interaction appears to be an important determinant of glomerular permeability. ⚠︎
