### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is caused by homozygous mutation in the KIRREL1 gene (607428) on chromosome 1q23.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is an autosomal recessive form of steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), with onset in childhood in the patients reported by Solanki et al. (2019). They found no extrarenal or syndromic features. Renal histology has ranged from minimal change disease with low-grade mesangial hypercellularity to focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, with extensive podocyte foot process effacement. Treatment response and renal outcome varied between the 2 patients described (Solanki et al., 2019).

### Clinical Features

Solanki et al. (2019) reported 2 patients with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome from 2 unrelated consanguineous families. Neither patient had extrarenal manifestations or syndromic features. Renal histology in both showed low-grade mesangial hypercellularity. Ultrastructural analysis showed extensive foot process effacement, increased mesangial matrix, and mesangial proliferation.

The first patient (A666_21) of Solanki et al. (2019), a boy, was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 5 years. His disease was steroid-resistant at initial presentation, and he was also resistant to various immunosuppressive drugs. He never achieved complete remission and had undulating proteinuria with only a partial response to treatment. Additional treatment with ACE inhibitors and ARBs did not help. However, his renal function remained normal throughout 12.5 years of follow-up. Renal histology showed only minor mesangial hypercellularity, consistent with minimal change disease. His younger sister reportedly had periorbital edema and proteinuria. Among extended relatives, 3 children had end-stage renal disease and 1 child had nephrotic syndrome. DNA was not available from his sister or these relatives, so their genotypes were unknown.

The second patient (B742_21) of Solanki et al. (2019), a boy of Italian descent, was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 14 years. His disease was steroid-resistant at initial presentation. He was treated with a calcium channel blocker and an ACE inhibitor for high blood pressure and proteinuria. Renal biopsy showed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and transmission electron microscopy showed podocyte foot process effacement. He achieved complete remission with treatment. Over 4 years of follow-up, however, he developed chronic kidney disease, which had progressed to CKD stage 2 at the time of the report.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NPHS23 in the families reported by Solanki et al. (2019) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Affected individuals from 2 consanguineous families carried homozygous KIRREL1 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) performed whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping in 596 individuals from 406 families with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome. In 2 patients from 2 unrelated consanguineous families with SRNS, they identified homozygous missense mutations in the KIRREL1 gene: R440C and S573L. Both mutations affected residues conserved to D. rerio, and each lay within the maximum homozygosity peak on chromosome 1. The authors stably expressed the mutant proteins in cultured human podocytes. Both mutants were mislocalized and were present at significantly lower levels in the membrane fraction than wildtype protein. Both also showed increased localization to the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes, which the authors interpreted as a trafficking defect. Podocyte monolayers expressing the mutants showed significantly increased transepithelial albumin flux compared with wildtype. KIRREL1 mutations were found in 2 of 406 families (0.4%), which the authors said emphasized how rarely KIRREL1 is a monogenic cause in a highly preselected SRNS cohort.

### Pathogenesis

Liu et al. (2003) used immunogold electron microscopy in rodents to localize Neph1 to the glomerular slit diaphragm. They showed that Neph1 interacts directly with nephrin and ZO-1. Native and recombinant Neph1 associated with each other as dimers and multimers and interacted with nephrin through their extracellular segments. This interaction was specific and was not shared by either protein with P-cadherin.

Solanki et al. (2019) found that KIRREL1 carrying either patient mutation (R440C or S573L) failed to localize properly to the membrane of human podocytes and instead accumulated in the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes. Neither mutation affected the membrane localization of NPHS1. Cells expressing the mutants showed increased permeability to labeled albumin. The authors concluded that the mutations adversely affect podocyte cell junctions and intercellular integrity.

### Clinical Management

In the S573L homozygous patient reported by Solanki et al. (2019), complete remission followed treatment with tacrolimus, a calcium channel blocker, and gradually increasing doses of an ACE inhibitor after corticosteroids were weaned off. He later progressed to chronic kidney disease. The R440C homozygous patient never achieved complete remission. The authors cautioned that genotype-treatment conclusions could not be drawn from a single case.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2003) noted that Neph1-deficient mice develop nephrotic syndrome at birth. In rodents, they disrupted the Neph1-nephrin interaction in vivo by injecting anti-Neph1 and anti-nephrin antibodies together, each at a dose that was subnephritogenic on its own. This caused proteinuria that did not depend on complement or leukocytes, and foot processes were preserved. Neph1 and nephrin protein expression in podocytes fell modestly. ZO-1 protein expression fell dramatically through the interaction of ZO-1 PDZ domains with the Neph1 cytoplasmic tail. mRNA expression of all 3 genes was unchanged. ⚠︎

The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and lay within the maximum homozygosity peak on chromosome 1. The altered residue is conserved to D. rerio. The variant had a minor allele frequency of 3.97 x 10(-6) in gnomAD.
