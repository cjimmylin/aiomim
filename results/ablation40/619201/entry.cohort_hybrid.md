### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is caused by homozygous mutation in the KIRREL1 gene (607428) on chromosome 1q23.1.

### Description

Nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is an autosomal recessive kidney disorder. It was reported in 2 unrelated boys from consanguineous families who were diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at ages 5 and 14 years. The nephrotic syndrome was steroid-resistant from presentation, and the patients had no extrarenal manifestations. Kidney biopsy showed low-grade mesangial hypercellularity, with minimal change disease in one patient and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis in the other. Response to treatment and renal outcome differed between the 2 patients (Solanki et al., 2019).

### Clinical Features

Solanki et al. (2019) reported 2 boys from unrelated consanguineous families (A666 and B742) who had steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) without extrarenal manifestations or syndromic features. Patient A666_21 was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 5 years. He was steroid-resistant at initial presentation and was also resistant to different immunosuppressive drugs. He never achieved complete remission and continued to have undulating proteinuria with a partial response to treatment. Additional treatment with an ACE inhibitor and an angiotensin receptor blocker did not help. His renal function stayed normal throughout, and he stopped attending clinic after 12.5 years of follow-up. On light microscopy, his kidney biopsy showed only minor mesangial hypercellularity, consistent with minimal change disease. He had a younger sister with reported periorbital edema and proteinuria. Among his extended relatives, 3 children had end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and 1 child had nephrotic syndrome. None of these relatives could be genotyped.

Patient B742_21, of Italian descent, was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 14 years and was steroid-resistant at initial presentation (Solanki et al., 2019). He was started on tacrolimus and weaned off corticosteroids. He also received a calcium channel blocker and an ACE inhibitor for high blood pressure and proteinuria, and he achieved complete remission. However, he developed chronic kidney disease during 4 years of follow-up, and it had progressed to CKD stage 2 at the time of the report. His kidney biopsy showed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis on light microscopy and podocyte foot process effacement on electron microscopy. Solanki et al. (2019) noted that renal histology in both patients showed low-grade mesangial hypercellularity. Ultrastructural analysis showed extensive foot process effacement, increased mesangial matrix, and mesangial proliferation. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Solanki et al. (2019) found homozygous KIRREL1 mutations in 2 affected individuals from unrelated consanguineous families. This finding is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance of NPHS23.

### Molecular Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) performed whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping in a cohort of 596 individuals from 406 families with SRNS. They identified homozygous missense mutations in the KIRREL1 gene in affected individuals from 2 unrelated consanguineous families: R440C in family A666 and S573L in family B742. This corresponded to a frequency of 0.4% (2 of 406 families) in this highly preselected cohort. Both altered residues were conserved down to D. rerio, and each mutation lay within a homozygosity peak on chromosome 1. DNA was not available from the affected relatives of patient A666_21, so segregation could not be tested. The R440C variant had a minor allele frequency of 3.97 x 10(-6) in gnomAD. The S573L variant had an allele frequency of 0.0011 in European populations and was present in the homozygous state in 1 gnomAD control. The authors therefore raised the possibility of variable expressivity or incomplete penetrance for this variant. Flag-tagged KIRREL1 carrying either mutation was stably expressed in cultured human podocytes. Both mutant proteins were mislocalized and were present at significantly lower levels in the membrane fraction than wildtype protein. Instead, they accumulated in the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes, which suggested a trafficking defect. Membrane localization of NPHS1 was not affected. In a transepithelial permeability assay, podocyte monolayers expressing the mutant proteins showed significantly increased albumin flux compared with wildtype.

### Clinical Management

Solanki et al. (2019) reported that one patient with KIRREL1 mutation achieved complete remission with tacrolimus, a calcium channel blocker, and an ACE inhibitor but later progressed to chronic kidney disease. The other patient was resistant to immunosuppressive drugs. The authors cautioned that this response in a single patient could not be generalized to other patients with KIRREL1 mutations.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2003) noted that Neph1-deficient mice develop nephrotic syndrome at birth. Using immunogold electron microscopy in rodents, they localized Neph1 to the glomerular slit diaphragm. Neph1 interacted directly with nephrin through their extracellular segments, and the PDZ domains of ZO-1 bound the cytoplasmic tail of Neph1. They disrupted the Neph1-nephrin interaction in vivo by injecting anti-Neph1 and anti-nephrin antibodies together, each at a subnephritogenic dose. This caused proteinuria that was independent of complement and leukocytes, and the foot processes were preserved. Neph1 and nephrin protein expression in podocytes fell modestly and ZO-1 protein expression fell dramatically, while mRNA levels of all 3 genes did not change.
