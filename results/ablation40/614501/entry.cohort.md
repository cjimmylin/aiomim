### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene (608241) on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is an autosomal recessive disorder reported in the Old Order Amish. It starts in the neonatal period with hypotonia and poor feeding. It is followed by severe global developmental delay with absent speech, seizures that are often intractable, and abnormalities on brain imaging. Craniofacial features include an abnormal skull shape, a bulbous nose, and a wide mouth. Some patients have cardiac, airway, and other congenital anomalies.

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia in 3 affected Old Order Amish individuals from 2 sibships, including 2 brothers. Affected neonates were hypotonic and fed poorly. Dysmorphic features that evolved over time included a bulbous nose, a wide mouth and tongue, a broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. The patients had severe psychomotor delay, could not walk, and had no speech. Other neurologic findings were strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. Brain imaging showed ventriculomegaly, a thin corpus callosum, and white matter abnormalities or hypomyelination. The skull surface was undulating and lumpy. EEG showed multifocal spike-wave discharges, and seizures were intractable. Some patients had drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, or generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Physical anomalies found in some patients were subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, and hydrocele. ⚠︎

Ammous et al. (2021) identified 51 Old Order Amish individuals from 27 sibships with SNIP1-related disorder. Twelve of them had died, at ages between 1 week and 11 years. They evaluated 35 of these individuals (19 males and 16 females, aged 1 month to 26 years), who came from 21 interrelated families and included the patients of Puffenberger et al. (2012). All had severe global developmental delay, were nonverbal, and had intellectual disability, hypotonia, hyporeflexia, seizures, an abnormal EEG, and feeding difficulties. Epilepsy was drug-resistant in some patients. Some showed developmental regression after status epilepticus, and 2 had hemiparesis after status epilepticus. Neuroimaging was available for 18 individuals, and all 18 had abnormalities. These included ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, a thin corpus callosum, hydrocephalus, septo-optic dysplasia, and Chiari malformation, and 1 patient had a cerebral arteriovenous malformation. Craniofacial findings included an abnormal skull shape with an irregular surface or craniosynostosis, a high-arched palate, and a wide mouth with a cupid's bow upper lip. Five patients had a cloverleaf skull with multisuture craniosynostosis, and 3 had Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate. Some patients had micrognathia, midface hypoplasia, a small upturned bulbous nose, downslanting palpebral fissures, or short hands with tapered fingers. Other findings in some patients were: - behavioral problems (irritability, autistic features, ADHD) and sleep-wake dysregulation - upper airway abnormalities (laryngomalacia, apnea, stridor) - congenital heart defects (ASD, VSD, aortic coarctation) and left ventricular noncompaction cardiomyopathy - nystagmus and/or strabismus, and hearing loss shown by a failed newborn hearing screen - small size for gestational age, intrauterine growth retardation, and failure to thrive - pulmonary aspiration and gastroesophageal reflux - hypothyroidism and hypoglycemia - spinal abnormalities (scoliosis, sacral dimple, tethered cord) - umbilical and inguinal hernias, congenital talipes equinovarus, and pectus excavatum ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Puffenberger et al. (2012) reported that the disorder is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern: the affected individuals in 2 Old Order Amish sibships were homozygous for a SNIP1 mutation. Ammous et al. (2021) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. All 35 affected individuals they evaluated were homozygous for the mutation, all parents were heterozygous, and all unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wild type.

### Mapping

Puffenberger et al. (2012) carried out autozygosity mapping with 10K SNP arrays in Old Order Amish patients. They mapped symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia to a 6.6-Mb region on chromosome 1 that contained 126 genes.

Ammous et al. (2021) carried out homozygosity mapping in 4 affected Amish individuals from 2 nuclear families. They found a single shared 1.65-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 1p34.3, between rs6667450 and rs10889902, that contained 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in 3 affected Old Order Amish individuals from 2 sibships. Within the mapped region on chromosome 1 they found 34 homozygous variants, and only one of these was novel: a c.1097A-G mutation in the SNIP1 gene, causing a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. Sibs and parents were sequenced when available to confirm segregation. The mutated residue is highly conserved and lies in the C terminus, where SNIP1 interacts with c-Myc, SMAD1, and SMAD2. PolyPhen-2 predicted the substitution to be probably damaging. The authors expressed mouse Snip1 in mIMCD3 cells. Wild-type Snip1 localized to the nucleus in a punctate pattern. The mutant corresponding to human E366G (mouse glu353-to-gly) looked more aggregated and was unstable, with band density 84.9% lower than wild type, although its transcript levels were comparable to wild type. The mutation was found in 5 of 203 Old Order Amish controls (2.5%), all of whom were carriers.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-exome sequencing in Old Order Amish patients. The only potentially pathogenic variant in the critical interval was the c.1097A-G (E366G) mutation in exon 4 of the SNIP1 gene. The affected residue is highly conserved and lies close to the C-terminal FHA domain. The authors confirmed that the mutation was homozygous in 35 affected individuals from 21 interrelated families. All parents were heterozygous, and all unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wild type.

### Pathogenesis

Puffenberger et al. (2012) proposed that reduced SNIP1 abundance likely lowers c-Myc activity and increases TGF-beta and NF-kappa-B signaling. They noted that such changes have been linked to abnormal development of the brain, skull, and limbs and to epileptogenesis in mice. ⚠︎

Ammous et al. (2021) performed blood transcriptome sequencing in 6 affected individuals. They found 75 significantly upregulated and 109 significantly downregulated genes. The most overrepresented pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. The dysregulated genes included seizure-associated genes such as CNTNAP2 and ROBO1, which was the most upregulated gene. The authors also noted that Snip1-null mice die as embryos, which suggested that the Amish mutation is unlikely to cause complete loss of function.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) recommended elective gastrostomy tube placement and careful optimization of anticonvulsants to treat infantile apnea and prevent status epilepticus. At diagnosis, they recommended early EEG, neuroimaging, and echocardiography. They also recommended monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism and yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) found 5 carriers of the SNIP1 c.1097A-G mutation among 203 Old Order Amish controls (2.5%).

In a control database of more than 5,000 Amish individuals, Ammous et al. (2021) found that the E366G allele had a frequency of 0.5% in the Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in the Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish, with no unaffected homozygotes. In gnomAD, the allele frequency was 0.001%. Eleven heterozygotes were listed there, and 10 of them were Amish.
