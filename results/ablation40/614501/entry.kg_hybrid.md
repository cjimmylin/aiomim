### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene (608241) on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures is an autosomal recessive multisystem disorder that has been reported in the Old Order Amish. Ammous et al. (2021) listed its cardinal features as hypotonia, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, a characteristic facial appearance, and skull abnormalities. Seizures begin in infancy or childhood and are often intractable. Some patients also have congenital heart defects, upper airway abnormalities, feeding difficulties, and other congenital anomalies. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Puffenberger et al. (2012) called the disorder 'symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia.' Ammous et al. (2021) used the term 'SNIP1-related disorder' and also referred to it as autosomal recessive psychomotor retardation, epilepsy, and craniofacial dysmorphism (PMRED).

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described an autosomal recessive disorder in Old Order Amish children that they called symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Affected neonates were hypotonic and fed poorly. Over time they developed dysmorphic features, including a bulbous nose, wide mouth and tongue, a broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. Affected children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak. Many kept the use of their hands to scoot, maneuver a wheelchair, or gesture. Some showed behavioral responses to language but did not engage socially or follow verbal commands. On examination, patients were typically subdued and had strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, a thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities (hypomyelination), and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular contour of the skull. Multifocal spike-wave discharges from central, occipital, and temporal regions usually began by 6 months of age. They were accompanied by focal or generalized seizures, which could take the form of dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, or generalized tonic-clonic events, and a single patient could have several intractable seizure types. Some patients had subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, or hydrocele.

Ammous et al. (2021) evaluated 35 of 51 known affected individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families (19 males and 16 females, aged 1 month to 26 years). All had molecularly confirmed disease, and 2 had been reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). Six children died between 9 months and 11 years of age from infection, sudden cardiopulmonary arrest, or accidental drowning. Phenotypic variation was substantial, including within sibships. The following features were present in all 35 patients: - severe, nonverbal global developmental delay - hypotonia - hyporeflexia - seizures - feeding difficulties - abnormal skull shape - high-arched palate - a wide mouth with cupid's bow upper lip Craniofacial features also included midface hypoplasia, downturned corners of the mouth with a thin cupid's bow upper lip, a large tongue, microretrognathia, malocclusion, a small upturned bulbous nose, long palpebral fissures, and proptosis. Proptosis was seen in individuals with craniosynostosis. Three patients had Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate. Skull abnormalities ranged from normocephaly to severe multisuture craniosynostosis, with a cloverleaf skull in 5 individuals. They also included an irregular skull surface with variable calvarial thickness and bony dysplasia. Other dysmorphic features were brachydactyly, broad thumbs, single palmar crease, congenital talipes equinovarus, pes planus, toenail hypoplasia, and pectus excavatum.

In the cohort of Ammous et al. (2021), all affected individuals developed epilepsy. Seizures began in infancy or childhood, were focal or generalized and intractable, and included myoclonic, absence, and tonic-clonic seizures. EEG was abnormal in all individuals assessed but had no characteristic pattern; findings included diffuse background slowing and frequent focal or multifocal discharges. In some infants, seizures first appeared as apnea or breath-holding spells, and subclinical seizures were detected in 6 individuals. Febrile illness, sleep deprivation, and hypoglycemic episodes could trigger or worsen seizures. No antiepileptic medication consistently controlled seizures. Some individuals had severe developmental regression after status epilepticus, and 2 were left with permanent hemiparesis. Patients identified in late childhood or adolescence were often diagnosed with mixed-tone spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy. Behavioral diagnoses included autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit disorder, and irritability and sleep/wake dysregulation were seen in infancy and early childhood. Most patients walked independently at ages ranging from 3 to 10 years. Some communicated with signs, gestures, and sounds.

Ammous et al. (2021) reported abnormal neuroimaging in all 18 individuals who had been imaged. Findings included: - hydrocephalus - ventriculomegaly - white matter abnormalities - thin corpus callosum - hypomyelination - irregular cortical ribbon - Chiari malformation - absent septum pellucidum - hypoplastic optic nerves - septo-optic dysplasia One individual had a cerebral arteriovenous malformation. Sixty percent of patients had congenital cardiac defects. These included hypoplastic left heart syndrome, aortic stenosis and bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation, aortic root dilatation, atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary artery stenosis, and mitral valve regurgitation. Twelve percent developed cardiomyopathy (left ventricular noncompaction). In the neonatal period, upper airway problems were common, including laryngomalacia, pharyngomalacia, and subglottic stenosis. These caused a weak cry, stridor, and apnea and occasionally required tracheostomy and supplementary home oxygen.

Ammous et al. (2021) also reported the following features: - Feeding: poor feeding in the neonatal period was universal, and many patients needed a gastrostomy tube. - Spine and chest: spinal abnormalities (sacral dimple, tethered cord, and scoliosis) occurred in 23%; 1 individual had pectus excavatum and hypoplastic ribs. - Gastrointestinal: gastroesophageal reflux, type III paraesophageal hernia, intestinal malrotation, hepatomegaly, and umbilical and inguinal hernias. - Growth: intrauterine growth retardation and failure to thrive were common. - Endocrine: a small proportion of patients had hypoglycemia, hypothyroidism, hypothermia, or dyshormonogenesis. - Eyes: strabismus, horizontal nystagmus, and myopia. - Hearing: chronic otitis media and hearing loss; 20% failed newborn hearing screening.

### Inheritance

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described the disorder as recessive in Old Order Amish children who were homozygous for a SNIP1 variant. Ammous et al. (2021) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance: all parents were heterozygous for the variant, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype.

### Mapping

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed autozygosity mapping with 10K SNP microarrays in 6 Amish patients with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. They mapped the disorder to a 6.6-Mb shared homozygous block on chromosome 1, between rs2153337 and rs2225251, that contained 126 genes.

Ammous et al. (2021) carried out a genome-wide SNP screen in 4 affected individuals from 2 Amish nuclear families. They identified a single shared region of homozygosity larger than 1 Mb: a 1.65-Mb region on chromosome 1p34.3, delimited by SNP markers rs6667450 and rs10889902, that contained 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in 3 Old Order Amish patients from 2 sibships with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Of 34 homozygous variants in the mapped region, only one was novel: a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with the disorder. PolyPhen-2 predicted it to be probably damaging. Glu366 is highly conserved and lies in the C-terminal region through which SNIP1 interacts with c-Myc, Smad1, and Smad2. The authors studied the corresponding mouse mutant, Snip1 glu353-to-gly, by transient overexpression in mIMCD3 cells. Wildtype protein localized to the nucleus in a punctate pattern consistent with transcriptional complexes. The mutant also localized to the nucleus but appeared more aggregated, and Western blotting showed that it was unstable: its band density was 84.9% lower than that of wildtype, although its transcript levels were comparable to wildtype.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 affected Amish individuals. The only potentially pathogenic variant in the 1p34.3 critical interval was the c.1097A-G (E366G) variant in exon 4 of SNIP1 that Puffenberger et al. (2012) had identified. Dideoxy sequencing confirmed the variant, and it cosegregated with the disorder. Targeted testing confirmed homozygosity in 35 affected individuals; all parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. Overall, 49 of 51 affected Old Order Amish individuals from 27 sibships were confirmed homozygous. The remaining 2 were not genotyped but had characteristic features and an affected sib who was homozygous for the variant. The residue is highly conserved and lies close to the C-terminal FHA domain. The variant was present in gnomAD at an allele frequency of 0.001%, with 11 heterozygotes (10 of them Amish) and no homozygotes.

### Pathogenesis

Puffenberger et al. (2012) proposed that reduced SNIP1 abundance probably leads to decreased c-Myc activity and increased TGF-beta and NF-kappa-B signaling. They noted that disrupting c-Myc or CBP/p300 signaling in mice can cause abnormal development of the brain, skull, craniofacial bones, and distal limbs. They also noted that increased TGF-beta/Smad2 or NF-kappa-B signaling in the mouse brain can independently initiate epileptogenesis.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-transcriptome sequencing of blood from 6 affected individuals in 4 sibships and compared them with sex-matched controls. They identified 75 significantly upregulated and 109 significantly downregulated genes. Among the most altered genes with a known clinical phenotype, 24 had established associations with neurologic disease, including the seizure-associated genes ROBO1 (the most upregulated gene), SOX5, CNTNAP2, PAFAH1B1, and TSNARE1. The most overrepresented Reactome pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, with upregulation of PARD3 and downregulation of F11R and RPS27A. The authors suggested that the E366G substitution disrupts the regulation of multiple developmental signaling pathways. They noted that Snip1-null mice die as embryos and suggested that the Amish variant is therefore unlikely to cause complete loss of function.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) recommended: - elective gastrostomy tube placement to support growth and limit pulmonary aspiration - careful optimization of anticonvulsants to treat apnea in infancy, maintain seizure control, and prevent status epilepticus and the developmental regression that follows it - early EEG in infants with suspected apneic episodes - neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis - consideration of upper gastrointestinal study, head ultrasound, and renal ultrasound - monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism - early speech, occupational, and physical therapy - yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments Behavioral therapy was beneficial in older children with autism spectrum disorder, and melatonin had been used successfully in some children with sleep dysregulation.

### Population Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) found 5 carriers of the SNIP1 E366G variant among 203 Old Order Amish controls (2.5%). They gave the population-specific allele frequency as 1.48% (6/406) and identified no homozygous controls.

Ammous et al. (2021) checked an in-house Amish control database of more than 5,000 individuals. The E366G founder variant had an allele frequency of 0.5% in the Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in the Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish, and no unaffected individual was homozygous.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, CRANIOFACIAL ABNORMALITIES, AND SEIZURES**  
SNIP1, GLU366GLY [c.1097A>G; p.E366G; NM_024700.4:c.1097A>G; p.Glu366Gly]

Puffenberger et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution, in Old Order Amish patients with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. The mutation was found by exome sequencing of 3 affected individuals from 2 sibships and was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It segregated with the disorder and was found in 5 of 203 Old Order Amish controls, none of whom was homozygous. When the corresponding mouse Snip1 mutant (E353G) was overexpressed in mIMCD3 cells, it localized to the nucleus with a more aggregated appearance than wildtype. Western blotting showed that it was unstable, with 84.9% lower band density than wildtype.

Ammous et al. (2021) confirmed homozygosity for the E366G mutation in 35 Old Order Amish individuals with the disorder from 21 interrelated families. All parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. In total, 49 of 51 known affected individuals were confirmed to be homozygous. The variant had an allele frequency of 0.5% in the Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in the Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish. No unaffected homozygotes were found in an Amish control database of more than 5,000 individuals or in gnomAD.
