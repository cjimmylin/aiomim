### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene (608241) on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures is an autosomal recessive disorder identified in the Old Order Amish. Its cardinal features are hypotonia, severe nonverbal global developmental delay, intellectual disability, intractable seizures, a characteristic facial appearance, and skull abnormalities ranging from an irregular skull surface to multisuture craniosynostosis. Congenital heart defects, neonatal upper airway abnormalities, feeding difficulties, and structural brain abnormalities are common (Puffenberger et al., 2012; Ammous et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Puffenberger et al. (2012) called this disorder symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Ammous et al. (2021) referred to it as SNIP1-related disorder.

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described an autosomal recessive disorder in Old Order Amish children, which they called symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Affected newborns were hypotonic and fed poorly. Over time they developed dysmorphic features, including a bulbous nose, wide mouth and tongue, a broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. Affected children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak. Many kept use of their hands, and some showed behavioral responses to language but did not engage socially or follow verbal commands. On examination, the children were typically subdued and had strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, a thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities/hypomyelination, and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular skull contour. Multifocal spike-wave discharges usually began by 6 months of age. Seizures were focal or generalized and included dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, and generalized tonic-clonic events, and a single patient could have several intractable seizure types. Some patients had subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, or hydrocele.

Ammous et al. (2021) evaluated 35 of 51 identified individuals with molecularly confirmed SNIP1-related disorder. The 35 came from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families, comprised 19 males and 16 females, and ranged in age from 1 month to 26 years. Two of them had been reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). The cardinal features were hypotonia, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, a characteristic facial appearance, and skull abnormalities. All patients had severe nonverbal global developmental delay, hypotonia, hyporeflexia, seizures, feeding difficulties, abnormal skull shape, a high-arched palate, and a wide mouth with a cupid's bow upper lip. Phenotypic variation was substantial, including within sibships. Six children died between 9 months and 11 years of age from infection, sudden cardiopulmonary arrest, or accidental drowning. ⚠︎

The craniofacial features reported by Ammous et al. (2021) were midface hypoplasia, a wide mouth with downturned corners and a thin cupid's bow upper lip, large tongue, high-arched palate, microretrognathia, malocclusion, a small upturned bulbous nose, long palpebral fissures, and proptosis. Three patients had Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate. Skull abnormalities ranged from normocephaly to severe multisuture craniosynostosis with a cloverleaf skull, which was present in 5 individuals. They also included an irregular skull surface with variable calvarial thickness and bony dysplasia. Other dysmorphic features were brachydactyly, broad thumbs, single palmar crease, clubfoot, pes planus, toenail hypoplasia, and pectus excavatum.

Ammous et al. (2021) found that all affected individuals developed epilepsy, with infantile- or childhood-onset intractable seizures that were focal or generalized (myoclonic, absence, tonic-clonic). EEG was abnormal in all individuals assessed but showed no characteristic pattern. In some infants, seizures first presented as apnea or breath-holding spells, and subclinical seizures were detected in 6 individuals. No antiepileptic drug consistently controlled the seizures. Some individuals had severe developmental regression after status epilepticus, and 2 of them had permanent hemiparesis. Behavioral diagnoses included autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit disorder. Most patients walked independently by age 3 to 10 years. Puffenberger et al. (2012) had reported that affected children did not learn to walk.

Ammous et al. (2021) found abnormalities in all 18 individuals who had neuroimaging. These included hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, hypomyelination, an irregular cortical ribbon, Chiari malformation, absent septum pellucidum, hypoplastic optic nerves, and septo-optic dysplasia. One patient had a cerebral arteriovenous malformation. Sixty percent of patients had congenital heart defects, including hypoplastic left heart syndrome, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation, aortic root dilatation, atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary artery stenosis, and mitral regurgitation. Twelve percent developed cardiomyopathy (left ventricular noncompaction). Neonatal upper airway problems (laryngomalacia, pharyngomalacia, subglottic stenosis) caused a weak cry, stridor, and apnea, and occasionally required tracheostomy.

Ammous et al. (2021) reported that neonatal poor feeding was universal and that many patients needed a gastrostomy tube. Intrauterine growth retardation and failure to thrive were common. Twenty-three percent had spinal abnormalities (sacral dimple, tethered cord, scoliosis). Gastrointestinal findings included gastroesophageal reflux, paraesophageal hernia, intestinal malrotation, hepatomegaly, and umbilical and inguinal hernias. A small proportion had endocrine abnormalities, including hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism. Ophthalmic features included strabismus, horizontal nystagmus, and myopia. About 20% failed newborn hearing screening.

### Inheritance

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described the disorder as autosomal recessive. Ammous et al. (2021) supported autosomal recessive inheritance: affected individuals were homozygous for a SNIP1 mutation, all parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype.

### Mapping

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed autozygosity mapping with 10K SNP microarrays in 6 Amish patients with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. They mapped the disorder to a 6.6-Mb shared homozygous block on chromosome 1, between rs2153337 and rs2225251, that contained 126 genes.

Ammous et al. (2021) screened 4 affected individuals from 2 Amish nuclear families for autozygous regions with a genome-wide SNP screen. They found a single shared region of homozygosity larger than 1 Mb: a 1.65-Mb region on chromosome 1p34.3, between SNP markers rs6667450 and rs10889902, containing 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in 3 affected individuals from 2 Old Order Amish sibships with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Of 34 homozygous variants in the mapped region, only one was novel: a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, which segregated with the disorder. PolyPhen-2 predicted it to be probably damaging. Glu366 is highly conserved and lies in the C-terminal region that interacts with c-Myc, Smad1, and Smad2. The mutation was found in 5 of 203 Old Order Amish controls (allele frequency 1.48%, 6/406), and none of the controls was homozygous. In mIMCD3 cells, the authors transiently overexpressed wildtype mouse Snip1 and the mutant glu353-to-gly, which is equivalent to human E366G. The wildtype protein localized to the nucleus in a punctate pattern consistent with transcriptional complexes. The mutant also localized to the nucleus but appeared more aggregated. On Western blot the mutant was unstable, with band density 84.9% lower than wildtype, although its transcript levels were comparable to wildtype.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 affected Amish individuals. The only potentially pathogenic variant in the 1p34.3 critical interval was the c.1097A-G transition (NM_024700.4) in exon 4 of SNIP1, resulting in the E366G substitution previously identified by Puffenberger et al. (2012). The residue is highly conserved and lies close to the C-terminal FHA domain. Targeted testing confirmed homozygosity in 35 affected individuals. All parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. In gnomAD, the variant had an allele frequency of 0.001% (11 heterozygotes, 10 of them Amish) and no homozygotes. Overall, 49 of 51 Old Order Amish individuals with the disorder, from 27 sibships, were confirmed homozygous for this founder mutation. The remaining 2 were not genotyped but had characteristic features and an affected sib who was homozygous.

### Pathogenesis

Puffenberger et al. (2012) proposed that reduced SNIP1 abundance probably lowers c-Myc activity and raises TGF-beta and NF-kappa-B signaling. They noted that disrupting c-Myc or CBP/p300 signaling in mice can cause abnormal development of the brain, skull, craniofacial bones, and distal limbs. They also noted that increased TGF-beta/Smad2 or NF-kappa-B signaling in mouse brain can initiate epileptogenesis.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-blood transcriptome sequencing in 6 affected individuals from 4 sibships and in sex-matched controls. They found 75 significantly upregulated and 109 significantly downregulated genes. Twenty-four of these had established associations with neurologic disease, including the seizure-associated genes ROBO1 (the most upregulated), SOX5, CNTNAP2, PAFAH1B1, and TSNARE1. The most overrepresented Reactome pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, with PARD3 upregulated and F11R and RPS27A downregulated. The authors suggested that the E366G mutation disrupts the regulation of multiple developmental signaling pathways. Because Snip1-null mice die as embryos, they suggested that E366G is unlikely to cause complete loss of function. They also noted that the substitution lies close to the FHA domain, within a conserved C-terminal intrinsically disordered region. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) gave several management recommendations. These included elective gastrostomy tube placement to support growth and limit aspiration. They also recommended careful optimization of anticonvulsants to treat infantile apnea, control seizures, and prevent status epilepticus and the developmental regression that follows it. Further recommendations were early EEG in infants with suspected apneic episodes, neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis, monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism, early supportive therapies, and yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments. Melatonin had been used successfully for sleep dysregulation in some children.

### Population Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) found the SNIP1 E366G mutation in 5 of 203 Old Order Amish controls, a carrier frequency of 2.5% and a population-specific allele frequency of 1.48% (6/406).

Ammous et al. (2021) screened an in-house Amish control database of more than 5,000 individuals. The E366G founder mutation had an allele frequency of 0.5% in the Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in the Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish, and no unaffected individual was homozygous. Twelve of 51 affected individuals (24%) had died, at ages between 1 week and 11 years.
