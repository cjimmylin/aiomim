### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene (608241) on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures is an autosomal recessive disorder reported in Old Order Amish individuals. Its cardinal features are hypotonia, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, a characteristic craniofacial appearance, and skull abnormalities (Puffenberger et al., 2012; Ammous et al., 2021). Seizures start in infancy and are often intractable. Some patients also have congenital malformations or cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, or endocrine features (Ammous et al., 2021).

### Nomenclature

Puffenberger et al. (2012) called the disorder 'symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia.' Ammous et al. (2021) called it 'SNIP1-related disorder.'

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) reported Old Order Amish children with a disorder they called symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Affected neonates were hypotonic and fed poorly. Dysmorphic features developed over time and included a bulbous nose, a wide mouth and tongue, a broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. The children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak. Many kept the use of their hands to scoot, steer a wheelchair, or gesture. Some responded to language with changes in behavior but did not engage socially or follow verbal commands. On examination the typical child was subdued and had strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. MRI showed ventriculomegaly, a thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities, and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular skull contour. Multifocal spike-wave discharges from central, occipital, and temporal regions usually began by 6 months of age. They were accompanied by focal or generalized seizures that could take the form of dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, or generalized tonic-clonic events. A single patient could have several intractable seizure types. Some patients had subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, or hydrocele.

Ammous et al. (2021) identified 51 affected Old Order Amish individuals in 27 sibships. They evaluated 35 of them in detail: 19 males and 16 females from 21 interrelated families, aged 1 month to 26 years. This group included new findings on 2 individuals previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). Six children in the evaluated group died between 9 months and 11 years of age from infection, sudden cardiopulmonary arrest, or accidental drowning. The phenotype varied considerably, including within sibships. All 35 individuals had severe global developmental delay with absent speech, as well as hypotonia, hyporeflexia, seizures, feeding difficulties, abnormal skull shape, high-arched palate, and a wide mouth with a cupid's bow upper lip. Other craniofacial features included midface hypoplasia, downturned mouth corners, a large tongue, microretrognathia, malocclusion, a small upturned bulbous nose, long palpebral fissures, and proptosis in individuals with craniosynostosis. Three patients had Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate. Skull findings ranged from normocephaly to severe multisuture craniosynostosis, with a cloverleaf skull in 5 individuals. They also included an irregular skull surface with variable calvarial thickness and bony dysplasia. Other dysmorphic features were brachydactyly, broad thumbs, single palmar crease, congenital talipes equinovarus, pes planus, toenail hypoplasia, and pectus excavatum. Seizure types included focal and generalized intractable seizures (myoclonic, absence, tonic-clonic) with onset in infancy or childhood. EEG was abnormal in all individuals assessed but showed no characteristic pattern. In some infants, seizures first appeared as apnea or breath-holding spells, and 6 individuals had subclinical seizures. Febrile illness, sleep deprivation, and hypoglycemic episodes could trigger or worsen seizures, and no antiepileptic drug consistently controlled them. Some individuals regressed severely after status epilepticus, and 2 had permanent hemiparesis afterward. Patients identified in late childhood or adolescence were often diagnosed with mixed-tone spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy. Behavioral problems, including autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit disorder, were present in 75%. Most affected individuals walked independently by 3 to 10 years of age. All 18 individuals with neuroimaging had abnormalities. These included hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, hypomyelination, an irregular cortical ribbon, Chiari malformation, absent septum pellucidum, hypoplastic optic nerves, and septo-optic dysplasia. One individual had a cerebral arteriovenous malformation. Congenital heart defects occurred in 60% and included hypoplastic left heart syndrome, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation, aortic root dilatation, atrial and ventricular septal defects, patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary artery stenosis, and mitral valve regurgitation. Cardiomyopathy (left ventricular noncompaction) occurred in 12%. Upper airway abnormalities, including laryngomalacia, pharyngomalacia, and subglottic stenosis, occurred in 75% and sometimes required tracheostomy. Other features included: - small for gestational age, intrauterine growth retardation, and failure to thrive - pulmonary aspiration - gastroesophageal reflux, intestinal malrotation, and hernias - spinal abnormalities - hypothyroidism and hypoglycemia - strabismus, horizontal nystagmus, and myopia - failed newborn hearing screen ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Puffenberger et al. (2012) found that the disorder in Old Order Amish patients was associated with a homozygous SNIP1 variant, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Ammous et al. (2021) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. All 35 affected individuals they tested were homozygous for the SNIP1 variant, all parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or homozygous wildtype.

### Mapping

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed autozygosity mapping with 10K SNP microarrays in 6 samples from Old Order Amish patients. They mapped the disorder to a 6.6-Mb shared homozygous block on chromosome 1, between rs2153337 and rs2225251, which contained 126 genes.

Ammous et al. (2021) ran a genomewide SNP screen in 4 affected individuals from 2 Amish nuclear families. They found a single shared region of homozygosity larger than 1 Mb: a 1.65-Mb region on chromosome 1p34.3 between rs6667450 and rs10889902, containing 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in 3 affected Old Order Amish individuals from 2 sibships. Of 34 homozygous variants in the mapped region, only one was novel: a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. Glu366 is highly conserved and lies in the C terminus, where SNIP1 interacts with c-Myc, Smad1, and Smad2. They overexpressed the corresponding mouse mutant (E353G) in mIMCD3 cells. Its transcript was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype, but the protein looked more aggregated in the nucleus than the punctate wildtype protein. On Western blot the mutant was unstable, with band density 84.9% lower than wildtype.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed exome sequencing in 2 affected Amish individuals. The only potentially pathogenic variant in the 1p34.3 critical interval was the homozygous SNIP1 c.1097A-G (E366G) mutation previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). Targeted testing confirmed homozygosity for the mutation in 35 affected individuals. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the families: all parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. The affected residue is highly conserved and lies close to the FHA domain at the C terminus.

### Pathogenesis

Puffenberger et al. (2012) proposed that reduced SNIP1 abundance probably lowers c-Myc activity and increases TGF-beta and NF-kappa-B signaling. They noted that disrupting c-Myc or CBP/p300 signaling in mice can cause abnormal development of the brain, skull, craniofacial bones, and distal limbs. They also noted that increased TGF-beta/Smad2 and NF-kappa-B signaling in the mouse brain can each initiate epileptogenesis.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-transcriptome sequencing on blood from 6 affected individuals in 4 sibships and compared them with sex-matched controls. They found 75 significantly upregulated and 109 significantly downregulated genes. The most overrepresented pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. ROBO1 was the most upregulated gene. Five dysregulated genes (ROBO1, SOX5, CNTNAP2, PAFAH1B1, and TSNARE1) are associated with seizures. Because Snip1-null mice die as embryos, the authors suggested that the Amish variant is unlikely to cause complete loss of function.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) made the following recommendations: - elective gastrostomy tube placement to support growth and limit pulmonary aspiration - careful optimization of anticonvulsants to treat apnea in infancy, maintain seizure control, and prevent status epilepticus and the developmental regression that follows it - early EEG in infants with suspected apneic episodes - neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis - consideration of upper gastrointestinal study, head ultrasound, and renal ultrasound - monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism - yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments - early occupational, speech, and physical therapy

### Population Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) found 5 carriers of the SNIP1 c.1097A-G mutation among 203 Old Order Amish controls (2.5%). They gave a population-specific allele frequency of 1.48% (6/406).

Ammous et al. (2021) searched an Amish control database of more than 5,000 individuals. The SNIP1 E366G founder mutation had an allele frequency of 0.5% in Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish, and no unaffected individual was homozygous. In gnomAD (v3.1), the variant had an allele frequency of 0.001%: 11 heterozygotes, 10 of them Amish, and no homozygotes.
