### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene (608241) on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures is an autosomal recessive disorder reported in Old Order Amish individuals. It is characterized by neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding, severe global developmental delay with absent speech and inability to walk, intractable seizures beginning in infancy, and abnormal brain imaging. Craniofacial abnormalities include an irregular or 'lumpy' skull surface and, in some patients, craniosynostosis, together with dysmorphic features such as a bulbous nose, wide mouth, and broad jaw (Puffenberger et al., 2012; Ammous et al., 2021).

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described 6 Old Order Amish individuals with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Affected neonates were hypotonic and fed poorly. Dysmorphic features that evolved over time included a bulbous nose, wide mouth and tongue, broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. Affected children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak. Examination typically revealed a subdued child with strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. MRI showed ventriculomegaly, thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities, and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular contour of the skull. Seizures typically began by 6 months of age, with multifocal spike-wave discharges from central, occipital, and temporal regions. Seizures could be focal or generalized and could appear as dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, or generalized tonic-clonic events, and a single patient could have several intractable seizure types. Some patients had subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, or hydrocele. ⚠︎

Ammous et al. (2021) evaluated 35 of 51 identified affected individuals (19 males and 16 females, aged 1 month to 26 years) from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families, including new findings for 2 individuals previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). All had severe global developmental delay and were nonverbal, with intellectual disability, hypotonia, and hyporeflexia. Seizures were universal, began in infancy or childhood, were focal and generalized, and were intractable; no antiepileptic drug consistently controlled them. In some infants seizures first presented as apnea or breath-holding spells, and seizures could be triggered by febrile illness, sleep deprivation, and hypoglycemia. Subclinical seizures were documented in 6 individuals. Some patients had developmental regression after status epilepticus, and 2 had permanent hemiparesis after status epilepticus. Neuroimaging was available for 18 individuals, all of whom had abnormalities, including hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, hypomyelination, irregular cortical ribbon, Chiari malformation, absence of the septum pellucidum, hypoplastic optic nerves, and septo-optic dysplasia; 1 individual had a cerebral arteriovenous malformation. Craniofacial features included an abnormal skull shape (irregular surface, craniosynostosis), with cloverleaf skull and multisuture craniosynostosis in 5 individuals, high-arched palate, wide mouth with cupid's bow upper lip, midface hypoplasia, small upturned bulbous nose, and, in some, micrognathia and proptosis; 3 individuals had Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate. Other features in some patients included behavioral problems (irritability, autistic features, ADHD), sleep-wake dysregulation, spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy, upper airway abnormalities (laryngomalacia, apnea, stridor), subglottic stenosis, congenital heart defects (ASD, VSD, aortic coarctation, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, bicuspid aortic valve, PDA), left ventricular noncompaction cardiomyopathy, horizontal nystagmus and/or strabismus, myopia, conductive hearing loss detected by failed newborn hearing screen, feeding difficulties, small size for gestational age, pulmonary aspiration, gastroesophageal reflux, intestinal malrotation, hypothyroidism, hypoglycemia, short hands with tapered fingers, spinal abnormalities (scoliosis, sacral dimple, tethered cord), umbilical and inguinal hernias, and congenital talipes equinovarus; 1 individual had hypoplastic ribs. Of the 51 affected individuals identified in 27 sibships, 12 had died, between 1 week and 11 years of age. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NEDHCS in the Amish families reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012) and Ammous et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Ammous et al. (2021) found the SNIP1 variant in homozygous state in all 35 affected individuals studied, with all parents heterozygous and all unaffected sibs heterozygous or wildtype.

### Mapping

By autozygosity mapping using 10K SNP arrays in 6 samples, Puffenberger et al. (2012) mapped symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia in Old Order Amish patients to a 6.6-Mb shared homozygous block on chromosome 1, between rs2153337 and rs2225251, containing 126 genes.

In 4 affected individuals from 2 Amish nuclear families, Ammous et al. (2021) identified a single shared region of homozygosity, a 1.65-Mb region on chromosome 1p34.3 between SNP markers rs6667450 and rs10889902, containing 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

In Old Order Amish patients with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia, Puffenberger et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. Exome sequencing in 3 affected individuals from 2 sibships revealed 34 homozygous variants in the mapped region, of which only this one was novel. Sibs and parents were sequenced, when available, to confirm segregation. Glu366 is highly conserved, and the substitution lies in the C terminus, where SNIP1 interacts with c-Myc, SMAD1, and SMAD2; PolyPhen-2 predicted it to be probably damaging. In mIMCD3 cells overexpressing mouse Snip1, the wildtype protein was nuclear with a punctate pattern consistent with transcriptional complexes, whereas the mutant corresponding to human E366G (mouse E353G) appeared more aggregated and was unstable on Western blot, with band density about 85% lower than wildtype despite comparable transcript levels.

By whole-exome sequencing in 2 affected Amish individuals, Ammous et al. (2021) identified the homozygous SNIP1 c.1097A-G (E366G) mutation as the only candidate variant in the critical interval. Targeted testing confirmed homozygosity in 35 affected individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families; all parents were heterozygous, and all unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. The variant affects a highly conserved residue near the FHA domain at the C terminus.

### Pathogenesis

Puffenberger et al. (2012) proposed that reduced SNIP1 abundance likely lowers c-Myc activity and increases TGF-beta and NF-kappa-B signaling, pathways that have been linked to abnormal brain, skull, craniofacial, and limb development and to epileptogenesis in mice. ⚠︎

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-transcriptome sequencing on blood from 6 affected individuals and identified 75 significantly upregulated and 109 significantly downregulated genes. The most overrepresented pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. ROBO1 was the most upregulated gene, and 5 dysregulated genes (ROBO1, SOX5, CNTNAP2, PAFAH1B1, and TSNARE1) were associated with seizures. Noting that Snip1-null mice die as embryos, Ammous et al. (2021) suggested that the Amish E366G variant may be unlikely to cause complete loss of function.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) recommended elective gastrostomy tube placement; careful optimization of anticonvulsants to treat infantile apnea and prevent status epilepticus; early EEG in infants with suspected apneic episodes; neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis; monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism; and yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments.

### Population Genetics

Using unlabeled probe melting analysis, Puffenberger et al. (2012) found 5 carriers of the SNIP1 c.1097A-G mutation among 203 Old Order Amish controls (2.5%), giving a population-specific allele frequency of 1.48% (6/406).

Ammous et al. (2021) found that the SNIP1 c.1097A-G founder mutation had an allele frequency of 0.5% in Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish in a control database of more than 5,000 individuals, with no unaffected homozygotes. In total, they identified 51 affected Old Order Amish individuals in 27 sibships.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, CRANIOFACIAL ABNORMALITIES, AND SEIZURES**  
SNIP1, GLU366GLY [c.1097A>G; p.E366G]

In Old Order Amish patients with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia, Puffenberger et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the C terminus. The mutation was identified by exome sequencing of 3 affected individuals from 2 sibships following autozygosity mapping, and segregation was confirmed in sibs and parents when available. It was found in 5 of 203 Old Order Amish controls (allele frequency 1.48%). In transfected cells, the corresponding mouse mutant protein appeared aggregated and was unstable, with about 85% lower band density than wildtype on Western blot.

Ammous et al. (2021) identified the homozygous E366G mutation in 35 affected individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families, including 2 previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). All parents were heterozygous, and all unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. The founder mutation had an allele frequency of 0.5% in Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish, with no unaffected homozygotes.
