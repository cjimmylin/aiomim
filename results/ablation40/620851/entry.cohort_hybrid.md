### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene (620823) on chromosome 12q24.31.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, seizures, and motor delay (Greene et al., 2024). Chen et al. (2024) found moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability in all of the patients they phenotyped in detail. Most patients walked late and most were nonverbal. Nearly all had dysmorphic facial features, and many had abnormal brain MRI findings and visual, gastrointestinal, and skeletal involvement.

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms in 46 participants in the 100,000 Genomes Project who carried RNU4-2 variants and were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Of these 46 cases, 91% had intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay. Several terms were significantly overrepresented compared with other cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality: microcephaly in 57% (vs 18%), drooling in 13% (vs 1%), proportionate short stature in 28% (vs 7%), generalized hypotonia in 39% (vs 13%), seizure in 52% (vs 27%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion in 13% (vs 2%). No terms were significantly underrepresented.

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures, whom they followed for 5 years. The authors provided photographs that showed facial features they considered recognizable for the disorder.

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed phenotypic information on 49 individuals (21 females and 28 males; median age at last evaluation, 10 years; range, 2 to 38 years) with RNU4-2 variants. Of these, 42 had n.64_65insT, 3 had other single-base insertions, and 4 had single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). All had moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability. Of the individuals older than 5 years, 83% were ambulatory, but they walked late (average 3.4 years; range, 17 months to 7.5 years), and some had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Only 3 individuals had fluent speech; some had a few words, and most were nonverbal. Other features included short stature (37 of 49), microcephaly (37 of 48; mostly congenital), intrauterine growth restriction (8 of 45), hypotonia (39 of 45), seizures (37 of 48), behavioral issues (30 of 45), autism spectrum disorder (21 of 44), and sleep issues (15 of 32). Seizure onset varied, with a median of 3 years and a range from the first year of life to 10 years. Seizure types included infantile spasms, focal seizures, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. Brain MRI was abnormal in 41 of 45 individuals. The most frequent findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, delayed myelination, and other nonspecific white matter abnormalities. Visual issues (38 of 48) included optic nerve hypoplasia, cortical blindness, strabismus, and nystagmus. Gastrointestinal problems included constipation (29 of 44), gastroesophageal reflux (21 of 43), and feeding difficulties (32 of 42), with gastrostomy tubes in 13 of 41. Bone and/or skeletal abnormalities (27 of 42) included osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, and hip dysplasia. Fewer individuals had hearing loss (10 of 46), endocrine problems (hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, pituitary abnormalities), or limb, genitourinary, dental, cardiac, or cutaneous concerns. All but 3 individuals had dysmorphic facial features. These varied but included a myopathic face with deep-set eyes (some widely and some narrowly spaced), epicanthus, a wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, and large cupped ears (some posteriorly rotated). Other facial features were full cheeks, a distinctive mouth with full lips and downturned corners, a high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. No significant differences were noted between males and females. ⚠︎

Among 46 Genomics England probands with the n.64_65insT variant, Chen et al. (2024) found significant enrichment, compared with all other probands with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD), for global developmental delay (n = 37), delayed gross motor development (n = 26), microcephaly (n = 26), delayed fine motor development (n = 24), hypotonia (n = 18), short stature (n = 15), drooling (n = 7), and absent speech (n = 6).

### Inheritance

Most RNU4-2 variants in ReNU syndrome occurred de novo (Greene et al., 2024; Schot et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Chen et al. (2024) described the disorder as autosomal dominant. Greene et al. (2024) reported 1 proband who inherited the n.76C>T variant from an affected mother. They also found that the mother of a proband with n.65A>G was likely mosaic for the variant. Chen et al. (2024) reported 1 individual with a maternally inherited n.76C>T variant and 1 individual whose mother appeared mosaic for n.65A>G. They also reported 2 affected sibs who carried n.77_78insT. In all 54 individuals in whom the parental origin of the variant could be determined, the variant was on the maternal allele (Chen et al., 2024).

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) performed a rare-variant genetic association analysis of 41,132 noncoding genes using whole-genome sequencing data from the 100,000 Genomes Project. They compared 5,529 unrelated cases with intellectual disability with 46,401 unrelated controls. RNU4-2 had an extremely strong dominant association (posterior probability of association about 1), much stronger than that of any other noncoding gene. Three variants had a posterior probability of pathogenicity above 0.5. Five more variants within a few base pairs of these were also found only in affected participants, forming 2 contiguous regions (n.62-70 and n.73-79) in which no unaffected participant had a rare variant. These regions lay within a locus markedly depleted of variation in gnomAD. Of the 47 cases with variants in these regions, 37 were de novo, and inheritance was unknown in 10 because parental genotypes were missing. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, occurred de novo in 33 different families. Greene et al. (2024) replicated the findings in 3 further collections: the NIHR BioResource, the 100,000 Genomes Project Pilot, and the UK Genomic Medicine Service. In these collections they found 27 more probands with a rare variant in the 2 regions, of whom 26 were affected. Of the 26 affected probands, 19 had a de novo variant, 6 had a variant of unknown inheritance, and 1 inherited n.76C>T from an affected mother.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 164 trios with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. They identified the recurrent de novo RNU4-2 variant n.64_65insT in a 5-year-old girl whose extensive previous genetic investigations, including a diagnostic genome, had failed to identify a cause.

In the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project, Chen et al. (2024) identified a recurrent single-base insertion in RNU4-2 (n.64_65insT). It had arisen de novo in 38 probands sequenced with their unaffected parents and was found in 8 more probands, in all of whom the detectable inheritance was consistent with de novo occurrence. These 46 individuals made up 0.52% of 8,841 probands with undiagnosed NDD. The variant was absent from 3,408 diagnosed NDD probands, 21,817 non-NDD probands, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and the All of Us dataset, and it was seen in a single UK Biobank individual. Chen et al. (2024) then defined an 18-bp region of RNU4-2 (nucleotides 62-79) that was depleted of variation in population datasets. They identified heterozygous variants in this region in 115 individuals with NDD, 61 from Genomics England and 54 from other cohorts. Sequencing data for both parents were available for 86 of the 115 individuals, and these confirmed that the variants had arisen de novo. Most of the 115 individuals (89; 77.4%) had n.64_65insT, and a second insertion, n.77_78insT, was seen in 6 individuals, 2 of whom were affected sibs. Single-base insertions in the region were strongly enriched in undiagnosed NDD probands compared with UK Biobank participants (OR = 1,531). SNVs across the region were modestly enriched (OR = 9.51), and those found in patients clustered at the 2 ends of the critical region. SNVs in the central portion, particularly at nucleotides 71 to 74, were also found in non-NDD individuals and population controls. Chen et al. (2024) also tested other brain-expressed snRNA genes, and none was significantly enriched for de novo or recessive variants in undiagnosed NDD probands.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) found the n.76C>T variant de novo in 4 families and inherited from an affected mother in 1 family. It was the only variant among the pedigrees that was present in gnomAD (allele count of 1 in 152,108). They suggested that n.76C>T may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases than the other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. The n.76C>G variant was found in 1 person without a neurodevelopmental abnormality, which further suggested to the authors that some changes at position 76 may have a limited or benign effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that children with SNVs had fewer reports of severe global developmental delay than those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40), and 2 of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV. The authors suggested that SNVs may cause a milder phenotype but noted that this observation rested on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals and needed confirmation in larger cohorts.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) noted that one variant region maps to a quasi-pseudoknot interaction between U4 and U6 snRNAs and the other to U4-U6 stem III. The predominant variant n.64_65insT could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the quasi-pseudoknot. They proposed that delayed U4-U6 unwinding, similar to that observed in WBP4 deficiency, might be the underlying mechanism.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that the critical region maps to the T-loop (quasi-pseudoknot) and stem III of the U4/U6 duplex. This region is loaded into the active site of the BRR2 helicase and is thought to position the U6 ACAGAGA sequence to receive the 5-prime splice site during spliceosome activation. In blood RNA-seq from 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants, the most pronounced difference from controls was increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites (mean 8.8 events vs 0.7 in controls). Twelve abnormal splicing events were shared by 2 or more individuals with RNU4-2 variants, and none of these occurred in 378 controls. The 5-prime splice sites with increased use showed more T at position +3 and more C at positions +4 and +5. These positions pair directly with U6 ACAGAGA, which the authors considered functional evidence that the variants disrupt accurate splice-site recognition. Chen et al. (2024) also found that RNU4-2 is consistently expressed at higher levels than RNU4-1 in the developing human prefrontal cortex, with greater chromatin accessibility in prenatal cortex. This supported RNU4-2 as the main U4 transcript in the brain.

### Diagnosis

Schot et al. (2024) suggested that the recognizable facial features might help prioritize unexplained individuals for targeted investigation of RNU4-2.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 is not directly captured by exome sequencing. Among 22 individuals with n.64_65insT who also had exome data in the DDD cohort, 2 had a single read at the variant position, which matched the reference sequence, and the other 20 had no reads mapping to RNU4-2. The authors concluded that detection from exome data has very low sensitivity and that any variant found this way needs independent confirmation.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 variants in 46 of 9,112 cases (0.50%) coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality in the 100,000 Genomes Project, making it the most prevalent cause of neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 was the second most prevalent cause (21 of 5,527 cases, 0.38%), after MECP2 (24 cases).

Chen et al. (2024) found variants in the 18-bp critical region in 60 of 8,841 (0.68%) genetically undiagnosed NDD probands in Genomics England, compared with 39 of 490,132 (0.008%) UK Biobank participants. Assuming a 40% diagnostic yield before recruitment, they estimated that RNU4-2 variants explain about 0.4% of all NDD. The rate was much lower in autism spectrum disorder cohorts (3 of 7,149). Maternal age was not associated with n.64_65insT, and the authors proposed that the high recurrence of this insertion could reflect germline selection or a high mutation rate.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, n.64_65T [n.64_65inst; n.64_65insT; NR_003137.2(RNU4-2):n.64_65insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291839:T:TA]

In the 100,000 Genomes Project, Greene et al. (2024) found that the most common RNU4-2 variant in patients with intellectual disability or neurodevelopmental abnormality was a heterozygous 1-bp insertion (n.64_65insT), which occurred de novo in 33 different families. The authors noted that G64 of U4 snRNA is thought to contribute to the stability of the ACAGAGA loop of U6 snRNA and that the insertion could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction supporting the quasi-pseudoknot. ⚠︎

Schot et al. (2024) identified the recurrent n.64_65insT variant de novo by trio whole-genome sequencing reanalysis in a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures.

Chen et al. (2024) identified the heterozygous n.64_65insT variant in 46 probands with undiagnosed NDD in Genomics England, where it arose de novo in 38 trios. Across cohorts it was carried by 89 of 115 individuals (77.4%) with variants in the RNU4-2 critical region. The variant was absent from gnomAD v4.0 and All of Us and was present in 1 UK Biobank individual. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in 8 individuals and confirmed its absence from both parents in 7. In 3 families, de novo occurrence was confirmed by both short- and long-read trio sequencing. All 46 individuals with n.64_65insT whose parental origin could be resolved carried the variant on the maternal allele. Blood RNA-seq from 3 carriers contributed to the finding of increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.

**.0002 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, n.76C-T [n.76C>T]

Greene et al. (2024) observed an n.76C-T transition in RNU4-2 de novo in 4 families. In 1 replication family it was inherited from an affected mother. It was the only variant among the pedigrees present in gnomAD (allele count of 1 in 152,108). Greene et al. (2024) predicted that it disrupts Watson-Crick interactions within U4 stem III and suggested that it may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases than other variants.

Chen et al. (2024) reported an individual with NDD who inherited the n.76C-T variant from the mother. They also showed a photograph of an individual with this variant at 22 months of age.

**.0003 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, n.77_78T [n.77_78inst; n.77_78insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291826:T:TA]

Greene et al. (2024) listed a 1-bp insertion (n.77_78insT) in RNU4-2 among the variants found in affected participants and predicted that it disrupts Watson-Crick interactions within U4 stem III.

Chen et al. (2024) identified the recurrent n.77_78insT insertion in 6 individuals with NDD, 2 of whom were affected sibs. The nucleotides at positions 76 to 78 are involved in base pairing with U6 in stem III. Blood RNA-seq from 1 carrier contributed to the finding of increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.

**.0004 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, n.65A-G [n.65A>G; p.N65A]

Greene et al. (2024) found a heterozygous n.65A-G transition in RNU4-2 in a male proband. His mother had a homozygous reference genotype call but was likely mosaic on inspection of whole-genome sequencing reads. The variant was predicted to disrupt the configuration of the U4 quasi-pseudoknot.

Chen et al. (2024) reported an individual with the n.65A-G variant whose mother appeared to be mosaic (54 reference and 8 alternate reads).
