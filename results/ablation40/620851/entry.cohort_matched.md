### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene (620823) on chromosome 12q24.31.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder. Chen et al. (2024) reported that all characterized individuals had moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability. Most walked, but late, and most were nonverbal. Nearly all had dysmorphic facial features, including a myopathic face, deep-set eyes, large cupped ears, full lips with downturned corners, and a large or protruding tongue. The disorder is caused by variants in RNU4-2, which encodes the U4 snRNA of the major spliceosome (Greene et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) identified 47 cases from 47 families in the UK 100,000 Genomes Project (100KGP) who carried rare RNU4-2 variants in the regions n.62-70 and n.73-79. Of 46 cases with phenotype coding, 91% had the term intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay. These frequencies were broadly in line with general term frequencies across intellectual disability cases. Microcephaly was present in 57% of cases, compared with 18% of other cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Other features recorded among the 46 cases included seizures (24 of 46), generalized hypotonia (18 of 46), proportionate short stature (13 of 46), drooling (6 of 46), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion (6 of 46). Compared with other cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality, the terms significantly overrepresented among RNU4-2 cases were microcephaly, drooling, proportionate short stature, generalized hypotonia, seizures, and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion. No terms were significantly underrepresented.

In replication collections (the NIHR BioResource, the 100KGP pilot, and the UK Genomic Medicine Service), Greene et al. (2024) identified a further 27 probands with a rare variant in the 2 regions of interest. Of these, 25 were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality, 1 was coded with abnormal brain morphology terms from which neurodevelopmental abnormality could be inferred, and 1 was unaffected. One NIHR BioResource pedigree was from Belgium. One affected proband inherited the n.76C>T variant from an affected mother. ⚠︎

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. She had recognizable facial features, which were shown in photographs.

Chen et al. (2024) identified 115 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) who carried variants in the RNU4-2 critical region. Of these, 61 were from Genomics England (60 probands and 1 sib) and 54 were from other cohorts. Detailed phenotypic information was collected for 49 individuals (21 females and 28 males; median age 10 years, range 2 to 38 years). Of these, 42 had n.64_65insT, 3 had other single-base insertions, and 4 had SNVs. All 49 had global developmental delay, and all 45 assessed had intellectual disability. All individuals had moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability. Most (83%) walked, but late (average 3.4 years). Speech abnormality was present in 45 of 48 individuals, and 35 of 45 were nonverbal. Abnormal gait was reported in 7 of 30. Other features included hypotonia (39 of 45), seizures (37 of 48), microcephaly (37 of 48), short stature (37 of 49), and intrauterine growth restriction (8 of 45). Behavioral issues were reported in 30 of 45 individuals, autism spectrum disorder in 21 of 44, and sleep issues in 15 of 32. Seizure onset varied, with a median of 3 years and a range from the first year of life to 10 years. Seizure types included infantile spasms, focal and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. Brain MRI was abnormal in 41 of 45 individuals. The most frequent MRI findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, and delayed myelination.

Vision issues were present in 38 of 48 individuals in the Chen et al. (2024) cohort. These included strabismus (23 of 45), nystagmus (18 of 40), optic nerve hypoplasia (8 of 37), and cortical blindness. Hearing loss was present in 10 of 46. Gastrointestinal problems included feeding difficulties (32 of 42), constipation (29 of 44), and gastroesophageal reflux (21 of 43), and 13 of 41 had a gastrostomy tube. Growth problems were reported in 30 of 43 individuals. Endocrine abnormalities, including hypothyroidism and growth hormone deficiency, were reported in 17 of 39. Bone or skeletal abnormalities were reported in 27 of 42 and included osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, and hip dysplasia. The authors also reported limb abnormalities (23 of 42), cutaneous abnormalities (25 of 44), dental abnormalities (17 of 43), genitourinary abnormalities (15 of 43), cardiac abnormalities (5 of 43), and drooling (7 of 46). Dysmorphic facial features were present in 45 of 48 individuals. These included a myopathic face, deep-set eyes, epicanthus, wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares, large cupped ears, full cheeks, full lips, downturned corners of the mouth, high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. No significant differences were noted between males and females. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Heterozygous RNU4-2 mutations cause ReNU syndrome, and the transmission pattern is autosomal dominant. Most mutations arose de novo. In the 100KGP discovery cohort of Greene et al. (2024), 37 of 47 variants were de novo, and inheritance was unknown for the other 10 because parental genotypes were missing. One parent was mosaic for n.65A>G. In the replication collections, 19 of 26 affected probands had acquired the variant de novo, and inheritance was unknown in 6. One proband had inherited n.76C>T from an affected mother.

Chen et al. (2024) had sequencing data for both parents in 86 of their 115 individuals, and all of these variants were confirmed to have arisen de novo. In 1 individual the n.65A>G variant appeared to be mosaic in the mother, and in another the n.76C>T variant was maternally inherited. The n.77_78insT variant was seen in 2 affected sibs. In all 54 individuals in whom parental origin could be determined, the de novo variant was on the maternal allele. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) performed a rare-variant burden analysis of 41,132 noncoding genes in 5,529 unrelated intellectual disability cases and 46,401 controls from the 100,000 Genomes Project. RNU4-2 showed an extremely strong dominant association (posterior probability about 1), much stronger than any other noncoding gene. Three variants had a posterior probability of pathogenicity above 0.5. The disease-associated variants clustered in 2 contiguous regions of RNU4-2 (n.62-70 and n.73-79). No unaffected participant carried a rare variant in these regions, which lay within a locus markedly depleted of variation in gnomAD. Among 47 cases from 47 families, the heterozygous variants included the single-base insertions n.64_65insT, n.64_65insG, n.65_66insT, n.68_69insA, and n.77_78insT and the substitutions n.63T-G, n.65A-G, n.67A-G, and n.76C-T. Of these 47 variants, 37 were de novo. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, arose de novo in 33 different families. In replication collections, Greene et al. (2024) identified 27 further probands with rare variants in the 2 regions, including n.64_65insT and n.76C-T. Of these probands, 25 had neurodevelopmental abnormality, 1 had abnormal brain morphology, and 1 was unaffected. RNU4-2 encodes the U4 snRNA, and before this work none of the 5 snRNAs of the major spliceosome had been implicated in a human disorder. ⚠︎

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed 164 whole genome sequencing trios from individuals with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. They identified a recurrent de novo heterozygous RNU4-2 variant (n.64_65insT; NR_003137.2) in a 5-year-old girl whose diagnostic genome analysis had previously been negative. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) studied probands with undiagnosed NDD in the Genomics England 100,000 genomes project. They found a recurrent single-base insertion in RNU4-2 (n.64_65insT) in 46 of 8,841 such probands (0.52%), and the insertion arose de novo where this could be tested. The variant was absent from 3,408 diagnosed NDD probands, 21,817 non-NDD probands, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and All of Us, and was seen in only 1 UK Biobank individual. Overall, Chen et al. (2024) identified heterozygous variants in an 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 (nucleotides 62-79) in 115 individuals with NDD. Most (77.4%) carried n.64_65insT. Another insertion, n.77_78insT, was seen in 6 individuals, including 2 affected sibs. Other variants included n.64_65insG, n.68_69insA, n.65A-G, n.76C-T, and n.78A-C. Single-base insertions in the region were strongly enriched in undiagnosed NDD probands compared with UK Biobank participants (OR = 1,531), and SNVs at the ends of the region were modestly enriched. In blood RNA-seq from 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants, the authors found significantly more abnormal use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites than in controls. They also tested 27 other snRNA genes expressed in the brain. None of these genes, nor any constrained subregion, was significantly enriched for de novo or recessive variants in undiagnosed NDD probands. RNU12 showed a nominal recessive enrichment that was not significant after multiple-testing correction. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) found the n.76C-T variant de novo in 4 families, inherited from an affected mother in 1 family, and once in gnomAD. They suggested that it may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases than the other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. Because n.76C-G was seen in a person without a neurodevelopmental abnormality, they suggested that some changes at position 76 may have a limited or benign effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that severe global developmental delay was reported less often in children with SNVs than in those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV. The authors suggested that SNVs may cause a milder phenotype but noted that this needs confirmation in larger cohorts.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 encodes the U4 snRNA of the U4 snRNP, one of the 5 snRNPs of the major spliceosome, and that it is ubiquitously expressed across adult tissues. One variant region maps to the U4-U6 quasi-pseudoknot and the other to U4-U6 stem III. The predominant variant, n.64_65insT, could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction. The authors proposed that the disease mechanism might be delayed U4-U6 unwinding, similar to that seen in WBP4 deficiency.

Chen et al. (2024) found that the critical region maps to the T-loop (quasi pseudoknot) and stem III of the U4/U6 duplex. This region is loaded into the BRR2 helicase active site and is thought to position the U6 ACAGAGA sequence to receive the 5-prime splice site during spliceosome activation. In blood RNA-seq from 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants, abnormal use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites was significantly more frequent than in controls (mean 8.8 vs 0.7 events). The sites used more often had more T at position +3 and more C at positions +4 and +5. These positions pair with the U6 ACAGAGA sequence, which indicates disrupted 5-prime splice-site recognition. RNU4-2 lies next to its paralog RNU4-1 on chromosome 12. Both genes are 141 bp long and differ by only 4 nucleotides. RNU4-2 was consistently expressed at higher levels than RNU4-1 in the developing human prefrontal cortex and showed much stronger chromatin accessibility in prenatal prefrontal cortex. These findings support RNU4-2 as the main U4 transcript in the brain.

### Diagnosis

Schot et al. (2024) suggested that the recognizable facial features of the disorder might allow other unexplained individuals with similar features to be prioritized for targeted investigation of RNU4-2. RNU4-2 is a noncoding gene that is not routinely investigated in diagnostic testing.

Chen et al. (2024) found that RNU4-2 is not captured by exome sequencing. Among 22 individuals with n.64_65insT who also had DDD exome data, none had a read showing the variant. The authors concluded that exome-based detection has very low sensitivity and that any variant found this way needs independent confirmation.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 variants in 46 of 9,112 cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality (0.50%) in the 100,000 Genomes Project, making RNU4-2 the most common cause of such abnormality in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 was the second most common cause (21 of 5,527 cases, 0.38%), after MECP2 (24 cases).

Chen et al. (2024) found variants in the 18-bp critical region in 0.68% of genetically undiagnosed NDD probands in Genomics England, compared with 0.008% of UK Biobank participants. Assuming a 40% prior diagnostic yield, they estimated that RNU4-2 variants explain about 0.4% of all NDD. The rate was much lower in autism cohorts (3 of 7,149). Maternal age was not associated with n.64_65insT (mean 30.2 years vs 29.7 years in other NDD probands). The authors proposed that the high recurrence of this insertion might reflect germline selection or a high local mutation rate.
