### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene (620823) on chromosome 12q24.31.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, seizures, and motor delay (Greene et al., 2024). Chen et al. (2024) described moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability in all affected individuals they studied. Most of these individuals were nonverbal and had dysmorphic facial features, and many had abnormal brain MRI findings and involvement of several organ systems. The disorder is caused by heterozygous, mostly de novo, variants in a small central region of RNU4-2. RNU4-2 encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) of the major spliceosome (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms for 46 cases with RNU4-2 variants from the 100,000 Genomes Project (100KGP) who were annotated with the term 'neurodevelopmental abnormality.' Of these cases, 91% had intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay. These rates were broadly in line with term frequencies across intellectual disability cases in general. Compared with other neurodevelopmental cases, several terms were significantly overrepresented among the RNU4-2 cases: microcephaly in 57% (vs 18%), drooling in 13% (vs 1%), proportionate short stature in 28% (vs 7%), generalized hypotonia in 39% (vs 13%), seizures in 52% (vs 27%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion in 13% (vs 2%). No terms were significantly underrepresented.

Schot et al. (2024) described a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures, whom they had followed for 5 years. The authors presented photographs that they considered to show recognizable facial features of the disorder.

Chen et al. (2024) compared 46 individuals carrying the recurrent RNU4-2 n.64_65insT variant in the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project (GEL) with all other neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) probands in GEL (n = 12,203). Several features were significantly enriched among carriers: global developmental delay (n = 37), delayed gross motor development (n = 26), microcephaly (n = 26; odds ratio (OR) = 6.62), delayed fine motor development (n = 24), hypotonia (n = 18), short stature (n = 15), drooling (n = 7; OR = 19.2), and absent speech (n = 6).

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed phenotypic information on 49 individuals (21 females and 28 males) with RNU4-2 variants. The median age at last evaluation was 10 years (range, 2 to 38 years). Forty-two had the n.64_65insT variant, 3 had other single-base insertions, and 4 had single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). All had moderate to severe global developmental delay (severe in 34 of 49) and intellectual disability (45 of 45). Of 36 individuals older than 5 years, 30 (83%) were ambulatory. Walking was achieved at an average age of 3.4 years (range, 17 months to 7.5 years), and some individuals had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Only 3 individuals had fluent speech. Of 45 with a speech abnormality, 35 were nonverbal and 10 had a few words. Other features were intrauterine growth restriction (8 of 45), short stature (37 of 49), microcephaly (37 of 48; congenital in 19 and acquired in 9), hypotonia (39 of 45), seizures (37 of 48), abnormal brain MRI (41 of 45), behavioral issues (30 of 45), autism spectrum disorder (21 of 44), sleep issues (15 of 32), hearing loss (10 of 46), and vision issues (38 of 48). The vision issues included optic nerve hypoplasia (8 of 37), strabismus (23 of 45), and nystagmus (18 of 40). Gastrointestinal features were constipation (29 of 44), gastroesophageal reflux (21 of 43), feeding difficulties (32 of 42), and gastrostomy tube placement (13 of 41).

Chen et al. (2024) reported dysmorphic facial features in 45 of 48 individuals. The features varied but consisted of a myopathic face with deep-set eyes (some widely spaced and some narrowly spaced), epicanthus, wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, large cupped ears (some posteriorly rotated), and full cheeks. They also included a distinctive mouth with full lips and downturned corners, a high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. Seizure onset varied, with a median of 3 years and a range from the first year of life to 10 years of age. Seizure types included infantile spasms, focal seizures, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. The most frequent brain MRI findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, delayed myelination, and other nonspecific white matter abnormalities. Involvement of several organ systems was reported for all individuals. Affected systems included vision (optic nerve hypoplasia, cortical blindness, strabismus, nystagmus), the gastrointestinal tract, and the skeleton (osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, hip dysplasia). In fewer individuals, there were hearing, endocrine (hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, pituitary abnormalities), limb, sleep, genitourinary, dental, cardiac, and cutaneous concerns. No significant differences in presentation were noted between males and females.

### Inheritance

Greene et al. (2024) found that most RNU4-2 variants in their cases had occurred de novo. One case had inherited the n.76C>T variant from an affected mother, and in another family the mother of a proband with the n.65A>G variant appeared to be mosaic. Chen et al. (2024) described the disorder as autosomal dominant, with heterozygous variants confirmed as de novo in 86 of 115 affected individuals. The parental allele of origin could be confidently determined in 54 individuals: 46 with n.64_65insT, 3 with other insertion variants, and 5 with SNVs. In all 54, the variant was on the maternal allele, in contrast to the well-established paternal bias of de novo small mutations. Chen et al. (2024) suggested that this might reflect negative selection in the male germline or imprinting. They found no association with maternal age for individuals with n.64_65insT.

### Heterogeneity

Chen et al. (2024) examined 28 snRNA genes expressed in the brain. Apart from RNU4-2, no snRNA gene or constrained snRNA subregion was significantly enriched for de novo or recessively inherited variants in undiagnosed NDD probands compared with non-NDD probands. The authors noted that these tests had low power.

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) used whole-genome sequencing data from the 100KGP to compare the burden of rare variants in 41,132 noncoding genes between 5,529 unrelated intellectual disability cases and 46,401 unrelated controls. RNU4-2 showed an extremely strong dominant association with intellectual disability (posterior probability of association approximately 1, log Bayes factor = 55). All other noncoding genes had a posterior probability of association below 0.5. Three variants, observed in 34 intellectual disability cases, had a posterior probability of pathogenicity above 0.5. None of these 34 cases had previously been solved by a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant. Eight additional participants outside the intellectual disability class carried one of these variants and had neurodevelopmental phenotypes. Five further variants nearby occurred only in affected participants. Together, the variants formed 2 contiguous regions, n.62-70 and n.73-79, in which no unaffected participant had a rare variant; these regions lay within a locus markedly depleted of variation in gnomAD. Of the 47 cases with a rare allele in these regions, the variant was de novo in 37 and of unknown inheritance in 10. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, occurred de novo in 33 different families. In replication analyses of 3 further UK collections (the NIHR BioResource for Rare Diseases, the 100KGP Pilot Project, and the Genomic Medicine Service), Greene et al. (2024) identified 27 more probands with a rare variant in the 2 regions. Twenty-six of these probands were affected. In the affected probands, the variant was de novo in 19 and of unknown inheritance in 6, and 1 had inherited n.76C>T from an affected mother. This brought the total to 73 unrelated cases.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 164 trios with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. In a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, they identified the recurrent de novo RNU4-2 variant NR_003137.2:n.64_65insT. Extensive previous genetic investigations, including a diagnostic genome, had failed to identify an alternative cause, and the authors concluded that the variant likely explained her phenotype.

Chen et al. (2024) searched genome-sequencing data from GEL and from additional cohorts: the NHS GMS, MSSNG, SSC, GREGoR, the Undiagnosed Diseases Network, and personal communication or Matchmaker Exchange. They identified heterozygous variants in an 18-bp region of RNU4-2 (chr12:120291825-120291842, GRCh38) in 115 individuals with NDD. Sixty-one were from GEL (60 probands and 1 additional sib), and 54 were from the other cohorts. This critical region was depleted of variation in population datasets. Variants in the region were found in 60 of 8,841 (0.68%) genetically undiagnosed GEL NDD probands, compared with 39 of 490,132 (0.008%) UK Biobank individuals (OR = 85.8). Most affected individuals (89 of 115; 77.4%) carried the recurrent single-base insertion n.64_65insT. A second recurrent insertion, n.77_78insT, was seen in 6 individuals, 2 of whom were affected sibs. Single-base insertions in the region were found in 54 of 8,841 undiagnosed GEL NDD probands, compared with 2 of 490,132 UK Biobank individuals (OR = 1,531). Fifteen individuals had SNVs, 10 of them confirmed de novo, and all had phenotypes consistent with those of individuals with insertions. These SNVs clustered at the 2 ends of the critical region. SNVs in the central portion, particularly at nucleotides 71 to 74, were also observed in non-NDD individuals and population controls.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) observed the n.76C>T variant as a de novo mutation in 4 families and as inherited from an affected mother in 1 family. It was the only variant among the 74 pedigrees that was present in gnomAD (allele count 1 in 152,108). The authors suggested that n.76C>T may sometimes cause a less severe phenotype than the other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. The n.76C>G variant was observed in a case without neurodevelopmental abnormality, further suggesting that certain changes at position 76 may have a limited or benign effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that children with SNVs had fewer reports of severe global developmental delay than those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV, and fewer organ systems were reported as involved in individuals with SNVs. The authors suggested that SNVs may result in a milder phenotype, but noted that this observation was based on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed published secondary-structure data for U4 snRNA. They found that one variant region maps to a quasi-pseudoknot interaction between U4 and U6 and the other maps to the U4-U6 stem III interaction. They predicted that the variants disrupt Watson-Crick interactions within stem III or the configuration of the quasi-pseudoknot, destabilizing U4 interactions with U6 snRNA and with RNA-binding proteins. The G at position 64 of U4 is thought to contribute to the stability of the U6 ACAGAGA loop, and n.64_65insT could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in WBP4 cause a strikingly similar syndrome, and WBP4 delays U4-U6 unwinding by SNRNP200. On this basis, Greene et al. (2024) suggested that a delay in U4-U6 unwinding might be the underlying disease mechanism.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that the 18-bp critical region (nucleotides 62-79) is loaded into the active site of the BRR2 helicase, encoded by SNRNP200, which unwinds the U4/U6 duplex during spliceosome activation. The n.64_65insT variant lies within the T-loop ('quasi pseudoknot'), and n.77_78insT lies in the region that base-pairs with U6 in stem III. Both regions are thought to stabilize U4/U6 pairing and to position the U6 ACAGAGA sequence to receive the 5-prime splice site. The authors performed RNA sequencing of blood from 5 GEL individuals: 3 with n.64_65insT, 1 with n.77_78insT, and 1 with n.78A>C. These individuals showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites (mean 8.8 events, compared with 0.7 in both unmatched and matched controls). Twelve abnormal splicing events were shared by 2 or more affected individuals and were absent from all 378 controls; 11 of the 12 involved increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites. Positions +3, +4, and +5 of the 5-prime splice sites pair with the U6 ACAGAGA sequence. At the sites with increased use, these positions shifted away from consensus, which the authors considered functional evidence that the variants disrupt accurate splice-site recognition during spliceosome activation. Chen et al. (2024) also showed that RNU4-2 is expressed at consistently higher levels than its homolog RNU4-1 in the developing human prefrontal cortex, with greater chromatin accessibility around RNU4-2. They concluded that RNU4-2 is the main U4 transcript in the brain.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 is not included in commercially available clinical exome capture. Twenty-two individuals with the n.64_65insT variant also had exome sequencing data in the Deciphering Developmental Disorders cohort. Only 2 of them had any read at the variant position, and in both cases the read matched the reference sequence. The authors concluded that identifying RNU4-2 variants from exome data has very low sensitivity and that any variants detected in this way need independent confirmation.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 cases in 46 of 9,112 (0.50%) neurodevelopmental abnormality-coded cases in the 100KGP, making RNU4-2 the most prevalent etiology for neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. The UK Genomic Medicine Service prescreens only for aneuploidy, copy-number variants, tandem repeats, and abnormal methylation. In that service, RNU4-2 accounted for 21 of 5,527 cases (0.38%), second only to MECP2 (24 cases).

Chen et al. (2024) assumed a 40% diagnostic yield before their undiagnosed GEL cohort was defined. On this basis, they estimated that variants in the RNU4-2 critical region explain about 0.4% of all individuals with NDD. RNU4-2 variants were much rarer in cohorts recruited primarily for autism spectrum disorder (3 of 7,149; 0.042%). The n.64_65insT variant was absent from gnomAD v4.0 and All of Us and was seen in a single UK Biobank individual. The authors suggested that its high recurrence might reflect a high mutation rate or positive selection in the germline.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 64T [n.64_65inst; n.64_65insT; NR_003137.2:n.64_65insT]

Greene et al. (2024) found the n.64_65insT variant in the RNU4-2 gene to be the most common variant among 100KGP cases with intellectual disability or neurodevelopmental abnormality. It occurred as a de novo mutation in 33 different families. The variant lies in the region of U4 snRNA that forms a quasi-pseudoknot with U6, and the authors noted that it could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction.

Schot et al. (2024) identified a de novo NR_003137.2:n.64_65insT variant in RNU4-2 in a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. The variant was found on reanalysis of whole-genome sequencing data after a negative diagnostic genome.

Chen et al. (2024) identified the heterozygous n.64_65insT variant (GRCh38 chr12:120291839:T:TA) in 89 of 115 (77.4%) individuals with NDD who had variants in the RNU4-2 critical region. In GEL, the variant arose de novo in 38 trio probands, and in 8 additional probands the detectable inheritance was consistent with de novo occurrence. It was absent from 3,408 diagnosed NDD probands, 21,817 non-NDD probands, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and All of Us, and was seen in a single UK Biobank individual. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in 8 individuals. In 46 carriers in whom it could be resolved, the variant was on the maternal allele. RNA sequencing of blood from 3 carriers contributed to the finding of increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.

**.0002 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 65A-G [n.65A>G]

In a male proband (pedigree G38) from the 100KGP, Greene et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous n.65A>G variant in the quasi-pseudoknot region of RNU4-2. His mother had a homozygous reference genotype call, but inspection of the aligned whole-genome sequencing reads indicated that she was likely mosaic for the variant.

Chen et al. (2024) reported an individual in whom the n.65A>G variant appeared to be mosaic in the mother (54 reference and 8 alternate reads).
