### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene (620823) on chromosome 12q24.31.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder. Its main features are moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, seizures and motor delay. Many patients have absent or very limited speech, distinctive facial features, abnormalities on brain MRI, and involvement of other organ systems. The disorder is caused by heterozygous, usually de novo, variants in a short critical region of RNU4-2, which encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) of the major spliceosome (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms in 46 cases from the UK 100,000 Genomes Project (100KGP) who had RNU4-2 variants and were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Intellectual disability (91%), neurodevelopmental delay (91%) and motor delay (61%) occurred at rates broadly similar to those in other intellectual disability cases. Several terms were significantly overrepresented compared with other neurodevelopmental cases: microcephaly (57% vs 18%), drooling (13% vs 1%), proportionate short stature (28% vs 7%), generalized hypotonia (39% vs 13%), seizures (52% vs 27%) and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion (13% vs 2%). No terms were significantly underrepresented.

Schot et al. (2024) described a 5-year-old girl, followed for 5 years, who had severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly and seizures. The authors suggested that the facial features seen in her photographs were recognizable for this novel disorder.

Chen et al. (2024) compared the 46 individuals carrying the RNU4-2 n.64_65insT variant in the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project (GEL) with 12,203 other probands with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD). The variant carriers were significantly enriched for global developmental delay (n = 37), delayed gross motor development (n = 26), microcephaly (n = 26; odds ratio 6.62), delayed fine motor development (n = 24), hypotonia (n = 18), short stature (n = 15), drooling (n = 7; odds ratio 19.2) and absent speech (n = 6).

Chen et al. (2024) also collected detailed phenotypic data on 49 individuals with RNU4-2 variants: 21 females and 28 males, with a median age of 10 years (range 2-38 years). Forty-two carried n.64_65insT, 3 carried other single-base insertions and 4 carried single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). All had moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability. Of those older than 5 years, 83% walked, at an average age of 3.4 years, and some had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Most were nonverbal, and only 3 had fluent speech. Other frequent features were: - hypotonia (87%) - microcephaly (77%, mostly congenital) - short stature (76%) - seizures (77%) - abnormal brain MRI (91%) - vision problems (79%) - feeding difficulties (76%) - constipation (66%) - behavioral problems (67%) - autism spectrum disorder (48%) Forty-five of 48 individuals had dysmorphic facial features. These varied but included a myopathic face with deep-set eyes (some widely and some narrowly spaced), epicanthus, a wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, large cupped ears (some posteriorly rotated), full cheeks, full lips with downturned corners, a high-arched palate and a large or protruding tongue.

In the same cohort, Chen et al. (2024) found that seizures began at a median age of 3 years (range, first year of life to 10 years). Seizure types included infantile spasms, focal, generalized tonic-clonic and febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. The most frequent brain MRI findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, delayed myelination and nonspecific white matter abnormalities. All individuals had involvement of multiple organ systems, including: - visual (cortical blindness) - gastrointestinal - skeletal (osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, hip dysplasia) - endocrine (hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, pituitary abnormalities) Presentation did not differ significantly between males and females.

### Inheritance

ReNU syndrome shows autosomal dominant inheritance. Most cases result from de novo variants (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Schot et al., 2024). Greene et al. (2024) reported 1 proband who inherited the n.76C>T variant from an affected mother. In a separate family, the mother of a proband with n.65A>G was probably mosaic for the variant.

Chen et al. (2024) could confidently resolve the parental allele of origin from nearby variants in 54 individuals: 46 with n.64_65insT, 3 with other insertions and 5 with SNVs. In all 54, the RNU4-2 variant was on the maternal allele, in contrast to the usual paternal bias of de novo mutations. The authors suggested that this could reflect imprinting, or negative selection in the male germline if RNU4-2 has an important role in spermatogenesis. They found no association with maternal age among individuals with n.64_65insT.

### Mapping

Greene et al. (2024) used whole-genome sequencing data from the 100KGP to compare the burden of rare variants in 41,132 noncoding genes between 5,529 unrelated intellectual disability cases and 46,401 unrelated controls, using BeviMed. RNU4-2 showed an extremely strong dominant association with intellectual disability (posterior probability of association about 1, log Bayes factor 55). Every other noncoding gene had a posterior probability of association below 0.5.

### Heterogeneity

Chen et al. (2024) assessed 28 brain-expressed snRNA genes. Apart from RNU4-2, no snRNA gene or constrained snRNA subregion was significantly enriched for de novo or biallelic variants in undiagnosed NDD probands compared with non-NDD probands. The authors noted that these tests had low power. Variants were not enriched in the corresponding central region of the paralog RNU4-1 in GEL.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 100KGP, Greene et al. (2024) found that 3 RNU4-2 variants, carried by 34 intellectual disability cases, had a posterior probability of pathogenicity above 0.5. None of the 34 had previously been solved by a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant, a significant depletion compared with the 20.1% of intellectual disability cases solved overall (P = 4.86 x 10(-4)). The 34 cases were also phenotypically more homogeneous than expected by chance. Eight further participants outside the intellectual disability class carried one of the 3 variants; 7 had the HPO term 'Neurodevelopmental abnormality' and 1 had related ICD10 codes.

Greene et al. (2024) identified 8 rare RNU4-2 variants that occurred only in affected participants. The variants lay in two contiguous regions, n.62-70 and n.73-79, in which no unaffected participant carried a rare variant; these regions sit within a locus markedly depleted of variation in gnomAD. Among the 47 cases with a variant in these regions, the variant was de novo in 37, and its inheritance was unknown in 10 because parental genotypes were missing. The variants map either to the U4 quasi-pseudoknot (n.63T>G, n.64_65insG, n.64_65insT, n.65A>G, n.65_66insT, n.67A>G, n.68_69insA) or to U4 stem III (n.76C>T, n.77_78insT). The authors predicted that they destabilize the interaction of U4 with U6 snRNA. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, had arisen de novo in 33 different families. In pedigree G38, the proband was heterozygous for n.65A>G; the variant was called homozygous reference in his mother, but inspection of the aligned whole-genome reads showed that she was probably mosaic for it.

Greene et al. (2024) replicated their findings in 3 further UK collections: the NIHR BioResource for Rare Diseases, the 100KGP Pilot Project and the Genomic Medicine Service (GMS). These collections contained 27 more probands with a rare variant in the two RNU4-2 regions of interest. Twenty-five were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality, 1 had brain morphology terms from which neurodevelopmental abnormality could be inferred, and 1 was unaffected. Among the 26 affected probands, the variant was de novo in 19 and of unknown inheritance in 6, and 1 had inherited n.76C>T from an affected mother. Including the replication collections, Greene et al. (2024) reported a total of 73 unrelated cases.

Schot et al. (2024) re-analyzed 164 whole-genome sequencing trios from individuals with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. In a girl whose earlier extensive genetic testing, including a diagnostic genome, had been negative, they identified the recurrent de novo RNU4-2 variant n.64_65insT (NR_003137.2). The authors concluded that this variant likely explained her phenotype.

Chen et al. (2024) searched genome sequencing data from GEL and other NDD cohorts (NHS GMS, MSSNG, SSC, GREGoR, UDN, Matchmaker Exchange). They identified heterozygous variants in an 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 (chr12:120291825-120291842; nucleotides 62-79) in 115 individuals with NDD: 61 from GEL (60 probands and 1 sib) and 54 from the other cohorts. Parental sequencing confirmed de novo occurrence in 86 of the 115. The region was depleted of variation in population datasets. Variants in the region were found in 60 of 8,841 (0.68%) undiagnosed GEL NDD probands, compared with 39 of 490,132 (0.008%) UK Biobank individuals (odds ratio 85.8).

The most frequent variant identified by Chen et al. (2024) was the highly recurrent single-base insertion n.64_65insT, which lies within the T-loop structure. In GEL it arose de novo in 38 trio probands. It was found in 8 more probands, in whom the inheritance that could be assessed was consistent with de novo origin. All 46 had undiagnosed NDD, representing 0.52% of 8,841 undiagnosed NDD probands. The variant was absent from 3,408 diagnosed NDD probands, 21,817 non-NDD probands, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0 and All of Us, and was seen in a single UK Biobank individual. Across all cohorts, 89 of the 115 affected individuals (77.4%) carried n.64_65insT. A second recurrent insertion, n.77_78insT, was found in 6 individuals, including 2 affected sibs; it lies in the stem III region that base-pairs with U6 snRNA. Other single-base insertions included n.68_69insA and n.64_65insG. In total, 100 individuals carried one of 6 different single-base insertions in the region. These insertions were strongly enriched in NDD: 54 of 8,841 undiagnosed GEL NDD probands carried one, compared with 2 of 490,132 UK Biobank individuals (odds ratio 1,531).

Chen et al. (2024) also identified SNVs in the critical region in 15 individuals, 10 of whom had confirmed de novo variants; all had phenotypes consistent with those of individuals carrying insertions. The SNVs clustered at the 2 ends of the 18-bp region, whereas SNVs in the central portion (nucleotides 71-74) were observed in non-NDD individuals and population controls. SNVs were modestly enriched in undiagnosed GEL NDD probands (6 of 8,841) compared with the UK Biobank (35 of 490,132; odds ratio 9.51). The reported SNVs included: - n.65A>G, which appeared to be mosaic in 1 mother (54 reference and 8 alternate reads) - n.76C>T, which was maternally inherited in 1 individual - n.78A>C

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) observed the n.76C>T variant as a de novo mutation in 4 families and as inherited from an affected mother in 1 family. It was the only variant among the 74 pedigrees that was present in gnomAD (allele count 1 in 152,108). The authors suggested that n.76C>T may sometimes cause a milder phenotype than the other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. A different change at the same position, n.76C>G, was seen in a person without neurodevelopmental abnormality, further suggesting that some changes at position 76 may have a limited or benign effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that children with RNU4-2 SNVs were less often reported to have severe global developmental delay than those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV, and individuals with SNVs had fewer organ systems involved. The authors suggested that SNVs may cause a milder phenotype, but noted that this observation rested on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 encodes the U4 snRNA component of the U4 snRNP, one of the five snRNPs of the major spliceosome. GTEx RNA-seq data showed that RNU4-2, like many major spliceosome components, is ubiquitously expressed across adult tissues. Before this work, none of the five major-spliceosome snRNAs had been implicated in a human disorder. Using published secondary-structure data, the authors found that one variant region maps to the quasi-pseudoknot interaction between U4 and U6 snRNA and the other maps to the U4-U6 stem III interaction. The G at position 64 of U4 snRNA is thought to help stabilize the ACAGAGA loop of U6 snRNA, and n.64_65insT could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the quasi-pseudoknot. Biallelic loss-of-function variants in WBP4, a spliceosome protein that delays U4-U6 unwinding by SNRNP200, cause a strikingly similar syndrome. The authors therefore suggested that a delay in U4-U6 unwinding might be the underlying disease mechanism.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that the 18-bp critical region of U4 lies between stem I and the 3-prime stem-loops. This region is loaded into the active site of the BRR2 helicase (encoded by SNRNP200), which unwinds the U4/U6 duplex during spliceosome activation. The T-loop ('quasi pseudoknot') containing n.64_65insT and the stem III region containing n.77_78insT are both thought to stabilize U4/U6 pairing and to position the U6 ACAGAGA sequence to receive the 5-prime splice site. The authors suggested that single-base insertions may destabilize the U4/U6 interaction or alter positioning of the ACAGAGA sequence. They performed RNA-seq on blood from 5 GEL individuals: 3 with n.64_65insT, 1 with n.77_78insT and 1 with n.78A>C. Compared with controls, these individuals showed significantly increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites (mean 8.8 vs 0.7 events). Twelve abnormal splicing events were shared by 2 or more affected individuals and absent from all 378 controls, and 11 of these involved unannotated 5-prime splice sites. The sites with increased use showed shifts away from consensus at positions +3, +4 and +5, which pair with U6 ACAGAGA. The authors concluded that RNU4-2 variants systematically disrupt 5-prime splice-site recognition.

Chen et al. (2024) examined bulk RNA-seq of 176 human prefrontal cortex samples spanning development (BrainVar). RNU4-2 was consistently expressed at significantly higher levels than its contiguous, highly homologous paralog RNU4-1, and ATAC-seq of prenatal prefrontal cortex showed much greater chromatin accessibility around RNU4-2. The authors concluded that RNU4-2 is the main U4 transcript in the brain, which could explain why variants in RNU4-2 but not RNU4-1 cause NDD.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 is not captured by clinical exome sequencing. Of 22 individuals with the n.64_65insT variant who also had exome data in the DDD cohort, only 2 had any read at the variant position, and in both cases the read matched the reference sequence. The authors concluded that detecting RNU4-2 variants from exome data has very low sensitivity and that any such variants need independent confirmation. Schot et al. (2024) identified n.64_65insT on re-analysis of whole-genome sequencing data in a patient whose earlier diagnostic genome had been negative.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 cases in 46 of 9,112 (0.50%) cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality in the 100KGP, making RNU4-2 the most prevalent etiology of neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. The UK Genomic Medicine Service prescreens only for aneuploidy, copy number variants, tandem repeats and abnormal methylation. In that service, RNU4-2 accounted for 21 of 5,527 cases (0.38%), making it the second most prevalent etiology after MECP2 (24 cases).

Assuming a 40% prior diagnostic yield, Chen et al. (2024) estimated that variants in the 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 explain about 0.4% of all individuals with NDD. For comparison, ANKRD11 explains 0.68%, the largest proportion explained by a single gene in the DDD cohort. RNU4-2 variants were much rarer in autism spectrum disorder cohorts (3 of 7,149; 0.042%). The authors suggested that the high recurrence of n.64_65insT might reflect germline selection, or a high mutation rate linked to the open chromatin and very high expression of RNU4-2.
