### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene (620823) on chromosome 12q24.31.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder. Chen et al. (2024) found moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability in all affected individuals. Other features were delayed ambulation, speech that was mostly absent, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, seizures, abnormalities on brain MRI, and distinctive facial features.

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) studied 47 unrelated cases from the UK 100,000 Genomes Project who carried rare RNU4-2 variants in 2 regions, n.62-70 and n.73-79. Forty-six of them were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Among these 46, 91% had intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay, which was broadly in line with term frequencies across all intellectual disability cases. Several features were more frequent than in other cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality: microcephaly in 57% (vs 18%), drooling in 13% (vs 1%), proportionate short stature in 28% (vs 7%), and generalized hypotonia in 39% (vs 13%). Seizures were present in 52%, and 6 of the 46 had an abnormality of the upper lip vermilion.

In 3 additional collections (NIHR BioResource, 100,000 Genomes Project Pilot, and the UK Genomic Medicine Service), Greene et al. (2024) found 27 more probands with a rare variant in the 2 regions of interest. One of these collections included a pedigree from Belgium. Of the 27 probands, 25 were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality and 1 was coded with abnormal brain morphology, from which neurodevelopmental abnormality could be inferred. The remaining proband was unaffected.

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. Photographs showed facial features that the authors considered recognizable for this disorder.

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed phenotypic data on 49 individuals (21 female and 28 male) with variants in the RNU4-2 critical region. Their median age was 10 years (range, 2 to 38 years). All 49 had global developmental delay, which was severe in 34. All 45 who were assessed had intellectual disability. Walking was delayed, and 7 of 30 had an abnormal gait. Forty-five of 48 had a speech abnormality, and 35 of 45 were nonverbal. Behavioral issues were reported in 30 of 45, autism spectrum disorder in 21 of 44, and sleep issues in 15 of 32. Thirty-nine of 45 had hypotonia, and 37 of 48 had seizures. Brain MRI was abnormal in 41 of 45; findings included reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, and delayed myelination. Growth was affected in many patients: intrauterine growth restriction in 8 of 45, short stature in 37 of 49, microcephaly in 37 of 48 (congenital in 19 of 37), and growth problems in 30 of 43. Hearing loss was found in 10 of 46. Thirty-eight of 48 had vision issues, including optic nerve hypoplasia (8 of 37), strabismus (23 of 45), nystagmus (18 of 40), and cortical blindness. Gastrointestinal problems included constipation (29 of 44), gastroesophageal reflux (21 of 43), and feeding difficulties (32 of 42); 13 of 41 were fed by gastrostomy tube. Other abnormalities were endocrine (17 of 39), bone or skeletal, including osteopenia and scoliosis (27 of 42), limb (23 of 42), genitourinary (15 of 43), dental (17 of 43), cardiac (5 of 43), and cutaneous (25 of 44). Forty-five of 48 had dysmorphic facial features, among them deep-set eyes, epicanthus, wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares, large cupped ears, full cheeks, downturned corners of the mouth, high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue.

### Inheritance

Most cases of ReNU syndrome are caused by de novo heterozygous mutations, and the disorder shows autosomal dominant transmission. Greene et al. (2024) reported the following inheritance data: - In their discovery set, 37 of 47 variants were de novo, and inheritance was unknown for the remaining 10. - The mother of 1 proband was mosaic for an n.65A-G variant. - Among the replication probands, 19 of 26 affected probands had de novo variants, and inheritance was unknown for 6. - One proband inherited n.76C-T from an affected mother. Chen et al. (2024) reported the following: - An n.77_78insT variant was present in 2 affected sibs. - In 1 individual, the n.65A-G variant appeared to be mosaic in the mother. - In another individual, n.76C-T was maternally inherited.

### Mapping

Greene et al. (2024) performed a rare-variant burden association analysis of 41,132 noncoding genes. The study compared 5,529 unrelated intellectual disability cases with 46,401 controls from the 100,000 Genomes Project. RNU4-2 showed an extremely strong dominant association with intellectual disability (posterior probability of association approximately 1; log Bayes factor 55), far stronger than any other noncoding gene.

### Heterogeneity

Chen et al. (2024) tested 28 snRNA genes that are expressed in the brain. In undiagnosed probands with neurodevelopmental disorders, no other snRNA gene, and no constrained subregion, was significantly enriched for de novo or recessive variants. The authors noted that statistical power was low.

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) identified RNU4-2 through a burden analysis of 100,000 Genomes Project data. The disease-associated heterozygous variants clustered in 2 contiguous regions, n.62-70 and n.73-79. No unaffected participant carried a rare variant in either region, and the locus as a whole was depleted of variation in gnomAD. Of 47 rare variants in 47 cases, 37 were de novo. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, arose de novo in 33 different families. Other variants included n.63T-G, n.64_65insG, n.65A-G, n.65_66insT, n.67A-G, n.68_69insA, n.76C-T, and n.77_78insT. Replication in 3 further collections brought the total to 73 unrelated cases.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed 164 diagnostic whole-genome sequencing trios from individuals with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. In a 5-year-old girl whose case had been unexplained, they identified the recurrent de novo heterozygous RNU4-2 variant n.64_65insT (NR_003137.2). ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) identified heterozygous variants in an 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 (nucleotides 62-79) in 115 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. These comprised 61 individuals from Genomics England (60 probands and 1 sib) and 54 from other cohorts. Eighty-nine of the 115 (77.4%) carried n.64_65insT. Five of the 11 other variants were also single-base insertions, among them n.77_78insT, which was found in 6 individuals; the rest were single-nucleotide variants such as n.65A-G, n.76C-T, and n.78A-C. Most variants were de novo. The n.64_65insT variant arose de novo in 38 Genomics England probands. It was absent from 3,408 diagnosed probands with neurodevelopmental disorders, 21,817 probands without neurodevelopmental disorders, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and All of Us. Single-base insertions in the critical region were strongly enriched in undiagnosed probands with neurodevelopmental disorders compared with UK Biobank (odds ratio, 1,531). In the 54 individuals in whom parental origin could be determined, all de novo variants were on the maternal allele.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) found the n.76C-T variant de novo in 4 families, inherited from an affected mother in 1 family, and once in gnomAD. They suggested that in some cases n.76C-T may cause a less severe phenotype than other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. An n.76C-G variant was found in an individual without neurodevelopmental abnormality, which suggested to the authors that some changes at position 76 may have limited or benign effects.

Chen et al. (2024) observed that severe global developmental delay was reported less often in children with single-nucleotide variants in the critical region than in those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). They suggested that single-nucleotide variants cause a milder phenotype, but noted that this rested on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 encodes the U4 snRNA component of the major spliceosome and that GTEx data show it to be expressed in all adult tissues. The variant regions map to the U4-U6 quasi-pseudoknot and stem III interactions. The authors proposed that n.64_65insT could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the quasi-pseudoknot. On this model, the underlying mechanism might be a delay in U4-U6 unwinding similar to that seen in WBP4 deficiency.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that the 18-bp critical region maps to the T-loop (quasi-pseudoknot) and stem III of the U4/U6 duplex. These structures are loaded into the BRR2 helicase and are thought to stabilize U4/U6 pairing and to position the U6 ACAGAGA sequence to receive the 5-prime splice site. In human prefrontal cortex, RNU4-2 was consistently expressed at higher levels than its homolog RNU4-1 throughout development, and it showed greater chromatin accessibility in prenatal cortex; these findings supported RNU4-2 as the main U4 transcript in the brain. Blood RNA-seq in 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites compared with controls. At these sites, the bases at positions +3, +4, and +5, which pair with U6 ACAGAGA, shifted away from the consensus. The authors concluded that the variants disrupt accurate recognition of the 5-prime splice site during spliceosome activation.

### Diagnosis

Schot et al. (2024) found a recurrent de novo RNU4-2 variant in a previously unexplained case by mining diagnostic whole-genome sequencing trios. They concluded that reanalyzing negative diagnostic genomes for noncoding variants can solve such cases.

Chen et al. (2024) found that exome sequencing does not capture RNU4-2. Twenty-two individuals with n.64_65insT also had exome data from the DDD study, and none had a read supporting the variant, indicating that exome sequencing has very low sensitivity for detecting it.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found that RNU4-2 cases accounted for 0.50% (46 of 9,112) of cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality in the 100,000 Genomes Project, making it the most prevalent etiology in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 accounted for 0.38% (21 of 5,527) of such cases, second only to MECP2 (24 cases).

Chen et al. (2024) estimated that variants in the 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 explain about 0.4% of all individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. Such variants were found in 0.68% of undiagnosed probands with neurodevelopmental disorders in Genomics England, compared with 0.008% of UK Biobank individuals. The rate was much lower in autism cohorts (3 of 7,149).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 64T [n.64_65inst]

Greene et al. (2024) found a heterozygous 1-bp insertion (n.64_65insT) in the RNU4-2 gene in cases with ReNU syndrome from the 100,000 Genomes Project. It was the most common variant, arising de novo in 33 different families. No unaffected participant carried a rare variant in this region.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed diagnostic whole-genome sequencing data and identified the recurrent de novo heterozygous n.64_65insT variant (NR_003137.2) in a 5-year-old girl. She had severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) found n.64_65insT in 89 of 115 individuals (77.4%) with neurodevelopmental disorders who carried variants in the RNU4-2 critical region. The variant arose de novo in 38 Genomics England probands. It was absent from 3,408 diagnosed probands with neurodevelopmental disorders, 21,817 probands without neurodevelopmental disorders, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and All of Us.
