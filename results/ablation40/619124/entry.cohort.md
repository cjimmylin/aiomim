### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive neurologic disorder. Seizures begin in the first months of life, mainly as epileptic spasms, myoclonic seizures, or focal motor seizures, and the EEG often shows a suppression-burst pattern or hypsarrhythmia. Patients have severe developmental delay and intellectual disability with abnormal muscle tone. Some patients also have features outside the central nervous system, including cleft palate, omphalocele, talipes equinovarus, joint contractures, and dysmorphic facial features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with a syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy that began in the neonatal period. In all 11 patients, seizures began in the first 2 months of life, mainly as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. Seven of 8 patients who had neonatal EEGs showed a suppression-burst or burst-attenuation pattern, and the 2 patients first recorded later showed hypsarrhythmia. The authors described increased muscle tone as a core feature, often together with axial hypotonia.

In Family A of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were healthy first cousins of Algerian origin. Their first child, a boy (case 1), had a cleft soft palate. He was first referred at 5 months of age with developmental regression, loss of visual contact, opisthotonus, and axial hypotonia. He had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia. At 6 years of age he could not sit unassisted, had spastic tetraparesis with limited extension of the knees and elbows, and did not communicate verbally. He also had scoliosis and profound intellectual disability, and his brain MRI was normal. His sister (case 2) also had a cleft soft palate. At 2 days of age she was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit with episodes of eye revulsion, cyanosis, and spasm-like movements, and her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern. At 26 months of age she had severe developmental delay, axial hypotonia, no head control, segmental hypertonia with limited extension of the knees, no visual contact, abnormal eye movements, and no verbal communication.

Family B of Chatron et al. (2020) was a consanguineous Egyptian family. In case 3, a girl, a detailed prenatal scan showed an omphalocele containing liver, intestinal atresia, and right talipes equinovarus, and polyhydramnios was also present. She was born at term by cesarean section with a birth weight of 2.900 kg and had an omphalocele and a cleft soft palate. From 2 weeks of age she had seizures in the form of eye twitches and epileptic spasms. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern that evolved to hypsarrhythmia. Her developmental delay was severe. Brain imaging showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum, cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, and enlarged lateral ventricles. Her dysmorphic features were hypertelorism, downslanting palpebral fissures, microretrognathia, low-set, abnormally shaped ears, and neck webbing. She also had multiple skin hemangiomas, limited range of movement in all joints, camptodactyly, rocker-bottom feet, scoliosis, a large clitoris with hypoplastic labia, and spasticity. She died at 2 years of age from failure to thrive and respiratory infection. Her similarly affected brother (case 4) had an omphalocele and bilateral talipes equinovarus at birth. He had seizures from 2 weeks of age (eye twitches and epileptic spasms), arthrogryposis, failure to thrive, global developmental delay, and dysmorphic features similar to his sister's, but no cleft palate. He died at 4 years of age from pulmonary infection and severe growth retardation.

In Family C of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were apparently healthy Turkish first cousins. Their first child, a girl (case 5), developed eyelid myoclonia at 2 weeks of age and, 1 week later, myoclonic seizures of the upper limbs. She later had generalized tonic-clonic seizures and refractory epilepsy. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern and photosensitivity. At 12 years of age she had profound intellectual disability and no speech. She had been generally hypotonic in infancy and later had tetraparesis with increased muscle tone. She also had a cleft palate, left-sided talipes, conductive hearing loss, and recurrent fever episodes without signs of infection, and her brain MRI was normal. Her brother (case 6) had a similar phenotype, with refractory myoclonic, tonic, and tonic-clonic seizures from the first day of life, a cleft palate, a suppression-burst EEG, and a normal brain MRI. His seizures worsened on a ketogenic diet. He died from refractory seizures at 2 years of age, when he had profound intellectual disability and tetraparesis with increased muscle tone.

In Family D of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were healthy Iranian second cousins, and there was a family history of epilepsy and developmental delay. Case 7, a boy and the second child of these parents, had myoclonic seizures within 1 day of birth and an EEG showing dysrhythmia. His seizures evolved into epileptic spasms. He had profound developmental delay and could not walk, and imaging showed prominent inner liquor spaces. His muscle tone was initially reduced, and he later developed spasticity, dystonia, and scoliosis. His dysmorphic features included low-set ears, a broad nasal bridge, mild hypertelorism, and a thin upper lip. Case 8, a girl from the same family, had epileptic spasms and myoclonic seizures at 7 days of age. Her EEG showed burst attenuation at first and scattered sharp waves later. At 15 months of age she could roll onto her sides but could not sit unsupported, and her head circumference was 44 cm (1.2 cm below the 3rd centile). She had axial hypotonia and mild dystonia. A cervical notch seen on brain MRI was considered likely unrelated to the GAD1 variant.

Family E of Chatron et al. (2020) was a consanguineous Turkish family. Case 9, a boy, was referred at 2 months of age for facial dysmorphism, developmental delay, cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, and pes equinovarus. Epileptic spasms were seen on examination. In retrospect they had been present from 1 month of age, and his EEG showed hypsarrhythmia. Other findings were flexion contractures of the elbows and knees, upslanting palpebral fissures, hypertelorism, microretrognathia, low-set ears, cervical and axillary webbing, umbilical hernia, hepatomegaly, intrauterine hydrocephalus, an atlantoaxial anomaly, nystagmus, alternating esotropia, bilateral pale optic discs, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, atrial septal defect, nephrocalcinosis, and sparse hair and eyebrows. His audiogram was normal at birth, but he failed a hearing test at 1 year of age. At 15 months of age he had no head control or eye contact, axial hypotonia, and limb spasticity. His older brother (case 10) was born at 29 weeks' gestation with cleft palate, short neck, narrow thorax, and pes equinovarus. He had myoclonic seizures and congenital heart disease (a large patent ductus arteriosus, a small atrial septal defect, and moderate pulmonary hypertension), as well as abdominal distension. He died on postnatal day 9 from sepsis.

In Family F of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were healthy Brazilian first cousins of Caucasian and African ancestry. Their first child, a girl (case 11), had fast facial twitches with blinking followed by staring from 7 days of age. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern with multifocal discharges. She later had focal seizures and epileptic spasms. Brain imaging showed mild cerebral atrophy with ventricular dilation. She had profound developmental delay and intellectual disability without speech, choreoathetosis, and lower-limb spasticity with brisk reflexes and a Babinski sign. At 6 years of age she weighed 17 kg and had an occipitofrontal circumference of 50 cm with positional plagiocephaly, as well as inverted nipples, hirsutism, and severe scoliosis. She had no dysmorphic facial features.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients from 6 unrelated families with a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures. The patients had severe developmental delay and reduced muscle tone, and some had variable features outside the central nervous system, including skeletal abnormalities, dysmorphic features, and cleft palate. Three of the 6 patients became seizure-free, but all still had severe intellectual disability. The authors suggested that GAD1 defects affect development and cognition independently of seizure control.

In Family A of Neuray et al. (2020), a Persian girl born to consanguineous parents had seizure onset at 2 months of age, with focal seizures with impaired awareness and a burst-suppression EEG at onset. At 10 years and 3 months of age she had global developmental delay, moderate intellectual disability, high myopia, mild hypotonia, and dysmorphic facial features including thick eyebrows and protruding ears. She also had clinodactyly, pes planus, scoliosis, hydronephrosis, nephrocalcinosis, and bilateral kidney stones. Her brain MRI was normal. In Family B, a Pakistani boy born to consanguineous parents had seizure onset at 6 months of age. His seizures were focal motor seizures with impaired awareness and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, with oral automatisms, and his EEG showed multifocal and generalized epileptogenic activity. At 7 years of age he had delayed milestones, severe intellectual disability, slight dysmorphic features (wide mouth, thin upper lip, bitemporal narrowing, and retrognathia), arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, brisk deep tendon reflexes, and stereotypic hand movements. Brain MRI showed a prominent ventricular space. ⚠︎

In Family C of Neuray et al. (2020), an African American boy had seizure onset at 2 months of age. He had focal seizures with impaired awareness and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, and his epilepsy was refractory. His EEG showed burst suppression at onset and later slowing with multifocal epileptic discharges. At 22 months of age he had no head control, could not sit, had no speech, and had severe intellectual disability. He also had mild hypotonia, spasticity of the lower extremities, and oropharyngeal dysphagia requiring a nasogastric tube. His brain MRI was normal. In Family D, a Sudanese boy born to consanguineous parents had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia from 2 months of age. At 18 months of age he had severe developmental delay, poor head control, inability to sit, and severe intellectual disability. He was severely hypotonic and had dysphagia related to a floppy epiglottis. His severe dysmorphic features included infraorbital creases, a severely depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, and prominent nasolabial folds, and he also had short arms and significant diastasis recti. His brain MRI was normal.

In Family E of Neuray et al. (2020), an Egyptian girl born to consanguineous parents had seizure onset at 6 months of age, with refractory bilateral tonic-clonic seizures and generalized epileptogenic EEG activity. At 4 years of age she was bedridden, had severe delay in all milestones and severe intellectual disability, and needed a nasogastric tube intermittently. She also had moderate hearing impairment, dysmorphic facial features, cleft palate, congenital bilateral hip dislocation with shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus, severe hypotonia, hyporeflexia, and dysphagia. Brain imaging showed moderate global brain atrophy. A similarly affected sib had died without genetic testing. In Family F, a Turkish child born to consanguineous parents had seizure onset at 2 months of age, with focal to bilateral motor seizures with impaired awareness and hypsarrhythmia. At 3 years of age the child had severe delay in all milestones, with no sitting or crawling, and severe intellectual disability, as well as high myopia and severe hypotonia. The brain MRI was normal.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 in the families reported by Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Most families were consanguineous, and affected individuals carried homozygous or compound heterozygous GAD1 variants that segregated with the disorder. Chatron et al. (2020) noted that the GAD1 pRec score of 0.934 in gnomAD was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance of loss-of-function variants.

### Molecular Genetics

In 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified biallelic GAD1 variants, and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. The variants were as follows: a homozygous splice site variant (c.1414-1G-C) in Family A; a homozygous in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA; lys232del) in Family B; a homozygous 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG) causing a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9) in Family C; a homozygous nonsense variant (c.1591C-T; R531X) in Families D and E; and a homozygous missense variant (c.1525G-A; E509K) in Family F. Four of the 6 families carried homozygous truncating variants, which the authors considered supportive of a complete loss-of-function mechanism.

Chatron et al. (2020) also tested some of the variants functionally or by modeling. In Family A, cDNA analysis showed that the c.1414-1G-C variant abolished normal splicing: there was no normal mRNA product, and 3 aberrant isoforms were present (skipping of exon 15, retention of 76 bases of intron 14, or deletion of the first 7 bases of exon 15). In a cell-free expression system, the lys232del GAD67 variant from Family B was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this result did not exclude a splicing effect predicted in silico. They considered the variant pathogenic because the family reproduced the cleft palate and omphalocele of the knockout mouse, although loss of function was not formally demonstrated. Structural modeling predicted that the E509K variant, which affects a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain, would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389 and would no longer fit into the domain core.

In 6 patients from 6 unrelated families with DEE89, Neuray et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify biallelic GAD1 variants. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation consistent with recessive inheritance. Five families carried homozygous variants: N564S (Family A), F324C (Family B), T347M (Family D), the nonsense variant Y29X (Family E), and the frameshift variant Gln190SerfsTer11 (Family F). The patient in Family C was compound heterozygous for R531X and a frameshift variant (c.670delC; Leu224SerfsTer5). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing. In Family D, the patient's sister was heterozygous for T347M and had a different neurodevelopmental condition without seizures. Four of the variants affected the PLP-binding domain and 4 were null alleles. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability for 2 of the missense variants and disrupted PLP binding for another, findings the authors considered supportive of a loss-of-function mechanism.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) observed phenotypic variability between 2 families carrying the same R531X variant: Family D had no pes equinovarus, joint contractures, or clefting, whereas these features were present in Family E. They suggested that genetic background or environmental factors that modulate movement in utero might explain this difference.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) proposed that the cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures seen in GAD1-related disease result from reduced fetal movements. They suggested that these non-neural features are probably secondary to severe involvement of the central nervous system, as in Gad1-null mice.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a good seizure response to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 treated patients. They cautioned that this response might reflect seizure type, since patients with epileptic spasms seemed to benefit most. They called for validation of vigabatrin as a targeted treatment and for early genomic sequencing in patients with early-onset seizures. In one of their patients, seizures worsened on a ketogenic diet.

Von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported treating a patient with GAD1 deficiency with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet, which they described as a rational therapy.

### Population Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) found the same GAD1 R531X variant in an Iranian family and a Turkish family. Given the relative geographic proximity of the 2 families, they suggested that the variant may lie on a common haplotype.

Neuray et al. (2020) noted that, according to gnomAD, GAD1 is moderately intolerant to missense variants (Z-score 2.32) and to predicted loss-of-function variants (9 observed vs 33.1 expected).

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking Gad67. The mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn mutant mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7% of normal, but the brain had no discernible defects. The authors stated that these findings were the first demonstration of a role for GAD67-derived GABA in the development of nonneural tissue. By contrast, their Gad65-null mice had normal brain GABA content and normal behavior, apart from a slight increase in susceptibility to seizures.

### History

Neuray et al. (2020) questioned whether a homozygous GAD1 S12C variant previously reported in spastic cerebral palsy (Lynex et al., 2004) is pathogenic. They pointed out that pathogenicity predictions for the variant conflict and that other candidate genes in the linked 2q24-q31.1 region were never investigated. They proposed that pathogenic GAD1 variants cause a distinctive neurodevelopmental disorder rather than spastic cerebral palsy.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 sibs (cases 1 and 2) with DEE89, born to first-cousin parents of Algerian origin, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1414-1G-C splice site variant in the GAD1 gene. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Both sibs had a cleft soft palate and early-onset epileptic spasms, with hypsarrhythmia in one and a suppression-burst EEG in the other. cDNA analysis showed no normal mRNA product and 3 aberrant isoforms: skipping of exon 15, retention of 76 bases of intron 14, or deletion of the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, NT695 [c.695_697delaga; p.K232del]

In 2 sibs (cases 3 and 4) from a consanguineous Egyptian family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA) in the GAD1 gene, resulting in deletion of lys232 (lys232del). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Both sibs had an omphalocele, talipes equinovarus, and epileptic spasms from 2 weeks of age, and the sister also had a cleft soft palate. In a cell-free expression system, the mutant GAD67 was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this did not exclude a splicing effect predicted in silico. They considered the variant pathogenic because the family reproduced the cleft palate and omphalocele of the knockout mouse, although loss of function was not formally demonstrated.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, NT812 [c.812_816delttaag; p.V271Dfs; p.V271fs]

In 2 sibs (cases 5 and 6) with DEE89, born to Turkish first-cousin parents, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG) in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Both sibs had refractory epilepsy with myoclonic seizures from the neonatal period, a suppression-burst EEG, profound intellectual disability, tetraparesis with increased muscle tone, and cleft palate.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; g.171715383C>T; p.R531*]

In affected members of 2 consanguineous families with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution. One family was Iranian (Family D, cases 7 and 8) and the other Turkish (Family E, cases 9 and 10), and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. The patients had neonatal-onset myoclonic seizures or epileptic spasms. Pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and clefting were present in Family E but absent in Family D. The authors suggested that the variant may lie on a common haplotype, given the relative geographic proximity of the 2 families.

Neuray et al. (2020) identified the R531X variant in compound heterozygous state with a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC) in an African American boy (Family C) with DEE89. The boy had seizure onset at 2 months of age, refractory epilepsy, and severe intellectual disability. His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.E509K]

In a girl (case 11) with DEE89, born to Brazilian first-cousin parents of Caucasian and African ancestry, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. The residue is highly conserved and lies in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that the substitution would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389 and that the residue would no longer fit into the domain core. The patient had facial twitches from 7 days of age, a suppression-burst EEG, epileptic spasms, and profound developmental delay, but no dysmorphic facial features.

**.0006 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ASN564SER [c.1691A>G; g.171716298A>G; p.N564S]

In a Persian girl (Family A) with DEE89, born to consanguineous parents, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1691A-G transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an asn564-to-ser (N564S) substitution. The variant was found by exome sequencing, and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation. It was reported only once in heterozygous state in gnomAD. The patient had focal seizures with impaired awareness from 2 months of age and moderate intellectual disability.

**.0007 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**
