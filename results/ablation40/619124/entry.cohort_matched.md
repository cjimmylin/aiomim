### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.1.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive syndromic disorder in which seizures begin in the first months of life. The seizures are mainly epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures, and EEG shows a suppression-burst pattern or hypsarrhythmia. Affected individuals have hypotonia and severe developmental impairment. Non-neural features vary between patients and include cleft palate, joint contractures, pes equinovarus, omphalocele, other skeletal abnormalities, and dysmorphic facial features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish, Iranian, and Brazilian origin with a syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. In all patients, seizures began in the first 2 months of life. The seizures were mainly epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures, with a suppression-burst EEG pattern or hypsarrhythmia. Cleft palate, joint contractures, pes equinovarus, and omphalocele were present in some patients but not others.

In family A of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were healthy first cousins of Algerian origin. Their first child (case 1) was a 6-year-old boy. When first referred at 5 months of age, he had developmental regression with loss of visual contact, opisthotonus, and axial hypotonia. He had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia, followed by generalized tonic-clonic seizures after age 3 years, and his epilepsy was drug-resistant. At 6 years he had spastic tetraparesis, limited extension of the knees and elbows, scoliosis, plagiocephaly, and profound intellectual disability. He had no verbal communication and could not sit unassisted. He had a cleft soft palate, and brain MRI was normal. His sister (case 2) was born at 37 weeks' gestation. At 2 days of age she was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for episodes of eye revulsion, cyanosis, and spasm-like movements, and EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern. At 26 months she had severe developmental delay, axial hypotonia, no head control, segmental hypertonia with limited extension of the knees, no visual contact, abnormal eye movements, no verbal communication, and profound intellectual disability. She also had a cleft soft palate. ⚠︎

In family B of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were first cousins of Egyptian origin. Their third child (case 3), a girl, was born after a pregnancy with polyhydramnios. At birth she had an omphalocele and a cleft soft palate, as well as jejunal atresia, right talipes equinovarus, and a branchial fistula. From 2 weeks of age she had epileptic spasms and eye twitches. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern that evolved to hypsarrhythmia. She had severe developmental delay, profound intellectual disability, spasticity, and failure to thrive. Her dysmorphic features included: - plagiocephaly with bitemporal narrowing, forehead nevus flammeus, and facial hirsutism - midface hypoplasia, facial asymmetry, and microretrognathia - bluish sclerae, large corneas, squint, hypertelorism, and downslanting palpebral fissures - a broad depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, and a smooth long philtrum - low-set abnormally shaped ears, a thin upper lip vermilion, delayed tooth eruption, and a high-arched palate She also had multiple skin hemangiomas, a short neck with anterior webbing, and axillary webbing. Movement was limited in all joints, and she had ulnar deviation at the wrists, camptodactyly, adducted thumbs, and rocker-bottom feet. Other findings were a short thorax with hypoplastic, widely spaced nipples, scoliosis, and a large clitoris with hypoplastic labia. Brain MRI showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum, progressive cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, and mildly enlarged lateral ventricles. She died at 2 years of age from a respiratory infection. Her brother (case 4) was similarly affected. At birth he had an omphalocele and bilateral talipes equinovarus, but no cleft palate. He had epileptic spasms and eye twitches with a suppression-burst EEG, arthrogryposis, global developmental delay, profound intellectual disability, hyperreflexia, spasticity, dysmorphic facial features, and failure to thrive. He died at 4 years of age from pulmonary infection and severe growth retardation.

In family C of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were first cousins of Turkish origin. Their first child (case 5) was a 12-year-old girl. Eyelid myoclonia began at 2 weeks of age, and a week later she also had myoclonic seizures of the upper limbs. She later had generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and her epilepsy was refractory. EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern, multifocal spike-wave complexes, and photosensitivity. She had generalized hypotonia in infancy and later developed tetraparesis with increased muscle tone. She had profound intellectual disability, no expressive or receptive speech, and no head control. Other features were a cleft palate, left talipes, conductive hearing loss, and recurrent fever without infection. She had no facial dysmorphism, and brain MRI was normal. Her brother (case 6) had a similar phenotype. From the first day of life he had refractory myoclonic, tonic, and tonic-clonic seizures, and EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern. Like his sister, he had a cleft palate. His seizures worsened on a ketogenic diet. He died from refractory seizures at 2 years of age, when he had profound intellectual disability and tetraparesis with increased muscle tone. His brain MRI was normal.

In family D of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were healthy second cousins from Iran, and there was a family history of epilepsy and developmental delay. Their second child (case 7), a boy, had myoclonic seizures within 1 day of birth, with EEG dysrhythmia, and the seizures evolved into epileptic spasms. His neurodevelopment was profoundly delayed: he was unable to walk and had profound intellectual disability. He was initially hypotonic and later developed spasticity, dystonia, and scoliosis. Brain imaging showed prominent inner liquor spaces. His facial features were low-set ears, a broad nasal bridge, mild hypertelorism, and a thin upper lip. His sister (case 8) was born at 38 weeks' gestation by cesarean section. At 7 days of age she had epileptic spasms and myoclonic seizures, with a burst-attenuation EEG pattern. At 15 months she could roll onto her sides but could not sit unsupported. Her head circumference was 44 cm, 1.2 cm below the 3rd centile. She also had axial hypotonia, mild dystonia, and profound intellectual disability. Neither sib had cleft palate or pes equinovarus.

In family E of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were first cousins of Turkish origin. Their second child (case 9), a boy, had intrauterine hydrocephalus. He was referred at 2 months of age for facial dysmorphism, developmental delay, cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, and pes equinovarus. Epileptic spasms were seen on examination. In retrospect they had been present from 1 month of age, but the parents had not recognized them as seizures. EEG showed hypsarrhythmia. He also had: - facial features: high-arched eyebrows, upslanting palpebral fissures, hypertelorism, a depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, a long philtrum, thin lips, low-set ears, an auricular pit, and microretrognathia - cervical and axillary webbing, flexion contractures of the elbows and knees, ulnar deviation of the wrists, and an atlantoaxial anomaly - rectus diastasis, an umbilical hernia, a palpable liver, and hirsutism of the back - eye findings: nystagmus, alternating esotropia, and bilateral pale optic discs - a failed hearing test at 1 year - recurrent aspiration pneumonia - a small patent foramen ovale and atrial septal defect, and focal septal hypertrophy - grade 1 nephrocalcinosis - length below the 3rd centile, a large anterior fontanel, and sparse hair and eyebrows - neurologic findings: no head control or eye contact, axial hypotonia, limb spasticity, and profound intellectual disability His sib (case 10), a boy, was born at 29 weeks' gestation. He had a cleft palate, short neck, narrow thorax, myoclonic seizures, pes equinovarus, hypertelorism, macrocephaly, short limbs, abdominal distension, and germinal matrix hemorrhage. His heart findings were a large patent ductus arteriosus, a small atrial septal defect, and moderate pulmonary hypertension. He died of sepsis on postnatal day 9.

In family F of Chatron et al. (2020), the parents were healthy first cousins from Brazil of Caucasian and African ancestry. Their first child (case 11) was a 7-year-old girl. At 7 days of age her family noticed fast facial twitches with blinking followed by staring. She went on to have focal seizures, epileptic spasms, and tonic seizures. EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern with multifocal discharges, and imaging showed mild cerebral atrophy and ventricular dilation. She had profound developmental delay and intellectual disability, absent speech, and inability to sit. Neurologic findings were choreoathetotic arm movements, dystonia, spastic lower limbs with brisk reflexes, a Babinski sign, and joint contractures. She also had positional plagiocephaly, inverted nipples, hirsutism, and severe scoliosis. She had no dysmorphic facial features and no cleft palate.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients from 6 families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian, and Turkish ancestry with early-infantile onset epilepsy and developmental delay. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age in all 6 patients and were controlled with medication in 3. All patients had intellectual disability despite seizure control, which suggested to the authors that the genetic defect affected development independently of seizure control. Non-CNS features varied and included skeletal abnormalities, dysmorphic features, and cleft palate.

The patient from family A of Neuray et al. (2020) was a girl from a consanguineous Persian family. Her seizures began at 2 months of age; they were focal seizures with impaired awareness, and EEG showed burst suppression at onset. All her milestones were delayed. She spoke in simple speech at 4 years and walked late, and at 10 years 3 months she had moderate intellectual disability. Other features were high myopia, thick eyebrows, protruding ears, long fingers with clinodactyly, pes planus, scoliosis, and mild hypotonia. She also had hydronephrosis, nephrocalcinosis, and bilateral kidney stones. Brain MRI was normal.

The patient from family B of Neuray et al. (2020) was a boy from a consanguineous Pakistani family. Seizures began at 6 months of age: focal motor seizures with impaired awareness and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. EEG showed multifocal and generalized epileptogenic activity. At 7 years he had global developmental delay and severe intellectual disability. Other findings were arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, brisk deep tendon reflexes, stereotypic hand movements, and oral automatisms. He had slight dysmorphic features (wide mouth, thin upper lip, bitemporal narrowing, and retrognathia). MRI showed a prominent ventricular space.

The patient from family C of Neuray et al. (2020) was a boy from a nonconsanguineous African American family. Seizures began at 2 months of age: focal seizures with impaired awareness and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, which were refractory. EEG showed burst suppression at onset and later slowing with multifocal epileptic discharges. At 22 months he had no head control, could not sit, had no speech, and had severe intellectual disability. He also had mild hypotonia, lower limb spasticity, and oropharyngeal dysphagia requiring a nasogastric tube. Brain MRI was normal.

The patient from family D of Neuray et al. (2020) was a boy from a consanguineous Sudanese family. Epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia began at 2 months of age. At 18 months he had severe global developmental delay, poor head control, inability to sit, and severe intellectual disability. He was severely hypotonic and had dysphagia due to a floppy epiglottis. His dysmorphic features were infraorbital creases, a severely depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, and prominent nasolabial folds. He also had short arms and diastasis recti. Brain MRI was normal.

The patient from family E of Neuray et al. (2020) was a girl from a consanguineous Egyptian family. A similarly affected sib had died without genetic testing. Her seizures began at 6 months of age; they were refractory bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, and EEG showed generalized epileptogenic activity. At 4 years she was bedridden, with severe global developmental delay and severe intellectual disability. She was the only patient in the series born with a cleft palate. She also had dysmorphic facial features, congenital bilateral hip dislocation with shallow acetabula, talipes equinovarus, and moderate hearing impairment. Neurologic findings were severe hypotonia and hyporeflexia, and she had dysphagia with intermittent nasogastric tube dependence. Imaging showed moderate global brain atrophy.

The patient from family F of Neuray et al. (2020) was a child from a consanguineous Turkish family. Seizures began at 2 months of age; they were focal to bilateral motor seizures with impaired awareness. EEG showed hypsarrhythmia and, on follow-up, generalized epileptiform activity. At 3 years the patient had severe delay in all milestones, could not sit or crawl, and had severe intellectual disability. The patient also had severe hypotonia and high myopia. Brain MRI was normal.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 is autosomal recessive. Chatron et al. (2020) found homozygous GAD1 mutations in affected members of 6 consanguineous families, and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease and a recessive mode of inheritance. They also noted that GAD1 has a gnomAD pRec score of 0.934, above the 0.9 threshold for intolerance to biallelic loss-of-function variants, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Neuray et al. (2020) confirmed by Sanger sequencing that the GAD1 variants in their families segregated with the phenotype in a recessive pattern.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) studied 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with DEE89 by whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing. They identified homozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene as the only likely pathogenic variants, and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. The mutations were: - family A (Algerian): a splice site mutation (c.1414-1G-C), predicted in silico to abolish the acceptor splice site of exon 15 - family B (Egyptian): an in-frame 3-bp deletion in exon 7 (c.695_697delAGA), removing a highly conserved lysine (lys232del) - family C (Turkish): a 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG), causing a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9) - families D (Iranian) and E (Turkish): the same nonsense mutation, c.1591C-T, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution - family F (Brazilian): a missense mutation resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution, absent from the gnomAD database In 4 families the mutations were predicted to truncate the protein, which supported a complete loss-of-function mechanism.

Chatron et al. (2020) studied the c.1414-1G-C mutation in cDNA from the heterozygous parents in family A. The mutant allele produced no normal mRNA but gave 3 aberrant isoforms: one skipping exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15. In a cell-free expression system, the lys232del protein was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype, so loss of function could not be formally shown. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing and that in silico tools predicted an effect on splicing. They considered the knockout mouse-like phenotype of cleft palate plus omphalocele in family B strongly supportive of pathogenicity. A structural model of GAD67 predicted that the E509K substitution, at a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain, would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389. The model also predicted that the larger, positively charged lysine side chain would not fit into the domain core.

Neuray et al. (2020) identified biallelic GAD1 mutations in 6 patients from 6 families with DEE89. Five patients were homozygous: - asn564-to-ser (N564S), in a Persian family - phe324-to-cys (F324C), in a Pakistani family - thr347-to-met (T347M), in a Sudanese family - tyr29-to-ter (Y29X), in an Egyptian family - a 1-bp deletion (c.568delC) causing a frameshift (Gln190SerfsTer11), in a Turkish family The Y29X and c.568delC mutations were absent from the population datasets the authors queried. In the Turkish family, both parents were heterozygous carriers. In the Sudanese family, a sister of the proband was heterozygous for T347M; she had a different neurodevelopmental condition without seizures. The sixth patient, an African American boy from a nonconsanguineous family, was compound heterozygous for R531X and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC) causing a frameshift (Leu224SerfsTer5). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the phenotype in a recessive pattern. Four of the 7 variants lay in the PLP-binding domain of GAD67 and 2 in the C-terminal catalytic domain. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability for F324C and N564S and broken hydrogen bonds in the PLP-binding domain for T347M. Together with the 4 null variants, these findings suggested a loss-of-function mechanism.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) observed phenotypic variability between families D and E even though both carried the same R531X mutation. Family D had no pes equinovarus, joint contractures, or cleft palate, whereas the affected members of family E had cleft palate and pes equinovarus. The authors suggested that genetic background, or environmental factors that modulate fetal movement, might explain this difference.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) proposed that the cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures in DEE89 reflect reduced fetal movement, as shown in Gad1-null mice. They suggested that these non-neural features are probably secondary to the severe central nervous system involvement caused by constitutive GABA deficiency. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) reported that seizures responded remarkably well to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 treated patients. They cautioned that the response might reflect seizure type, because patients with epileptic spasms seemed to benefit most. They called for validation of vigabatrin as a targeted treatment and for early genomic sequencing in patients with early-onset seizures. In 1 of their patients (case 6), seizures worsened on a ketogenic diet.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported a patient with GAD1 deficiency who was treated with a rationally chosen therapy of vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet.

### Population Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) found the GAD1 R531X mutation in an Iranian family and a Turkish family. Because the 2 families come from neighboring regions, the authors suggested that the mutation may lie on a shared ancestral haplotype.

Neuray et al. (2020) noted that, according to gnomAD, GAD1 shows moderate intolerance to missense variants (Z = 2.32) and to predicted loss-of-function variants (9 observed vs 33.1 expected).

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking the 67-kDa isoform of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD67). Homozygous null mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn GAD67-null mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%, but the brain showed no discernible defects. The authors concluded that this was the first demonstration of a role for GAD67-derived GABA in the development of nonneural tissue, specifically palate formation. By contrast, their earlier GAD65-null mice had normal brain GABA content and behavior, apart from a slight increase in seizure susceptibility.

### History

Neuray et al. (2020) questioned whether a previously reported homozygous GAD1 ser12-to-cys variant caused spastic cerebral palsy. They noted that predictions of the variant's pathogenicity conflict and that other genes in the 2q24-q31.1 locus had not been investigated. They proposed that pathogenic GAD1 mutations be regarded as the cause of a distinctive neurodevelopmental disorder rather than of spastic cerebral palsy.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

Chatron et al. (2020) studied 2 sibs with DEE89 (cases 1 and 2) born to first-cousin parents of Algerian origin (family A). Both sibs were homozygous for a G-to-C transversion at the acceptor splice site of exon 15 of the GAD1 gene (c.1414-1G-C). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. In silico splicing programs predicted that the mutation abolishes the acceptor splice site. cDNA analysis in the heterozygous parents found no normal mRNA from the mutant allele and 3 aberrant isoforms: one skipping exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delaga; p.K232del]

Chatron et al. (2020) studied 2 sibs with DEE89 (cases 3 and 4) born to first-cousin parents of Egyptian origin (family B). Both sibs were homozygous for a 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA), which removes a highly conserved lysine at position 232 (lys232del). Both had omphalocele, and 1 also had cleft soft palate. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. In a cell-free expression system the mutant protein was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype. The authors noted, however, that this assay bypasses splicing and that in silico tools predicted an effect on splicing. They considered the knockout mouse-like phenotype in this family strongly supportive of pathogenicity.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, NT812 [c.812_816delttaag; p.V271Dfs; p.V271fs]

Chatron et al. (2020) studied 2 sibs with DEE89 (cases 5 and 6) born to first-cousin parents of Turkish origin (family C). Both sibs were homozygous for a 5-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene (c.812_816delTTAAG), resulting in a frameshift and premature termination (Val271AspfsTer9). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Both sibs had refractory myoclonic seizures beginning in the first weeks of life, a suppression-burst EEG, profound intellectual disability, and cleft palate.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; g.171715383C>T; p.R531*]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported this mutation in 2 consanguineous families with DEE89, one Iranian (family D, cases 7 and 8) and one Turkish (family E, cases 9 and 10). The affected members of both families were homozygous for a c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Because the 2 families come from neighboring regions, the authors suggested that the mutation may lie on a shared ancestral haplotype. The phenotype differed between the 2 families: those in family D had no cleft palate or pes equinovarus, whereas those in family E had both.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported an African American boy from a nonconsanguineous family (family C) with DEE89. He was compound heterozygous for the R531X mutation and a 1-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene (c.670delC). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.E509K]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported a 7-year-old Brazilian girl with DEE89 (case 11) whose parents were first cousins (family F). She was homozygous for a c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. The mutation was absent from gnomAD, and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Structural modeling placed the substitution at a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. The model predicted that it would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389, and that the larger, positively charged lysine side chain would not fit into the domain core.
