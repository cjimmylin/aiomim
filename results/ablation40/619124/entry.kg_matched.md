### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.1.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive disorder. Seizures begin in early infancy, often in the first weeks of life, and are mainly epileptic spasms, myoclonic seizures, or focal motor seizures. Early EEG may show suppression-burst or hypsarrhythmia. Patients have severe developmental delay, intellectual disability, and abnormal muscle tone. Some patients also have non-CNS features, including cleft palate, joint contractures or pes equinovarus, omphalocele, skeletal abnormalities, and dysmorphic features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish, Iranian, and Brazilian origin. All had a syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, with seizure onset in the first 2 months of life. All parents were first or second cousins. At onset, seizures were mainly myoclonic seizures (n = 5) or epileptic spasms (n = 6). EEG at onset showed suppression-burst or burst attenuation in 7 of 8 patients recorded in the neonatal period, and hypsarrhythmia in 2 patients first recorded later. All living patients had severe psychomotor delay and profound intellectual disability. Brain MRI was normal in 5 patients. Seven patients had cleft palate, 6 had joint contractures, 5 had pes equinovarus, 4 had scoliosis, and 2 had omphalocele. Three had facial dysmorphic features without clear common elements. Head circumference was normal in all but 1 patient. The authors described markedly increased muscle tone, often with axial hypotonia, as a core feature. Four patients died before 4 years of age.

In Family A of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to Algerian first-cousin parents: a 6-year-old boy and a 2.5-year-old girl. Both had cleft soft palate. The boy was first seen at 5 months with developmental regression, opisthotonus, axial hypotonia, and hypsarrhythmia. He later developed spastic tetraparesis and had no verbal communication. His metabolic screening and brain MRI were normal. The girl had spasms from 2 days of age with a suppression-burst EEG. Her seizures were controlled at 3 months with levetiracetam and vigabatrin. At 26 months she had severe developmental delay and axial hypotonia, with no head control or visual contact.

In Family B of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to Egyptian first-cousin parents. Omphalocele, intestinal atresia, and talipes equinovarus were detected prenatally in the girl. She also had cleft soft palate, multiple dysmorphic features, arthrogryposis with camptodactyly, scoliosis, hemangiomas, and genital anomalies. Her seizures, consisting of eye twitches and epileptic spasms, began at 2 weeks of age. The EEG showed suppression-burst that evolved to hypsarrhythmia, and she became seizure-free on vigabatrin. MRI showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum and progressive atrophy, and she died at 2 years. Her brother had omphalocele, bilateral talipes equinovarus, arthrogryposis, seizures from 2 weeks, and similar dysmorphism. He died at 4 years.

In Family C of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to a Turkish first-cousin couple. A 12-year-old girl had eyelid myoclonia from 2 weeks of age and generalized tonic-clonic seizures from 5 months. Her initial EEG showed suppression-burst, and her epilepsy was refractory. She had profound intellectual disability, tetraparesis with increased muscle tone, cleft palate, talipes, and conductive hearing loss. Her brain MRI was normal. Her brother had refractory myoclonic, tonic, and tonic-clonic seizures from the first day of life, cleft palate, and a suppression-burst EEG. He died from refractory seizures at 2 years. Both grandfathers had adult-onset tonic-clonic seizures, which the authors did not assume were caused by the same genetic defect.

In Family D of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to Iranian second-cousin parents. The boy had myoclonic seizures from day 1 that evolved into epileptic spasms. He had profound developmental delay and could not walk, and he later developed spasticity, dystonia, and scoliosis. His sister had epileptic spasms and myoclonic seizures from 7 days of age, with a burst-attenuation EEG. Her MRI showed only a cervical junction notch, which the authors considered likely unrelated to GAD1. Neither sib had pes equinovarus, joint contractures, or cleft palate.

In Family E of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to Turkish first-cousin parents. The proband had facial dysmorphism, cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, flexion contractures, cervical and axillary webbing, and hypotonia. Epileptic spasms were present from 1 month of age, with hypsarrhythmia on EEG. CT showed minimal hydrocephalus and an atlantoaxial anomaly. He also had nystagmus, pale optic discs, hearing loss, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, and nephrocalcinosis. His older brother was born at 29 weeks with cleft palate, myoclonic seizures, congenital heart disease, and pes equinovarus. He died from sepsis on day 9.

In Family F of Chatron et al. (2020), a 7-year-old Brazilian girl was born to first-cousin parents of Caucasian and African ancestry. Her seizures began at 7 days of age, and her EEG showed multifocal discharges with periods of suppression-burst. The seizures were refractory until vigabatrin was started at 11 months. She had profound developmental delay/intellectual disability, choreoathetotic movements, spasticity with Babinski sign, and severe scoliosis, but no dysmorphic facial features. MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy, with later ventricular dilation.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients (3 female, 3 male) from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian, and Turkish ancestry. Five of the 6 families were consanguineous. All patients were born at term after a normal pregnancy. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures with or without impaired awareness. Two patients also had epileptic spasms, 4 had focal non-motor seizures, and 5 had bilateral motor seizures. Seizures were controlled in 3 patients and drug-resistant in 3. EEG at onset showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Brain MRI was normal except in 2 patients: one had slight ventricular enlargement and the other had moderate global atrophy. ⚠︎

All patients of Neuray et al. (2020) had severe developmental delay. Most never achieved speech or non-verbal communication, and only 1 developed simple speech. Intellectual development did not accelerate even in patients who became seizure-free. Five of the 6 patients had reduced muscle strength or hypotonia. Four could not hold their head up or could not sit or crawl. Two depended on a nasogastric tube because of dysphagia. Four had mild dysmorphic facial features, and only 1 had cleft palate. Other features seen in some but not all patients were hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, congenital hip dislocation, high myopia, and hearing impairment. Metabolic workups were normal.

The patient from family A of Neuray et al. (2020) was a Persian girl whose parents were double first cousins. Seizures began at 2 months, and EEG showed burst suppression. Her seizures were later controlled, and the last one occurred at age 7 years. She had moderate intellectual disability and spoke simply at age 4 years. Other features were high myopia, dysmorphic features (thick eyebrows, protruding ears), scoliosis, clinodactyly, pes planus, mild hypotonia, hydronephrosis, nephrocalcinosis, and bilateral kidney stones.

The Pakistani boy from family B of Neuray et al. (2020) had mild dysmorphic features, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, and severe intellectual disability. The Sudanese boy from family D had severe hypotonia, severe dysmorphic features, diastasis recti, and epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia.

The patient from family E of Neuray et al. (2020) was Egyptian, with first-cousin parents, and was the only patient in their series born with cleft palate. The authors noted that this was of interest because Gad67-null mice die of cleft palate. The same patient also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with a shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus, hearing impairment, severe hypotonia, refractory seizures, and moderate global brain atrophy. A similarly affected sib had died without genetic testing.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 in the families reported by Chatron et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. All affected individuals were born to consanguineous parents, and rare homozygous GAD1 variants segregated with the disease in all families. The GAD1 pRec score in gnomAD (0.934) also fit autosomal recessive inheritance of loss-of-function variants. Neuray et al. (2020) confirmed by Sanger sequencing that the GAD1 variants segregated with the phenotype in their families, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Heterogeneity

Neuray et al. (2020) questioned an earlier report linking a homozygous GAD1 c.35C-G (S12C) variant to spastic cerebral palsy. They pointed out that pathogenicity predictions for this variant conflicted and that the mapped 2q24-q31.1 locus contains other candidate genes. They argued that pathogenic GAD1 variants cause a distinctive neurodevelopmental disorder rather than spastic cerebral palsy.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) identified homozygous GAD1 variants in 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with DEE89 (Families A-F). The variants segregated with the disease in all families.

In Family A of Chatron et al. (2020), exome sequencing of the 2 affected Algerian sibs identified a homozygous splice site mutation in the GAD1 gene (c.1414-1G-C). In silico tools predicted that the mutation abolishes the acceptor splice site of exon 15. cDNA analysis in lymphoblastoid cells from the parents confirmed the absence of normal mRNA product and showed 3 aberrant isoforms: one skipping exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one deleting the first 7 bases of exon 15.

In the 2 affected Egyptian sibs of Family B, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of GAD1 (c.695_697delAGA) that removes a highly conserved lysine (lys232del). The variant was absent from public databases (1000 Genomes, GME, ExAC/gnomAD) and was predicted to disrupt splicing. In a cell-free expression system, however, mutant GAD67 was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing. They considered that the co-occurrence of cleft palate and omphalocele, which recapitulates the knockout mouse, strongly supported pathogenicity, although loss of function could not be formally demonstrated.

In the 2 affected Turkish sibs of Family C, Chatron et al. (2020) used genome sequencing to identify a homozygous 5-bp deletion in GAD1 (c.812_816delTTAAG). The deletion causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9) and segregated with the disease.

Chatron et al. (2020) identified the same homozygous nonsense mutation in GAD1 (c.1591C-T, R531X) in affected members of Family D, of Iranian (Persian) origin, and Family E, of Turkish origin. Because the 2 families came from relatively nearby regions, the authors suggested that they might share a common haplotype.

In the Brazilian girl of Family F, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous missense mutation in GAD1 (c.1525G-A, E509K). The variant was absent from gnomAD and affects an evolutionarily highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that it disrupts salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389. It also predicted that the larger, positively charged lysine would no longer fit into the domain core.

Neuray et al. (2020) identified biallelic GAD1 variants in 6 patients from 6 unrelated families with DEE89. Five families carried homozygous variants, and the sixth patient was compound heterozygous. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variants segregated with the phenotype.

In the Persian patient from family A of Neuray et al. (2020), whose parents were double first cousins, whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous c.1691A-G transition in GAD1, resulting in an asn564-to-ser (N564S) substitution in the C-terminal catalytic domain. The variant had been reported only once in gnomAD, in the heterozygous state, and had a CADD score of 26.1. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability.

In the Pakistani boy from consanguineous family B, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.971T-G transversion in GAD1, resulting in a phe324-to-cys (F324C) substitution in the PLP-binding domain. The variant was present in the heterozygous state in gnomAD (frequency 0.00000796) and had a CADD score of 28. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability.

In the African American boy from family C, Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous GAD1 mutations. One was a c.1591C-T nonsense mutation (R531X), reported only in the heterozygous state in gnomAD, with a CADD score of 43. The other was a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC) causing a frameshift (Leu224SerfsTer5). The mother carried c.670delC, and the father was not available for testing.

In the Sudanese boy from family D, whose parents were second cousins, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1040C-T transition in GAD1, resulting in a thr347-to-met (T347M) substitution. The variant had been reported twice in the heterozygous state in gnomAD and had a CADD score of 29.1. In silico analysis predicted that it breaks H-bonds in the PLP-binding domain. His heterozygous sister had a different neurodevelopmental condition without seizures.

In the Egyptian patient from family E, whose parents were first cousins, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.87C-G nonsense mutation in GAD1 (Y29X). The variant was absent from all population databases queried and had a CADD score of 35. It was predicted to cause nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and complete loss of function.

In the Turkish patient from family F, whose parents were first cousins, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion in GAD1 (c.568delC) causing a frameshift (Gln190SerfsTer11). The variant was absent from gnomAD and other population databases, and both parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) noted phenotypic variability between Families D and E even though both carried the same GAD1 R531X mutation. Pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and clefting were absent in Family D. The authors suggested that the difference might reflect genetic background or environmental factors that modulate fetal movement in utero.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) proposed a loss-of-function mechanism because 4 of their 6 families carried truncating variants. They suggested that the cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures reflect reduced fetal movements, as shown in Gad1-null mice. These features would therefore probably be secondary to the severe CNS involvement caused by GABA deficiency. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) also proposed loss of function as the mechanism. Four of their variants were null variants, the missense variants were predicted to impair PLP binding or catalytic activity, and gnomAD constraint metrics showed that GAD1 is intolerant of loss-of-function variants. They suggested that loss of GAD1 causes a GABA imbalance in the brain that leads to seizures. Because intellectual disability persisted after seizure control, they hypothesized that GAD1 defects affect development independently of the seizures.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a remarkably good seizure response to vigabatrin, a GABA transaminase inhibitor, in 5 of 7 treated patients. They cautioned that the response appeared to depend on seizure type, with patients who had epileptic spasms benefiting in particular. They called for validation of vigabatrin as a targeted treatment and advocated early genomic sequencing in patients with early-onset seizures.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported treating a patient with GAD1 deficiency with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet, which they described as a rational therapy.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) used gene targeting to generate mice lacking the 67-kDa isoform of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD67 -/-). The mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn GAD67 -/- mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%, but the brain showed no discernible defects. The authors concluded that this was the first demonstration of a role for GAD67-derived GABA in the development of nonneural tissue. By contrast, their earlier GAD65 -/- mice had normal brain GABA content and normal behavior apart from a slight increase in seizure susceptibility.

Chatron et al. (2020) noted that the combination of cleft palate and omphalocele in their patients recapitulated the phenotype of Gad1-knockout mice. These mice die at birth with cleft palate and reduced cortical GABA, and about half show omphalocele.
