### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.1.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Seizures begin in the neonatal period or early infancy, often as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures, and the EEG shows suppression-burst or hypsarrhythmia. Patients have severe to profound developmental delay and intellectual disability with abnormal muscle tone. Non-neural features vary between patients and include cleft palate, joint contractures, pes equinovarus, omphalocele, skeletal abnormalities and dysmorphic facial features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish (2 families), Iranian and Brazilian origin. Seizures began in the first 2 months of life in all patients. In the 10 patients whose early history was available, onset was in the first month. At onset, seizures were mainly myoclonic seizures (n = 5) or epileptic spasms (n = 6). Seven of 8 patients who had an EEG during the neonatal period showed a suppression-burst or burst-attenuation pattern. Two patients whose first EEG was recorded later showed hypsarrhythmia. Antiepileptic drugs eventually controlled seizures in 6 patients, and in 5 of these, seizures were controlled for long periods after vigabatrin was added. All living patients had severe psychomotor delay, and intellectual disability was profound in all who could be assessed. Head circumference was normal in all patients but one. The authors described markedly increased muscle tone (spasticity, tetraparesis, segmental hypertonia, dystonia) as a core feature, and patients often also had axial hypotonia. Brain MRI was normal in 5 patients. One patient had a hypoplastic corpus callosum with progressive cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, and another had mild cerebral atrophy with ventricular enlargement at 6 years of age. Among the non-neurologic features, 7 patients had cleft palate, 6 had joint contractures, 5 had pes equinovarus and 4 had scoliosis. Three patients had dysmorphic facial features with no clear shared elements. Four patients died before 4 years of age.

In the Egyptian family reported by Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs had omphalocele, which reproduced the phenotype of the Gad1-null mouse. The sister also had polyhydramnios, jejunal atresia, cleft soft palate and a branchial fistula. She had multiple joint contractures with camptodactyly and rocker-bottom feet, and dysmorphic features that included hypertelorism, downslanting palpebral fissures, microretrognathia, low-set abnormal ears, skin hemangiomas, neck and axillary webbing, and clitoral enlargement. She died at 2 years of age. Her brother had omphalocele, bilateral talipes equinovarus and arthrogryposis, and he died at 4 years of age. In one of the Turkish families, the proband had cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, flexion contractures, neck and axillary webbing and facial dysmorphism, with epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia from 1 month of age. His older brother was born at 29 weeks' gestation with cleft palate, myoclonic seizures, congenital heart disease and pes equinovarus, and he died of sepsis on day 9. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian and Turkish ancestry. The clinical table listed the parents as consanguineous in 5 families. All patients were born at term after a normal pregnancy. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures with or without impaired awareness. Two patients also had epileptic spasms, 4 had focal non-motor seizures and 5 had bilateral motor seizures. The EEG at onset showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Seizures were controlled with medication in 3 patients and were drug-resistant in 3. Brain MRI was normal in all but 2 patients, one with slight ventricular enlargement and one with moderate global atrophy. All patients had severe developmental delay. Intellectual disability was severe in 5 and moderate in 1. Only 1 patient developed simple speech, and this patient remained seizure-free on medication. The other patients remained severely impaired even when their seizures were controlled. Five patients had reduced muscle tone or strength. In 4 this was severe enough to cause limited head control and inability to sit or crawl, and 2 depended on a nasogastric tube because of dysphagia. Four patients had slight dysmorphic facial features, and only 1 had cleft palate. That patient also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus and hearing impairment. Other features without clear common elements included high myopia, hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs and scoliosis. Metabolic workups were normal. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 in the families reported by Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Chatron et al. (2020) found that homozygous GAD1 variants segregated with the disease in 6 consanguineous families. Neuray et al. (2020) confirmed recessive segregation by Sanger sequencing in 6 families.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) performed whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing in 11 patients with DEE89 from 6 consanguineous families. In every family, a rare homozygous GAD1 variant was the only likely pathogenic variant retained. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variants segregated with the disease in each family. In 4 families, the variants were predicted to truncate the protein. These were a splice site mutation (c.1414-1G-C), a 5-bp frameshift deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG), and a nonsense mutation (R531X) shared by an Iranian and a Turkish family. In lymphoblastoid cells from the parents in the family with the splice site variant, cDNA analysis showed no normal mRNA product and 3 aberrant isoforms. An Egyptian family carried an in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA, K232del). In a cell-free system, the K232del protein was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype, but the authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing. A Brazilian family carried a missense mutation (E509K) at a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. The authors noted that the gnomAD pRec score for GAD1 (0.934) was consistent with intolerance to biallelic loss-of-function variants. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) performed whole-exome sequencing in 6 patients with DEE89 from 6 unrelated families and identified biallelic GAD1 variants in all of them. The variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated in a recessive pattern. Five patients were homozygous: 3 for missense variants (F324C, T347M and N564S), 1 for a nonsense variant (Y29X) and 1 for a frameshift variant (c.568delC). An African American patient was compound heterozygous for R531X and a frameshift variant (c.670delC). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing. Four of the 7 variants lay in the pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate (PLP)-binding domain and 2 in the C-terminal catalytic domain. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability for F324C and N564S and broken hydrogen bonds in the PLP-binding domain for T347M. The authors concluded that the findings supported a loss-of-function mechanism.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) observed phenotypic variability between 2 families carrying the same homozygous R531X mutation. Pes equinovarus, joint contractures and cleft palate were absent in the Iranian family but present in the Turkish family. The authors suggested that genetic background or environmental factors that modulate fetal movement might explain the difference.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) noted that in Gad1-null mice, cleft palate and omphalocele result from reduced fetal movement. They proposed that the cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus and joint contractures in their patients likewise reflect reduced fetal movement and are probably secondary to the severe CNS involvement.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a remarkably good seizure response to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 patients treated with the drug. They cautioned that the response may have depended on seizure type, because patients with epileptic spasms appeared to benefit most. They called for vigabatrin to be validated as a targeted treatment and advocated early genomic sequencing in patients with early-onset seizures.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported rational therapy with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet in a patient with GAD1 deficiency.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking GAD67, the 67-kD isoform of glutamic acid decarboxylase. The GAD67-null mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn null mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%, but the brain showed no discernible defects. The authors concluded that GAD67-derived GABA has a role in the development of nonneural tissue.

### History

Neuray et al. (2020) questioned the pathogenicity of a homozygous GAD1 ser12-to-cys (S12C) variant previously reported in a family with spastic cerebral palsy. They noted that pathogenicity predictions for this variant conflict and that other genes in the linked 2q24-q31.1 region had not been investigated. They proposed that GAD1 pathogenic variants be considered the cause of a distinctive neurodevelopmental disorder rather than spastic cerebral palsy.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Algerian family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1414-1G-C transversion in intron 14 of the GAD1 gene. In silico programs predicted that the variant abolishes the acceptor splice site of exon 15. In lymphoblastoid cells from the parents, cDNA analysis showed no normal mRNA product and 3 aberrant isoforms: one skipping exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14 and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15. Both sibs had cleft soft palate. One had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia, and the other had neonatal seizures with a suppression-burst EEG.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delaga; p.K232del; c.695_697delAGA; p.(Lys232del); p.(Lys231del)]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with DEE89, omphalocele, talipes equinovarus, arthrogryposis and dysmorphic features, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA) in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene. The deletion removes a highly conserved lysine (Lys232del; given as Lys231del in the authors' clinical table). The variant was not present in public databases, and in silico tools predicted an effect on splicing. In a cell-free system, the mutant protein was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing, and they considered the knockout-mouse-like phenotype in the family (cleft palate and omphalocele) strongly supportive of pathogenicity. One of the 2 sibs had cleft palate.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, NT812 [c.812_816delttaag; p.V271Dfs; p.V271fs; c.812_816delTTAAG; p.(Val271Aspfs*9)]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Turkish family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG) in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a frameshift and premature termination (Val271AspfsTer9). Both sibs had neonatal-onset refractory epilepsy with a suppression-burst EEG, cleft palate and profound intellectual disability. The younger sib died of refractory seizures at 2 years of age.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; g.171715383C>T; p.R531*; p.(Arg531*)]

In affected members of 2 consanguineous families with DEE89, one Iranian and one Turkish, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution. Given the geographic proximity of the 2 families, the authors suggested that the mutation might lie on a common haplotype. The phenotype differed between the families: pes equinovarus, joint contractures and cleft palate were absent in the Iranian family.

In an African American boy with DEE89, Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: R531X and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.E509K; p.(Glu509Lys)]

In a girl from a consanguineous Brazilian family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. The variant was absent from gnomAD. Structural modeling predicted that it would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318 and lys389, and that the larger lysine side chain would no longer fit into the domain core. The girl had seizures from 7 days of age, a suppression-burst EEG, profound intellectual disability, dystonia and severe scoliosis. She did not have cleft palate.

**.0006 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ASN564SER [c.1691A>G; g.171716298A>G; p.N564S; p.(Asn564Ser)]

In a Persian girl with DEE89, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1691A-G transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an asn564-to-ser (N564S) substitution in the C-terminal domain. The variant was reported once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD, and in silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability. The girl had seizure onset at 2 months, moderate intellectual disability, scoliosis and nephrocalcinosis.

**.0007 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, PHE324CYS [c.971T>G; g.171702542T>G; p.F324C; p.(Phe324Cys)]

In a Pakistani boy with DEE89 born to consanguineous parents, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.971T-G transversion in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a phe324-to-cys (F324C) substitution in the PLP-binding domain. The variant was seen in the heterozygous state in gnomAD (minor allele frequency 0.00000796), and prediction tools rated it damaging. The boy had seizure onset at 6 months, severe intellectual disability and arthrogryposis of the lower limbs.

**.0009 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, THR347MET [c.1040C>T; g.171704223C>T; p.T347M; p.(Thr347Met)]

In a Sudanese boy with DEE89 born to consanguineous parents, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1040C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a thr347-to-met (T347M) substitution in the PLP-binding domain. In silico analysis predicted broken hydrogen bonds in that domain. The variant was reported twice in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. The boy had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia from 2 months of age, severe hypotonia, dysphagia and dysmorphic features. His heterozygous sister had a different neurodevelopmental condition without seizures.

**.0010 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, TYR29TER [c.87C>G; g.171678601C>G; p.Y29*; p.(Tyr29Ter)]

In an Egyptian girl with DEE89 born to first-cousin parents, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.87C-G transversion in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a tyr29-to-ter (Y29X) substitution. The variant was absent from the population databases queried and was predicted to cause nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. The girl had refractory seizures, severe hypotonia, cleft palate, congenital bilateral hip dislocation, talipes equinovarus and hearing impairment. A similarly affected sib died without genetic testing.

GAD1, 1-BP DEL, 568C [c.568delc; g.171693323delc; p.Q190Sfs; p.Q190fs; c.568delC; p.(Gln190SerfsTer11)]

In a Turkish patient with DEE89 born to first-cousin parents, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion (c.568delC) in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a frameshift (Gln190SerfsTer11). The variant was absent from gnomAD, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. The patient had seizures from 2 months of age with hypsarrhythmia, severe hypotonia and severe developmental delay.
