### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene, which encodes the 67-kD isoform of glutamate decarboxylase (GAD67).

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive disorder. Seizures begin in the neonatal period or early infancy, often as epileptic spasms, myoclonic seizures, or focal motor seizures. EEG at onset often shows a suppression-burst pattern or hypsarrhythmia. Patients have severe to profound developmental delay and intellectual disability and abnormal muscle tone, which may include axial hypotonia, spasticity, or dystonia. Some patients also have cleft palate, joint contractures, pes equinovarus, scoliosis, omphalocele, or dysmorphic facial features, and some have died in early childhood (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish (2 families), Iranian, and Brazilian origin with a syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. All seizures began within the first 2 months of life. In the 10 patients with an available early history, seizures began in the first month of life, mainly as epileptic spasms (6 patients) or myoclonic seizures (5 patients). In 7 of the 8 patients recorded during the neonatal period, the first EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern or a variant with interburst attenuation. The 2 patients whose first EEG was recorded later in the disease course had hypsarrhythmia. Seizures were eventually controlled by antiepileptic drugs in 6 patients. All living patients had severe psychomotor delay, and intellectual disability was profound in all who could be assessed. Other neurologic features included axial hypotonia, spasticity, tetraparesis, hyperreflexia, dystonia, and hyperkinetic movements. Head circumference was normal in all but 1 patient. Brain MRI was normal in 5 patients. Abnormal findings included a hypoplastic corpus callosum with progressive cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, a posterior cervical junction notch, an atlantoaxial anomaly with minimal hydrocephalus, and mild cerebral atrophy with ventricular enlargement at 6 years of age. Outside the nervous system, 7 patients had cleft palate, 6 had joint contractures, 5 had pes equinovarus, and 4 had scoliosis. Both affected sibs from an Egyptian family had omphalocele. Three patients had dysmorphic facial features without clear common elements, including hypertelorism, a broad or depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, low-set ears, and microretrognathia. Four patients had died by the time of the report. The Egyptian sibs died at 2 and 4 years of age, with failure to thrive and respiratory or pulmonary infection. A Turkish boy died of refractory seizures at 2 years of age, and a preterm infant born at 29 weeks' gestation died of sepsis at 9 days of age.

Individual patients in the series of Chatron et al. (2020) had additional features. The Egyptian proband had intestinal atresia, a branchial fistula, skin hemangiomas, cervical and axillary webbing, camptodactyly, rocker-bottom feet, and abnormal external genitalia. One Turkish proband had Hirschsprung disease, nystagmus, pale optic disks, a hearing loss that developed after birth, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, and grade 1 nephrocalcinosis. Another Turkish patient had a conductive hearing loss and recurrent episodes of fever. In 1 Turkish family, both grandfathers had tonic-clonic seizures that began at about 40 years of age. Chatron et al. (2020) did not think these seizures were caused by the same genetic defect.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian, and Turkish ancestry with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. In 5 families the parents were consanguineous. All patients were born at term after a normal pregnancy. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures with or without impaired awareness. Two patients also had epileptic spasms, 4 had focal nonmotor seizures, and 5 had bilateral motor seizures. EEG at seizure onset showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Seizures were controlled with medication in 3 patients and were drug-resistant in 3. Developmental delay was severe. Most patients did not achieve speech or nonverbal communication, and only 1 developed simple speech. Intellectual disability was moderate in 1 patient and severe in 5. Five patients had reduced muscle strength. Four could not sit or crawl or had limited head control, and 2 needed nasogastric tube feeding because of dysphagia. Four patients had mild dysmorphic facial features, and only 1 had cleft palate. That patient also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation, talipes equinovarus, and impaired hearing. Other features without clear common elements included high myopia, clinodactyly, scoliosis, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, short arms, diastasis recti, hirsutism, hydronephrosis, nephrocalcinosis, kidney stones, and reduced head circumference. Brain MRI was normal in all but 2 patients: 1 had slight ventricular enlargement and 1 had moderate global atrophy. Metabolic workups were normal. Intellectual disability remained severe even in patients who became seizure-free. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) found that the GAD1 variants segregated with the disorder in the families they studied, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) identified homozygous GAD1 variants in 11 patients with DEE89 from 6 unrelated consanguineous families, which were found through GeneMatcher. The variants were found by whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and segregated with the disorder in each family. Four families carried variants predicted to truncate the protein: a splice-site mutation (c.1414-1G-C), a 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG), and a nonsense mutation (R531X) that was shared by an Iranian family and a Turkish family. In 1 family, cDNA analysis showed that the splice-site mutation resulted in no normal mRNA product. An Egyptian family carried an in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA) removing a conserved lysine. In a cell-free system, the mutant protein was expressed at a level comparable to that of wildtype. A Brazilian family carried a missense mutation (E509K) in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Chatron et al. (2020) proposed a loss-of-function mechanism.

In 6 patients with DEE89 from 6 unrelated families, Neuray et al. (2020) identified homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene. The variants were found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and they segregated with the disorder. Five patients were homozygous: 3 for missense mutations (F324C, T347M, and N564S), 1 for a nonsense mutation (Y29X), and 1 for a 1-bp deletion (c.568delC). An African American patient was compound heterozygous for the R531X mutation and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). The variants were absent from or very rare in gnomAD. In silico analysis predicted that the missense variants would reduce protein stability or disrupt hydrogen bonds in the pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate-binding domain. Neuray et al. (2020) suggested a loss-of-function mechanism.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) noted phenotypic variability between 2 families with the same homozygous R531X mutation. Pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and cleft palate were present in the Turkish family but absent in the Iranian family. They suggested that differences in genetic background or in environmental factors affecting fetal movement might explain this. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) noted that cleft palate and omphalocele in Gad1-null mice result from reduced fetal movement. They suggested that the cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures in their patients were probably secondary to severe central nervous system involvement.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) found that seizures responded well to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 treated patients. In these 5 patients, seizures were controlled for long periods after vigabatrin was added. Vigabatrin inhibits GABA transaminase. Chatron et al. (2020) noted that the good response seemed to depend on seizure type, as patients with epileptic spasms in particular appeared to benefit.

Von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported treatment with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet in a patient with GAD1 deficiency.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) found that mice lacking Gad67 were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate on the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67-null mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%. The brain showed no discernible defects.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Algerian family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous G-to-C transversion (c.1414-1G-C) in the acceptor splice site of exon 15 of the GAD1 gene. The mutation was found by exome sequencing. cDNA analysis using lymphoblastoid cells from the parents showed no normal mRNA product from the mutant allele. Instead there were 3 aberrant isoforms: one lacking exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15. Both sibs had cleft soft palate, epileptic spasms, and profound intellectual disability.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delAGA; p.Lys232del; p.Lys231del]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA) in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene. The deletion removed a highly conserved lysine (lys232del in the text; lys231del in the table). It was found by exome sequencing and was not present in public databases. In silico analysis predicted that it would disrupt splicing. In a cell-free expression system, the mutant protein was expressed at a level comparable to that of wildtype. Chatron et al. (2020) noted that this assay bypasses splicing. Both sibs had omphalocele, epileptic spasms, multiple contractures, and dysmorphic features. Both died, at 2 and 4 years of age.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, NT812 [c.812_816delTTAAG; p.Val271Aspfs*9]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Turkish family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG) in the GAD1 gene. The deletion caused a frameshift and premature termination (Val271AspfsTer9). The mutation was found by whole-genome sequencing. Both sibs had cleft palate, refractory myoclonic and tonic-clonic seizures with a neonatal suppression-burst pattern, and profound intellectual disability. The brother died at 2 years of age.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; p.Arg531*]

In affected members of a consanguineous Iranian family and a consanguineous Turkish family with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disorder in both families. Chatron et al. (2020) suggested that the 2 families might share a common haplotype. Pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and cleft palate were present in the Turkish family but not in the Iranian family.

In an African American boy with DEE89, Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: R531X and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC) predicted to cause a frameshift (Leu224SerfsTer5). The mutations were found by exome sequencing. His mother carried the c.670delC variant, but his father was not available for testing. R531X had been reported once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.Glu509Lys]

In a girl with DEE89 born to consanguineous Brazilian parents, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and was absent from gnomAD. Structural modeling predicted that it would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389.

**.0006 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ASN564SER [c.1691A>G; p.Asn564Ser]

In a Persian girl with DEE89 whose parents were double first cousins, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1691A-G transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an asn564-to-ser (N564S) substitution in the C-terminal domain. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and had been reported once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability. The patient had moderate intellectual disability and developed simple speech, and her seizures were controlled.

**.0007 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, PHE324CYS [c.971T>G; p.Phe324Cys]

In a Pakistani boy with DEE89 born to consanguineous parents, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.971T-G transversion in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a phe324-to-cys (F324C) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and had a minor allele frequency of 0.00000796 in gnomAD. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability.

GAD1, 1-BP DEL, 670C [c.670delC; p.Leu224Serfs*5]

In an African American boy with DEE89, Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC) predicted to cause a frameshift (Leu224SerfsTer5), and R531X. His mother carried the c.670delC variant.

**.0009 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, THR347MET [c.1040C>T; p.Thr347Met]

In a Sudanese boy with DEE89 whose parents were second cousins, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1040C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a thr347-to-met (T347M) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and had been reported twice in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. In silico analysis predicted that it would break hydrogen bonds in the pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate-binding domain. The patient's sister was heterozygous for the variant and had a different neurodevelopmental condition without seizures.

**.0010 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**

In an Egyptian girl with DEE89 whose parents were first cousins, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.87C-G transversion in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a tyr29-to-ter (Y29X) substitution. The mutation was absent from all population datasets queried. This patient had cleft palate, congenital hip dislocation, and refractory seizures.

**.0011 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 1-BP DEL, 568C [c.568delC; p.Gln190SerfsTer11]

In a Turkish patient with DEE89 whose parents were first cousins, Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion (c.568delC) in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a frameshift (Gln190SerfsTer11). The mutation was absent from gnomAD, and both parents were heterozygous carriers.
