### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.1.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Seizures begin in the neonatal period or early infancy and are followed by severe to profound developmental delay and intellectual disability. Chatron et al. (2020) described it as a syndromic disorder in which the encephalopathy is variably associated with cleft palate, joint contractures and/or pes equinovarus, and omphalocele. Neuray et al. (2020) emphasized early-infantile onset epilepsy and hypotonia, with variable non-CNS features such as skeletal abnormalities, dysmorphic features, and cleft palate.

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish (2 families), Iranian, and Brazilian origin. In all 10 patients whose early history was available, seizures began in the first month of life. Seizures at onset were mainly myoclonic seizures (5 patients) or epileptic spasms (6 patients). Seven of the 8 patients with neonatal recordings had a suppression-burst or burst-attenuation EEG pattern at onset, and the 2 patients first recorded later had hypsarrhythmia. Six patients eventually achieved seizure control with antiepileptic drugs. All living patients had severe psychomotor delay, and intellectual disability was profound in every patient who could be assessed. Brain MRI was normal in 5 patients. Head circumference was normal in all but 1 patient. Seven patients had cleft palate, 6 had joint contractures, 5 had pes equinovarus, and 4 had scoliosis. Both patients from one family had omphalocele. Three patients had facial dysmorphic features without clear common elements. Four patients died before 4 years of age. The authors described markedly increased muscle tone as a core clinical feature, although patients often had axial hypotonia.

In family A of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to first-cousin parents of Algerian origin, and both had cleft soft palate. The boy was first seen at 5 months of age with developmental regression, loss of visual contact, opisthotonus, axial hypotonia, and hypsarrhythmia on EEG. Brain MRI and metabolic screening of blood, urine, and CSF were normal. At 6 years of age he could not sit unassisted, had spastic tetraparesis, and had no verbal communication. His sister was admitted at 2 days of age with eye revulsion, cyanosis, and spasm-like movements, and her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern. Her seizures were controlled at 3 months with levetiracetam and vigabatrin. At 26 months she had severe developmental delay, axial hypotonia, no head control, segmental hypertonia, no visual contact, and no verbal communication.

In family B of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to first-cousin Egyptian parents. In the girl, prenatal scanning detected a liver-containing omphalocele, intestinal atresia, and right talipes equinovarus. She also had cleft soft palate, multiple dysmorphic features (including hypertelorism, downslanting palpebral fissures, microretrognathia, and abnormal low-set ears), and limited movement of all joints with camptodactyly. Further findings were scoliosis, skin hemangiomas, and genital anomalies (a large clitoris with hypoplastic labia). Seizures, consisting of eye twitches and epileptic spasms, began at 2 weeks of age. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern that evolved to hypsarrhythmia, and she became seizure-free on vigabatrin at 9 months. Brain MRI showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum and progressive cerebral and cerebellar atrophy. She died at 2 years of age of failure to thrive and respiratory infection. Her brother had omphalocele, bilateral talipes equinovarus, arthrogryposis, failure to thrive, and seizures from 2 weeks of age, with dysmorphism similar to his sister's. He became seizure-free on vigabatrin at 2 years and died at 4 years of age.

In family C of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to a first-cousin Turkish couple. The 12-year-old girl had eyelid myoclonia from 2 weeks of age and generalized tonic-clonic seizures from 5 months. Her initial EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern, and her epilepsy was refractory. She had profound intellectual disability, tetraparesis with increased muscle tone, cleft palate, talipes, and conductive hearing loss. Brain MRI was normal. Her brother had refractory myoclonic, tonic, and tonic-clonic seizures from the first day of life, cleft palate, and a suppression-burst EEG, and he died from refractory seizures at 2 years of age. Both grandfathers had adult-onset tonic-clonic seizures. The authors did not assume that these seizures were caused by the same genetic defect.

In family D of Chatron et al. (2020), 2 sibs were born to second-cousin parents from Iran. The boy had myoclonic seizures from 1 day after birth that evolved into epileptic spasms. He had profound developmental delay, could not walk, and later developed spasticity, dystonia, and scoliosis. His sister had epileptic spasms and myoclonic seizures from 7 days of age, with burst attenuation on EEG. Her MRI showed only a cervical junction notch, which the authors considered likely unrelated to GAD1. Neither sib had pes equinovarus, joint contractures, or cleft palate.

In family E of Chatron et al. (2020), the proband was born to first-cousin parents. His features included facial dysmorphism, cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, flexion contractures of the elbows and knees, cervical and axillary webbing, and hypotonia. Epileptic spasms had been present from 1 month of age, and his EEG showed hypsarrhythmia. Brain CT showed minimal hydrocephalus and an atlantoaxial anomaly. He also had nystagmus, pale optic discs, failed hearing testing at 1 year, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, and grade 1 nephrocalcinosis. His older brother was born at 29 weeks' gestation with cleft palate, myoclonic seizures, congenital heart disease, and pes equinovarus, and died of sepsis on postnatal day 9.

In family F of Chatron et al. (2020), a 7-year-old girl was born to healthy first-cousin Brazilian parents of Caucasian and African ancestry. Seizures began at 7 days of age, and her EEG showed multifocal discharges and periods of suppression-burst. Her seizures were refractory until vigabatrin was started at 11 months of age. She had profound developmental delay/intellectual disability, choreoathetotic arm movements, lower limb spasticity with brisk reflexes and Babinski sign, and severe scoliosis. She had no dysmorphic facial features. MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy at 13 months of age and ventricular dilation at 6 years.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian, and Turkish ancestry. Most were born to consanguineous parents, and all were born at term after a normal pregnancy. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were predominantly focal motor seizures with or without impaired awareness. Two patients also had epileptic spasms, 4 had focal non-motor seizures, and 5 had bilateral motor seizures. Seizures were controlled in 3 patients and drug-resistant in 3. EEG at onset showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Brain MRI was normal except in 2 patients, one with slight ventricular enlargement and one with moderate global atrophy. All patients had severe developmental delay, and intellectual development did not accelerate even after seizure control. Most never achieved speech or non-verbal communication; only 1 developed simple speech. Five patients had reduced muscle strength, and 4 had limited head control or could not sit or crawl. Two patients depended on a nasogastric tube because of dysphagia. Four had mild dysmorphic facial features, and only 1 had cleft palate. Other features without clear common elements were hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, and congenital hip dislocation. Metabolic workups were normal.

Among the individual patients reported by Neuray et al. (2020), the Persian girl (whose parents were double first cousins) had seizure onset at 2 months with burst suppression on EEG. Her last seizure occurred at age 7 years. She had moderate intellectual disability, simple speech at age 4 years, high myopia, thick eyebrows, protruding ears, scoliosis, clinodactyly, pes planus, mild hypotonia, hydronephrosis, nephrocalcinosis, and bilateral kidney stones. The Pakistani boy had mild dysmorphic features, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, brisk deep tendon reflexes, and severe intellectual disability. The Sudanese boy had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia, severe hypotonia, dysphagia, severe dysmorphic features, short arms, and diastasis recti. The Egyptian patient was the only one born with cleft palate. This patient also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with a shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus, hearing impairment, severe hypotonia, refractory seizures, and moderate global brain atrophy, and a similarly affected sib had died without genetic testing.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 in the families reported by Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In both studies, Sanger sequencing confirmed that the biallelic GAD1 variants segregated with the disease. Chatron et al. (2020) also noted that the GAD1 pRec score in gnomAD (0.934) was in line with autosomal recessive inheritance of loss-of-function variants.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) studied 11 patients with DEE89 from 6 consanguineous families using whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing. In all affected individuals, they identified rare homozygous variants in the GAD1 gene, which were retained as the only likely pathogenic variants. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variants segregated with the disease in all families. Four of the 6 families carried variants predicted to truncate the protein: a splice site mutation, a frameshift, and a nonsense mutation (R531X) that was shared by an Iranian and a Turkish family. One family carried an in-frame deletion of a conserved lysine (K232del), and one carried a missense mutation (E509K). In family A, cDNA analysis of parental lymphoblastoid cells showed that the splice site mutation produced no normal mRNA. In family B, the K232del protein was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype in a cell-free expression system.

Neuray et al. (2020) studied 6 patients with DEE89 from 6 unrelated families using whole-exome sequencing, and they identified biallelic GAD1 variants in all affected individuals. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation. Five patients were homozygous: 3 for missense variants (N564S, F324C, and T347M), 1 for a nonsense variant (Y29X), and 1 for a frameshift variant. An African American boy was compound heterozygous for the R531X nonsense variant and a 1-bp deletion. All variants were ultrarare. In silico analysis predicted that the missense variants reduce protein stability or disrupt H-bonds in the pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate (PLP)-binding domain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) noted phenotypic variability between their families D and E, even though both carried the same homozygous R531X mutation. Family D lacked the pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and clefting seen in family E. The authors suggested that the difference might be influenced by genetic background or by environmental factors that modulate movement in utero.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) concluded that a complete loss-of-function mechanism was supported because 4 of their 6 families carried truncating variants. Studies of Gad1-null mice had shown that cleft palate and omphalocele result from reduced fetal movements. On that basis, Chatron et al. (2020) suggested that the cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures in their patients were probably secondary to the severe CNS involvement.

Neuray et al. (2020) also proposed a loss-of-function mechanism. Their evidence was that 4 of the variants were null variants, that the missense variants were predicted to impair PLP binding or catalytic activity, and that gnomAD constraint metrics showed intolerance of GAD1 to loss-of-function variants. Because severe intellectual disability persisted in their patients despite seizure control, they hypothesized that the genetic defect affects development independently of the seizures.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a remarkably good seizure response to vigabatrin, an inhibitor of GABA transaminase, in 5 of the 7 patients treated with the drug. They cautioned that the response seemed to be driven by seizure type, because patients with epileptic spasms in particular appeared to benefit. They advocated validating vigabatrin as a targeted treatment and performing early genomic sequencing in patients with early-onset seizures.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported rational therapy with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet in a patient with GAD1 deficiency.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking GAD67, the 67-kDa isoform of glutamic acid decarboxylase. The Gad67 -/- mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67 -/- mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%. The brain showed no discernible defects.

Chatron et al. (2020) noted that the co-occurrence of cleft palate and omphalocele in their patients reproduced the phenotype of Gad1-knockout mice. Those mice die at birth with cleft palate and reduced cortical GABA, and about half also show omphalocele.

### History

Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) both discussed an earlier report of a homozygous GAD1 missense variant (S12C) in a family with autosomal recessive spastic cerebral palsy. Chatron et al. (2020) noted that no functional studies had been performed to validate that finding. Neuray et al. (2020) pointed out that pathogenicity predictions for the variant were conflicting and that the mapped 2q24-31.1 locus contained other candidate genes that had not been investigated. They argued that pathogenic GAD1 variants should be considered the cause of a distinctive neurodevelopmental disorder rather than spastic cerebral palsy.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 sibs with DEE89 born to first-cousin parents of Algerian origin (family A), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1414-1G-C transversion in the GAD1 gene. In silico programs predicted that the mutation abolishes the acceptor splice site of exon 15. cDNA analysis of lymphoblastoid cells from the parents confirmed the absence of normal mRNA product. It showed 3 aberrant isoforms: one skipping exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delaga; p.K232del; c.695_697delAGA; p.Lys232del]

In 2 sibs with DEE89 born to first-cousin Egyptian parents (family B), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA). The deletion removes a highly conserved lysine (lys232del; K232del). Both sibs had omphalocele, and the girl also had cleft soft palate. The variant was not present in the 1000 Genomes Project, Greater Middle East, or ExAC/gnomAD databases. In silico tools predicted that it causes a splice site loss. However, in a cell-free expression system the mutant GAD67 was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing. They considered that the recapitulation of the knockout mouse phenotype (cleft palate and omphalocele) strongly supported pathogenicity, although loss of function could not be formally demonstrated.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, NT812 [c.812_816delttaag; p.V271Dfs; p.V271fs; c.812_816delTTAAG; p.Val271AspfsTer9]

In 2 sibs with DEE89 born to first-cousin Turkish parents (family C), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG) in the GAD1 gene, found by whole-genome sequencing. The deletion causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; g.171715383C>T; p.R531*; p.Arg531Ter]

Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution, in patients with DEE89 from 2 consanguineous families: an Iranian family (family D) and a Turkish family (family E). Because the families came from relatively nearby regions, the authors suggested that a common haplotype was possible. The phenotypes of the 2 families differed: pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and cleft palate were absent in family D.

In an African American boy with DEE89 (family C), Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: the R531X mutation and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). The R531X variant had been reported only in the heterozygous state in gnomAD and had a CADD score of 43. The boy's mother carried c.670delC; his father was not available for testing.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.E509K; p.Glu509Lys]

In a Brazilian girl with DEE89 born to first-cousin parents (family F), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. The mutation was absent from gnomAD. It affects an evolutionarily highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that it disrupts salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389. Modeling further predicted that the larger, positively charged lysine would no longer fit into the domain core.
