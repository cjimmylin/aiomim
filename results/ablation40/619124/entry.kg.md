### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.1.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic GAD1 mutations. Seizures begin in the neonatal period or early infancy, and affected individuals have hypotonia and severe developmental delay. Non-central nervous system features vary and include skeletal abnormalities, dysmorphic features and cleft palate (Neuray et al., 2020). Chatron et al. (2020) also reported joint contractures, pes equinovarus and omphalocele.

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish, Iranian and Brazilian origin with a syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. In all patients, seizures began in the first 2 months of life, mainly as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. Early EEG showed suppression-burst, burst attenuation or hypsarrhythmia. Eight patients had joint contractures and/or pes equinovarus, 7 had cleft palate and 2 had omphalocele. All living patients had profound psychomotor delay, and 4 died before 4 years of age. ⚠︎

In Family A, Chatron et al. (2020) described 2 sibs, a boy and a girl, born to Algerian first-cousin parents. Both had cleft soft palate. By 5 months of age the boy had developmental regression, axial hypotonia and hypsarrhythmia. At 6 years he had spastic tetraparesis and no speech. His sister had spasms from 2 days of age, and her EEG showed suppression-burst. Levetiracetam and vigabatrin controlled her seizures by 3 months, but she had severe developmental delay with axial hypotonia.

In Family B, Chatron et al. (2020) described a sister and brother born to Egyptian first-cousin parents. Both had omphalocele, talipes equinovarus and arthrogryposis. At 2 weeks of age both developed epileptic spasms and eye twitches, and both became seizure-free on vigabatrin. The girl also had cleft soft palate, jejunal atresia, multiple dysmorphic features, a hypoplastic corpus callosum, cerebral atrophy and scoliosis. The girl died at 2 years of age and the boy at 4 years.

In Family C, Chatron et al. (2020) described a sister and brother born to Turkish first-cousin parents. Both had cleft palate and refractory epilepsy that began in the neonatal period with myoclonic seizures, and EEG showed suppression-burst. Brain MRI was normal in both. At 12 years of age the sister had profound intellectual disability, tetraparesis, talipes, conductive hearing loss and photosensitivity. The brother died of refractory seizures at 2 years of age. ⚠︎

Chatron et al. (2020) described 2 sibs from an Iranian second-cousin family (Family D). Both had myoclonic seizures and epileptic spasms from the first week of life and profound developmental delay. Neither had cleft palate, joint contractures or pes equinovarus. The authors also described 2 brothers from a Turkish first-cousin family (Family E). The younger brother, the proband, had cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, flexion contractures, facial dysmorphism and hypsarrhythmia, with epileptic spasms from 1 month of age. His older brother was born at 29 weeks' gestation with cleft palate, congenital heart disease and pes equinovarus. He had myoclonic seizures and died on day 9. ⚠︎

In Family F, Chatron et al. (2020) described a 7-year-old girl born to Brazilian first-cousin parents. Facial twitches began at 7 days of age, and EEG showed suppression-burst. Her seizures remained refractory until vigabatrin was started at 11 months of age. She had profound developmental delay, choreoathetotic movements, lower limb spasticity, severe scoliosis, and cerebral atrophy with ventricular dilation. She did not have cleft palate or dysmorphic facial features.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients (3 male and 3 female) from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian and Turkish ancestry. Four of the families were consanguineous. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures. Some patients also had epileptic spasms, focal non-motor seizures or bilateral motor seizures. Medication controlled seizures in 3 patients, and 3 were drug-resistant. At seizure onset, EEG showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Brain MRI was normal except in 2 patients: 1 had slight ventricular enlargement and 1 had moderate global atrophy. All patients had severe developmental delay, and most never developed speech or nonverbal communication. Intellectual disability was severe in 5 patients and moderate in 1. Five patients had reduced muscle strength or tone. Of these, 4 lacked head control or could not sit or crawl, and 2 depended on a nasogastric tube because of dysphagia. Four patients had mild facial dysmorphism, and 1 had cleft palate. Other features with no common pattern included hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, congenital hip dislocation, high myopia and hearing impairment. Metabolic workups were normal. Cognitive development did not improve even when seizures were controlled: 3 of the 6 patients became seizure-free, yet all had severe intellectual disability. The authors hypothesized that GAD1 variants affect development independently of seizure control. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) described an Egyptian patient (Family E, patient III-3) born to first-cousin parents. She was the only patient in their series born with cleft palate. She also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with a shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus, hearing impairment, severe hypotonia and global brain atrophy. A similarly affected sib died without genetic testing.

### Inheritance

Chatron et al. (2020) found that GAD1 variants segregated with the disease in all 6 families in a manner consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. They noted that GAD1 has a gnomAD pRec score of 0.934. Neuray et al. (2020) found that GAD1 variants segregated with the disease according to autosomal recessive inheritance, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The variants were homozygous in 5 families and compound heterozygous in 1.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) identified homozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene in 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with DEE89. Four of the 6 families carried truncating variants, which the authors considered support for a loss-of-function mechanism. In 2 sibs from an Algerian family (Family A), exome sequencing found a homozygous splice site mutation (c.1414-1G-C). It was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with the disease. In the affected sibs of Egyptian Family B, the authors found a homozygous 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA; Lys232del). In the affected sibs of Turkish Family C, whole-genome sequencing found a homozygous 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG). The same homozygous nonsense mutation (R531X) was present in Persian Family D and Turkish Family E. A Brazilian patient (Family F) had a homozygous missense mutation (E509K).

Neuray et al. (2020) identified biallelic GAD1 mutations in 6 patients from 6 unrelated families with DEE89. Five patients were homozygous: 3 for missense mutations (N564S, F324C and T347M), 1 for a nonsense mutation (Y29X) and 1 for a frameshift mutation (c.568delC). An African American boy was compound heterozygous for a nonsense mutation (R531X) and a frameshift mutation (c.670delC). The variants were identified by exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The authors noted that the missense variants lay in the PLP-binding and C-terminal catalytic domains.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) noted phenotypic variability between Families D and E, which carried the same R531X mutation. The patients in Family D lacked pes equinovarus, joint contractures and cleft palate. The authors suggested that genetic background, or environmental factors affecting movement in utero, might explain the difference.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) suggested that the non-neural features (cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus and joint contractures) are secondary to reduced fetal movement caused by severe central nervous system involvement, as described in Gad1-null mice. They noted that biallelic loss-of-function variants in humans fully reproduced the combination of epilepsy, cleft palate and omphalocele seen in animal models.

Neuray et al. (2020) concluded that the phenotype probably results from loss of function. They based this on the 4 null variants, the location of the missense variants in the PLP-binding and C-terminal catalytic domains, and the moderate intolerance of GAD1 to loss-of-function variation. They suggested that reduced GABA synthesis disrupts the balance of GABAergic signaling and so predisposes to seizures. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a remarkably good seizure response to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 treated patients. They cautioned that the response may reflect seizure type, because epileptic spasms respond well to vigabatrin whatever the cause, and called for validation of vigabatrin as a targeted treatment.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported treating a patient with GAD1 deficiency with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking GAD67 by gene targeting. Gad67 -/- mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67 -/- mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%, but the brain showed no discernible defects. The authors concluded that this was the first demonstration of a role for GAD67-derived GABA in the development of nonneural tissue. In contrast, their earlier GAD65 -/- mice showed no change in brain GABA content or behavior apart from a slight increase in susceptibility to seizures.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 affected sibs from a consanguineous Algerian family (Family A) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1414-1G-C transversion at the splice acceptor site of intron 14 of the GAD1 gene. The mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with the disease. cDNA analysis of the parents' lymphoblastoid cells found no normal mRNA from the mutant allele. Instead there were 3 aberrant isoforms: one skipping exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delaga; p.K232del]

In the affected sibs of an Egyptian family (Family B) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA). The deletion removes a highly conserved lysine (Lys232del, reported as Lys231del in the table). It was absent from public databases, and in silico tools predicted that it disrupts splicing. In a cell-free assay, the mutant protein was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing and could not formally demonstrate loss of function, but they considered the variant pathogenic given the phenotype.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, 812TTAAG [c.812_816delttaag; p.V271Dfs; p.V271fs]

In the affected sibs of a Turkish family (Family C) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) used whole-genome sequencing to identify a homozygous 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG) in the GAD1 gene. The deletion causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Sanger sequencing confirmed that it segregated with the disease.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; g.171715383C>T; p.R531*]

Chatron et al. (2020) found a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution, in affected members of a Persian family (Family D) and a Turkish family (Family E) with DEE89. Given the families' relative geographic proximity, they suggested that a common haplotype was possible. The phenotype differed between the 2 families: patients in Family D lacked pes equinovarus, joint contractures and cleft palate.

In an African American boy with DEE89 (Family C), Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: the R531X mutation and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). The R531X variant had a CADD score of 43 and had been reported only in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. His mother carried the c.670delC variant. His father was not available for testing.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.E509K]

In a 7-year-old Brazilian girl with DEE89 born to first-cousin parents (Family F), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. The mutation was absent from gnomAD and affects an extremely well-conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that it disrupts salt bridges with arg316, lys318 and lys389 and that the mutant residue no longer fits into the domain core.

**.0006 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ASN564SER [c.1691A>G; g.171716298A>G; p.N564S]

In a Persian girl with DEE89 born to double-first-cousin parents (Family A), Neuray et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1691A-G transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an asn564-to-ser (N564S) substitution. Her phenotype was relatively mild: she had moderate intellectual disability, simple speech, and seizures that became controlled. The variant had been seen once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD and had a CADD score of 26.1. SIFT, MutationTaster and PolyPhen-2 predicted it to be pathogenic. It lies in the C-terminal catalytic domain, and in silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability. ⚠︎

GAD1, THR347MET [c.1040C>T; g.171704223C>T; p.T347M]

In a Sudanese boy with DEE89 whose parents were second cousins (Family D), Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1040C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a thr347-to-met (T347M) substitution. He had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia, severe hypotonia and severe dysmorphic features. The variant had been reported twice in the heterozygous state in gnomAD and had a CADD score of 29.1. In silico analysis predicted that it breaks hydrogen bonds in the PLP-binding domain. A heterozygous sister had a different neurodevelopmental condition without seizures.

**.0009 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, TYR29TER [c.87C>G; g.171678601C>G; p.Y29*]

In an Egyptian girl with DEE89 whose parents were first cousins (Family E), Neuray et al. She had cleft palate, congenital bilateral hip dislocation, talipes equinovarus, hearing impairment, severe hypotonia and global brain atrophy. The variant was absent from all population databases queried and had a CADD score of 35. The authors predicted that it causes nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and complete loss of function.
