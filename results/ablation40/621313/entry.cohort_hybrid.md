### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene (604618) on chromosome 1q23.3.

### Description

Brain small vessel disease-4 is an autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease that begins in adulthood. Rutten et al. (2024) defined it by a triad of movement disorders, massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and intracerebral hemorrhage. Neuropsychologic deterioration and psychiatric illness have also been reported. Patients excrete deaminated glutathione in the urine and have a positive urine sulfite test (Rendu et al., 2019).

### Nomenclature

Rendu et al. (2019) called the basal ganglia cavitations in their family a 'deep cavitating state.' They suggested that these lesions may correspond to the 'cerebral porosis' of the historical classification of lacunar brain lesions. Rutten et al. (2024) called the disorder NIT1 small vessel disease.

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) reported 3 brothers who had psychiatric and/or neurologic disorders and cystic lesions of the basal ganglia. The index patient (II.4) had a history of nonsevere mental retardation. He was admitted to a psychiatry unit for an acute psychotic episode, which was followed by generalized seizures. Two years later, at age 34, he presented with a sudden pure motor left hemiparesis. CT scan and MRI showed bilateral small cystic lesions in the basal ganglia. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and echocardiography were normal, and a dipstick urine test for sulfites was positive. Over 20 years he developed severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia, and he died at age 57. A brother (II.5) had diabetes mellitus and had lived an active professional life. At age 58 he was admitted to intensive care with sudden coma and cardiovascular collapse. CT and MRI showed the same basal ganglia lesions ('deep cavitating state'), and his urine sulfite test was positive. He gradually recovered but had neuropsychological deterioration (BREF 14/18, MMSE 22/30). A third brother (II.7) had been diagnosed with schizophrenia 40 years earlier and lived in a psychiatric institution. His clinical examination was normal, but CT and MRI showed the same brain lesions, and his urine sulfite test was positive. The mother and the asymptomatic sibs who were tested had negative urine sulfite tests.

Rutten et al. (2024) reported 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees: 3 sibs and 4 single cases. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. On brain MRI, all had striking abnormalities, with numerous and massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia. Three patients had non-lobar intracerebral hemorrhage between ages 45 and 60, which was fatal in 2 of them. At brain autopsy, there were large electron-dense deposits in the walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries.

### Biochemical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) performed qualitative urine organic acid analysis by GC/MS. In the urine of the 3 affected brothers, they found a compound whose mass spectrum matched a deaminated glutathione (dGSH) derivative, identical to the one excreted in the urine of Nit1-knockout mice. The compound was undetectable in the other sibs tested and in the mother. The authors proposed that dGSH accumulation was probably responsible for the positive urine sulfite test. In patient fibroblasts, mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) activity was increased 2-fold, whereas cytoplasmic Cu/Zn SOD activity was unchanged. Rutten et al. (2024) also found that metabolic analysis of patient urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Rendu et al. (2019) described the disorder as autosomal recessive, based on compound heterozygous NIT1 mutations in affected brothers born to an asymptomatic mother who carried one of the mutations. Rutten et al. (2024) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance in 5 pedigrees whose patients were homozygous or compound heterozygous for NIT1 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Rendu et al. (2019) performed exome sequencing in 2 of 3 affected brothers (II.5 and II.7). Both were compound heterozygous for 2 rare variants in the NIT1 gene: a c.457G-A transition predicted to cause a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution, and a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA) predicted to cause a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41). The c.670dupA mutation was inherited from the asymptomatic mother. The father's DNA was not available, but molecular cloning showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles. Asymptomatic sibs whose DNA was available carried neither variant. The c.457G-A change affects the last nucleotide of exon 4, and sequencing of NIT1 transcripts from a skin biopsy of patient II.5 showed that it affected splicing. NIT1 protein was undetectable on Western blot of fibroblasts from patient II.5.

Rutten et al. (2024) performed exome sequencing in 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees. Three sibs were compound heterozygous for a c.727C-T transition in the NIT1 gene, predicted to cause an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and a 2-bp deletion (c.198_199del) predicted to create a stop codon (Ala68Ter). The 4 other patients were single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees, and all were homozygous for the R243W mutation. NIT1 protein was absent on Western blotting of patient fibroblasts.

### Pathogenesis

Rendu et al. (2019) noted that NIT1 encodes an amidase needed to recycle deaminated glutathione (dGSH) into 2-ketoglutarate and cysteinylglycine. They hypothesized that dGSH may be a toxic metabolite that interferes with glutathione metabolism and, in the long term, causes redox damage. In support, they cited the increased mitochondrial SOD activity in patient fibroblasts, which they described as an adaptive response to damage from reactive oxygen species.

### Diagnosis

Rendu et al. (2019) noted that sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency were the only previously known disorders combining brain damage with a positive urinary sulfite test, and these were excluded in their family. They suggested that the urine sulfite test could easily be used to screen cohorts of psychiatric patients for the disease.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 BRAIN SMALL VESSEL DISEASE 4**  
NIT1, GLY153ARG [c.457G>A; p.G153R; p.Gly153Arg]

Rendu et al. (2019) studied 2 brothers with brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) and basal ganglia cavitations (II.5 and II.7). Both were compound heterozygous for a c.457G-A transition in the NIT1 gene, predicted to cause a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution at a highly conserved residue, and a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA). The c.457G-A change is located at the last nucleotide of exon 4, and sequencing of patient NIT1 transcripts showed that it affected splicing. Asymptomatic sibs did not carry the variant, and NIT1 protein was undetectable in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎

**.0002 BRAIN SMALL VESSEL DISEASE 4**

In 2 brothers with BSVD4 (II.5 and II.7), Rendu et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygosity for a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA) in the NIT1 gene and the G153R mutation. The duplication was predicted to cause a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41) and loss of function. It was inherited from the asymptomatic mother, and molecular cloning showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles.
