### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene (604618) on chromosome 1q23.3.

### Description

Brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is an autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease caused by loss-of-function variants in the NIT1 gene. Rutten et al. (2024) described it as a triad of movement disorders, massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and intracerebral hemorrhage, with onset in mid-adulthood. Rendu et al. (2019) had earlier described affected individuals with neurologic and/or psychiatric disease, bilateral cystic lesions of the basal ganglia, and a positive urine sulfite test.

### Nomenclature

Rendu et al. (2019) referred to the disorder in their family as a familial deep cavitating state. Rutten et al. (2024) called the disorder NIT1-small vessel disease.

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) reported 3 brothers who had neurologic and/or psychiatric disorders and bilateral small cystic lesions of the basal ganglia on CT and MRI. The index patient (II.4) had a history of nonsevere mental retardation. He had been admitted to a psychiatry unit for an acute psychotic episode, followed by generalized seizures. Two years later, at age 34 years, he presented with a sudden pure motor left hemiparesis, and stroke was suspected. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and cardiac echocardiography were normal. Over a 20-year period he worsened progressively, with severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia. He died in 2011, and no autopsy was performed. In 2014 a brother (II.5), a 58-year-old man with diabetes mellitus who had led an active professional life, was admitted to intensive care for a sudden coma with cardiovascular collapse. CT and MRI showed the same abnormalities as in his brother. He gradually recovered but had neuropsychological deterioration (BREF, 14/18; MMSE, 22/30). A third brother (II.7) had been in a psychiatric institution for schizophrenia diagnosed 40 years earlier. His clinical examination was normal, but CT and MRI showed the same brain lesions. The authors noted that the basal ganglia cavities differed from the cavitary lesions often reported in adults and might correspond to the so-called 'cerebral porosis' of historical classifications of lacunar lesions. ⚠︎

Rutten et al. (2024) reported 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees: 3 sibs and 4 single cases from 4 pedigrees. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous, massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia. Three patients had non-lobar intracerebral hemorrhage between ages 45 and 60 years, which was fatal in 2 of them.

### Biochemical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) found a positive urine dipstick test for sulfites in all 3 affected brothers. The mother and the asymptomatic sibs who were tested had negative results. Sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency were excluded. Qualitative urinary organic acid analysis by GC/MS showed a compound whose mass spectrum was identical to that of deaminated glutathione (dGSH). The spectrum was similar to that of a compound in the urine of Nit1-knockout mice. The compound was found in the affected brothers but was undetectable in the mother and in the other sibs tested. The authors suggested that dGSH accumulation was probably responsible for the positive urine sulfite test.

Rutten et al. (2024) reported that metabolic analysis of patient urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of BSVD4 is autosomal recessive. In the family reported by Rendu et al. (2019), the 3 affected brothers were compound heterozygous for NIT1 mutations, one of which was inherited from the asymptomatic mother. Rutten et al. (2024) found homozygous or compound heterozygous NIT1 variants in affected individuals and concluded that the disorder is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 brothers with BSVD4, Rendu et al. (2019) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: a c.457G-A transition resulting in a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution, and a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA) predicted to cause a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41). Whole-exome sequencing was performed in 2 of the brothers (II.5 and II.7). The c.670dupA mutation was inherited from the asymptomatic mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but cloning of a PCR fragment spanning both variants from patient II.5 showed that they lay on different alleles. Neither variant was present in the asymptomatic sibs whose DNA was available. The c.457G-A variant affected the last nucleotide of exon 4. It was predicted to affect splicing, and sequencing of NIT1 transcripts from a skin biopsy of patient II.5 showed this. NIT1 protein was undetectable on Western blot of fibroblasts from patient II.5. ⚠︎

Rutten et al. (2024) performed exome sequencing in 7 patients with BSVD4 from 5 unrelated pedigrees. The first patients identified were 3 sibs who were compound heterozygous for mutations in the NIT1 gene: a c.727C-T transition resulting in an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and a 2-bp deletion (c.198_199del) resulting in a premature stop codon (Ala68Ter). The 4 other patients were single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees, and all were homozygous for the R243W mutation. Western blotting showed no NIT1 protein in patient fibroblasts, and metabolic analysis of urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function.

### Pathogenesis

Rendu et al. (2019) noted that NIT1 is an amidase that recycles deaminated glutathione (dGSH) into 2-ketoglutarate and cysteinylglycine. In fibroblasts from a patient with BSVD4, cytoplasmic Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was unchanged, but mitochondrial MnSOD activity was 2-fold higher than in controls. The authors proposed that loss of NIT1 function leads to accumulation of dGSH. They suggested that dGSH may be a toxic metabolite that interferes with glutathione metabolism and, in the long term, may cause redox damage and oxidative stress.

Rutten et al. (2024) found large electron-dense deposits in the vessel walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries at brain autopsy of a patient with BSVD4.

### Diagnosis

Rendu et al. (2019) suggested that the urine sulfite test could easily be used to screen cohorts of psychiatric patients for the disorder.

The mutations were found by whole-exome sequencing. The c.457G-A change lies at the last nucleotide of exon 4.
