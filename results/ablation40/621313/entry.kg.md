### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene (604618) on chromosome 1q23.3.

### Description

Rutten et al. (2024) described NIT1-small vessel disease as an autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease caused by loss-of-function variants in NIT1. It is characterized by a triad of movement disorders, massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and intracerebral hemorrhage.

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) described 3 brothers with bilateral small cystic lesions of the basal ganglia (a deep cavitating state) and positive urine sulfite dipstick tests. The index patient (II.4) had nonsevere mental retardation and an acute psychotic episode followed by generalized seizures. At age 34 years he developed a sudden pure motor left hemiparesis. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and echocardiography were normal. Over 20 years he progressed to severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia, and he died at age 57 years.

A brother of the index patient reported by Rendu et al. (2019) (II.5) had diabetes mellitus and had led an active professional life. At age 58 years he had a sudden coma with cardiovascular collapse. CT and 3T MRI showed the same basal ganglia cystic lesions, and his urine sulfite test was positive. He recovered but had neuropsychological deterioration (BREF 14/18, MMSE 22/30). A third brother (II.7), who had been diagnosed with schizophrenia 40 years earlier, had a normal clinical examination but the same brain lesions and a positive urine sulfite test.

Rutten et al. (2024) reported 7 patients with NIT1-small vessel disease from 5 unrelated pedigrees, comprising 3 sibs and 4 single cases. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous and massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia. Three patients had a non-lobar intracerebral hemorrhage between ages 45 and 60 years, which was fatal in 2 of them. Brain autopsy showed large electron-dense deposits in the walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries.

### Biochemical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) found that the 3 affected brothers had positive urine sulfite dipstick tests, whereas the mother and the asymptomatic sibs who were tested did not. GC/MS organic acid analysis detected a urinary compound with a mass spectrum identical to deaminated glutathione (dGSH), matching the compound excreted by Nit1-knockout mice. This compound was present in the 3 affected brothers but undetectable in the mother and the other sibs tested. The authors proposed that dGSH accumulation was probably responsible for the positive sulfite test. They excluded sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency.

Rutten et al. (2024) found by Western blotting that NIT1 protein was absent from patient fibroblasts, and metabolic analysis of patient urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzyme function.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of BSVD4 in the family reported by Rendu et al. (2019) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance; the authors concluded that the disorder was a novel autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by NIT1 mutations. Rutten et al. (2024) likewise described NIT1-small vessel disease as an autosomal recessive disorder, finding compound heterozygous or homozygous NIT1 variants in affected individuals.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 affected brothers (II.5 and II.7) from the family with BSVD4 reported by Rendu et al. (2019), exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: a c.457G-A transition, resulting in a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution at a highly conserved residue and affecting the last nucleotide of exon 4, and a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA), resulting in a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41) predicted to cause loss of function. Sequencing of the NIT1 transcript from a skin biopsy of patient II.5 showed that the c.457G-A variant affected splicing. Cloning showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles. The c.670dupA variant was inherited from the asymptomatic mother; the father's DNA was not available. Asymptomatic sibs whose DNA was available carried neither variant. NIT1 protein was undetectable on Western blot of patient fibroblasts.

In 3 sibs with BSVD4, Rutten et al. (2024) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: a c.727C-T transition, resulting in an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and a 2-bp deletion (c.198_199del), resulting in an ala68-to-ter (A68X) substitution. Four additional patients, each a single case from 1 of 4 unrelated pedigrees, were homozygous for the R243W mutation. NIT1 protein was absent from patient fibroblasts on Western blotting, and urinary metabolic analysis confirmed loss of NIT1 enzyme function.

### Pathogenesis

In fibroblasts from patient II.5, Rendu et al. (2019) found unchanged cytoplasmic Cu/Zn SOD activity but a 2-fold increase in mitochondrial MnSOD activity. They suggested that accumulation of deaminated glutathione causes oxidative stress and redox damage, and noted that other effects of NIT1 deficiency, such as altered Wnt/beta-catenin signaling, could contribute. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Rendu et al. (2019) suggested that the urine sulfite test could easily be used to screen cohorts of psychiatric patients for the disorder.
