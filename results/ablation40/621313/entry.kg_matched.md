### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene (604618) on chromosome 1q23.3.

### Description

Brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is an autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease caused by loss-of-function variants in the NIT1 gene. It is characterized by a triad of movement disorders, massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and intracerebral hemorrhage (Rutten et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) described 3 brothers from 1 family with neurologic and/or psychiatric disease and bilateral small cystic (cavitating) lesions of the basal ganglia on CT and MRI. The index patient (II.4) had mild mental retardation. At age 32 years, he was admitted for an acute psychotic episode followed by generalized seizures. At age 34, he had a sudden pure motor left hemiparesis that suggested stroke, but arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and echocardiography were normal. Over 20 years he worsened progressively, with severe neuropsychologic deterioration and spastic tetraplegia, and he died in 2011. His brother (II.5) had diabetes mellitus and had led an active professional life. At age 58, he presented with sudden coma and cardiovascular collapse, and after recovery he had neuropsychologic deterioration (MMSE 22/30). A third brother (II.7) had been diagnosed with schizophrenia 40 years earlier; his clinical examination was normal, but he had the same brain lesions.

Rutten et al. (2024) reported 7 patients with BSVD4 from 5 unrelated pedigrees, comprising 3 sibs and 4 single cases. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous, massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia. Three patients had a nonlobar intracerebral hemorrhage between 45 and 60 years of age, which was fatal in 2 of them.

### Biochemical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) found that all 3 affected brothers in their family had a positive urine dipstick test for sulfites, whereas the mother and asymptomatic sibs tested negative. Sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency were excluded. GC/MS analysis of urinary organic acids identified a compound whose mass spectrum matched deaminated glutathione (dGSH), the same compound found in the urine of Nit1-knockout mice. This compound was present in the affected brothers and undetectable in the mother and the other sibs tested. The authors suggested that dGSH accumulation was probably responsible for the positive sulfite test.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of BSVD4 in the family reported by Rendu et al. (2019), in which affected brothers carried compound heterozygous NIT1 mutations, was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Rutten et al. (2024) also described BSVD4 as an autosomal recessive disorder, finding homozygous or compound heterozygous NIT1 mutations in affected individuals. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 brothers with BSVD4, Rendu et al. (2019) found compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene (NM_005600) by whole-exome sequencing of 2 of the affected brothers. One was a c.457G-A transition that caused a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution at a highly conserved residue. This variant lies at the last nucleotide of exon 4 and was predicted to affect splicing. Sequencing of NIT1 transcripts from a skin biopsy of patient II.5 confirmed abnormal splicing. The other was a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA) that caused a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41) and was predicted to cause loss of function. The duplication was inherited from the asymptomatic mother; paternal DNA was not available. Cloning showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles. Asymptomatic sibs whose DNA was available carried neither variant. NIT1 protein was undetectable on Western blot of fibroblasts from patient II.5. The authors concluded that the disorder was an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by NIT1 mutations, combining neurologic signs, basal ganglia lesions, and a positive urine sulfite test. ⚠︎

In 3 sibs with BSVD4, Rutten et al. (2024) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: c.727C-T, resulting in an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and c.198_199del, resulting in an Ala68Ter change. Western blotting of patient fibroblasts showed no NIT1 protein, and metabolic analysis of urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function.

Rutten et al. (2024) identified the R243W mutation in NIT1 in homozygous state in 4 additional patients with BSVD4. Each patient was a single case from one of 4 unrelated pedigrees.

### Pathogenesis

Rendu et al. (2019) found that fibroblasts from a patient with BSVD4 had unchanged cytoplasmic Cu/Zn SOD activity but a 2-fold increase in mitochondrial MnSOD activity compared with controls. The authors suggested that loss of NIT1 amidase activity, which recycles deaminated glutathione, leads to dGSH accumulation. They proposed that over time this may cause toxicity and oxidative stress. ⚠︎

At brain autopsy of a patient with BSVD4, Rutten et al. (2024) found large electron-dense deposits in the vessel walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries.

### Diagnosis

Rendu et al. (2019) suggested that the urine sulfite test could easily be used to screen cohorts of psychiatric patients for the disease.
