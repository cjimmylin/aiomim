### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene (604618) on chromosome 1q23.3.

### Description

Brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is an autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease caused by loss-of-function variants in NIT1. Rutten et al. (2024) characterized it by a triad of movement disorders, massively dilated basal ganglia perivascular spaces, and intracerebral hemorrhage. Rendu et al. (2019) earlier described the disorder as a metabolic disorder associating neurologic and psychiatric signs, cystic cavitating lesions of the basal ganglia ('deep cavitating state'), and a positive urine sulfite test. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) reported 3 brothers with cystic lesions of the basal ganglia. The index patient (II.4) was a 34-year-old man admitted to the emergency ward for a sudden left hemiparesis. He had a history of nonsevere mental retardation and had been admitted to a psychiatry unit 2 years earlier for an acute psychotic episode followed by generalized seizures. CT scan and MRI showed bilateral small cystic lesions affecting the basal ganglia. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and cardiac echocardiography were normal. His condition progressively worsened over a 20-year period, with severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia, and he died in 2011; no autopsy was performed.

In 2014, a brother (II.5) of the index patient in the family reported by Rendu et al. (2019), a 58-year-old man with a history of diabetes mellitus who had lived an active professional life, was admitted to intensive care for a sudden coma with cardiovascular collapse. He progressively recovered but showed neuropsychological deterioration (BREF, 14/18; MMSE, 22/30). He had basal ganglia cystic lesions and a positive urine sulfite test. A third brother (II.7), aged 60 years, had been in a psychiatric institution for schizophrenia diagnosed 40 years earlier. His clinical examination was normal, but CT scan and MRI showed the same brain lesions, and his urine sulfite test was positive.

Rutten et al. (2024) reported 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees: 3 sibs and 4 single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous and massively dilated basal ganglia perivascular spaces. Three patients had non-lobar intracerebral hemorrhage between ages 45 and 60 years, which was fatal in 2 of them. Brain autopsy in 1 patient showed large electron-dense deposits in the vessel walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

By urine organic acid analysis, Rendu et al. (2019) detected a deaminated glutathione (dGSH) derivative, identical to that excreted by Nit1-knockout mice, in the urine of the affected brothers but not in their unaffected sibs or mother. They proposed that dGSH accumulation was probably responsible for the positive urine sulfite test. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of BSVD4 in the family reported by Rendu et al. (2019) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The affected brothers carried compound heterozygous NIT1 mutations, one of them inherited from their asymptomatic mother, and their asymptomatic sibs carried neither mutation. Rutten et al. (2024) found homozygous or compound heterozygous NIT1 variants in their patients and described the disorder as autosomal recessive.

### Molecular Genetics

Rendu et al. (2019) suspected an inherited recessive disorder and performed exome sequencing on 2 affected brothers (II.5 and II.7) from the family. They identified compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: a c.457G-A transition, resulting in a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution, and a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA), resulting in a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41). The c.457G-A mutation affects the last nucleotide of exon 4 and was shown to affect splicing. The c.670dupA mutation was inherited from the asymptomatic mother, the 2 variants were on different alleles, and the asymptomatic sibs carried neither variant. Nit1 protein was undetectable on Western blot of patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎

In 3 sibs with BSVD4, Rutten et al. (2024) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: a c.727C-T transition, resulting in an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and a 2-bp deletion (c.198_199del), resulting in a premature termination codon (Ala68Ter). Four additional patients, single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees, were all homozygous for the R243W mutation. NIT1 protein was absent on Western blot of patient fibroblasts, and metabolic analysis of patient urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function.

### Pathogenesis

Rendu et al. (2019) found a 2-fold increase in mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) activity, but unchanged Cu/Zn SOD activity, in patient fibroblasts, suggesting oxidative stress. They hypothesized that dGSH may be a toxic metabolite that interferes with glutathione metabolism and induces redox damage.

### Diagnosis

Rendu et al. (2019) noted that sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency, the only previously known disorders with brain damage and a positive urinary sulfite test, were excluded in their patients. They suggested that the urine sulfite test could be used to screen cohorts of psychiatric patients for the disease.
