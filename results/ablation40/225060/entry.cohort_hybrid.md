### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome (CLPED1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the NECTIN1 gene (600644), also known as PVRL1, on chromosome 11q23.3. Heterozygosity for a NECTIN1 mutation has been associated with sporadic nonsyndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate (OFC7) in northern Venezuela.

### Description

Suzuki et al. (1998) described Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia (ED4) as an autosomal recessive disorder with unusual facies, dental anomalies, hypotrichosis, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and onychodysplasia, syndactyly, and cleft lip/cleft palate. Yoshida et al. (2015) summarized the clinical features of cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome as a distinctive facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, ectodermal dysplasia, and cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers and/or toes, with mental retardation in some cases.

### Nomenclature

Suzuki et al. (2000) noted that CLPED1 had previously been designated ED4. Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) used the name Zlotogora-Ogur syndrome for autosomal recessive ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, and syndactyly.

### Clinical Features

Zlotogora et al. (1987) reported 2 sibs who had cleft lip and palate, sparse scalp hair, malformed protruding ears, and partial syndactyly of the fingers and toes. The older child also had mental retardation and pili torti.

Oğur and Yüksel (1988) reported 2 Turkish brothers, born of a second-cousin marriage, who had syndactyly of all 4 limbs, ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip and palate, renal anomalies, and mental retardation.

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) reported 3 Brazilian brothers born to normal consanguineous parents (F = 1/16). They had ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, syndactyly of fingers 2-3, accessory nipples, and ear anomalies.

Bustos et al. (1991) described 27 individuals from 7 families on Margarita Island in northeastern Venezuela (Nueva Esparta State). The families were probably related to each other. Affected individuals had ectodermal dysplasia and other anomalies, with variable expressivity, and females were affected as severely as males. All parents were normal. The authors stated that the clinical picture shared by all patients did not fit any of the known ectodermal dysplasias and that previously published cases did not resemble their patients.

Yoshida et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old Japanese boy, the first child of a consanguineous marriage. He had hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia with sparse, brittle, fine, dry hair and hypodontia, a distinctive facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers, and mild mental retardation. Scanning electron microscopy of the hair showed pili torti and pili trianguli et canaliculi.

### Inheritance

Zlotogora et al. (1987) concluded that the syndrome they observed in 2 sibs was most probably inherited as an autosomal recessive disorder. Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) described 3 affected brothers born to normal consanguineous parents and called the disorder autosomal recessive. Bustos et al. (1991) concluded that the Margarita Island condition was an autosomal recessive syndrome because all parents were normal, females were affected as severely as males, and the families came from an isolated, inbred population. Suzuki et al. (2000) found that CLPED1 was autosomal recessive. In the Japanese patient reported by Yoshida et al. (2015), who was born of a consanguineous marriage, the mutation was homozygous and both parents were heterozygous for it. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Using an affected-only DNA-pooling strategy, Suzuki et al. (1998) mapped the gene for Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia (ED4) to 11q23 by linkage disequilibrium mapping. Haplotype analysis of 4 complex Margarita Island families placed the gene in an interval of about 1 to 2 Mb spanned by just 2 YACs.

### Molecular Genetics

By positional cloning, Suzuki et al. (2000) identified PVRL1 (NECTIN1), which encodes nectin-1, as the gene responsible for autosomal recessive CLPED1. Nectin-1 is an immunoglobulin-related transmembrane cell-cell adhesion molecule that is part of the NAP cell adhesion system. It is also the principal cell surface receptor for alpha-herpesviruses (HveC). ⚠︎

Sözen et al. (2001) stated that homozygosity for a PVRL1 nonsense mutation, W185X, causes CLPED1 on Margarita Island. They found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for this mutation and sporadic nonsyndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate in northern Venezuela.

In a 7-year-old Japanese boy with CLPED1, born of a consanguineous marriage, Yoshida et al. (2015) analyzed exon 2 of the PVRL1 gene and found a homozygous nonsense mutation, c.400C-T (R134X). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The authors noted that all 4 PVRL1 mutations identified in this syndrome up to that time, including theirs, resulted in truncated proteins. These proteins lack the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1, which are needed to start the cell-cell adhesion process.

### Population Genetics

Bustos et al. (1991) found affected families in a relatively isolated, inbred population with very small neighboring communities on Margarita Island, Venezuela. Suzuki et al. (2000) suggested that the high frequency of CLPED1 on Margarita Island might result from heterozygotes being resistant to infection by alpha-herpesviruses, for which nectin-1 is the principal receptor.
