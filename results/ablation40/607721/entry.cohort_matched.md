### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is a RASopathy with Noonan syndrome-like features and distinctive ectodermal abnormalities (Motta et al., 2019). Mazzanti et al. (2003) described the condition as combining a facial appearance not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, a behavioral phenotype, growth hormone (GH) deficiency, darkly pigmented hairless skin, and loose anagen hair. Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that affected individuals had reduced growth, often with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most of them, and easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair.

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) suggested that the combination of features in their 3 patients matched no known condition, proposed that it might be a distinct, previously unreported syndrome, and named it 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.'

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. She had short, blond, easily pluckable hair that had never been cut. The trichogram showed an absolute prevalence of abnormally shaped anagen bulbs lacking inner and outer root sheaths, and a scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reported 19 patients with loose anagen hair (14 children and 5 adults). Associated conditions included Noonan syndrome in a 5-year-old boy. In all patients the trichogram showed a predominance of anagen hairs (80 to 100%) devoid of sheaths. The authors concluded that loose anagen hair may be sporadic, may occur together with developmental or acquired conditions, or, less often, may be familial.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children with short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), mild dilatation of the pulmonary root in 2 of them, and a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented and hairless skin and hair with the aspect of loose anagen hair.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) studied 25 sporadic subjects with a relatively consistent phenotype previously termed Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, all carrying the same SHOC2 mutation. They had facial features typical of Noonan syndrome, reduced growth frequently associated with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most, a hypernasal voice, and hair anomalies including easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair; loose anagen hair was confirmed microscopically in 12. Some had darkly pigmented skin, eczema, or ichthyosis, and facial and other features variably included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, short or webbed neck, and pectus anomalies. Mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were significantly overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population. Referring pediatricians felt that several subjects had features suggestive of Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome as newborns or young infants, and the phenotype combined features from across the neuro-cardio-facial-cutaneous disorders family. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin; 6 had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that the clinical features of SHOC2 mutation-positive patients partly overlap with those of typical Noonan or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, and that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair is distinctive in these patients.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with the SHOC2 S2G mutation who presented with fetal distress requiring premature delivery at 32 weeks. He had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress after birth. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction; the cardiac lesion rapidly progressed, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors noted that the cardiac findings and features were atypical compared with previously reported individuals with this mutation and concluded that the case expanded the phenotype to include fetal distress and fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in a premature infant.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 male patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. Patient 1 had prenatal polyhydramnios, cystic hygroma, and macrosomia, followed by pyloric stenosis, severe feeding difficulties and failure to thrive requiring gastrostomy, mild pulmonic valve stenosis, axial hypotonia, small optic nerves, delayed motor development, macrocephaly (OFC +3 SD) with a large anterior fontanel and dolichocephaly, sparse thin hair, long eyelashes, bilateral ptosis, downslanting palpebral fissures, hepatosplenomegaly, hemangiomas, and fine wrinkling of the hands. Bone marrow biopsies showed markedly hypercellular marrow with increased reticulin staining consistent with myelofibrosis, and blasts were present in peripheral blood; he died at age 2.5 years after splenectomy in preparation for bone marrow transplantation. Patient 2 had polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, severe feeding difficulties, macrocephaly, unilateral ptosis, posteriorly angulated ears, increased wrinkling of the palms, loose skin, bilateral inguinal hernias, and hypotonia, so that Costello syndrome was considered; he had a subdural effusion requiring drainage, intellectual disability with independent walking at age 5 years and a few single words at 9 years, short stature, acanthosis nigricans, and eczema. Patient 3 had polyhydramnios and fetal macrosomia, tracheostenosis, chronic lung disease, a neonatal seizure, right bundle branch block, biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aortic insufficiency, mild pulmonic stenosis, and failure to thrive requiring gastrostomy; Costello syndrome was suggested clinically. At age 9 years his height was 125 cm (10th centile), weight 29.4 kg (50th centile), and OFC 60 cm (+5 SD), and he was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome. Patient 4, of Mexican ancestry, had poor growth, atrial septal defect, patent foramen ovale, trivial mitral regurgitation, severe eczema, poorly growing, easily pluckable blond hair much lighter than that of his family, very long eyelashes, mild to moderate intellectual disability, and no macrocephaly; brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly and subependymal heterotopia, and MRI at 6 years for headaches showed development of a Chiari I malformation in the context of platybasia and basilar invagination, with C3-C4 vertebral fusion. Patient 5 had neonatal hypotonia, feeding difficulties requiring a nasogastric tube, patent ductus arteriosus, an abnormal mitral valve with 3 papillary muscles and redundant chordae, short stature, macrocephaly (OFC +2 SD), attention deficit disorder, and a duplicated renal collecting system; his hair grew slowly and had never been cut, but he never had hair loss or easily pluckable hair. ⚠︎

Across their 5 patients, Gripp et al. (2013) found skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay in all, a structural cardiac anomaly in 4 of 4, hypotonia and macrocephaly in 4 of 5, and polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, or need for a feeding tube in 3 of 5. They described distinctive brain abnormalities, including relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus and a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium, and suggested that this combination likely accounted for the high rate of cerebellar tonsillar ectopia and postnatally developed Chiari I malformation; including literature cases, 3 of 8 patients with MRI had a Chiari I malformation. The myelofibrosis in patient 1 was the first hematologic neoplasm reported with the SHOC2 mutation.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a novel SHOC2 mutation associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

### Inheritance

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 shows autosomal dominant inheritance. Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that all cases with the SHOC2 S2G mutation were sporadic and that the heterozygous mutation was de novo in every family in which parental DNA was available, with paternity confirmed. De novo occurrence of the mutation was also reported by Hoban et al. (2012) and Gripp et al. (2013). Hannig et al. (2014) reported transmission of a heterozygous SHOC2 variant from a mildly affected father to his daughter. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent pro49-to-arg (P49R) mutation in the PPP1CB gene who had sparse, thin, slow-growing hair similar to that seen in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, as well as craniosynostosis. They concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair is very similar to Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair.

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) used in silico protein network analysis seeded with known RAS-MAPK disease proteins to rank candidate Noonan syndrome genes, and resequenced the top candidate, SHOC2, in 96 mutation-negative Noonan syndrome patients. They found a heterozygous c.4A-G transition, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 4 unrelated individuals. Among a further 410 mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, 21 had the same change; no other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. The mutation was absent in the parents and de novo in the families with parental DNA available (3 of the 4 in the first cohort and 12 in the second), with paternity confirmed. Detection of the mutation in DNA from several tissues indicated that it was a germline mutation. Functional studies showed that the S2G substitution created an N-myristoylation site: SHOC2(S2G), but not wildtype SHOC2 or a disease-unrelated S2A mutant, took up [3H]-myristic acid in Cos-1 cells. Wildtype SHOC2 was distributed through the cytoplasm and nucleus in starved cells and moved to the nucleus after EGF stimulation, whereas SHOC2(S2G) was targeted to the cell membrane in both conditions; an N-myristoyltransferase inhibitor reduced or abolished membrane localization, but the mutant still did not translocate to the nucleus. Wildtype and mutant SHOC2 did not heterodimerize, ruling out a dominant-negative effect through sequestration of the wildtype protein, and SHOC2(S2G) increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A neuroblastoma cells but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells, indicating cell type-specific MAPK activation. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders without mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1, and found the S2G mutation in 8 of them. They found no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a premature male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo heterozygous S2G mutation in SHOC2 by clinical testing of genes causative of Noonan syndrome and related disorders. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the recurrent heterozygous S2G mutation in SHOC2 in 5 patients. In patient 1, the mutation was found by postmortem research testing of stored buccal cell-derived DNA and was not present in either parent; in patient 2 it was de novo.

In a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with a mild RASopathy and her father, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The parents were not consanguineous. Both carriers had features overlapping Noonan syndrome, cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, and Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the M173I mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation by only about 10% above basal, whereas wildtype SHOC2 fully rescued it; protein half-life, subcellular localization, and binding to M-RAS and RAF-1 were unchanged. The mutant failed to precipitate endogenous PP1c effectively and supported only a mild EGF-induced increase in RAF-1 ser338 phosphorylation, and the authors concluded that reduced SHOC2-PP1c binding causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT, resulting in a gln269_his270delinshis-tyr (Q269_H270delinsHY) change, associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The mutant bound more strongly to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade, showing that it is an activating mutation. Structural analyses suggested that the mutation may change the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains of SHOC2.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) reported that their patients with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed markedly from those with the S2G mutation: the proband lacked short stature, cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, and loose anagen hair, and her father had tall stature and very subtle features other than sparse hair and clubbed nails. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) proposed that constitutive membrane localization of myristoylated SHOC2(S2G) would prolong PP1C-mediated dephosphorylation of RAF1 at ser259, sustaining RAF1-driven MAPK activation. They also suggested that the association with loose anagen hair points to disturbed proliferation, survival, or differentiation of epithelial stem cell-derived cells in hair follicles.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that the absence of the common somatic JAK2 V617F mutation in their patient with primary myelofibrosis suggested that the SHOC2 mutation may have been the predisposing change and that RAS/MAPK activation may be an alternative initiating step toward myeloproliferative disorders; they suggested studying JAK2-negative myelofibrosis cases for the SHOC2 S2G mutation.

Hannig et al. (2014) suggested that, because the M173I variant causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway, deregulation of the pathway, rather than only its upregulation, is the critical factor in RASopathies.

Young et al. (2018) found that MRAS interacted with SHOC2 and protein phosphatase-1 (PP1) to form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylated the inhibitory S259 site on RAF kinases (S259 on CRAF, S365 on BRAF). MRAS and SHOC2 acted as PP1 regulatory subunits making the complex highly specific for RAF, and MRAS also acted as a targeting subunit, because membrane localization was needed for efficient RAF dephosphorylation and regulation of the ERK pathway. The predicted structure of SHOC2 closely resembles the A subunit of PP2A. Noonan syndrome mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB always increased formation of the ternary complex but did not necessarily increase binding to other interactors, and the authors concluded that Noonan syndrome in people with these mutations is likely driven at the biochemical level by enhanced formation of the complex.

Motta et al. (2019) found that SHOC2 Q269_H270delinsHY, unlike SHOC2 S2G, is not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane, and concluded that different mechanisms of SHOC2 dysregulation lead to the same result, upregulated MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

Tosti et al. (1997) found that the trichogram in every patient with loose anagen hair showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) without sheaths, whereas pathologic study of hair from 5 patients found no specific follicular abnormalities apart from frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath; they judged pathology to be of little diagnostic value.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve analysis method to detect the SHOC2 S2G mutation and proposed it as a useful screening tool for this apparently common mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that 4 of their 5 patients had been suspected of having Costello syndrome and that the ectodermal, growth, and developmental features of patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation subjectively resemble those of Costello syndrome due to the HRAS G13C mutation, supporting a gene panel approach to molecular testing in patients with a RASopathy phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Combining previously published series, Gripp et al. (2013) estimated that SHOC2 mutations occur in about 6% (12/188) of mutation-negative Noonan syndrome patients, or about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome.

### Animal Model

In C. elegans, Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype SHOC2, but not SHOC2(S2G), could functionally replace sur-8. Expression of SHOC2(S2G) at the early L3 stage caused protruding vulva, reduced egg laying, and a bag-of-worms phenotype; the same phenotype was seen with wildtype SHOC2 tagged with an N-myristoylation sequence, and both proteins were targeted to the cell membrane. The vulval defect was one of morphogenesis rather than vulval cell fate induction: descendants of P5.p and/or P7.p abnormally detached from the cuticle in 17 of 48 larvae expressing SHOC2(S2G) and in 10 of 22 expressing myristoylated wildtype SHOC2.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G]

In 25 sporadic subjects with Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation was de novo in all families with parental DNA available, with paternity confirmed, and was found in DNA from several tissues, indicating germline origin. The substitution created an N-myristoylation site, leading to myristoylation of the mutant protein, constitutive targeting to the cell membrane, failure to translocate to the nucleus after EGF stimulation, and cell type-specific enhancement of EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation. In C. elegans, SHOC2(S2G), unlike wildtype SHOC2, could not replace sur-8 and caused a vulval morphogenesis defect. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who were negative for mutations in other known genes. All 8 had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin; 6 had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in a male infant born at 32 weeks because of fetal distress, who had rapidly progressive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the heterozygous S2G mutation in 5 male patients, in whom it was absent from both parents of patient 1 and de novo in patient 2. Patient 1 developed primary myelofibrosis and died at age 2.5 years, and several patients had enlarged subarachnoid spaces, a small posterior fossa, and cerebellar tonsillar ectopia or Chiari I malformation.

**.0002 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry and her father, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The girl had right optic nerve hypoplasia, bilateral nystagmus, speech delay, hyperactive behavior, macrocephaly (98th centile), bilateral ptosis, sparse slow-growing hair that was not loose, wide-spaced nipples, and clubbed nails, with average stature and normal skin pigmentation. Her father was 6 feet 3 inches tall and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. In functional studies, the mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation by only about 10% above basal in SHOC2-depleted cells and bound PP1c poorly, indicating loss of function.

**.0003 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, GLN269_HIS270DELINSHISTYR [c.807_808delinstt; p.Q269_H270delinsHY]

Motta et al. (2019) reported a c.807_808delinsTT change in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a gln269_his270delinshis-tyr (Q269_H270delinsHY) change, associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The mutant bound more strongly to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased MAPK signaling, indicating an activating effect, but unlike S2G it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.
