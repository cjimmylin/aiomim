### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair combines features resembling Noonan syndrome with distinctive ectodermal abnormalities. Mazzanti et al. (2003) and Cordeddu et al. (2009) described short stature, often with growth hormone (GH) deficiency, macrocephaly, cognitive deficits with hyperactive behavior, a hypernasal voice, and cardiac anomalies. The hair is easily pluckable, sparse, thin and slow-growing (loose anagen hair), and the skin is darkly pigmented, often with eczema or ichthyosis. Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population.

Bertola et al. (2017) described a similar disorder caused by mutation in PPP1CB; see heterogeneity.

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed that their 3 patients might have a distinct, previously unreported syndrome and named it 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.' Motta et al. (2019) called the RASopathy caused by the recurrent SHOC2 p.Ser2Gly mutation 'Mazzanti syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. She had short, blond, easily pluckable hair that had never been cut. On trichogram, nearly all bulbs were abnormally shaped anagen bulbs without inner and outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked clefts between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

In a review of 19 patients with loose anagen hair (14 children and 5 adults), Tosti et al. (1997) included a 5-year-old boy who also had Noonan syndrome. In every patient the trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) without sheaths.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children with the same facial phenotype, which was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome. All 3 had short stature, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, and mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Two had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. They also shared a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented, hairless skin and hair defined as loose anagen hair. The authors felt that this combination, together with the behavioral phenotype and GH deficiency, fit no known condition.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) reviewed 25 individuals with a SHOC2 mutation and found a consistent phenotype. Facial features appeared typical of Noonan syndrome. Common features included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, low-set, posteriorly rotated ears, a short, webbed neck, and pectus anomalies. Growth was reduced, often with proven GH deficiency. Patients had cognitive deficits and a distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most. The hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin and slow-growing, and in 12 subjects loose anagen hair was confirmed by microscopic examination of pulled hairs. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis, and the voice was characteristically hypernasal. Most had cardiac anomalies; mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were significantly overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population. Referring pediatricians had felt that several patients had features suggestive of Costello syndrome or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome as newborns or young infants.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the SHOC2 p.S2G mutation. All had short stature, sparse hair and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that the clinical features partly overlapped with those of typical Noonan or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome and that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair was distinctive in SHOC2 mutation-positive patients.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a de novo SHOC2 p.Ser2Gly mutation. Fetal distress required premature delivery at 32 weeks. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed rapidly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors noted that his cardiac findings and features were not typical of previously reported patients with this mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 males with the SHOC2 p.Ser2Gly mutation. Four of them (Patients 1, 2, 3 and 5) had been suspected of having Costello syndrome. All 5 had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay. All 4 who were assessed had a structural cardiac anomaly. Hypotonia and macrocephaly occurred in 4 of 5. Three of 5 had polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, or required a feeding tube. None had been formally diagnosed with loose anagen hair by microscopy. All had sparse, very slow-growing hair of unruly texture and unusually light color, together with unusually long eyelashes. Patient 5 never had hair loss or easily pluckable hair.

Brain imaging in the Gripp et al. (2013) series showed relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces, which the authors considered most consistent with benign external hydrocephalus. All 4 patients assessed had a relatively small posterior fossa, indicated by a vertical tentorium. The authors suggested that this combination accounted for the high rate of cerebellar tonsillar ectopia. Patient 4, a boy of Mexican ancestry, had mild ventriculomegaly and periventricular nodular heterotopia. By age 6 years he had developed a Chiari I malformation, in the context of platybasia, basilar invagination, and fusion of the C3 and C4 vertebrae. He also had an atrial septal defect, severe eczema, and blond, easily pluckable hair that was much lighter than his family's. Including literature cases, 3 of 8 patients with MRI had a Chiari I malformation.

Patient 1 of Gripp et al. (2013) had polyhydramnios, a cystic hygroma, pyloric stenosis, mild pulmonic valve stenosis, and macrocephaly (+3 SD). At 2 years 3 months he was suspected of having juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia, but bone marrow findings were most consistent with primary myelofibrosis, and the JAK2 V617F mutation was absent. He died at 2.5 years after splenectomy in preparation for bone marrow transplantation. Patient 3 had biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aortic insufficiency, and mild pulmonic stenosis. He developed aggressive behavior and was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome at 9 years.

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found the heterozygous SHOC2 mutation in sporadic patients with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 and showed it to be de novo in all families with parental DNA available. De novo occurrence was also reported by Hoban et al. (2012) and Gripp et al. (2013). ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent p.Pro49Arg mutation in the PPP1CB gene. His hair was sparse, thin and slow-growing, like the hair in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair closely resembles the SHOC2-related disorder. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) used an in silico protein network analysis, seeded with known RAS-MAPK disease proteins, to rank candidate genes for Noonan syndrome. They resequenced the top candidate, SHOC2, in 96 Noonan syndrome patients without mutations in known genes. Four unrelated sporadic individuals carried a heterozygous c.4A-G transition predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. In 3 of these, parental DNA was available; the change was absent in both parents and paternity was confirmed. The mutation was present in DNA from several tissues, which indicated germline origin. Among 410 further mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, 21 had the same change, and it was de novo in all 12 families with parental DNA available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. In Cos-1 cells, the S2G mutant took up [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype SHOC2 and a disease-unrelated S2A mutant did not. The S2G mutant was targeted to the cell membrane and did not move to the nucleus after EGF stimulation. In Neuro2A cells it increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutation in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. Eight carried the S2G mutation. No SHOC2 mutations were found in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a premature infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) identified the de novo SHOC2 S2G mutation by clinical testing of genes for Noonan syndrome and related disorders.

Gripp et al. (2013) found the SHOC2 S2G mutation in 5 patients. In Patients 1 and 2 it was de novo; in Patient 1, testing of stored buccal DNA after death found the mutation, and it was absent from both parents.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous SHOC2 c.519G-A (M173I) variant in a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry and her father. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. The girl had macrocephaly, ptosis, optic nerve hypoplasia, nystagmus, speech delay, hyperactive behavior, and sparse, slow-growing hair that was not loose. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, or ichthyosis. Her father was tall and had intellectual disability, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. The authors concluded that this phenotype differed markedly from that of patients with the S2G mutation. In SHOC2-depleted cells, the M173I mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 activity only slightly and bound PP1c poorly, which suggested loss of function. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of Rasopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT (p.Gln269_His270delinsHisTyr), associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The mutant protein bound more strongly to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade. Unlike the S2G mutant, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) proposed that the membrane-targeted, myristoylated SHOC2 S2G protein would prolong PP1C-mediated dephosphorylation of RAF1 at ser259, sustaining RAF1-stimulated MAPK activation. Wildtype and mutant SHOC2 did not heterodimerize, which ruled out a dominant-negative effect through sequestration of the wildtype protein. The authors also suggested that the association with loose anagen hair points to disturbed proliferation, survival or differentiation of epithelial stem cell-derived cells in hair follicles.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that their patient with myelofibrosis lacked the common somatic JAK2 V617F mutation. They suggested that the SHOC2 mutation may have predisposed to the myelofibrosis and that Ras/MAPK activation may be an alternative initiating step toward myeloproliferative disorders.

Young et al (2018) showed that MRAS, SHOC2 and protein phosphatase-1 form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site of RAF kinases. Noonan syndrome mutations in SHOC2, MRAS and PPP1CB always promoted formation of this complex but did not necessarily increase binding to other interactors. The authors concluded that Noonan syndrome in individuals with these mutations is likely driven at the biochemical level by enhanced ternary complex formation.

Motta et al. (2019) concluded that different mechanisms of SHOC2 dysregulation lead to the same result, upregulated MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

Tosti et al. (1997) found that the pathology of hair from patients with loose anagen hair showed no specific features apart from frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath. They considered pathology of little diagnostic value; the trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs without sheaths.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve analysis method to detect the SHOC2 S2G mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that the ectodermal, growth and developmental features of patients with SHOC2 S2G subjectively resembled those of Costello syndrome caused by HRAS G13C. They supported a gene panel approach to molecular testing in patients with a rasopathy phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Assuming no overlap between the series of Cordeddu et al. (2009) and Komatsuzaki et al. (2010), Gripp et al. (2013) estimated that SHOC2 mutations occur in about 6% (12 of 188) of Noonan syndrome patients without a mutation in a known gene. They estimated this to be about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

In C. elegans, Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype SHOC2, but not SHOC2 S2G, could functionally replace SUR-8. Expressing SHOC2 S2G at the early L3 stage caused protruding vulva, reduced egg laying, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. The same phenotypes were seen with wildtype SHOC2 tagged with an N-myristoylation sequence. The defect affected vulval morphogenesis rather than vulval cell fate induction: descendants of P5.p and/or P7.p abnormally detached from the cuticle in 17 of 48 larvae expressing SHOC2 S2G and in 10 of 22 expressing myristoylated wildtype SHOC2.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G; p.Ser2Gly]

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 25 individuals with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair (NSLH1). Where parental DNA was available, the mutation was de novo: in 3 of 4 individuals in the first cohort and in 12 families in the second. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. The substitution creates an N-myristoylation site. The mutant protein took up [3H]-myristic acid, was targeted to the plasma membrane, did not translocate to the nucleus after EGF stimulation, and increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutation in other known genes.

Hoban et al. (2012) found the de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant with fetal distress and rapidly progressive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) found the c.4A-G (S2G) mutation in 5 males with NSLH1. It was de novo in 2 of them. One patient developed primary myelofibrosis without the JAK2 V617F mutation.
