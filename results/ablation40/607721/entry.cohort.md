### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is a RASopathy with a facial appearance suggestive of, but not completely typical of, Noonan syndrome. Affected individuals have reduced growth that is often associated with growth hormone (GH) deficiency, cognitive deficits, a distinctive hyperactive behavior, and ectodermal anomalies. These include easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair (loose anagen hair) and darkly pigmented skin. Cardiac anomalies and a hypernasal voice may also occur (Mazzanti et al., 2003; Cordeddu et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed that the combination of a facial appearance not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, a behavioral phenotype, GH deficiency, darkly pigmented hairless skin, and loose anagen hair was a distinct, previously unreported syndrome. They named it 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.'

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. She had short, blond, easily pluckable hair that had never been cut. The trichogram showed an absolute prevalence of abnormally shaped anagen bulbs lacking inner and outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reviewed 19 patients diagnosed with loose anagen hair (14 children and 5 adults). Associated conditions included Noonan syndrome in a 5-year-old boy. In all cases the trichogram showed a predominance of anagen hairs (80 to 100%) devoid of sheaths. The authors concluded that loose anagen hair may be sporadic, may occur together with developmental or acquired conditions, or, less commonly, may be familial.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children with short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, and severe GH deficiency. They also had mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder and a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented, hairless skin and loose anagen hair. Two of the children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) characterized 25 unrelated subjects with a SHOC2 mutation, all of them sporadic cases. The subjects had reduced growth, frequently with proven GH deficiency, and cognitive deficits. They showed a distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most of them. Their hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow-growing, and loose anagen hair was confirmed microscopically in 12. Some subjects had darkly pigmented skin, eczema, or ichthyosis. The voice was hypernasal. Other features in some subjects were macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, short webbed neck, and pectus anomalies. Cardiac anomalies were present in some. Mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population. Referring pediatricians felt that several subjects had features suggestive of Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome as newborns or young infants. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All of them had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a de novo SHOC2 S2G mutation. He presented with fetal distress that required premature delivery at 32 weeks. After birth he was noted to have dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. An echocardiogram showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiomyopathy progressed rapidly, and he was discharged for palliative care. The authors noted that his cardiac findings and other features were not typical of patients previously reported with this mutation, and they concluded that the case expanded the clinical phenotype.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 male patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay. All 4 with available data had a structural cardiac anomaly. Brain imaging showed relative megalencephaly, enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a small posterior fossa with a vertical tentorium. Four of the 5 patients had initially been suspected of having Costello syndrome. The light-colored, slow-growing scalp hair with dolichocilia resembled the hair seen in Costello syndrome caused by the HRAS gly13-to-cys mutation. Including their own and previously reported patients, Chiari 1 malformation was present in 3 of 8 SHOC2 mutation-positive patients with MRI data. The authors attributed it to relative megalencephaly combined with a small posterior fossa and noted that it can develop after birth.

Gripp et al. (2013) described each of their 5 patients. Patient 1 had a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and was born with a cystic hygroma and macrosomia. His course included pyloric stenosis and severe failure to thrive requiring a gastrostomy tube. He also had mild pulmonic valve stenosis, low axial tone, small optic nerves, and delayed motor development. Examination showed macrocephaly (OFC +3 SD), a large anterior fontanel, sparse thin hair, ptosis, hepatosplenomegaly, and hemangiomata. Brain imaging showed enlarged subarachnoid spaces, a vertical tentorium, mild cerebellar tonsillar ectopia, and a thin corpus callosum. Bone marrow biopsies showed markedly hypercellular marrow with a marked generalized increase in reticulin staining, consistent with myelofibrosis, and blasts were present in the peripheral blood. He died at age 2.5 years after splenectomy in preparation for a bone marrow transplant. Patient 2 was born to a 21-year-old mother and a 40-year-old father after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios, and he had increased birth weight. Macrocephaly, unilateral ptosis, posteriorly angulated ears, increased palmar wrinkling, loose skin, bilateral inguinal hernias, and hypotonia were noted, and Costello syndrome was considered. He had severe feeding difficulties and a subdural effusion requiring drainage. At 9 5/12 years he was nonverbal with intellectual disability, and his height was between the 3rd and 5th centiles. His skin findings included acanthosis nigricans and eczema. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, hypogenesis of the corpus callosum, decreased white matter volume, a vertical tentorium, and cerebellar tonsillar ectopia. ⚠︎

Patient 3 of Gripp et al. (2013) had polyhydramnios and macrosomia. Postnatal problems were tracheostenosis, chronic lung disease, and a neonatal seizure. He had right bundle branch block and mild to moderate biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy at 6 months of age, as well as aortic insufficiency and mild pulmonic stenosis. Failure to thrive required a gastrostomy. At age 9 years his height was 125 cm (10th centile), his weight 29.4 kg (50th centile), and his OFC 60 cm (+5 SD). At that age he was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome, and he showed aggressive behavior. He also had coarse facial features, osteoporosis, and strabismus. Patient 4 was of Mexican ancestry. He was born at 37 weeks' gestation to a 32-year-old mother and a 41-year-old father, with a weight of 2.9 kg. His medical problems included failure to thrive, elevated PT/PTT, rectal prolapse, chronic ear infections, an atrial septal defect, and trivial mitral regurgitation. He had severe eczema, sparse, thin, easily pluckable blond hair, and mild to moderate intellectual disability, and he was not macrocephalic. Brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly and subependymal heterotopia. When he was imaged again at 6 years for headaches, a Chiari 1 malformation had developed in the context of platybasia and basilar invagination, and there was segmental fusion of the C3 and C4 vertebrae. Patient 5 had neonatal hypotonia and feeding difficulties and used a nasogastric feeding tube until about age 1 year. He had a patent ductus arteriosus and an abnormal mitral valve with 3 papillary muscles and redundant chordae. Other findings were a duplicated renal collecting system and attention deficit disorder. At age 3 weeks, brain MRI showed a relatively small cerebellar vermis and hemispheres and increased extra-axial fluid spaces. His hair was slow-growing but not easily pluckable, and his OFC was +2 SD.

Hannig et al. (2014) reported a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry, born to nonconsanguineous parents. She was referred for right optic nerve hypoplasia, bilateral nystagmus, speech delay, poor hair growth, and hyperactive behavior. She had average stature (47th percentile), macrocephaly (98th percentile), and sparse, slow-growing hair that was not loose. Her skin pigmentation was normal. She also had bilateral ptosis, wide-spaced nipples, and clubbed nails. MRI showed irregularities in the midbrain, pons, and medulla. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair. Her father carried the same SHOC2 variant. He was 6 feet 3 inches tall and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. Echocardiograms and renal ultrasounds were normal in both father and daughter.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a patient with a novel SHOC2 mutation and prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NSLH1 is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that the heterozygous SHOC2 mutation had occurred de novo in sporadic cases. Gripp et al. (2013) and Hoban et al. (2012) also found de novo occurrence. Hannig et al. (2014) reported transmission of a heterozygous SHOC2 variant from a mildly affected father to his affected daughter. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent pro49-to-arg (P49R) mutation in the PPP1CB gene. His hair was sparse, thin, and slow-growing, similar to that seen in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair is very similar to Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair.

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified SHOC2 as a candidate gene through in silico protein network analysis. In 96 patients with Noonan syndrome and no mutation in the known genes, they found a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in SHOC2, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 4 unrelated individuals. They then screened 410 further mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or related phenotypes and found the same change in 21. The mutation had occurred de novo in 3 of the 4 subjects in the first cohort and in all 12 families of the second cohort for which parental DNA was available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found, which suggested a specific pathogenetic role for S2G. Functional studies showed that the S2G substitution creates an N-myristoylation site that targets the protein to the plasma membrane. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutation in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They found the S2G mutation in 8 of these patients. No SHOC2 mutations were found in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a premature male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) detected the de novo heterozygous SHOC2 S2G mutation. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the heterozygous SHOC2 S2G mutation in 5 patients. In patient 1, the mutation was found by postmortem research testing of stored buccal cell DNA and was absent from both parents' buccal cell DNA. In patient 2 it had also occurred de novo. Patient 4 was referred to the authors after molecular diagnosis of the SHOC2 mutation. ⚠︎

In a 5-year-old girl with a RASopathy phenotype, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in SHOC2, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. She had inherited the variant from her father, who also had mild features. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the M173I variant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation only about 10% above the basal level, whereas wildtype SHOC2 rescued it fully. The variant did not affect protein half-life, subcellular localization, or binding to M-RAS and RAF-1. However, the M173I mutant failed to precipitate endogenous PP1c effectively and supported only a mild increase in RAF-1 ser338 phosphorylation after EGF stimulation. The authors concluded that reduced binding of SHOC2 to PP1c caused the ERK1/2 pathway dysregulation and that the variant was a loss-of-function variant.

In a patient with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Motta et al. (2019) identified a c.807_808delinsTT mutation in SHOC2, predicting gln269_his270delinsHisTyr. They called it the third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation. The mutation was activating: the mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade. Structural analyses suggested that it may affect the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains of SHOC2. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) concluded that the clinical features of SHOC2 mutation-positive patients partly overlap with those of typical Noonan or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome. They also concluded that easily pluckable, loose anagen hair is distinctive in these patients.

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the phenotype of patients with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed significantly from that of patients with the S2G mutation. Their patients lacked short stature, cardiac anomalies, and loose anagen hair. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype SHOC2 was distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus in starved cells and was restricted to the nucleus after EGF stimulation. Growth factor-stimulated nuclear translocation of endogenous SHOC2 was confirmed in primary skin fibroblasts. The S2G substitution created an N-myristoylation site: SHOC2 S2G incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, whereas the wildtype protein and an S2A mutant did not. The S2G mutant was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane and failed to translocate to the nucleus upon EGF stimulation. Its membrane localization was reduced by the N-myristoyltransferase inhibitor 2-hydroxymyristic acid. SHOC2 S2G did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2, which excluded a dominant-negative effect. Its expression enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells, indicating cell type-specific MAPK activation.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported the first hematologic neoplasm in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation, myelofibrosis in a 2-year-old. Because the patient lacked the JAK2 val617-to-phe mutation, the authors suggested that the SHOC2 mutation may have predisposed to myelofibrosis through activation of the Ras/MAPK pathway. They proposed testing JAK2-negative myelofibrosis cases for the SHOC2 S2G mutation.

Young et al. (2018) found that MRAS interacts with SHOC2 and protein phosphatase-1 (PP1) to form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme. This holoenzyme dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site of RAF kinases (S259 on CRAF, S365 on BRAF). Noonan syndrome mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB all promoted formation of this complex but did not necessarily increase binding to other interactors. The authors concluded that, at the biochemical level, Noonan syndrome in individuals with SHOC2, MRAS, or PPP1CB mutations is likely driven by enhanced ternary complex formation.

Motta et al. (2019) showed that, unlike the recurrent S2G mutant, the SHOC2 Q269_H270delinsHY mutant was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane. They concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 functional dysregulation converge toward upregulation of MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

In all patients with loose anagen hair studied by Tosti et al. (1997), the trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) lacking sheaths. Pathologic study of hair from 5 patients found no morphologic abnormalities of the hair follicles apart from frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath. The authors concluded that pathology is of little diagnostic value.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve analysis method to detect the SHOC2 S2G mutation. They proposed it as a useful screening tool for this apparently common mutation.

### Population Genetics

Gripp et al. (2013) pooled 2 earlier case series and estimated that SHOC2 mutations occur in about 6% (12 of 188) of patients with Noonan syndrome who lack a mutation in other known genes. They calculated that this corresponds to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome.

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that in C. elegans, wildtype human SHOC2 rescued sur-8 function but SHOC2 S2G did not. Expressing SHOC2 S2G or myristoylation-tagged wildtype SHOC2 at early L3 caused protruding vulva, egg-laying defects, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. These resulted from abnormal vulval morphogenesis, and vulval cell fate specification was not altered.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G]

Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 25 unrelated sporadic subjects with Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. Four were from a cohort of 96 mutation-negative Noonan syndrome patients and 21 from a further 410 mutation-negative subjects. The mutation had occurred de novo in 3 of the 4 subjects in the first cohort and in all 12 families of the second cohort for which parental DNA was available. The substitution created an N-myristoylation site. The mutant protein incorporated myristic acid, was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane, and failed to translocate to the nucleus upon EGF stimulation. It did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2, and it enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells. Unlike wildtype SHOC2, it did not rescue sur-8 function in C. elegans. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutation in other RAS/MAPK pathway genes. All 8 had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified the de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant. He presented with fetal distress, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and rapidly progressive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the heterozygous S2G mutation in 5 male patients with NSLH1. The mutation was shown to be de novo in 2 of them. Their findings included structural brain anomalies, such as a small posterior fossa with a vertical tentorium and Chiari 1 malformation. One patient also had myelofibrosis. ⚠︎

**.0002 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old European girl, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. She had optic nerve hypoplasia, nystagmus, speech delay, sparse slow-growing hair, hyperactive behavior, and macrocephaly, but no short stature or loose anagen hair. She had inherited the variant from her father, who had intellectual disability, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation only about 10% above basal levels. It also failed to precipitate endogenous PP1c effectively. The authors concluded that M173I is a loss-of-function variant.

**.0003 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, GLN269_HIS270DELINSHISTYR [c.807_808delinstt; p.Q269_H270delinsHY]

In a patient with a RASopathy and prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Motta et al. (2019) identified a c.807_808delinsTT mutation in the SHOC2 gene, predicting gln269_his270delinsHisTyr. The mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased MAPK signaling. Unlike the S2G mutant, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane. ⚠︎
