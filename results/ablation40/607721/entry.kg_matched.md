### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is a RASopathy. Its features include Noonan syndrome-like facial features, macrocephaly, short neck with redundant skin, short stature often with growth hormone (GH) deficiency, cognitive deficits with hyperactive behavior, cardiac anomalies, and distinctive ectodermal findings. These ectodermal findings include darkly pigmented skin and sparse, thin, slow-growing, easily pluckable hair that shows the features of loose anagen hair (Mazzanti et al., 2003; Cordeddu et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) noted that their 3 patients did not look completely typical of Noonan syndrome. The behavioral phenotype, GH deficiency, darkly pigmented hairless skin, and loose anagen hair did not fit any known condition. They suggested that the children might represent a distinct, previously unreported syndrome and named it 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.'

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with Noonan syndrome and loose anagen hair. Her hair was short and blond, had never been cut, and came out easily when pulled. On trichogram, almost all hairs had abnormally shaped anagen bulbs without inner or outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked clefts between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) described 19 patients with loose anagen hair (LAH), 14 children and 5 adults aged 8 months to 47 years, who were diagnosed at a university medical center from January 1990. One of them, a 5-year-old boy, also had Noonan syndrome. In all patients the trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) without sheaths. Pathologic study of hair from 5 patients showed no morphologic abnormalities of the hair follicles other than frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath, and the authors judged these findings nonspecific and of little diagnostic value. Three adults, each the only affected member of the family, had developed diffuse hair shedding suddenly 1 to 3 years before evaluation. From this the authors concluded that LAH does not arise only in childhood and that adult-onset LAH can be mistaken for telogen hair loss. In 2 families, an affected adult was the parent of an affected child. The authors concluded that LAH may be sporadic, may occur together with developmental or acquired conditions, or, less often, may be familial. ⚠︎

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children who shared short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, and mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Two of the children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. All 3 had an unusual combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented, hairless skin and hair with the appearance described as loose anagen hair.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that 25 individuals with a SHOC2 mutation shared a consistent phenotype, previously termed Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair. Their facial features looked typical of Noonan syndrome and included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, and low-set, posteriorly rotated ears. They also had short webbed necks and pectus anomalies. Other features were reduced growth, often with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most patients, and easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies were present in most, and mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were more frequent than in the general Noonan syndrome population. The voice was hypernasal. As newborns or young infants, several patients had been thought to have features suggestive of Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome. In 12 of the mutation-positive patients, microscopic examination of pulled hairs confirmed loose anagen hair.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported that all 8 of their patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six of the 8 had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that the clinical features of SHOC2 mutation-positive patients partly overlap with those of typical Noonan or CFC syndrome, and that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair is distinctive in these patients.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a SHOC2 mutation who presented with fetal distress that required premature delivery at 32 weeks. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed rapidly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors noted that his cardiac and other findings were not typical of previously reported individuals with this SHOC2 mutation and therefore expanded the clinical phenotype.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 unrelated male patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay. All 4 who were assessed had a structural cardiac anomaly. Four of the 5 had hypotonia and macrocephaly, and 3 of the 5 had polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, or needed a feeding tube.

Patient 1 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy born by cesarean section at 38 weeks for macrosomia. The pregnancy was complicated by polyhydramnios and a third-trimester cystic hygroma. He had pyloric stenosis that needed surgical repair, severe failure to thrive that required a gastrostomy at 5 months, mild pulmonic valve stenosis, low axial tone, and motor delay. At 2 years 4 months his OFC was +3 SD, and he had sparse, very fine hair, ptosis, downslanting palpebral fissures, a short neck, hepatosplenomegaly, hemangiomas, and numerous fine wrinkles on the hands. Juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia was suspected at 2 years 3 months, but bone marrow biopsy findings were most consistent with primary myelofibrosis. He died at 2.5 years after a splenectomy performed in preparation for bone marrow transplant.

Patient 2 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy born at term after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios, with a birth weight at the 95th centile. He had severe early feeding difficulties and failure to thrive, macrocephaly, ptosis, posteriorly angulated ears, increased palmar wrinkling, loose skin, bilateral inguinal hernias, and hypotonia. Presumed bilateral chronic subdural hematomas were drained. He had marked intellectual disability: he walked independently at 5 years and used only a few single words at 9 years. At 9 years 5 months he had short stature, OFC +2 SD, sparse hair, deep palmar creases, joint laxity, a hyperpigmented lesion, and apparent acanthosis nigricans. ⚠︎

Patient 3 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy born at 36 weeks after polyhydramnios and fetal macrosomia. As a neonate he had tracheostenosis, chronic lung disease, and a seizure. He later developed right bundle branch block, biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aortic insufficiency, and mild pulmonic stenosis, and he needed a gastrostomy tube. He had marked macrocephaly (OFC +5 SD at 9 years), developmental delay, aggressive behavior, pervasive developmental disorder, and Tourette syndrome. His hair was coarse and straight.

Patient 4 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy of Mexican ancestry. He had poor growth, short stature, global developmental delay with mild to moderate intellectual disability, an atrial septal defect, rectal prolapse, strabismus, and nystagmus. His sparse blond hair was easily pluckable and much lighter than his family's hair, and his eyelashes were very long. He also had a webbed neck, pectus excavatum, ligamentous laxity, fine palmar wrinkles, and cafe-au-lait macules. Brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly and subependymal (periventricular nodular) heterotopia. By 6 years he had developed a Chiari I malformation, together with platybasia, basilar invagination, C3-C4 vertebral fusion, and anomalous venous anatomy.

Patient 5 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy with neonatal hypotonia and feeding difficulties who needed nasogastric tube feeding until about 1 year of age. He had a patent ductus arteriosus, an abnormal mitral valve with 3 papillary muscles, short stature, macrocephaly (OFC +2 SD), pectus excavatum, hemangiomas, dry hyperpigmented skin, and attention deficit disorder. Brain MRI at 3 weeks showed a relatively small cerebellum and enlarged extraaxial fluid spaces. His hair grew slowly, but he never had hair loss or easily pluckable hair.

Gripp et al. (2013) found distinctive brain abnormalities in their SHOC2 mutation patients. All 4 patients assessed had relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium. Three of the 4 had cerebellar tonsillar ectopia, which the authors attributed to the combination of a large brain and a small posterior fossa. Combining their series with the literature, 3 of 8 patients who had MRI had a Chiari I malformation, and 2 of these malformations appeared to have developed after birth. One patient had periventricular nodular heterotopia. Gripp et al. (2013) also reported myelofibrosis in a 2-year-old with the SHOC2 mutation, the first hematologic neoplasm described in a SHOC2 mutation-positive patient. His bone marrow tested negative for the somatic JAK2 V617F mutation. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that the SHOC2 S2G mutation occurred de novo in all families with parental DNA available, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Gripp et al. (2013) also found that the mutation was de novo in 2 patients. Hannig et al. (2014) reported a heterozygous SHOC2 variant (M173I) in a girl with a mild RASopathy and in her mildly affected father. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with a Noonan-like syndrome who carried the recurrent p.Pro49Arg mutation in the PPP1CB gene. His hair was sparse, thin, and slow-growing, like the ectodermal finding typical of Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair is very similar to Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair. He was the fifth individual reported with a PPP1CB mutation and the fourth with p.Pro49Arg, which suggested a mutational hotspot. He also had craniosynostosis, which is rare in RASopathies.

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) chose SHOC2 as the best candidate gene using in silico protein network analysis seeded with known RAS-MAPK mutant proteins. They sequenced SHOC2 in 96 individuals with Noonan syndrome who had no mutations in known genes. They found a heterozygous 4A-G transition, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 4 unrelated individuals, all of whom were sporadic cases. In the 3 with parental DNA available, the parents did not carry the mutation and paternity was confirmed, showing that the mutation was de novo. The mutation was present in DNA from several tissues, consistent with germline origin. The authors then sequenced SHOC2 in another 410 mutation-negative individuals with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype and found the same S2G change in 21 of them. It was de novo in all 12 families with parental DNA available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found, which the authors took as strong evidence that the S2G substitution has a specific pathogenic role. In total, 25 individuals carried the mutation. Functional studies showed that the mutation caused aberrant N-myristoylation and constitutive membrane targeting of SHOC2. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They found the S2G mutation in 8 patients. They found no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

Hoban et al. (2012) used clinical testing of the genes that cause Noonan syndrome and related disorders in a male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. They identified a de novo SHOC2 mutation predicting ser2-to-gly, a change previously reported as pathogenic.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the recurrent SHOC2 S2G mutation in 5 unrelated boys. It was de novo in patients 1 and 2. Sequencing of other RAS/MAPK pathway genes, including PTPN11, SOS1, RAF1, KRAS, and HRAS, gave normal results. In patient 1, the mutation was found after his death by research testing of stored buccal-cell DNA. ⚠︎

Hannig et al. (2014) used RASopathy gene panel sequencing in a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with a mild RASopathy. They identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. Her father, who had subtle features, also carried the variant. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals, and chromosome microarray was negative. When expressed in SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation to only about 10% above basal levels, whereas wildtype SHOC2 fully rescued it. The mutant also failed to precipitate endogenous PP1c effectively.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT (gln269_his270delinsHisTyr), which was associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. In functional studies, the mutant protein bound MRAS and PPP1CB more strongly and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade, supporting an activating effect.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the phenotype associated with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed significantly from that of patients with the S2G mutation. Their proband had craniofacial features suggestive of Noonan syndrome and sparse, slow-growing hair more typical of CFC syndrome. She did not have short stature, cardiac anomalies, or loose anagen hair. Her father, who also carried the variant, was 6 feet 3 inches tall and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails, and his echocardiogram was normal. The authors described the father's features as very subtle and, apart from the sparse hair and clubbed nails, not clearly indicating a RASopathy. They concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that the S2G substitution creates an N-terminal glycine that makes SHOC2 a substrate for N-myristoyltransferase (NMT). In Cos-1 cells, SHOC2 S2G incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype SHOC2 and a disease-unrelated S2A mutant did not. Wildtype SHOC2 was distributed through the cytoplasm and nucleus in starved cells and moved to the nucleus after EGF stimulation. In contrast, S2G was targeted to the cell membrane in both states. This was confirmed in 293T and Neuro2A cells and by cell fractionation. The NMT inhibitor 2-hydroxymyristic acid reduced or abolished the membrane localization. Even without efficient myristoylation, the mutant did not move to the nucleus. Wildtype SHOC2 and the S2G mutant did not heterodimerize, which ruled out a dominant-negative effect through sequestration of wildtype protein at the membrane. S2G did not significantly change ERK activation in Cos-1 or 293T cells. In Neuro2A neuroblastoma cells, however, it increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation compared with wildtype SHOC2, indicating cell type-specific enhancement of MAPK activation. Citing SHOC2's role as a regulatory subunit of PP1C, the authors proposed that membrane-bound S2G would prolong PP1C-mediated dephosphorylation of RAF1 at ser259 and so sustain RAF1-driven MAPK activation. They also suggested that loose anagen hair reflects disturbed proliferation, survival, or differentiation of epithelial stem cell-derived cells in hair follicles.

Gripp et al. (2013) suggested that germline or somatic SHOC2 mutations may be causally involved in myelofibrosis, with activation of the RAS/MAPK pathway acting as an alternative initiating step. They proposed testing JAK2 mutation-negative cases of myelofibrosis for SHOC2 S2G.

Hannig et al. (2014) found that the SHOC2 M173I mutant was expressed at a level similar to wildtype and had a normal protein half-life and normal subcellular localization. It still formed a complex with M-RAS and RAF-1 but failed to precipitate endogenous PP1c effectively. EGF-induced RAF-1 ser338 phosphorylation increased only mildly in cells expressing the mutant. The authors concluded that M173I causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway through reduced binding to the catalytic subunit of PP1c. They suggested that deregulation of the ERK1/2 pathway, rather than upregulation alone, is the critical factor in causing RASopathies.

Young et al. (2018) showed that SHOC2 and MRAS act as regulatory subunits of protein phosphatase-1 (PP1). Together they form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that specifically dephosphorylates the inhibitory 'S259' site on RAF kinases (S259 on CRAF, S365 on BRAF), leading to RAF activation. MRAS also acts as a targeting subunit, because the complex must be at the membrane to dephosphorylate RAF efficiently and to regulate the ERK pathway in cells. The predicted structure of SHOC2 closely resembles the A subunit of PP2A, which the authors suggested is a case of convergent structural evolution. Noonan syndrome-associated mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB invariably promoted formation of the complex among these 3 proteins but did not necessarily increase binding to other interactors. The authors concluded that Noonan syndrome in people with SHOC2, MRAS, or PPP1CB mutations is likely driven at the biochemical level by enhanced formation of this ternary complex.

Motta et al. (2019) found in structural analyses that the SHOC2 gln269_his270delinsHisTyr mutation might affect the relative orientation of the protein's 2 leucine-rich repeat domains. Unlike S2G, the mutant was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane. The authors concluded that different mechanisms of SHOC2 functional dysregulation converge on upregulated MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) confirmed loose anagen hair by microscopic examination of pulled hairs in 12 mutation-positive patients.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve analysis method to detect the SHOC2 S2G mutation and proposed it as a useful screening tool for this apparently common mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that Costello syndrome was initially suspected in 4 of their 5 patients. The patients' ectodermal, growth, and developmental features, including light-colored, slow-growing, unruly scalp hair with dolichocilia, closely resembled those of Costello syndrome caused by HRAS G13C. The authors said this overlap supports gene panel-based molecular testing in patients with a RASopathy phenotype. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Based on earlier case series, Gripp et al. (2013) estimated that SHOC2 mutations occur in about 6% (12 of 188) of Noonan syndrome patients without a molecular diagnosis, which corresponds to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome.

### Animal Model

In C. elegans, Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype SHOC2 could substitute for SUR-8 in a sur-8(rf);let-60(gf) background, but SHOC2 S2G could not. Expressing S2G in let-60(gf) worms did not suppress the multivulva phenotype, which excluded a dominant-negative effect. In wildtype worms, expressing S2G at the early L3 stage caused abnormal vulval development, with protruding vulva, reduced egg laying, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. Myristoylation-tagged wildtype SHOC2 caused the same effects, but untagged wildtype SHOC2 did not. Both S2G and myr::SHOC2 were targeted to the plasma membrane. The defects reflected abnormal vulval morphogenesis rather than altered cell fate specification: P5.p and/or P7.p descendants detached from the cuticle in 17 of 48 S2G larvae and in 10 of 22 myr::SHOC2 larvae.
