### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is characterized by Noonan-like facial features, including macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, and low-set posteriorly rotated ears. Patients also have a short webbed neck and pectus anomalies. Other features include reduced growth, often with proven growth hormone (GH) deficiency, cognitive deficits, and hyperactive behavior that improves with age. The hair is easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow-growing, and is loose anagen hair on microscopy. Most patients have darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis, cardiac anomalies are common, and the voice is hypernasal (Cordeddu et al., 2009). ⚠︎

Mazzanti et al. (2003) concluded that the combination of a facial appearance not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, a behavioral phenotype, GH deficiency, darkly pigmented hairless skin, and loose anagen hair did not fit any known condition. They suggested that it might represent a distinct syndrome.

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed the name 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair' for the disorder they observed in 3 children. Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that individuals with the SHOC2 S2G mutation shared a consistent phenotype that had previously been given this name.

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with Noonan syndrome and loose anagen hair. Her hair was short and blond, had never been cut, and was easily plucked. On trichogram, nearly all bulbs were abnormally shaped anagen bulbs that lacked inner and outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reported 19 patients who had been diagnosed with loose anagen hair (LAH) at a university medical center since 1990. There were 14 children and 5 adults, aged 8 months to 47 years. Associated diseases included Noonan syndrome in a 5-year-old boy and alopecia areata in a 3-year-old boy. In all cases, the trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) without sheaths. LAH could begin in adulthood: 3 sporadic adults had diffuse hair shedding that started suddenly 1 to 3 years before evaluation. The authors noted that adult-onset LAH can easily be misdiagnosed as telogen hair loss. On follow-up, LAH remained stable in 4 children and 1 adult, and hair density improved considerably in 2 children and 1 adult.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children with short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, and enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces. They also had a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, and mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Two of the children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. All 3 had an unusual combination of ectodermal abnormalities, including darkly pigmented hairless skin and hair defined as loose anagen hair.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that 25 individuals with the SHOC2 S2G mutation shared a consistent phenotype. Facial features were Noonan-like, with macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, and low-set posteriorly rotated ears. Patients also had a short webbed neck and pectus anomalies. Growth was reduced, frequently with proven GH deficiency. They had cognitive deficits and hyperactive behavior that improved with age. The hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow-growing, and loose anagen hair was confirmed microscopically in 12 patients. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies were common, with mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects overrepresented, and the voice was hypernasal. In infancy, several patients had been thought to resemble Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported that all 8 of their patients with the SHOC2 p.S2G mutation had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair is distinctive in SHOC2 mutation-positive patients, whose features partially overlap with those of typical Noonan or CFC syndrome.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a SHOC2 mutation who was delivered prematurely at 32 weeks because of fetal distress. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiogram showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed quickly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors stated that his cardiac findings and other features were not typical of previously reported individuals with this mutation and expanded the clinical phenotype.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 unrelated male patients with Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair who carried the SHOC2 p.Ser2Gly mutation. All 5 had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, developmental delay, and unusually long eyelashes (dolichocilia). All 4 patients who were assessed had a structural cardiac anomaly. Hypotonia and macrocephaly were each present in 4 of 5 patients. Polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, and use of a feeding tube were each present in 3 of 5. The authors described distinctive brain abnormalities: relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium (4 of 4). They suggested that this combination accounted for the high rate of cerebellar tonsillar ectopia (3 of 4). Including cases from the literature, 3 of 8 patients who had MRI showed a Chiari I malformation. ⚠︎

Patient 1 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy born to 37-year-old parents after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and cystic hygroma. He was macrosomic at birth. He had pyloric stenosis, severe failure to thrive needing a gastrostomy tube, mild pulmonic valve stenosis, low axial tone, small optic nerves, motor delay, and an OFC of +3 SD. Juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia was suspected at age 2 years 3 months. He developed hepatosplenomegaly, and bone marrow biopsy was most consistent with primary myelofibrosis. The karyotype was normal, and there was no monosomy 7 and no JAK2 p.Val617Phe mutation. He died at age 2.5 years after a splenectomy performed in preparation for bone marrow transplant. This was the first hematologic neoplasm reported in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation.

Patient 3 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy who had biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and right bundle branch block at age 6 months. He later developed aortic insufficiency and mild pulmonic stenosis, and his OFC was +5 SD at age 9 years. He had severe aggressive behavior and was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome, for which he took psychotropic medication.

Patient 4 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy of Mexican ancestry with global developmental delay and mild to moderate intellectual disability. He had an atrial septal defect, blond, sparse, easily pluckable hair, and long eyelashes. Brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly, periventricular nodular heterotopia, and a thick, dysplastic corpus callosum. By age 6 years he had developed a Chiari I malformation with platybasia and basilar invagination, together with C3-C4 vertebral fusion and unusual venous sinus anatomy. Periventricular nodular heterotopia had not previously been reported in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation.

Hannig et al. (2014) reported a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry, born to nonconsanguineous parents, who carried a SHOC2 M173I variant. She had right optic nerve hypoplasia, bilateral nystagmus, speech delay, poor hair growth, and hyperactive behavior. Her stature was average. She had macrocephaly (98th percentile), sparse and slow-growing hair that was not loose, normal skin pigmentation, bilateral ptosis, widely spaced nipples, and clubbed nails. MRI showed multiple irregularities of unknown significance in the midbrain, pons, and medulla. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair. Her father carried the same variant. He was tall (6 feet 3 inches) and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. Apart from the sparse hair and clubbed nails, his features were very subtle and did not clearly indicate a RASopathy. Echocardiograms and renal ultrasounds were negative in both the father and the proband.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous SHOC2 S2G mutation occurred de novo in all patients whose parents were tested, including those reported by Cordeddu et al. (2009), Hoban et al. (2012), and Gripp et al. (2013). Hannig et al. (2014) found that a girl inherited a heterozygous SHOC2 M173I variant from her mildly affected father. ⚠︎

Among the patients with LAH reported by Tosti et al. (1997), 2 adults were the parents of 2 affected children. The other 3 adults were the only members of their families with LAH. The authors concluded that LAH may be sporadic, may occur together with developmental or acquired conditions, or, less commonly, may be familial.

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent p.Pro49Arg mutation in PPP1CB, a gene newly associated with RASopathies. His sparse, thin, slow-growing hair resembled the ectodermal finding typical of Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair is very similar to Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair.

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) used an in silico protein network analysis, seeded with known mutant proteins of the RAS/MAPK pathway, to prioritize SHOC2 as a candidate gene for Noonan syndrome. They sequenced SHOC2 in 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who did not have mutations in known genes. In 4 unrelated sporadic individuals, they identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. In the 3 families tested, the mutation was absent from the parents, and paternity was confirmed, indicating de novo origin. The mutation was present in several tissues, indicating germline origin. In a further 410 mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, they found the S2G change in 21 patients. De novo occurrence was proven in the 12 families with available parental DNA. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found, suggesting a specific pathogenetic role for S2G; in total, 25 subjects carried the mutation. In functional studies, SHOC2 S2G expressed in Cos-1 cells incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype SHOC2 and an S2A mutant unrelated to disease did not. This showed that the substitution creates an N-myristoylation site. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They found the previously identified p.S2G mutation in 8 patients. They identified no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In an infant with Noonan syndrome and fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo missense mutation in the SHOC2 gene predicting p.Ser2Gly. The mutation was found by clinical testing of genes that cause Noonan syndrome and related disorders.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the recurrent SHOC2 c.4A-G mutation (p.Ser2Gly) in 5 patients with Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair. In patient 1, the mutation was found by postmortem testing of buccal DNA and was absent from both parents. The mutation was also de novo in patient 2. Sequencing of other RASopathy genes was normal in the patients.

Using a RASopathy gene panel, Hannig et al. (2014) found a heterozygous c.519G-A variant in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution, in a girl and her father. The proband had inherited the variant from her father. It was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. Met173 is located in leucine-rich repeat 4 and is conserved among vertebrate SHOC2 orthologs. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, wildtype SHOC2 fully rescued EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation. The M173I mutant restored it to only about 10% above basal levels. The mutant's half-life, subcellular localization, and binding to M-RAS and RAF1 were unchanged. However, it failed to precipitate PP1c effectively and produced only a mild increase in RAF1 Ser338 phosphorylation. The authors concluded that p.M173I causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT (p.Gln269_His270delinsHisTyr), which was associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Structural analyses indicated that the mutation might affect the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains. In functional studies, the mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade, supporting an activating role. Unlike SHOC2 S2G, the mutant was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the patients with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed markedly from patients with the S2G mutation. The proband had Noonan syndrome-like craniofacial features and CFC-like sparse hair. However, she did not have short stature, cardiac defects, or loose anagen hair. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype SHOC2 was distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus in starved Cos-1 cells and translocated to the nucleus upon EGF stimulation. This translocation was also seen for endogenous SHOC2 in primary fibroblasts. In contrast, SHOC2 S2G was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane and failed to translocate to the nucleus. This targeting depended on myristoylation and was reversed by an NMT inhibitor. The mutant did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2, excluding a dominant-negative effect. It enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells, indicating cell type-specific activation of the MAPK pathway. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that their patient with myelofibrosis lacked the common somatic JAK2 mutation, which is present in most patients with myelofibrosis. They suggested that germline or somatic SHOC2 mutation may be causally involved in myelofibrosis through activation of the RAS/MAPK pathway.

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS interacts with SHOC2 and protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) to form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme. This complex dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site on RAF kinases (S259 on CRAF, S365 on BRAF). MRAS and SHOC2 acted as PP1 regulatory subunits that make the complex highly specific for RAF. MRAS also acted as a targeting subunit, because the complex needed membrane localization to dephosphorylate RAF efficiently and to regulate the ERK pathway in cells. The predicted structure of SHOC2 closely resembles the A subunit of PP2A. The authors identified several regions in SHOC2 involved in complex formation, as well as residues in MRAS that help explain why MRAS binds specifically to SHOC2-PP1. Noonan syndrome mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB always promoted formation of the complex with the other 2 partners, but did not necessarily promote binding to other interactors. The authors concluded that, at the biochemical level, Noonan syndrome in people with SHOC2, MRAS, or PPP1CB mutations is likely driven by enhanced formation of the ternary complex.

Motta et al. (2019) concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 functional dysregulation converge toward upregulation of MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

Tosti et al. (1997) studied hair pathology in 5 patients with LAH. Apart from frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath, they found no morphologic abnormalities of the hair follicles. They concluded that pathologic findings are of little diagnostic value in LAH.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid detection system for the SHOC2 p.S2G mutation, based on melting curve analysis, to screen for this apparently common mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that 4 of their 5 patients had originally been suspected of having Costello syndrome. In their subjective assessment, the ectodermal, growth, and developmental features of patients with SHOC2 p.Ser2Gly closely resembled those of Costello syndrome due to HRAS p.Gly13Cys. They supported a gene panel approach to molecular testing.

### Population Genetics

Gripp et al. (2013) combined data from earlier case series, in which 12 of 188 mutation-negative patients with Noonan or Noonan-like syndrome had SHOC2 mutations. From these data they estimated that SHOC2 mutations account for about 6% of mutation-negative Noonan syndrome, or about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome.

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that, in C. elegans with a sur-8rf;let-60gof background, wildtype human SHOC2 could functionally replace SUR-8, whereas S2G could not. S2G did not act as a dominant negative. Expression of SHOC2 S2G or myristoylation-tagged wildtype SHOC2 at early L3 caused protruding vulva, egg-laying defects, and bag-of-worms phenotypes. These resulted from abnormal vulval morphogenesis, with asymmetric invaginations involving P5.p/P7.p descendants in 17 of 48 S2G larvae, and vulval cell fate specification was not altered. Both proteins were targeted to the membrane in worm cells.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G]

Cordeddu et al. (2009) sequenced SHOC2 in 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who did not have mutations in known genes. In 4 unrelated sporadic individuals, they identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation was absent from the parents in the 3 families tested and was present in several tissues. In a further 410 mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, they found the mutation in 21 patients. It occurred de novo in all 12 families with available parental DNA. The substitution created an N-myristoylation site: the mutant protein incorporated [3H]-myristic acid in Cos-1 cells. It was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane in a myristoylation-dependent manner and failed to translocate to the nucleus upon EGF stimulation. It enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the p.S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in other known genes.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo p.Ser2Gly mutation in SHOC2 in a premature male infant with Noonan syndrome and fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the c.4A-G (p.Ser2Gly) mutation in 5 male patients with Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair. The mutation was de novo in 2 of them, and sequencing of other RASopathy genes was normal. One patient developed primary myelofibrosis and died at age 2.5 years.

**.0002 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with a RASopathy phenotype and in her father, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A variant in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The variant was found by sequencing a RASopathy gene panel, and the proband had inherited it from her father.
