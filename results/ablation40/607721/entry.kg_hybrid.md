### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is a RASopathy. Its features resemble Noonan syndrome, and it also has distinctive ectodermal abnormalities (Mazzanti et al., 2003; Motta et al., 2019). Mazzanti et al. (2003) described affected children with short stature, macrocephaly, a short neck with redundant skin, severe growth hormone (GH) deficiency, mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), darkly pigmented hairless skin, and loose anagen hair. In a larger series of mutation-positive individuals, Cordeddu et al. (2009) also noted cardiac anomalies, with mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects overrepresented, and a hypernasal voice.

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed that their 3 patients might represent a distinct, previously unreported syndrome, which they named 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.' Motta et al. (2019) referred to the disorder caused by the recurrent SHOC2 S2G mutation as Mazzanti syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with Noonan syndrome and loose anagen hair. Her hair was short and blond, was easily pluckable, and had never been cut. On trichogram, almost all hairs had abnormally shaped anagen bulbs that lacked inner and outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reviewed 19 patients with loose anagen hair (14 children and 5 adults, aged 8 months to 47 years). Associated conditions were Noonan syndrome in a 5-year-old boy and alopecia areata in a 3-year-old boy. In all cases, a trichogram showed a predominance of anagen hairs (80 to 100%) without sheaths. Pathologic study of hair from 5 patients showed no morphologic abnormalities of the hair follicles apart from a high incidence of fragmentation of the inner root sheath. The authors concluded that the pathologic findings had no specific features and were of little diagnostic value.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children who shared the same facial phenotype. All had short stature, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, and mild psychomotor delay with ADHD. Two of the 3 had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. The children also had a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented, hairless skin and hair of an unusual appearance defined as loose anagen hair. The authors noted that the children's appearance was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome. ⚠︎

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that 25 individuals carrying the SHOC2 S2G mutation had a relatively consistent phenotype. Their facial features looked typical of Noonan syndrome and included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, and low-set, posteriorly rotated ears. Common features also included a short, webbed neck and pectus anomalies. Patients had reduced growth, often with proven GH deficiency, as well as cognitive deficits and distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most. Their hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow-growing. In 12 patients, loose anagen hair was confirmed by microscopic examination of pulled hairs. Most had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Most also had cardiac anomalies, and mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were significantly overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population. The voice was characteristically hypernasal. In several patients, the referring pediatricians had thought that the newborn or young infant had features suggestive of Costello syndrome or cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All 8 had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that the clinical features of SHOC2 mutation-positive patients partly overlap with those of typical Noonan syndrome or CFC syndrome, and that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair is distinctive in these patients.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. Fetal distress required premature delivery at 32 weeks. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed rapidly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors noted that his cardiac findings and other features were not typical of previously reported individuals with this mutation and expanded the clinical phenotype. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 male patients with the SHOC2 p.Ser2Gly mutation. All had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay. All 4 who were assessed had a structural cardiac anomaly. Four of the 5 had hypotonia and macrocephaly. Three of the 5 had polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, or needed a feeding tube. None was formally diagnosed with loose anagen hair, but all had sparse, very slow-growing hair with an unruly texture and unusually light color, as well as unusually long eyelashes. Four of the 5 patients had been suspected of having Costello syndrome.

Patient 1 of Gripp et al. (2013) was born by cesarean section at 38 weeks for macrosomia after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and a third-trimester cystic hygroma. He had pyloric stenosis that required surgical repair. Severe failure to thrive led to gastrostomy tube placement at 5 months. He also had mild pulmonic valve stenosis, low axial tone, and motor delay. At 2 4/12 years his OFC was 54.3 cm (+3 SD). He had sparse, very fine hair, ptosis, downslanting palpebral fissures, a short neck, hepatosplenomegaly, hemangiomas, and numerous fine wrinkles on the hands. Juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia was suspected at 2 3/12 years, but the bone marrow findings were most consistent with primary myelofibrosis. Bone marrow DNA was negative for the JAK2 V617F mutation. He died at 2.5 years after splenectomy in preparation for bone marrow transplantation. The authors described this as the first hematologic neoplasm in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation. Because the JAK2 mutation was absent, they suggested that the SHOC2 mutation may have predisposed to the myelofibrosis.

Patient 2 of Gripp et al. (2013) was born at term after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios, with a birth weight of 4.14 kg (95th centile). He had severe early feeding difficulties and failure to thrive. Other features were macrocephaly, unilateral ptosis, posteriorly angulated ears, increased palmar wrinkling, loose skin, bilateral inguinal hernias, and hypotonia. Neurosurgical drainage of presumed bilateral chronic subdural hematomas yielded clear fluid. He had notable intellectual disability: he walked independently at 5 years and reportedly used few single words at 9 years. At 9 5/12 years his height was 125 cm (3rd-5th centile) and his OFC was 56.4 cm (+2 SD). He had sparse hair, deep palmar creases, joint laxity, a hyperpigmented lesion, and apparent acanthosis nigricans.

Patient 3 of Gripp et al. (2013) was born at 36 weeks after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and fetal macrosomia. He had tracheostenosis, chronic lung disease, and a seizure on day 4 of life. At 6 months he had right bundle branch block and mild to moderate biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Aortic insufficiency and mild pulmonic stenosis followed. He needed a gastrostomy tube. His OFC was 60 cm (+5 SD) at 9 years. He had developmental delay and aggressive behavior, and was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome at 9 years. His hair was coarse and straight. ⚠︎

Patient 4 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy of Mexican ancestry. He had poor growth, global developmental delay with mild to moderate intellectual disability, an atrial septal defect, rectal prolapse, strabismus, and nystagmus. His blond, sparse hair grew poorly, was easily pluckable, and was much lighter than that of his parents and sister. His eyelashes were very long. He also had a mildly webbed neck, pectus excavatum, ligamentous laxity, increased fine palmar wrinkles, and cafe-au-lait macules. Brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly and subependymal heterotopia. By 6 years, a Chiari I malformation had developed in the context of platybasia and basilar invagination. He also had segmental fusion of C3 and C4 and unusual intracranial venous anatomy.

Patient 5 of Gripp et al. (2013) had neonatal hypotonia and feeding difficulties and needed nasogastric tube feeding until about 1 year of age. He had a patent ductus arteriosus and an abnormal mitral valve with 3 papillary muscles. At 8 3/12 years he had short stature and an OFC of +2 SD. He also had pectus excavatum, hemangiomas, dry hyperpigmented skin, and attention deficit disorder. Brain MRI at 3 weeks showed a relatively small cerebellum and increased extraaxial fluid spaces. His hair grew slowly, but he never had hair loss or easily pluckable hair.

Gripp et al. (2013) described distinctive brain abnormalities in patients with the SHOC2 mutation. These were relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium, which was seen in all 4 patients assessed. Cerebellar tonsillar ectopia occurred in 3 of 4 patients, which the authors attributed to the combination of a large brain and a small posterior fossa. Combining their patients with those in the literature, 3 of 8 patients who had MRI had a Chiari I malformation. Periventricular nodular heterotopia was seen in 1 patient.

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that the heterozygous SHOC2 S2G mutation occurred de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available. Hoban et al. (2012) and Gripp et al. (2013) also reported de novo occurrence of the mutation. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with a Noonan-like syndrome who carried the p.Pro49Arg mutation in the PPP1CB gene. He had sparse, thin, slow-growing hair similar to the typical ectodermal finding in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. He was the fifth reported individual with a PPP1CB mutation and the fourth with p.Pro49Arg, which suggested a mutational hotspot. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair closely resembles Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair.

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) selected SHOC2 as the best candidate gene using in silico protein network analysis seeded with known mutant RAS-MAPK proteins. They sequenced SHOC2 in 96 individuals with Noonan syndrome who were negative for mutations in known disease genes. In 4 unrelated individuals they found a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. All 4 cases were sporadic. In the 3 with parental DNA available, the parents did not carry the change and paternity was confirmed, indicating de novo occurrence. The mutation was present in DNA from several tissues, consistent with germline transmission. The authors then analyzed SHOC2 in 410 mutation-negative individuals with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype. They found the same 4A-G change in 21 of them and showed that it was de novo in the 12 families with parental DNA available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was identified in the cohort. In functional studies, the S2G mutation introduced an N-myristoylation site, which caused aberrant targeting of SHOC2 to the plasma membrane and impaired its translocation to the nucleus after growth factor stimulation. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They found the S2G mutation in 8 patients. No SHOC2 mutations were found in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) used clinical testing of genes that cause Noonan syndrome and related disorders to identify the previously reported de novo SHOC2 missense mutation predicting p.Ser2Gly.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the recurrent SHOC2 p.Ser2Gly mutation in 5 patients, and showed that it was de novo in 2 of them. In patient 1 the mutation was found by postmortem research testing of stored buccal-cell DNA and was absent from both parents. In all 5 patients, sequencing of other RAS/MAPK pathway genes, including HRAS, had given normal results.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT (p.Gln269_His270delinsHisTyr), which was associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Structural analyses suggested that the mutation might affect the relative orientation of SHOC2's 2 leucine-rich repeat domains. In functional studies, the mutant protein bound MRAS and PPP1CB more strongly and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade. Unlike SHOC2 S2G, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous SHOC2 c.519G-A (M173I) variant in a 5-year-old girl and her father. They noted that the phenotype differed significantly from that of patients with the S2G mutation. The proband had craniofacial features suggestive of Noonan syndrome and sparse, slow-growing hair more typical of CFC syndrome. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair. Her father had very subtle features. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of Rasopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that the S2G substitution makes SHOC2 a substrate for N-myristoyltransferase (NMT). In Cos-1 cells, SHOC2 S2G incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype SHOC2 and the disease-unrelated S2A mutant did not. Wildtype SHOC2 was distributed through the cytoplasm and nucleus in starved cells and was restricted to the nucleus after EGF stimulation. In contrast, S2G was targeted to the cell membrane in both conditions. The NMT inhibitor 2-hydroxymyristic acid reduced or abolished this membrane localization. Even without efficient myristoylation, the mutant did not translocate to the nucleus. Wildtype and S2G SHOC2 did not heterodimerize, which ruled out a dominant-negative effect through sequestration of the wildtype protein. S2G increased EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation compared with wildtype in Neuro2A cells, but not significantly in Cos-1 or 293T cells. The authors concluded that acquired N-myristoylation leads to constitutive membrane targeting and to cell context-specific increases in MAPK activation. They also proposed that loose anagen hair reflects disturbed proliferation, survival, or differentiation of epithelial stem cell-derived cells in hair follicles.

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS, SHOC2, and protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory 'S259' site of RAF kinases, and that MRAS and SHOC2 act as PP1 regulatory subunits. Noonan syndrome-associated mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB invariably promoted complex formation with each other, but not necessarily with other interactors. The authors concluded that Noonan syndrome in individuals with these mutations is likely driven at the biochemical level by enhanced ternary complex formation.

Motta et al. (2019) concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 functional dysregulation converge toward upregulation of MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve analysis to detect the SHOC2 S2G mutation and proposed it as a useful screening tool for this apparently common mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that 4 of their 5 patients had been suspected of having Costello syndrome. Subjectively, their ectodermal, growth, and developmental findings closely resembled those of Costello syndrome caused by HRAS p.Gly13Cys. The authors stated that this overlap supports a gene panel approach to molecular testing in patients with a RASopathy phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Gripp et al. (2013) combined the series of Cordeddu et al. (2009) and Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) and estimated that SHOC2 mutations occur in 12 of 188 (about 6%) of patients with Noonan syndrome who lack a molecular diagnosis. They calculated that this corresponds to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype human SHOC2 could replace SUR-8 functionally in C. elegans, whereas SHOC2 S2G could not. Expressing S2G in let-60 gain-of-function worms did not suppress the multivulva phenotype, which excluded a dominant-negative effect. In a wildtype background, expressing S2G at the early L3 stage caused abnormal vulval development, with protruding vulva, decreased egg laying, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. These effects were also seen with myristoylation-tagged wildtype SHOC2 but not with wildtype SHOC2. The defects reflected abnormal vulval morphogenesis rather than altered vulval cell fate specification: descendants of P5.p and/or P7.p detached from the cuticle in 17 of 48 larvae expressing S2G.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G; p.Ser2Gly]

In 25 individuals with Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. Four were found among 96 mutation-negative individuals with Noonan syndrome and 21 among 410 mutation-negative individuals with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype. The mutation was de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available. The mutation introduced an N-myristoylation site. This led to myristoylation of SHOC2, targeting of the protein to the plasma membrane, impaired nuclear translocation after growth factor stimulation, and cell type-specific enhancement of MAPK activation. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) identified the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutations in other known genes.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified the de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the c.4A-G (p.Ser2Gly) mutation in 5 patients. It was de novo in 2 of them. One patient developed myelofibrosis.

**.0002 RASOPATHY, MILD**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

Hannig et al. (2014) used Rasopathy gene panel sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The proband was a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with macrocephaly, ptosis, nystagmus, optic nerve hypoplasia, and sparse, slow-growing hair that was not loose. Her father, who had intellectual disability, sparse hair, and clubbed nails, also carried the variant. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation to only about 10% above basal levels. It bound PP1c poorly and produced only a mild increase in RAF-1 Ser338 phosphorylation. Protein stability, subcellular localization, and complex formation with M-RAS and RAF-1 were unaffected.
