### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN; 601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

CMS11 is an autosomal recessive congenital myasthenic syndrome in which mutations in the RAPSN gene cause endplate acetylcholine receptor (AChR) deficiency (Ohno et al., 2002). Rapsyn, a 43-kD postsynaptic protein, plays an essential role in clustering AChR at the endplate (Ohno et al., 2002). Burke et al. (2003) distinguished early- and late-onset phenotypes. In their review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients present in the first year of life. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis at birth and other congenital malformations, and intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises. Engel et al. (2015) described a milder phenotype with facial malformations in Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for a promoter E-box mutation. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin who had congenital myasthenia with facial malformations. The malformations included an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was mainly restricted to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive, and the patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. Antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor were absent. Repetitive nerve stimulation at 3 Hz gave a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus produced no repetitive compound muscle action potential.

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with 6 affected individuals who had been diagnosed with a phenotypically unique form of congenital myasthenic syndrome. Four of the families were consanguineous, and all came from the same geographic region.

Burke et al. (2003) identified rapsyn mutations in 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome and noted 2 distinct phenotypes. Early-onset disease was frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. Late-onset disease developed in adolescence or adulthood and was at first mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency. At birth he had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity. Because of respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue, he spent a total of 71 days in the neonatal intensive care unit and 47 days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Imaging, a battery of metabolic tests, and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. Muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency present in the first year of life and a few present later. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis at birth and other congenital malformations. Intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises that result in anoxic encephalopathy. The clinical features can suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but ocular ductions are intact in most patients.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that the form of congenital myasthenia with facial malformations seen in Iraqi and Iranian Jews is transmitted as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) identified recessive RAPSN mutations in patients with endplate AChR deficiency.

### Mapping

In 5 Iranian Jewish families with congenital myasthenic syndrome, Menold et al. (1998) excluded linkage to the regions on chromosomes 2 and 17 containing the AChR subunit genes. They also excluded linkage to the region on chromosome 17p to which familial infantile myasthenia had been localized.

### Heterogeneity

Menold et al. (1998) suggested that the clinical differences among patients with congenital myasthenic syndromes correlate with locus heterogeneity. They proposed that a defect in a different gene was responsible for CMS in the Iranian Jewish families they studied.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in AChR subunit genes and identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for L14P in tetratricopeptide repeat 1 (TPR1) and N88K in TPR3. Two were homozygous for N88K. One carried N88K and 553ins5, which causes a frameshift in TPR5. Endplate studies in each patient showed decreased staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development. Expression studies in HEK cells showed that none of the mutations impaired rapsyn self-association, but all 3 reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified 2 E-box mutations in the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation that changes an E-box at -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8 were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. They carried a -38A-G mutation that changes another E-box at -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without affecting the consensus CANNTG sequence. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, -38A-G gained, and -27C-G lost, binding affinity for different components of nuclear extracts of C2C12 myotubes. In luciferase reporter assays, both mutations attenuated reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes. The -27C-G mutation also attenuated expression in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin, and it markedly attenuated the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter. Ohno et al. (2003) concluded that impaired transcription from the RAPSN promoter region predicts reduced rapsyn expression and endplate AChR deficiency.

Burke et al. (2003) identified rapsyn mutations in 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome. The N88K mutation was common to all of them.

Gaudon et al. (2010) described 3 patients with a characteristic phenotype of recessive CMS and a mutation in the RAPSN gene. Familial analysis showed that direct sequencing had failed to detect one mutant allele. Allelic quantification of the patients' DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN. These deletions represented 15% of the authors' CMS patients with RAPSN mutations.

In a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency, Das et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 confirmed pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene. Earlier testing, including a limited genetic evaluation for reversible cytochrome c oxidase deficiency and an oligonucleotide microarray, had been negative.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found 2 asymptomatic individuals homozygous for the N88K mutation. These individuals carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination events may have occurred within the rapsyn gene and that this may have implications for phenotypic expression of the disease.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that there are no genotype-phenotype correlations in rapsyn deficiency, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the E-box mutation c.-38A-G have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe masticatory and facial muscle weakness, and hypernasal speech.

### Pathogenesis

Goldhammer et al. (1990) suggested that the facial abnormalities in their patients might be secondary to the neuromuscular defect, or primary and unrelated to it.

Engel et al. (2015) reviewed the functions of rapsyn. Rapsyn concentrates and anchors AChR in the postsynaptic membrane and is required for development of the junctional folds. They reported that in rapsyn deficiency, multiple synaptic contacts appear on single muscle fibers. The endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds are not well differentiated. Multiple small nerve terminals lie over poorly developed junctional folds, and AChR is distributed patchily on the postsynaptic membrane. According to Engel et al. (2015), different mutations hinder rapsyn self-association or binding to AChR, impede agrin-MuSK-LRP4-mediated clustering of AChR, or decrease rapsyn expression.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) noted that the late-onset phenotype was initially mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis. They stated that recognizing it should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy.

Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended that SNP markers and a gene dosage method be used in addition to direct DNA sequencing, particularly when genetic counseling is at issue.

Engel et al. (2015) listed congenital contractures and sudden apneic episodes provoked by fever or stress among the phenotypic clues to rapsyn mutations. They also listed rapsyn as an occasional cause of limb-girdle and axial weakness. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

The patients described by Goldhammer et al. (1990) responded to cholinesterase inhibitors.

Das et al. (2014) began treating their patient with pyridostigmine at 16 months of age. Treatment led to a dramatic improvement in muscle tone and strength and a steady resolution of joint contractures. Four months after treatment began, he was beginning to bear weight and could sit unsupported and vocalize full words.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to AChE inhibitors. Some derive additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some benefit from the added use of ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) noted that congenital myasthenia gravis was frequent in both the Iranian and Iraqi Jewish communities. Iranian Jews are an ancient community with a very high degree of inbreeding and strong ties with Babylonian Jewry in Iraq.

Haplotype analysis by Ohno et al. (2003) showed that the RAPSN -38A-G mutation in Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin arose from a common founder.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) used 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb to characterize the haplotype associated with the N88K mutation. They found a common haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes, and this haplotype was present in all heterozygous carriers of N88K. They concluded that this finding supported a founder effect.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Engel et al. (2015) reported that rapsyn deficiency accounted for 51 (14.3%) of 356 index patients with CMS investigated at the Mayo Clinic between 1988 and 2014.

**.0002 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, ASN88LYS [p.N88K]

In 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency, Ohno et al. (2002) identified an asn88-to-lys (N88K) mutation in tetratricopeptide repeat 3 (TPR3) of rapsyn. Two patients were homozygous for the mutation. One was compound heterozygous for N88K and L14P, and one carried N88K and 553ins5. Expression studies in HEK cells showed that N88K did not hinder rapsyn self-association but diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Burke et al. (2003) found the N88K mutation in each of 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome and rapsyn mutations. The patients had either early- or late-onset phenotypes.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found a common intragenic SNP haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes and in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, which supported a founder effect. Two asymptomatic N88K homozygotes carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Expression studies in HEK cells showed that the mutation did not hinder rapsyn self-association but diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -38A-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter region in 7 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome (patients 2-8). The patients were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin and had facial malformations.
