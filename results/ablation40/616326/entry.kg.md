### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 (CMS11), which is associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN; 601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

Congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 (CMS11) is an autosomal recessive disorder of neuromuscular transmission. It is caused by deficiency of rapsyn, which leads to endplate acetylcholine receptor (AChR) deficiency (Ohno et al., 2002). Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients present in the first year of life and a few present later. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis at birth, and intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises. In Near-Eastern Jewish patients with a promoter mutation, the phenotype is milder, with ptosis, prognathism, facial and masticatory weakness, and hypernasal speech (Goldhammer et al., 1990; Engel et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin who had congenital myasthenia with facial malformations. The malformations were an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was mostly limited to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive, and the patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. Antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor were absent. Repetitive nerve stimulation at 3 Hz produced a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus did not produce a repetitive compound muscle action potential.

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with 6 affected individuals who had been diagnosed with a phenotypically unique form of congenital myasthenic syndrome. Four of the families were consanguineous. Because all of the families came from the same geographic region, the authors considered it highly likely that they carried the same ancestral CMS mutation.

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in AChR subunit genes. In each case, endplate studies showed decreased staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development.

Ohno et al. (2003) reported that all 7 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome who carried a RAPSN promoter mutation (-38A-G) had facial malformations. All were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin.

Burke et al. (2003) described 2 distinct phenotypes, early onset and late onset, in 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn-related congenital myasthenic syndrome. The early-onset form was often associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The late-onset form began in adolescence or adulthood and was at first mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency who presented at birth with weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity. Because of respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue, he spent 71 days in the neonatal intensive care unit and 47 days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Imaging, metabolic tests, and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. Muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with CMS due to rapsyn deficiency present in the first year of life and a few present later. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis and other congenital malformations at birth. Intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises that lead to anoxic encephalopathy. The clinical features can suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but the ocular ductions are intact in most patients.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that this form of congenital myasthenia, which clustered by ethnicity in Iraqi and Iranian Jews and occurred with facial malformations, is inherited as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) found homozygous and compound heterozygous RAPSN mutations in affected patients, confirming autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Heterogeneity

In 5 Iranian Jewish families with CMS, Menold et al. (1998) excluded linkage to the regions of chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes. They also excluded linkage to the region of chromosome 17p to which familial infantile myasthenia had been mapped. The authors suggested that the clinical differences among patients with CMS correlate with locus heterogeneity and that a defect in a different gene causes CMS in these patients.

Ohno et al. (2002) noted that many CMSs with endplate AChR deficiency had been traced to mutations in AChR subunit genes, but that the genetic defect had remained unknown in a subset of patients. They found that rapsyn mutations account for some of these cases.

### Molecular Genetics

In 4 patients with CMS and endplate AChR deficiency, Ohno et al. (2002) identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for an L14P mutation in tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) 1 and an N88K mutation in TPR3. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. The fourth patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5) that causes a frameshift in TPR5. In HEK cell expression studies, none of the mutations hindered rapsyn self-association, but all diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) determined that the major transcriptional start site of RAPSN lies 172 nucleotides upstream of the translational start site. In 1 patient with CMS (patient 1), they identified a -27C-G mutation in an E-box of the RAPSN promoter. The allele carrying this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. In 7 patients (patients 2 through 8), all of Oriental Jewish stock of Iraqi or Iranian origin, they identified a -38A-G mutation in another E-box of the promoter. Electrophoretic mobility shift and luciferase reporter assays showed that both mutations altered binding of components of myotube nuclear extracts and reduced reporter expression. The authors predicted that impaired transcriptional activity of the RAPSN promoter would reduce rapsyn expression and cause endplate AChR deficiency.

Burke et al. (2003) identified rapsyn mutations in 16 unrelated patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome. All 16 carried the N88K mutation.

Gaudon et al. (2010) described 3 patients who had a typical recessive CMS phenotype and mutations in the RAPSN gene. Family analysis showed that direct sequencing had missed 1 mutant allele in each patient. Allelic quantification of the patients' DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions in RAPSN.

In a boy with CMS for whom extensive testing, including microarray, had been nondiagnostic, Das et al. (2014) identified 2 pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene by whole-exome sequencing.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found 2 asymptomatic individuals who were homozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation and carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination events may have occurred within the rapsyn gene and that this may have implications for the phenotypic expression of the disease.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that rapsyn deficiency shows no genotype-phenotype correlations, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients who are homozygous for the E-box mutation c.-38A-G have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe weakness of the masticatory and facial muscles, and hypernasal speech.

### Pathogenesis

Goldhammer et al. (1990) suggested that the facial abnormalities in their patients could either result from the neuromuscular defect or be primary and unrelated to it. They noted that the defect in neuromuscular transmission had yet to be identified.

Ohno et al. (2002) noted that rapsyn is essential for clustering AChR at the endplate. Its 7 TPRs mediate self-association, a coiled-coil domain binds AChR, and a RING-H2 domain binds beta-dystroglycan. Because the patients' mutations did not hinder rapsyn self-association but did diminish coclustering of AChR with rapsyn, the authors concluded that the endplate AChR deficiency results from impaired rapsyn-mediated AChR clustering. ⚠︎

Engel et al. (2015) summarized that rapsyn concentrates and anchors AChR in the postsynaptic membrane and is required for development of the junctional folds. They described its domains: a myristoylated N-terminal region that links it to the membrane, 7 TPRs needed for self-aggregation and MuSK binding, a coiled-coil domain that interacts with AChR subunits, and a C-terminal domain that binds beta-dystroglycan. In rapsyn deficiency, multiple synaptic contacts appear on single muscle fibers. The endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds are poorly differentiated. Different RAPSN mutations hinder rapsyn self-association or binding to AChR, impede agrin-MuSK-LRP4-mediated clustering of AChR, or decrease rapsyn expression.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) noted that recognizing the late-onset rapsyn phenotype, which can be mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis, should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy.

Gaudon et al. (2010) found that 3 genomic rearrangements accounted for 15% of their CMS patients with RAPSN mutations. They emphasized that SNP markers and a gene dosage method should be used alongside direct DNA sequencing, particularly when genetic counseling is at issue.

Engel et al. (2015) listed phenotypic clues that point to rapsyn deficiency: congenital contractures and sudden apneic episodes provoked by fever or stress. They added that apneic episodes also occur with ChAT mutations and Na-channel myasthenia, and that limb-girdle and axial weakness is occasionally seen with rapsyn mutations. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Das et al. (2014) started their patient with RAPSN-related CMS on pyridostigmine at 16 months of age. Muscle tone and strength improved dramatically, and the joint contractures steadily resolved. Four months later, he was beginning to bear weight, could sit unsupported, and vocalized full words.

Engel et al. (2015) reported that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to AChE inhibitors. Some get additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some from the addition of ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) reported that congenital myasthenia gravis was among the diseases frequent in both the Iranian and Iraqi Jewish communities. He noted that Iranian Jews are an ancient community with a very high degree of inbreeding and strong ties to Babylonian Jewry in Iraq.

Haplotype analysis by Ohno et al. (2003) showed that the RAPSN -38A-G promoter mutation in Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin arose from a common founder.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) used 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb of the RAPSN gene to characterize the haplotype associated with the N88K mutation. A common haplotype found in 3 affected N88K homozygotes was present in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, which the authors concluded supported a founder effect.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Engel et al. (2015) noted that Indo-Europeans carry a common N88K mutation in RAPSN, and that other mutations occur in the promoter region and throughout the RAPSN open reading frame. Among 356 index patients with CMS investigated at the Mayo Clinic between 1988 and 2014, rapsyn deficiency accounted for 51 index cases (14.3%).

**.0001 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, ASN88LYS [p.N88K]

Ohno et al. (2002) identified an asn88-to-lys (N88K) mutation in TPR3 of the RAPSN gene in all 4 of their patients with CMS and endplate AChR deficiency. Two patients were homozygous for the mutation. One was compound heterozygous for N88K and L14P, and 1 was compound heterozygous for N88K and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5). In HEK cell expression studies, N88K did not hinder rapsyn self-association but diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Burke et al. (2003) found the N88K mutation in all 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn-related congenital myasthenic syndrome.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found a common intragenic SNP haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes that was present in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, which supported a founder effect. They also found 2 asymptomatic N88K homozygotes who carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

**.0004 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -27C-G

In a patient with CMS (patient 1), Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -27C-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter. The mutation changes an E-box at positions -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying the mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, the mutant lost binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. In luciferase reporter assays, it attenuated reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes and in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin. The mutation also markedly attenuated the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter.

**.0005 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -38A-G

In 7 patients with CMS (patients 2 through 8), all of Oriental Jewish stock of Iraqi or Iranian origin, Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -38A-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter. The patients had facial malformations. The mutation changes an E-box at positions -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without affecting the CANNTG consensus. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, the mutant gained binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. In luciferase reporter assays, it attenuated reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes. Haplotype analysis showed that the mutation arose from a common founder.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A-G E-box mutation have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe weakness of the masticatory and facial muscles, and hypernasal speech.
