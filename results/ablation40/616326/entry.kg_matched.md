### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RAPSN gene (601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

Congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 (CMS11) is an autosomal recessive disorder of neuromuscular transmission caused by deficiency of rapsyn, a 43-kD postsynaptic protein that is essential for clustering acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the endplate. The result is endplate AChR deficiency (Ohno et al., 2002). Most patients present in the first year of life and a few present later. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis at birth, and intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises (Engel et al., 2015). Burke et al. (2003) distinguished an early-onset form and a late-onset form, the latter beginning in adolescence or adulthood.

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin who had congenital myasthenia and facial malformations. The malformations were an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was mainly limited to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive.

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with 6 affected individuals who had been diagnosed with a phenotypically unique form of congenital myasthenic syndrome. Four of the families were consanguineous, and all came from the same geographic region. For this reason the authors considered it highly likely that the families carried the same ancestral CMS mutation.

Ohno et al. (2002) performed endplate studies in 4 patients with RAPSN mutations. Each showed reduced staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development.

Ohno et al. (2003) reported that the 7 patients with CMS who carried the RAPSN -38A-G promoter mutation were of Oriental Jewish ancestry of Iraqi or Iranian origin and had facial malformations.

Among 16 patients with rapsyn-related myasthenic syndrome, Burke et al. (2003) described 2 distinct phenotypes: early onset and late onset. Early-onset disease was frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. Late-onset disease developed in adolescence or adulthood.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency. At birth he had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures and facial deformity. Because of respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue, he spent 71 days in the neonatal intensive care unit and 47 days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Imaging, metabolic and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. Muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with CMS due to rapsyn deficiency present in the first year of life, while a few present later. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis at birth and other congenital malformations. Intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises that lead to anoxic encephalopathy. The clinical features can suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but ocular ductions are intact in most patients.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that this form of congenital myasthenia with facial malformations, which clustered in specific ethnic groups, is inherited as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) found homozygous or compound heterozygous RAPSN mutations in affected patients, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

In 5 Iranian Jewish families with CMS, Menold et al. (1998) tested for linkage to the regions on chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes. They also tested the region on chromosome 17p where familial infantile myasthenia had been mapped. Linkage to all of these regions was excluded.

### Heterogeneity

Menold et al. (1998) suggested that the clinical differences among patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome correlate with locus heterogeneity. They proposed that CMS in the Iranian Jewish families was caused by a defect in a gene other than the AChR subunit genes and the familial infantile myasthenia gene.

Ohno et al. (2002) noted that many congenital myasthenic syndromes with endplate AChR deficiency had been traced to mutations in AChR subunit genes, but the genetic defect had remained unknown in a subset of patients. In 4 such patients they identified rapsyn mutations as the cause.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with CMS and endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes. In these patients they identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene, and all 4 patients carried the N88K mutation. Two patients were homozygous for N88K, which lies in tetratricopeptide repeat 3 (TPR3). One patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and L14P, which lies in TPR1. The fourth patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and a 553ins5 insertion that causes a frameshift in TPR5. In expression studies in HEK cells, none of the 3 mutations impaired rapsyn self-association, but all 3 reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified 2 mutations in E-box elements of the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with CMS. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation, and the allele carrying this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8, who were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin, carried a -38A-G mutation. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, -38A-G gained binding affinity, and -27C-G lost binding affinity, for different components of nuclear extracts from C2C12 myotubes. Both mutations reduced reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes. The authors concluded that impaired transcription from the RAPSN promoter predicts reduced rapsyn expression and AChR deficiency at the endplate, and they noted that these were the first E-box mutations reported.

Burke et al. (2003) identified RAPSN mutations in 16 unrelated patients with congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome. All 16 patients carried the N88K mutation.

Gaudon et al. (2010) described 3 patients with a characteristic phenotype of recessive CMS and a mutation in the RAPSN gene. Family analysis showed that direct sequencing had missed one mutant allele in each patient. Allelic quantification of the patients' DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN. In this series, genomic rearrangements accounted for 15% of CMS patients with RAPSN mutations. ⚠︎

Das et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 confirmed pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene in a boy with CMS. Earlier testing, consisting of a limited genetic evaluation for reversible cytochrome c oxidase deficiency and an oligonucleotide microarray, had been negative.

Engel et al. (2015) noted that Indo-Europeans harbor a common N88K mutation in RAPSN, and that other mutations occur in the promoter region and throughout the open reading frame. According to the review, the different mutations hinder rapsyn self-association or binding to AChR, impede agrin-MuSK-LRP4-mediated AChR clustering, or decrease rapsyn expression.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found 2 asymptomatic individuals who were homozygous for the rapsyn N88K mutation and carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination events may have occurred within the rapsyn gene and that this may affect how the disease is expressed.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that rapsyn deficiency shows no genotype-phenotype correlations, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the E-box mutation c.-38A>G have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe weakness of the masticatory and facial muscles, and hypernasal speech.

### Pathogenesis

Goldhammer et al. (1990) suggested that the facial abnormalities could be secondary to the neuromuscular defect or could be primary and unrelated to it. They stated that the defect in neuromuscular transmission remained to be identified.

Engel et al. (2015) summarized that rapsyn concentrates and anchors AChR in the postsynaptic membrane and is required for development of the junctional folds. In rapsyn deficiency, single muscle fibers carry multiple synaptic contacts. The endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds are poorly differentiated. Multiple small nerve terminals lie over poorly developed junctional folds, and AChR is distributed in a patchy pattern on the postsynaptic membrane.

### Diagnosis

In the patients described by Goldhammer et al. (1990), antibodies to the AChR were absent. Repetitive nerve stimulation at 3 Hz gave a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus did not produce a repetitive compound muscle action potential.

Burke et al. (2003) noted that the late-onset rapsyn phenotype was at first misdiagnosed as seronegative myasthenia gravis. They stated that recognizing this phenotype should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy.

Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended using SNP markers and a gene dosage method in addition to direct DNA sequencing, particularly when genetic counseling is at issue.

Engel et al. (2015) listed rapsyn among the phenotypic clues that point to specific CMS disease proteins. They listed rapsyn mutations as causes of congenital contractures and of sudden apneic episodes provoked by fever or stress, and as an occasional cause of limb-girdle and axial weakness. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Goldhammer et al. (1990) reported that their patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors.

Das et al. (2014) reported that starting pyridostigmine at age 16 months in a boy with rapsyn deficiency led to a dramatic improvement in muscle tone and strength and a steady resolution of joint contractures. Four months after treatment began, he was starting to bear weight, could sit unsupported, and could say full words.

Engel et al. (2015) reported that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to AChE inhibitors. Some derive additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some benefit from the addition of ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) reported that congenital myasthenia gravis was frequent in both the Iranian and the Iraqi Jewish communities. Iranian Jews are an ancient community with a very high degree of inbreeding and strong historical ties to Babylonian (Iraqi) Jewry.

Haplotype analysis by Ohno et al. (2003) showed that the RAPSN -38A-G promoter mutation, which they found in Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin, arose from a common founder.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) characterized the haplotype associated with the rapsyn N88K mutation using 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb. They found a common haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes, and the same haplotype was present in all heterozygous carriers of N88K. The authors concluded that this common haplotype supports a founder effect.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Among 356 index patients with CMS investigated at the Mayo Clinic between 1988 and 2014, Engel et al. (2015) found that 51 (14.3%) had rapsyn deficiency.

Ohno et al. (2002) found the N88K mutation, which lies in tetratricopeptide repeat 3 (TPR3) of rapsyn, in all 4 patients they studied with CMS and endplate AChR deficiency. Two patients were homozygous for the mutation. One was compound heterozygous for N88K and L14P, and one was compound heterozygous for N88K and 553ins5. In HEK cells, N88K did not impair rapsyn self-association but reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Burke et al. (2003) found the N88K mutation in all 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn-related myasthenic syndrome that they studied.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found a common haplotype of 7 intragenic SNPs in 3 affected N88K homozygotes and in all heterozygous carriers, which supports a founder effect. They also found 2 asymptomatic N88K homozygotes whose second haplotype differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

**.0004 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -27C-G

In a patient with CMS (patient 1), Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -27C-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter region. The mutation changes an E-box at -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying the mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, the mutation lost binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. It reduced reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes and in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin, and it markedly reduced the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter.

**.0005 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -38A-G

Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -38A-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter region in 7 patients with CMS (patients 2-8). The patients were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. The mutation changes an E-box at -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without altering the consensus CANNTG sequence. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, the mutation gained binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts, and it reduced reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes. Haplotype analysis showed that the mutation arose from a common founder.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A>G E-box mutation have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe weakness of the masticatory and facial muscles, and hypernasal speech.
