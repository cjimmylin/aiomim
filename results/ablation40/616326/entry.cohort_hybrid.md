### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN; 601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

CMS11 is a congenital myasthenic syndrome caused by defects in rapsyn, a 43-kD postsynaptic protein that is essential for clustering of the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the motor endplate. The endplates show decreased staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development (Ohno et al., 2002). Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients present in the first year of life and a few present later. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis or other congenital malformations at birth, and intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises. Most patients respond well to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin who had congenital myasthenia with facial malformations. The malformations included an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was restricted predominantly to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive, and the patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. Antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor were absent. Repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz produced a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus did not evoke a repetitive compound muscle action potential. The authors considered this a newly recognized form of congenital myasthenia with distinctive ethnic clustering.

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with 6 affected individuals who had been diagnosed with a phenotypically unique form of CMS. Four of the families were consanguineous, and all came from the same geographic region.

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes. Endplate studies in each patient showed decreased staining for rapsyn and AChR, as well as impaired postsynaptic morphologic development.

Burke et al. (2003) reported 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome due to rapsyn mutations and noted 2 distinct phenotypes. The early-onset form was frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The late-onset form developed in adolescence or adulthood and was initially mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency who had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity at birth. Because of respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue, he spent a total of 71 days in the neonatal intensive care unit and 47 days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Imaging, metabolic testing, and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. A muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency present in the first year of life and a few present later. Arthrogryposis at birth and other congenital malformations occur in nearly one-third of patients. Intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises that result in anoxic encephalopathy. The clinical features can suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but ocular ductions are intact in most patients.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that congenital myasthenia with facial malformations in Iraqi and Iranian Jews is transmitted as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) identified recessive rapsyn mutations, in the homozygous or compound heterozygous state, in patients with endplate AChR deficiency.

### Mapping

In 5 Iranian Jewish families with CMS, Menold et al. (1998) excluded linkage to the regions on chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes. They also excluded linkage to the region on 17p to which familial infantile myasthenia had been localized.

### Heterogeneity

On the basis of their exclusion of linkage, Menold et al. (1998) suggested that the clinical differences among patients with CMS correlate with locus heterogeneity, and that a defect in a different gene was responsible for CMS in the Iranian Jewish families they studied.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes and identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for L14P in TPR1 and N88K in TPR3, 2 patients were homozygous for N88K, and 1 patient carried N88K and a 553ins5 mutation that causes a frameshift in TPR5. Expression studies in HEK cells showed that none of the mutations hindered rapsyn self-association, but all 3 diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified 2 mutations in E-box sequences in the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with CMS. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation, and the allele harboring this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8 were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations, and they carried a -38A-G mutation. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays and luciferase reporter assays showed that both mutations altered binding of nuclear factors and impaired transcriptional activity.

Burke et al. (2003) identified rapsyn mutations in 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome. All 16 carried the N88K mutation.

Gaudon et al. (2010) described 3 patients with a characteristic phenotype of recessive CMS who carried RAPSN mutations. Family analysis showed that direct sequencing had failed to detect 1 of the mutant alleles. Allelic quantification of the patients' DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN, which accounted for 15% of the authors' CMS patients with RAPSN mutations.

In a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency, Das et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 confirmed pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Among 16 unrelated patients who all carried the RAPSN N88K mutation, Burke et al. (2003) observed 2 distinct phenotypes: an early-onset form frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises, and a late-onset form developing in adolescence or adulthood.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found 2 asymptomatic individuals homozygous for N88K. These individuals carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination events within the rapsyn gene may have implications for the phenotypic expression of the disease.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that there are no genotype-phenotype correlations in rapsyn deficiency, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the E-box mutation c.-38A>G have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe masticatory and facial muscle weakness, and hypernasal speech.

### Pathogenesis

Goldhammer et al. (1990) suggested that the facial abnormalities in their patients might be secondary to the neuromuscular defect, or might be primary and unrelated to it.

Ohno et al. (2002) noted that rapsyn plays an essential role in clustering AChR at the endplate. Its 7 tetratricopeptide repeats subserve self-association, a coiled-coil domain binds AChR, and a RING-H2 domain associates with beta-dystroglycan. Rapsyn self-association precedes recruitment of AChR to rapsyn clusters. The mutations they identified did not hinder self-association but diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) concluded that impaired transcriptional activity of the RAPSN promoter region would be predicted to reduce rapsyn expression and cause endplate AChR deficiency. They noted that no E-box mutation had been reported previously.

Engel et al. (2015) noted that in rapsyn deficiency, multiple synaptic contacts appear on single muscle fibers. The endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds are poorly differentiated. Depending on the mutation, the defect hinders rapsyn self-association or binding to AChR, impedes agrin-MuSK-LRP4-mediated clustering of AChR, or decreases rapsyn expression.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) noted that the late-onset rapsyn phenotype was initially mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis, and that recognizing it should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy.

Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended that SNP markers and a gene dosage method be used in addition to direct sequencing of RAPSN, particularly when there is a genetic counseling issue.

Das et al. (2014) reached a diagnosis of rapsyn deficiency by whole-exome sequencing after imaging, metabolic, electrodiagnostic, and microarray studies and a muscle biopsy had given nonspecific results. They highlighted next-generation sequencing as an important diagnostic tool that can lead to life-saving treatment.

### Clinical Management

Goldhammer et al. (1990) found that their patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors.

Das et al. (2014) started a boy with rapsyn deficiency on pyridostigmine at 16 months of age. Treatment led to a dramatic improvement in muscle tone and strength and a steady resolution of his joint contractures. Four months after starting treatment, he was beginning to bear weight, could sit unsupported, and could vocalize full words.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. Some derive additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some benefit from the added use of ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

In a review of hereditary disorders among Iranian Jews, a community with a very high degree of inbreeding, Zlotogora (1995) noted that congenital myasthenia gravis was frequent in both the Iranian and Iraqi Jewish communities.

By haplotype analysis, Ohno et al. (2003) showed that the RAPSN -38A-G mutation in Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin arose from a common founder.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) used 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb to characterize the haplotype associated with the N88K mutation. In 3 affected N88K homozygotes, they identified a common haplotype that was also present in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, which supported a founder effect.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Engel et al. (2015) reported that 51 (14.3%) of 356 CMS index patients investigated at the Mayo Clinic between 1988 and 2014 had rapsyn deficiency. They also stated that Indo-Europeans harbor a common N88K mutation in RAPSN. ⚠︎

**.0002 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, ASN88LYS [p.N88K; N88K]

Ohno et al. (2002) identified an N88K mutation in TPR3 of rapsyn in 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. One patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and L14P, and another carried N88K and a 553ins5 frameshift mutation. Expression studies in HEK cells showed that N88K diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn without hindering rapsyn self-association.

Burke et al. (2003) found the N88K mutation in each of 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn mutations. The patients had either an early-onset or a late-onset phenotype.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) identified a common haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes that was also present in all heterozygous carriers, which supported a founder effect. Two asymptomatic N88K homozygotes carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

**.0003 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, 5-BP INS, NT553 [c.553ins5; 553ins5]

In a patient with endplate AChR deficiency, Ohno et al. (2002) identified a 553ins5 mutation in the RAPSN gene, which causes a frameshift in TPR5, together with the N88K mutation. Expression studies in HEK cells showed that the mutation diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

**.0004 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -27C-G [-27C-->G]

In a patient with CMS (patient 1), Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -27C-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter region that changes an E-box at positions -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele harboring the mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. The mutation lost binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts and attenuated reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes and in MyoD- or myogenin-transfected HEK cells. It also markedly attenuated the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter.

**.0005 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -38A-G [-38A-->G; c.-38A>G]

In 7 patients with CMS (patients 2 to 8) who were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations, Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -38A-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter region. The mutation changes an E-box at positions -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG and does not affect the consensus CANNTG sequence. Haplotype analysis showed that the mutation arose from a common founder. The mutation gained binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts and attenuated reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A>G E-box mutation have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe masticatory and facial muscle weakness, and hypernasal speech.
