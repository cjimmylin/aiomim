### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RAPSN gene (601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

Congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 (CMS11) is an autosomal recessive postsynaptic disorder of neuromuscular transmission. It is caused by deficiency of the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the endplate, which results from defects in rapsyn, a postsynaptic protein needed to cluster AChR at the endplate (Ohno et al., 2002; Dunne and Maselli, 2004). Most patients present in the first year of life, sometimes with arthrogryposis and respiratory crises, but some present later (Burke et al., 2003; Engel et al., 2015). Most patients respond well to acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors (Engel et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin who had congenital myasthenia with associated facial malformations. The facial malformations were an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was mainly restricted to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive, and the patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. None had antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor. Repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz produced a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus did not produce a repetitive compound muscle action potential.

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with 6 affected individuals who had been diagnosed with a phenotypically unique form of CMS. Four of the families were consanguineous, and all came from the same geographic region.

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with myasthenic syndrome and endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes. In each case, endplate studies showed decreased staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development.

Ohno et al. (2003) reported 8 patients with CMS. Seven of them (patients 2 to 8) were of Oriental Jewish stock of Iraqi or Iranian origin and had facial malformations.

Burke et al. (2003) studied 16 unrelated patients with a congenital or hereditary myasthenic syndrome and rapsyn mutations. They identified 2 distinct phenotypes. The early-onset form was frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The late-onset form developed in adolescence or adulthood and was initially mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency. At birth he had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity, and he also had respiratory difficulty and muscle fatigue. Imaging was normal, and electrodiagnostic studies were also normal. Muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

Engel et al. (2015) reviewed CMS due to rapsyn deficiency. Most patients presented in the first year of life, and a few presented later. Nearly one-third had arthrogryposis or other congenital malformations at birth. Intercurrent infections or fever could trigger respiratory crises that led to anoxic encephalopathy. The features could suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but ocular ductions were intact in most patients.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that this form of congenital myasthenia with facial malformations, which clustered in Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin, is transmitted as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) found recessive RAPSN mutations, in the homozygous or compound heterozygous state, in patients with endplate AChR deficiency. Dunne and Maselli (2004) also described rapsyn-related CMS as a recessive form of postsynaptic CMS.

### Mapping

In 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with 6 affected individuals, 4 of them consanguineous, Menold et al. (1998) excluded linkage to the regions on chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes. They also excluded linkage to the 17p region where familial infantile myasthenia had been mapped.

### Heterogeneity

Menold et al. (1998) suggested that clinical differences among patients with CMS correlate with locus heterogeneity. They proposed that a defect in a different gene causes CMS in Iranian Jewish families.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes and identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for L14P, in tetratricopeptide repeat 1 (TPR1), and N88K, in TPR3. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. The fourth patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and 553ins5, a frameshift in TPR5. N88K was therefore present in all 4 patients. In expression studies in HEK cells, none of the 3 mutations hindered rapsyn self-association, but all 3 diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) found E-box mutations in the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with CMS. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation, which changes an E-box at positions -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 to 8 were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. They carried a -38A-G mutation, which changes another E-box at positions -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG and does not affect the consensus CANNTG sequence. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, the -38A-G mutation gained binding affinity, and the -27C-G mutation lost binding affinity, for different components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. Both mutations reduced reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes. The -27C-G mutation also reduced expression in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin, and it markedly reduced the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter. Ohno et al. (2003) also determined that the major transcriptional start site of RAPSN lies 172 nucleotides upstream of the translational start site.

Burke et al. (2003) identified RAPSN mutations in 16 unrelated patients with a congenital or hereditary myasthenic syndrome. All of them carried the N88K mutation.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) studied 3 affected individuals who were homozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation.

Gaudon et al. (2010) described 3 patients with a characteristic phenotype of recessive CMS and RAPSN mutations. In these patients, family analysis showed that direct sequencing had failed to detect one allelic mutation. Allelic quantification of the patients' DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN. Multiexon deletions that direct sequencing did not detect accounted for 15% of their CMS patients with RAPSN mutations.

In a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency, Das et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 confirmed pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene.

Engel et al. (2015) stated that Indo-Europeans carry a common N88K mutation in RAPSN. Other mutations occur in the promoter region and throughout the open reading frame of the gene.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Burke et al. (2003) studied 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn mutations, all of whom carried N88K. The patients showed 2 distinct phenotypes. One was an early-onset form frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The other was a late-onset form that developed in adolescence or adulthood.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) identified 2 asymptomatic individuals who were homozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation. These individuals carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination within the rapsyn gene may have implications for the phenotypic expression of the disease.

Engel et al. (2015) found no genotype-phenotype correlations in rapsyn deficiency, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A-G E-box mutation had a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe masticatory and facial muscle weakness, and hypernasal speech.

### Pathogenesis

Goldhammer et al. (1990) suggested that the facial abnormalities in their patients might be secondary to the neuromuscular defect, or might be primary and unrelated to it. They stated that further studies were needed to identify the neuromuscular transmission defect.

Ohno et al. (2002) noted that rapsyn, a 43-kD postsynaptic protein, is essential for clustering AChR at the endplate. Its 7 tetratricopeptide repeats mediate self-association, its coiled-coil domain binds AChR, and its RING-H2 domain associates with beta-dystroglycan, linking rapsyn to the subsynaptic cytoskeleton. Rapsyn self-association precedes recruitment of AChR to rapsyn clusters. The 3 RAPSN mutations they identified did not hinder rapsyn self-association, but all of them diminished coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) concluded that impaired transcriptional activity of the RAPSN promoter region would be expected to reduce rapsyn expression and cause endplate AChR deficiency. They noted that no E-box mutation had been reported before.

Engel et al. (2015) reviewed the functions of rapsyn. Rapsyn concentrates and anchors AChR in the postsynaptic membrane and is needed for development of the junctional folds. In rapsyn deficiency, multiple synaptic contacts appeared on single muscle fibers. The endplate AChR deficiency was milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds were poorly differentiated. Depending on the mutation, the defect hindered rapsyn self-association or binding to AChR, impeded agrin-MuSK-LRP4-mediated AChR clustering, or decreased rapsyn expression.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) noted that the late-onset rapsyn phenotype was initially mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis. They stated that recognizing this phenotype should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy.

Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended analyzing SNP markers and using a gene dosage method in addition to direct sequencing of RAPSN, particularly when genetic counseling is at issue.

In their patient, Das et al. (2014) reached a diagnosis of rapsyn-deficient CMS by whole-exome sequencing after imaging, metabolic, electrodiagnostic, and microarray studies and a muscle biopsy had given nonspecific results. They highlighted next-generation sequencing as an important diagnostic tool that can lead to life-saving treatment.

### Clinical Management

Das et al. (2014) began treating a boy with rapsyn deficiency with pyridostigmine at 16 months of age. His muscle tone and strength improved dramatically, and his joint contractures steadily resolved. Four months after treatment began, he was starting to bear weight, could sit unsupported, and could say full words.

Engel et al. (2015) reported that most patients with rapsyn deficiency responded well to AChE inhibitors. Some gained additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some benefited when ephedrine or albuterol was added.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) reviewed hereditary disorders among Iranian Jews, a community with a very high degree of inbreeding. He noted that congenital myasthenia was frequent in both the Iranian and the Iraqi Jewish communities.

By haplotype analysis, Ohno et al. (2003) found that the RAPSN promoter -38A-G mutation, carried by 7 Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin, arose from a common founder.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) studied the RAPSN N88K mutation using 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb of the gene. A common haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes was also present in all heterozygous N88K carriers. The authors concluded that this finding supports a founder effect.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Engel et al. (2015) reported that 51 (14.3%) of 356 index patients with CMS investigated at the Mayo Clinic between 1988 and 2014 had rapsyn deficiency.
