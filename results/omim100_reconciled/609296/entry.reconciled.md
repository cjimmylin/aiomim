### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb anomalies, and urogenital malformations (BILU), also known as Hoffman syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOP2B gene (126431) on chromosome 3p24.

### Description

BILU syndrome, also called Hoffman syndrome, is an autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency. Broderick et al. (2019) described it as agammaglobulinemia or hypogammaglobulinemia with facial dysmorphism and limb anomalies. Other reported features include urogenital malformations, and patients have absent or reduced B cells with normal T cells (Edery et al., 2001; Papapietro et al., 2020).

### Nomenclature

Hügle et al. (2011) concluded that their mother-daughter pair and the sporadic girl reported by Hoffman et al. (2001) had the same condition, which they called Hoffman syndrome. The acronym BILU (B-cell immunodeficiency, limb anomalies, and urogenital malformations) was used for the autosomal dominant disorder described in 2 unrelated families (Tischkowitz et al., 2004; Papapietro et al., 2020). Papapietro et al. (2020) concluded that BILU syndrome and Hoffman syndrome are the same disease, TOP2B deficiency. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hoffman et al. (2001) reported a 6-year-old girl with isolated humoral immunodeficiency and an unusual combination of dysmorphic features. She had microcephaly, micrognathia, sickle-shaped eyebrows, hypoplastic alae nasi, thenar hypoplasia, triphalangeal thumbs, partial syndactyly of toes 4 and 5, recessed great toes, hypoplastic first metatarsals, an anterior anus, and hypoplastic labia minora. She also had postnatal growth retardation and developmental delay, and her development was substantially slower than that of her twin. She had recurrent sinopulmonary infections and pneumococcal bacteremia. Laboratory evaluation showed hypogammaglobulinemia, absent B cells, and a 46,XX karyotype. Searches of the literature and the London Dysmorphology Database found no matching syndrome, and the authors suggested that she might represent a new syndrome of unknown cause. ⚠︎

Edery et al. (2001) reported a 3-generation family in which B-cell immunodeficiency occurred together with distal limb abnormalities and urogenital malformations.

Tischkowitz et al. (2004) reported a second family with B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb abnormalities, genitourinary malformations, and mild dysmorphic features, and further described the phenotype in females. ⚠︎

Hügle et al. (2011) reported a 7-year-old girl with almost complete loss of B cells, facial dysmorphism, and malformations of the limbs and genitalia. Her mother had similar dysmorphic features and a milder form of the B-cell deficiency. The authors reviewed the features of these 2 patients and of the girl reported by Hoffman et al. (2001).

Broderick et al. (2019) studied 2 unrelated families with autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency. Ages at presentation were 7 and 35 years in family 1 and 6 years in family 2. The probands had recurrent infections with polysaccharide-encapsulated bacteria, severe hypogammaglobulinemia, absent CD19+ B cells, facial dysmorphism, and limb anomalies. T-cell responses to mitogens were normal. The patients formed no germinal centers and did not develop protective serologic titers after immunization. However, detectable circulating immunoglobulins indicated that some B cells were present below clinical limits of detection. CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell numbers were normal, and NK cells were reduced in 2 patients. A third patient (family 3), who presented at 5 years of age, was first reported as having an atypical form of ablepharon-macrostomia syndrome and was later found to have humoral immunodeficiency with no detectable peripheral B cells and normal T-cell mitogen stimulation. No malignancy had been observed in these patients. ✗

Papapietro et al. (2020) studied the 2 previously described BILU families, which originated from Cyprus (family A) and France (family B). The patients had absent or reduced B cells with normal T and myeloid cells. Multicolor flow cytometry of a bone marrow aspirate from patient II:4 of family A showed no CD19+ cells of any stage, including pro-B, pre-B, immature, and mature B cells. T-cell, NK-cell, and myeloid lineages were normal.

### Inheritance

Edery et al. (2001) suggested that the association of B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb abnormalities, and urogenital malformations in a 3-generation family might be a new autosomal dominant syndrome. Tischkowitz et al. (2004) reported a second family with autosomal dominant inheritance and suggested that the association was a true syndrome. Hügle et al. (2011) described an affected mother and daughter. Papapietro et al. (2020) showed that the TOP2B mutation in family A arose de novo in an affected woman and was passed to her affected daughter, and Broderick et al. (2019) identified a de novo heterozygous TOP2B mutation in 1 of their families. Both findings are consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Broderick et al. (2019) noted a brief clinical report of a patient with progressive microcephaly, developmental delay, and hypotonia who carried a TOP2B variant outside the TOPRIM domain (H58Y). They found no similarities to their own patients and considered it unclear whether that variant is causative.

### Molecular Genetics

Tischkowitz et al. (2004) studied affected individuals from 2 BILU families. Sequencing found no mutation in the candidate gene HOXA13, and there was no evidence of a microdeletion involving HOXA13 or the HOXA cluster as a whole.

Broderick et al. (2019) performed whole-genome sequencing of affected and unaffected members of 2 families with Hoffman syndrome. TOP2B was the only gene with a heterozygous variant in each family that was present only in affected members. One variant was a de novo in-frame deletion (c.1761_1763delAGA; p.EE587E) and the other was a missense mutation (c.1448C-T; S483L). The authors did not state which variant occurred in which family. In a third patient, independent reanalysis of clinical whole-exome sequencing found a heterozygous c.1897G-A transition in TOP2B, resulting in a gly633-to-ser (G633S) substitution. All mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and all 3 affect the TOPRIM (DNA gating) domain. The authors noted that TOP2B has a probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) of 1, which suggested disease through haploinsufficiency or partial dominance. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, constructs carrying the patient mutations failed to complement a temperature-sensitive top2 allele and did not support recovery of top2-null haploid spore clones, indicating loss of topoisomerase II function. Strains heterozygous for the mutations grew more slowly and decatenated kinetoplast DNA less effectively than wildtype, indicating a partial dominant-negative effect.

In the Cypriot BILU family (family A), Papapietro et al. (2020) identified a heterozygous c.1453G-C transversion in TOP2B, resulting in an ala485-to-pro (A485P) substitution. It was the only coding mutation absent from the healthy parents (I:3 and I:4). It arose de novo in their affected daughter (II:4) and was passed to her affected daughter (III:1). Sanger sequencing found the same mutation in the patients of the French family (family B). Recombinant TOP2B A485P had more than 10-fold lower DNA relaxation and decatenation activity. Coexpression with wildtype TOP2B lowered decatenation activity, which suggested a dominant-negative effect rather than haploinsufficiency. TOP2B protein was reduced in patient T cells, fibroblasts, and iPSCs. In HEK-293 cells, the mutant protein produced a degradation product, suggesting reduced stability, but it still interacted with TOP2A.

Erdős et al. (2021) reported an inherited TOP2B mutation and suggested that it may confirm mutational hotspots in the TOPRIM domain of TOP2B.

### Pathogenesis

Broderick et al. (2019) found that Top2b mRNA and protein were more highly expressed in wildtype mouse B cells than in T cells. They proposed that the disorder is restricted to B cells because loss of topoisomerase function preferentially reduces transcription of long genes. They noted that mouse B-cell transcription factor genes (EBF 390 kb, Pax5 178 kb, Foxo1 85 kb) are much longer than T-cell genes (Tcf1 30 kb, Notch1 45 kb) or shared genes (Rag1 11 kb, E2A 24 kb). In published ChIP-seq data and etoposide-induced END-seq breaks from mouse primary B cells, Top2b, but not Top2a, was found at the promoter and the intron 5 enhancer of Pax5. This suggested that defective Top2b activity could impair germline transcription and Igh locus accessibility, with negative effects on V(D)J recombination.

Papapietro et al. (2020) found that the complete absence of CD19+ cells in a patient's bone marrow indicated a block in B-cell development after the common lymphoid progenitor stage. They noted that the block is leaky, because some patients have immunoglobulins and small numbers of B cells.

### Diagnosis

Broderick et al. (2019) stated that bone marrow biopsy had not been clinically indicated in their patients, so the stage at which B-cell development fails in humans remained open. Their mouse data suggested a reduced bone marrow B-cell compartment, with surviving cells less able to respond to antigen or general stimuli.

### Population Genetics

Papapietro et al. (2020) noted that the A485P mutation was absent from more than 90,000 subjects in gnomAD. In contrast, 42 unaffected gnomAD subjects carried heterozygous loss-of-function TOP2B variants, which argued against haploinsufficiency as the disease mechanism.

### Animal Model

Broderick et al. (2019) found that Top2b-null mice did not survive the perinatal period. B cell-specific deletion of Top2b (using mb1-cre or cd19-cre) reduced peripheral CD19+ B cells and altered splenic follicle architecture. It also reduced PrePro B, Pre B, and immature B cells in bone marrow and B cells at every splenic developmental stage. Kappa-deleting recombination excision circles and Pax5 expression were reduced, but Rag1 expression was unchanged.

Broderick et al. (2019) also made knockin mice carrying the mouse equivalent of the patient EE587E mutation. Homozygotes were not viable. Top2b +/EE587E heterozygotes had a reduced spleen-to-body ratio, reduced total serum IgG, impaired antigen-specific antibody responses to ovalbumin and Pneumovax-23, and fewer B cells at most developmental stages. Their B cells showed reduced Pax5 and Foxo1 expression and increased DNA strand breaks. Peripheral B cells were reduced even compared with Top2b +/- mice, whereas T cells were unaffected. After LPS stimulation, with or without IL-4, heterozygous B cells secreted less total IgG, IgG3, and IgG2b, and more than 90% did not advance beyond the first division. Splenic T cells produced normal amounts of IL-2 in response to PMA and ionomycin and proliferated normally, consistent with the normal T-cell mitogen responses seen in patients. No malignancy was observed in the mice.

Knockin mice heterozygous for the equivalent mutation had reduced B cells, reduced serum IgG, and impaired antibody responses, while their T cells were normal.

In a Cypriot family with BILU syndrome (family A), Papapietro et al. (2020) identified a heterozygous c.1453G-C transversion in the TOP2B gene, resulting in an ala485-to-pro (A485P) substitution. The mutation was absent from the healthy parents, arose de novo in their affected daughter (II:4), and was passed to her affected daughter (III:1).
