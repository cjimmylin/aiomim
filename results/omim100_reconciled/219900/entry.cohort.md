### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis, also called intermediate cystinosis, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene (606272) on chromosome 17p13.2.

### Description

Late-onset (intermediate) cystinosis is a rare form of cystinosis. It is milder than the infantile nephropathic form. Attard et al. (1999) described symptoms beginning between 12 and 15 years of age. Thoene et al. (1999) found that the disease arose from 1 mild CTNS mutation combined with 1 mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or from homozygosity for a mutation predicted to be mild.

### Nomenclature

The disorder has been called adolescent cystinosis (Goldman et al., 1971), late-onset cystinosis (Langman et al., 1985; Attard et al., 1999), and intermediate cystinosis (Thoene et al., 1999).

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of cystinosis.

Spear et al. (1971) reported polykaryocytosis (multinucleated cells) of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis. They also described an unusual clinical variant of the disorder.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with the intermediate form of cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) described late-onset cystinosis as a rarer form of the disorder, with symptoms beginning between 12 and 15 years of age. They studied 2 late-onset patients whose phenotype was milder than infantile nephropathic cystinosis. They also studied 3 patients whose cystinosis began before 7 years of age but whose course was milder than the infantile nephropathic form.

### Inheritance

Intermediate cystinosis is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Thoene et al. (1999) found that affected individuals carried 2 CTNS mutations: either 1 mild mutation with 1 mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or a homozygous mild mutation. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis, Thoene et al. (1999) identified mutations in the CTNS gene, 2 of which had not been described before. The disease resulted either from 1 mild mutation combined with 1 mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis (compound heterozygosity) or from homozygosity for a mutation predicted to be mild. Thoene et al. (1999) transfected cystinotic fibroblasts with normal CTNS cDNA or with a cDNA carrying a mutation that causes intermediate cystinosis. Both partially corrected the cells' phenotype. ⚠︎

Attard et al. (1999) found CTNS mutations in 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis. The mutations affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin, which the authors said accounted for the milder phenotype. In 3 patients whose disease began before 7 years of age but followed a milder course than infantile nephropathic cystinosis, they found missense mutations. They suggested these mutations allow production of functional protein.

Macías-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 patients with cystinosis from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) found 23 different CTNS mutations, 14 of them novel, in patients with infantile nephropathic, late-onset, and atypical cystinosis. They had 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Of these, 12 had 2 severely truncating mutations, consistent with loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions, which the authors predicted would disrupt transmembrane domains and abolish protein function. In contrast, the mutations in 2 late-onset patients affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin, accounting for their milder phenotype. In 3 patients with onset before 7 years of age and a milder course, the missense mutations were suggested to allow production of functional protein.
