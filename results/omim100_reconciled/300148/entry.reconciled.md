### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MEHMO syndrome (MEHMO) is caused by hemizygous mutation in the EIF2S3 gene (300161) on chromosome Xp22.11.

### Description

MEHMO syndrome is an X-linked intellectual disability syndrome. Its name comes from its cardinal features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. In the family originally reported, patients did not live beyond 2 years (Steinmuller et al., 1998; DeLozier-Blanchet et al., 1999).

### Clinical Features

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) described 2 brothers with a severe syndrome of postnatal growth retardation and mental retardation. Features included extreme microcephaly, obesity developing during infancy, microgonadism, and a characteristic amphora-shaped facies. The neurologic examination was highly abnormal, with hypertonia, hyperreflexia, nystagmus, and extremely irritable and agitated behavior. The first child also had neonatal hypoglycemia and chronic constipation, and he died at age 4.5 years. The authors noted that the brothers most resembled patients with Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFL). However, the dwarfism and mental retardation were much more severe than in any reported BFL patient, and the neurologic and dysmorphic features differed significantly. They considered 2 possibilities: a particularly severe, perhaps allelic, variant of BFL, or a previously undescribed disorder.

Steinmuller et al. (1998) studied a large 3-generation family and described a previously unrecognized X-linked syndrome, which they termed MEHMO. Its clinical hallmarks were mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Life expectancy of patients was less than 2 years.

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) reported a child with MEHMO and lactic acidosis. Activities of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I, III, and IV in muscle were markedly reduced. Histologic staining of muscle showed mitochondrial proliferation and lipid storage. On electron microscopy, mitochondria were abnormal and enlarged, with concentric cristae and electron-dense bodies.

Borck et al. (2012) reported a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry in the maternal line in which 3 males had an intellectual disability syndrome with apparent X-linked inheritance. Common features were moderate to severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. Each affected individual also had unique features. Individual III:1 had cleft lip and palate and behavioral impairments. Individual III:2 had generalized seizures, and brain MRI at age 1 year showed a very thin corpus callosum, microcephaly, and mild enlargement and asymmetry of the lateral ventricles. Individual II:3 had postpubertal microgenitalism and obesity.

Moortgat et al. (2016) reported 2 unrelated families with suspected X-linked syndromic intellectual disability. In both families, affected males had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy.

Skopkova et al. (2017) reported 4 families with MEHMO syndrome. The first index patient (family 1) was a boy born to nonconsanguineous Slovak parents and had a healthy older sister. Microcephaly was detected prenatally at 28 weeks' gestation. He developed partial complex seizures at age 4 months, which were resistant to anticonvulsants, and MEHMO was diagnosed at 10 months. He had severe mental and motor delay, micropenis, cryptorchidism, central obesity, dysphagia, central hypotonia with peripheral hypertonia, and short stature. Dysmorphic features included full cheeks, large ears, downturned corners of the mouth, epiblepharon, long eyelashes, thick eyebrows, tapered fingers, and talipes. Brain imaging showed delayed myelination, cerebral atrophy, and a thin corpus callosum. Other findings included: - neonatal hypoglycemia - non-autoimmune diabetes mellitus treated with insulin from age 10 months - panhypopituitarism - atrial septal defect - elevated hepatic enzymes - severe combined dyslipidemia - recurrent bronchitis and bronchopneumonia - abnormal muscle mitochondria with decreased activity of respiratory chain complex II He was last reported at age 5 years. During intercurrent illness his condition worsened markedly, with more seizures and poorer diabetes control, which the authors compared to vanishing white matter disease.

The index patient in family 2 of Skopkova et al. (2017) was the first child of a nonconsanguineous Slovak family with no other known affected individuals. His mother was unaffected. He had low birth weight, microcephaly, neonatal hypoglycemia, and hypogenitalism. Seizures were diagnosed at age 6 months; they were resistant to therapy and had no EEG correlate. Mental and motor delay was severe: at age 2 years his development corresponded to the third month of life, and he had no voluntary movements or social interaction. Other features were hypotonia, central obesity, short stature, partial hypopituitarism, and non-autoimmune diabetes mellitus treated with insulin from age 10 months. Brain imaging showed delayed myelination, cerebral atrophy, and a thin corpus callosum. He was last reported at age 3 years. ⚠︎

Family 3 of Skopkova et al. (2017) was the German family previously reported by Steinmuller et al. (1998), with 4 affected males. The index patient (IV:3) was diagnosed prenatally with microcephaly. Therapy-resistant epileptic seizures were diagnosed at age 7 weeks. All affected males had the following features: - microcephaly and therapy-resistant seizures - hypogenitalism - severe mental and motor delay - central obesity, hypertonia, and postnatal growth retardation - dysmorphic features: edematous hands and feet, tapered fingers, single palmar crease, and bilateral talipes - increased acylcarnitine and fatty acids The index patient also had a narrow forehead, full cheeks, downturned corners of the mouth, and large ears. Facial telangiectasia was present in only 1 patient. The following were each present in only 1 of the 4 patients: low birth weight, diabetes with ketoacidosis at age 6 months, and a small thyroid. The patients died at ages 2 years, 7 months, 2 years, and 10 months. Female carriers in the family were unaffected. ⚠︎

The index patient in family 4 of Skopkova et al. (2017) was a boy of Northern European descent from the United States, born to nonconsanguineous parents and last examined at age 4 years 7 months. He had microcephaly, hypospadias, cryptorchidism, developmental delay, and short stature. He developed obesity, with a BMI at the 97th centile, after a longer period of failure to thrive. Neurologic findings were central hypotonia with peripheral hypertonia and spasticity. He also had chronic lung disease, dysphagia, delayed gastric emptying, grade 5 vesicoureteral reflux, and congenital scoliosis. He had no epileptic seizures, his EEG was normal, and he did not have diabetes.

### Inheritance

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) found an X-linked recessive gene tempting, given the importance of the X chromosome in mental retardation. However, they concluded that the disorder in their 2 affected brothers was probably recessive, either autosomal or X-linked. ⚠︎

Steinmuller et al. (1998) established X-linked inheritance of MEHMO by linkage analysis in a large 3-generation family. Later reports were consistent with X-linked recessive inheritance. Affected males were hemizygous for EIF2S3 mutations, and female obligate carriers were heterozygous (Moortgat et al., 2016). In the original family, all female mutation carriers were unaffected (Skopkova et al., 2017).

### Mapping

Steinmuller et al. (1998) studied a large 3-generation family using haplotype and 2-point linkage analyses. They assigned the MEHMO locus to Xp21.1-p22.13, in a region flanked by CYBB and DXS365. DeLozier-Blanchet et al. (1999) also reported assignment of the disease locus to Xp21.1-p22.13.

Borck et al. (2012) studied a Moroccan Jewish family with X-linked intellectual disability. Targeted linkage analysis identified a 10.7-Mb candidate region on Xp22.11-p21.1 containing 35 annotated genes.

### Heterogeneity

Borck et al. (2012) sequenced the EIF2S3 exons in 60 affected individuals from families with nonspecific, potentially X-linked intellectual disability and found no additional mutations. They suggested that EIF2S3 mutations confer a specific phenotype.

Skopkova et al. (2017) found no EIF2S3 variants in the patients reported by Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) or by Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002). The Leshinsky-Silver patient carried a nonsense mutation in SERAC1. The authors suggested that the Delozier-Blanchet family may carry a mutation outside the examined region or in another gene.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 3 affected males of the Moroccan Jewish family, Borck et al. (2012) used exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution. Capillary sequencing confirmed the mutation, and it cosegregated with the disorder in the family. The variant was not found in 188 X chromosomes from Moroccan Jewish controls, in dbSNP132, in the 1000 Genomes data, or in more than 8,000 alleles in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project. Ile222 lies in the GTP-binding domain and is strictly conserved in animals. The authors expressed tagged mutant eIF2-gamma in HeLa cells. The mutant protein pulled down substantially less eIF2-beta, whereas eIF2-alpha binding was not impaired, indicating that the mutation disrupts formation of the eIF2 complex. Borck et al. (2012) also noted a V151L variant in EIF2S3 that an earlier large-scale resequencing project had reported in a family with X-linked intellectual disability. In yeast, the corresponding mutation (V210L) caused defects in growth, GCN4 expression, and start site selection, and overexpression of tRNAi-Met suppressed these defects. The authors considered a causative role for V151L plausible. ⚠︎

Moortgat et al. (2016) performed X-chromosome exome sequencing in 2 unrelated families with suspected X-linked syndromic intellectual disability. They identified 2 hemizygous mutations in the EIF2S3 gene in affected males: a c.777T-G transversion resulting in an ile259-to-met (I259M) substitution, and a 4-bp deletion (c.1394_1397del) resulting in a frameshift (Ile465SerfsTer4). Female obligate carriers were heterozygous. In zebrafish, morpholino knockdown of the eif2s3 ortholog recapitulated the microcephaly and short stature seen in the patients.

Skopkova et al. (2017) identified the same hemizygous 4-bp deletion in the last exon of EIF2S3 (c.1394_1397delTCAA; Ile465SerfsTer4) in affected males from 3 families with MEHMO syndrome. These included the 2 Slovak families and the German family originally reported by Steinmuller et al. (1998). The deletion cosegregated with the disorder in the families and is predicted to cause a frameshift and a premature stop codon. Identity-by-descent analysis excluded close relationship between the families but suggested a founder effect with distant relatedness. The truncated eIF2-gamma protein was produced in patient fibroblasts. A yeast/human eIF2-gamma chimera carrying the mutation impaired eIF2 function: it caused slow growth that was worse at 37 degrees C, derepressed GCN4 expression nearly 3-fold, and increased UUG/AUG initiation about 2-fold. In the patient from family 4, they found a hemizygous c.324T-A transversion resulting in a ser108-to-arg (S108R) substitution. He had inherited it from his healthy mother, and his healthy brother could not be tested. The yeast equivalent of S108R (Asp167Arg) had no effect, and the authors interpreted S108R as a variant of unknown significance. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that all patients with the C-terminal frameshift mutation in EIF2S3 had the full MEHMO phenotype. Patients with missense mutations were less severely affected and had only a subset of the symptoms. The authors proposed that more severe EIF2S3 mutations cause the full MEHMO phenotype and less deleterious ones a milder form.

### Pathogenesis

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) studied muscle from a child with MEHMO and lactic acidosis. They found markedly reduced activities of respiratory chain complexes I, III, and IV, mitochondrial proliferation, lipid storage, and abnormal enlarged mitochondria with concentric cristae. They concluded that MEHMO is a mitochondrial disorder and one of very few X-linked mitochondrial syndromes. Later, Skopkova et al. (2017) found no EIF2S3 variant in this patient but found a nonsense mutation in SERAC1. ⚠︎

Borck et al. (2012) contrasted this disorder with vanishing white matter disease, which is caused by mutations in eIF2B. They noted that patients with the eIF2-gamma mutation do not show loss of white matter.

Skopkova et al. (2017) found that fibroblasts from patients with the frameshift mutation had a 2.5-fold increase in DDIT3 transcript and elevated CHOP protein, indicating activation of the integrated stress response. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

In yeast, Borck et al. (2012) studied mutations at val281 of eIF2-gamma, the residue corresponding to human ile222. Both V281T and V281K impaired eIF2-beta binding. They increased GCN4-lacZ expression 2.5-fold and 40-fold, respectively, and raised the UUG/AUG initiation ratio 2- to 3-fold. Overexpression of eIF2-beta suppressed these defects. Only V281K impaired growth. ⚠︎

Moortgat et al. (2016) found that morpholino-based knockdown of eif2s3, the zebrafish ortholog of EIF2S3, recapitulated the microcephaly and short stature seen in patients, supporting the pathogenicity of the identified variants.
