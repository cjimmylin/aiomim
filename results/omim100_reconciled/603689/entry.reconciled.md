### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding titin (TTN; 188840) on chromosome 2q31.

### Description

Myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is an autosomal dominant myopathy. Onset is insidious in adult life, and the disorder progresses slowly. Patients present with predominantly distal, proximal or respiratory muscle weakness, and respiratory failure develops while they can still walk (Birchall et al., 2005; Pfeffer et al., 2012). Muscle involvement is highly selective. Muscle biopsies show myofibrillar changes, including F-actin-positive myofibrillar plaques, eosinophilic inclusions and rimmed vacuoles (Edstrom et al., 1990; Pfeffer et al., 2014). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Pfeffer et al. (2014) concluded that TTN mutations are a cause of myofibrillar myopathy. They proposed that hereditary myopathy with early respiratory failure (HMERF) caused by TTN mutations be classified as MFM-titinopathy.

### Clinical Features

Edstrom et al. (1990) described 16 patients from 7 pedigrees with an unusual adult-onset limb-girdle myopathy. Respiratory muscle weakness was a prominent early finding in all cases. In many patients, acute respiratory incapacity was the reason for the first neurologic examination. Other early symptoms were neck flexor weakness and sometimes foot extensor weakness. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed a myopathy, and serum muscle enzyme concentrations were normal or slightly elevated. Muscle biopsies contained myofibrillar plaques that stained strongly with rhodamine-conjugated phalloidin, an F-actin marker. On electron microscopy, the plaques consisted of moderately dense thin filaments and were related to Z-disc splitting or extended from Z-discs.

Nicolao et al. (1999) studied 2 Swedish families with autosomal dominant myopathy. Affected members had proximal weakness, early respiratory failure and characteristic cytoplasmic bodies in muscle biopsies.

Chinnery et al. (2001) assessed 24 individuals spanning 3 generations of a family with an autosomal dominant myopathy, 11 of whom were affected. Muscle pathology was documented in 7 of the 11 affected individuals. The disorder presented in mid-adult life with tibialis anterior weakness. Lung function tests showed progressive global respiratory muscle weakness that was detectable from presentation, with preferential diaphragmatic involvement in some cases. Hip girdle and shoulder girdle weakness appeared later in the disease course. Onset was earlier and progression faster in the second generation. Clinical and pathologic features correlated strikingly. Clinically unaffected muscles showed minimal change. Moderately affected muscles showed fiber splitting, eosinophilic inclusions and rimmed vacuoles. Severely involved muscles had been replaced by fat and fibrous connective tissue. The inclusions were Congophilic and reacted with antibodies to desmin, beta-amyloid and phosphorylated tau protein.

Birchall et al. (2005) performed muscle MRI in 7 patients with autosomal dominant myopathy with early respiratory failure: 4 males and 3 females aged 37 to 66 years. They described the disorder as a dominant limb myopathy with early respiratory failure while patients were still ambulant. Onset was insidious in late adult life, and muscle involvement was highly selective both clinically and pathologically. MRI showed selective early involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus muscles that could not be detected clinically.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) studied 31 TTN mutation carriers from 3 families. The disorder presented with predominantly distal, proximal or respiratory muscle weakness. Onset was highly variable, ranging from early adulthood to a mild phenotype in advanced age. Patients developed neuromuscular ventilatory failure while still ambulant. Weakness occurred earlier and was more severe in the lower extremities, and some patients had axial muscle weakness. Respiratory function deteriorated progressively. No patient developed cardiomyopathy over up to 31 years of follow-up. Muscle MRI in 21 affected patients showed characteristic abnormalities, with semitendinosus involvement in 20 of the 21, including 3 presymptomatic patients. Some muscle biopsies showed eosinophilic inclusions and rimmed vacuoles. ⚠︎

Pfeffer et al. (2014) reported 11 patients from 5 British families (F.1 to F.5). Onset was in the fourth or fifth decade, with tripping, falling, myalgia and difficulty walking. Weakness progressed slowly and involved proximal, distal and axial muscles. CK was as high as 1,195 IU/L. Pulmonary function testing showed respiratory muscle weakness in 10 of the 11 patients. Four patients needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation 20 to 30 years after onset. Features not previously noted in the disorder included scapular winging, which was often asymmetric, elbow and Achilles tendon contractures, and variable spinal rigidity. Some patients had calf or quadriceps hypertrophy. All patients remained ambulant except patient F.2A, who lost ambulation after a disease course of about 25 years. F.2A also had mild left ventricular impairment and palpitations, and patient F.4B had supraventricular tachycardia. Two subjects, F.2C and F.4C, reported only mild myalgias and had mild or very mild weakness. F.2C had reduced FVC, and F.4C had raised CK. Muscle MRI showed early involvement of the semitendinosus and peroneus longus muscles. Presymptomatic patient F.2C already showed the characteristic fatty infiltration of these 2 muscles. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic or dystrophic changes, rimmed vacuoles, and eosinophilic and dark-blue Gomori trichrome inclusions. Six of 7 biopsies showed accumulation of desmin, myotilin, p62 and ubiquitin. In 3 biopsies (F.1B, F.4A and F.5), myotilin aggregates had a characteristic 'cheetah skin' appearance. The biopsy from patient F.4A also showed accumulation of gamma-sarcoglycan and dystrophin. ⚠︎

Pfeffer et al. (2014) also described 2 patients with similar phenotypes who carried TTN variants of uncertain significance. Patient F.6 had onset in the thirties with difficulty lifting the lower limbs. This patient had a mildly raised CK (204 IU/L), mild neck weakness, calf hypertrophy and asymmetric scapular winging, with FVC of 52% sitting. Biopsy showed dystrophic features with rimmed vacuoles. Patient F.7 was originally from Brazil and had several affected family members, but none of them were available for assessment. This patient had onset in the thirties, a mildly raised CK (290 IU/L), proximal upper and lower limb weakness, severe ankle dorsiflexion weakness, deltoid atrophy and mild scapular winging. Respiratory function was normal. MRI showed major bilateral involvement of the semitendinosus and fatty infiltration of the tibialis anterior and peroneus longus muscles. Biopsy showed rimmed vacuoles and cytoplasmic bodies.

### Inheritance

Edstrom et al. (1990) found evidence of autosomal dominant inheritance in most of the 7 pedigrees they studied. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also observed in the families reported by Nicolao et al. (1999) and Chinnery et al. (2001). In 3 families reported by Pfeffer et al. (2012), the TTN mutation segregated with the phenotype. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Nicolao et al. (1999) performed a genomewide linkage screen in 2 Swedish families with autosomal dominant myopathy with proximal weakness and early respiratory failure. They established linkage to chromosome 2q24-q31, with a maximum combined 2-point lod score of 4.87 at theta = 0 for marker D2S1245. Haplotype analysis placed the gene in a 17-cM region between markers D2S2384 and D2S364.

Xiang et al. (1999) performed a genomewide scan in a French family and obtained a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.11 for markers in a 4-cM region between D2S1272 and D2S1260. This was the highest lod score the family could achieve. They concluded that the gene responsible for the disorder in this family was likely located on chromosome 2q21.

Chinnery et al. (2001) excluded linkage in their family to all known loci associated with distal myopathies. They concluded that the disorder was genetically and phenotypically distinct.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) combined genomewide linkage analysis with whole-exome sequencing and identified a shared haplotype of 2.93 Mbp on chromosome 2. This haplotype contained the likely causal variant in the TTN gene.

### Heterogeneity

Xiang et al. (1999) studied a French family whose clinical phenotype and muscle biopsy findings resembled those of the Swedish families mapped to 2q24-q31 by Nicolao et al. (1999). They completely excluded linkage to the 2q24-q31 region in this family. They instead proposed a likely locus on chromosome 2q21.

### Molecular Genetics

Lange et al. (2005) reported that a human mutation in the protein kinase domain of titin causes hereditary muscle disease. The mutation disrupted a mechanically regulated signaling pathway involving nbr1, p62/SQSTM1 and MuRF2.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) combined genomewide linkage analysis with whole-exome sequencing in families with hereditary myopathy with early respiratory failure. They identified a g.274375T-C change in the TTN gene, resulting in a cys30071-to-arg (C30071R) substitution, within a shared haplotype of 2.93 Mbp on chromosome 2. The variant segregated with the phenotype in 21 individuals from the original family and in 9 subjects from a second family, which had the same highly selective pattern of muscle involvement on MRI. It also segregated in a third familial case with a similar phenotype.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) screened 127 unrelated, undiagnosed UK probands with features compatible with myofibrillar myopathy for the TTN C30071R and R32450W mutations. They identified the heterozygous C30071R mutation in 5 probands (3.9%), including 4 of 72 (5.5%) with a clinical diagnosis of myofibrillar myopathy. No patient had a mutation at residue 32450. The mutation co-segregated with disease in families F.1, F.2 and F.4. Two further patients carried novel heterozygous variants of uncertain pathogenicity in the 119th fibronectin type III (FN3) domain: P30091L (g.274436C-T) in patient F.6 and N30145K (g.274599C-G) in patient F.7. The 61-year-old brother of patient F.6 was a healthy carrier of the P30091L variant. Pfeffer et al. (2014) cautioned that TTN is highly polymorphic. Exome data from 239 subjects contained 71 novel TTN coding changes of uncertain significance, which appeared to be randomly distributed across the gene, and none of them was in the 119th FN3 domain.

### Pathogenesis

Edstrom et al. (1990) suggested that the muscle biopsy changes pointed to defective myofibrillogenesis, and they raised the possibility of defective actin polymerization.

Lange et al. (2005) identified a signaling complex in which the titin kinase domain interacts with the zinc-finger protein nbr1 through a mechanically inducible conformation. Nbr1 targets the ubiquitin-associated protein p62/SQSTM1 to sarcomeres. p62 in turn interacts with MuRF2, a muscle-specific E3 ligase that binds the transactivation domain of SRF. Mechanical inactivity induced nuclear translocation of MuRF2, which reduced nuclear SRF and repressed transcription. These findings link titin kinase mechanosensing to the control of muscle gene expression and protein turnover.

### Diagnosis

Birchall et al. (2005) found that muscle MRI showed selective early involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus that could not be detected clinically. Closely related muscles progressed at different rates. They proposed that these MRI findings were specific to the disorder and allowed early noninvasive diagnosis in at-risk individuals.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) concluded that the disease should be considered in patients with adult-onset proximal or distal myopathy and early respiratory failure, even when muscle pathology is nonspecific. They considered the muscle MRI findings characteristic and recommended MRI as an initial investigation that should prompt TTN screening. They also concluded that whole-exome sequencing is a reliable, cost-effective way to screen the 363-exon TTN gene, provided the gene is adequately captured.

### Clinical Management

Pfeffer et al. (2014) noted that MFM-titinopathy did not appear to be associated with major cardiac involvement. They nevertheless recommended cardiac surveillance for all patients with TTN mutations. They also recommended sequencing of the relevant TTN FN3 domain in the diagnostic workup of myofibrillar myopathy, particularly in patients with early respiratory involvement and supportive muscle MRI findings.

### Population Genetics

Nicolao et al. (1999) found that affected individuals from 2 Swedish families shared an identical haplotype, which suggested a common origin.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) found that the probands of 5 new families and 3 previously reported families with the TTN C30071R mutation shared a common haplotype with a core region of 171 kbp. The probability that unrelated families shared this haplotype by chance was less than 10(-6). The authors suggested a British founder effect and an ancient mutation.
