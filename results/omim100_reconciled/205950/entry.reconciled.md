### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive pyridoxine-refractory sideroblastic anemia-2 (SIDBA2) is caused by mutation in the SLC25A38 gene (610819). ⚠︎

### Description

Guernsey et al. (2009) described the sideroblastic anemias as a heterogeneous group of congenital and acquired blood disorders. Their morphologic hallmark is ringed sideroblasts, which are bone marrow erythroid precursors with abnormal iron deposits inside their mitochondria. The form described in this entry is a congenital, nonsyndromic, autosomal recessive sideroblastic anemia that does not respond to pyridoxine (Jardine et al., 1994; Guernsey et al., 2009).

### Clinical Features

Manabe et al. (1982) described a female infant with congenital refractory sideroblastic anemia. She did not respond to pyridoxine therapy. A marked reduction of delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) synthetase activity in her erythroblasts was found both with and without pyridoxal phosphate treatment.

Jardine et al. (1994) reported a son and daughter of unaffected parents who had transfusion-dependent, pyridoxine-refractory sideroblastic anemia from birth. Their hemoglobin levels were 4.3 and 6.4 g/dl, respectively. Delta-aminolevulinate synthase activity in erythroblasts from the sister's fractionated marrow was 135 pmol delta-aminolevulinate formed/10(6) erythroblasts/hour, which was within the normal range of 110 to 650 pmol.

Guernsey et al. (2009) studied patients with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia, which is marked by ringed sideroblasts in the bone marrow.

### Biochemical Features

Manabe et al. (1982) found normal mitochondrial neutral protease activity in the erythroblasts of a female infant with congenital pyridoxine-refractory sideroblastic anemia. This protease specifically inactivates the apo form of ALA synthetase, and the apoenzyme's sensitivity to the protease was also normal. They speculated that the low ALA synthetase activity resulted from impaired production of the enzyme rather than faster destruction, which could explain why the patient did not respond to pyridoxine.

In contrast, Jardine et al. (1994) found normal erythroblast delta-aminolevulinate synthase activity (135 pmol/10(6) erythroblasts/hour; normal range, 110-650 pmol) in an affected girl whose brother was also affected.

### Inheritance

Jardine et al. (1994) concluded that sideroblastic anemia in a brother and sister of unaffected parents was strongly suggestive of an autosomal recessive trait. The mother had no dimorphic erythrocyte population and showed random X inactivation in peripheral leukocytes, indicating that she was not a carrier of an X-linked sideroblastic anemia mutation. Guernsey et al. (2009) defined an autosomal recessive form of nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia associated with mutations in the SLC25A38 gene.

### Mapping

Jardine et al. (1994) excluded ALAS2 as the cause of sideroblastic anemia in an affected sib pair. They identified a G-to-A transition polymorphism 220 nucleotides 3-prime of the ALAS2 AATAAA polyadenylation signal. Analysis of 148 alleles by mismatch PCR and XmnI restriction digestion gave a gene frequency of 25%. Analysis of this intragenic ALAS2 polymorphism showed that the affected sibs had inherited different maternal alleles. Sequencing in the affected son found no mutations in the ALAS2 coding region, the intron/exon boundaries, or the 1-kb 5-prime promoter region.

Guernsey et al. (2009) used positional cloning to identify the gene underlying a previously unknown form of autosomal recessive nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia.

### Molecular Genetics

In patients with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic congenital sideroblastic anemia, Guernsey et al. (2009) used a positional cloning approach and identified mutations in the SLC25A38 gene, which encodes an erythroid-specific mitochondrial carrier family protein. They showed that SLC25A38 is important for heme biosynthesis in eukaryotes.
