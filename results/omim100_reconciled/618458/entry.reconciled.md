### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Knobloch syndrome-2 (KNO2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PAK2 gene (605022).

### Description

Menzel et al. (2004) described Knobloch syndrome as a disorder characterized by high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration with retinal detachment, and congenital encephalocele. Antonarakis et al. (2021) described Knobloch syndrome type 2 (KNO2) in a family whose affected members had intellectual disability, autistic behavior, retinal degeneration, and encephalocele.

### Clinical Features

Wilson et al. (1998) reported 2 sibs with high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration, and occipital encephalocele or a scalp lesion. One sib had very early onset of severe eye disease, and the other was notable for a very mild scalp defect. Both sibs appeared to have an unusual pulmonary lymphatic condition.

Antonarakis et al. (2021) restudied the pedigree published in 1998 by Wilson et al. (1998), which they designated KNO2. They described the 2 affected sibs as having intellectual disability, autistic behavior, retinal degeneration, and encephalocele. The parents were unaffected. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Menzel et al. (2004) described Knobloch syndrome as autosomal recessive. In the KNO2 family, however, Antonarakis et al. (2021) found the same heterozygous PAK2 mutation in both affected sibs and did not find it in their unaffected parents. They concluded that KNO2 in this family was dominant and that the mutation, which appeared de novo in both sibs, most likely reflected germline mosaicism in one parent. Deep sequencing of the parents' blood DNA did not detect mosaicism.

### Heterogeneity

Menzel et al. (2004) studied 2 unrelated Knobloch syndrome families, one from Hungary and one from New Zealand. They confirmed the involvement of COL18A1 in Knobloch syndrome. Linkage to the COL18A1 locus was excluded in the New Zealand family, which was evidence for a second Knobloch syndrome locus that they named KNO2.

### Molecular Genetics

In the New Zealand family with Knobloch syndrome that did not show linkage to COL18A1, Menzel et al. (2004) used mutation analysis to exclude COL15A1, a multiplexin collagen similar to COL18A1, as the gene responsible for KNO2. ⚠︎

Antonarakis et al. (2021) performed whole-exome sequencing in the 2 affected sibs and the unaffected parents of the KNO2 family originally reported by Wilson et al. (1998). They identified a heterozygous glu435-to-lys (E435K) mutation in the PAK2 gene as the cause of the disorder. The mutation was present in both sibs and appeared to be de novo, which suggested germline mosaicism in a parent. Deep sequencing of the parents' blood DNA did not detect mosaicism. The mutation lies in the kinase domain. Structural analysis predicted that it might compromise kinase activity, and in transfected HEK293T cells it caused a partial loss of kinase activity. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Wilson et al. (1998) reviewed the literature on Knobloch syndrome and suggested that mesoderm might be involved in the morphogenesis of the disorder.
