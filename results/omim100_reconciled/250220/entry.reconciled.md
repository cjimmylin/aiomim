### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Sedaghatian type (SMDS) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the GPX4 gene (138322) on chromosome 19p13.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia is a neonatal lethal form of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia (SMD). Its features are severe metaphyseal chondrodysplasia with mild limb shortening, platyspondyly, cardiac conduction defects, and central nervous system abnormalities (Smith et al., 2014).

### Clinical Features

Sedaghatian (1980) reported 2 brothers, born within a year of each other, who had the same skeletal disorder: severe congenital metaphyseal involvement, mild rhizomelic shortness of the upper limbs, and mild platyspondyly. Both died at 3 days of age of cardiorespiratory insufficiency. Only one had laboratory tests before death, which showed low calcium, high phosphorus, and high alkaline phosphatase levels. At autopsy, this infant had pulmonary, renal, and adrenal hemorrhage, subendocardial myocarditis, and myocardial necrosis. The parents were normal and later had an affected daughter.

Opitz et al. (1987) reported a second Iranian family. The affected infant died on day 4 and had histologic evidence of severe epimetaphyseal dysplasia. Opitz et al. (1987) suggested that Sedaghatian syndrome may be more complexly pleiotropic than the radiographs indicate. They noted that one of Sedaghatian's original patients also had microphthalmia, ear asymmetry, depressed nasal bridge, broad nose, short neck, prominent sternum, and short lower extremities.

Peeden et al. (1992) reported another infant with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia who had a lethal course.

Elcioglu and Hall (1998) described the radiologic findings in 2 unrelated patients with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. After reviewing the literature, they identified further anomalies, including disproportionately long fibulae, dysharmonious maturation, and turricephaly.

Koutouby et al. (2000) reported an infant with spondylometaphyseal dysplasia born to nonconsanguineous Yemeni parents. The radiologic findings were consistent with lethal metaphyseal chondrodysplasia of the Sedaghatian type. All previously reported patients had died within 4 days of life, but this patient survived 161 days. Extensive investigations were performed, and analysis of genomic DNA from the patient and parents did not identify an abnormal gene.

English et al. (2006) reported a girl with Sedaghatian spondylometaphyseal dysplasia born to nonconsanguineous Caucasian parents. She had metaphyseal dysplasia, was hypotonic from birth, and had recurrent cyanotic episodes starting within a few hours of delivery. Cerebral imaging showed absence of the corpus callosum and marked frontotemporal pachygyria. She developed seizures on day 14 of life and died at 17 days of age. English et al. (2006) suggested that the disorder may be associated with neuronal migration abnormalities and proposed a novel association with agenesis of the corpus callosum.

Aygun et al. (2012) reported a patient with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia who had metaphyseal dysplasia, congenital atrioventricular block, a simplified gyral pattern, hypogenesis of the corpus callosum, and severe cerebellar hypoplasia. Aygun et al. (2012) suggested that central nervous system malformations might be a major component of the disorder. They recommended evaluating these malformations in detail, as this might uncover the underlying pathophysiology.

### Inheritance

Sedaghatian (1980) suggested autosomal recessive inheritance, because the parents of the 2 affected brothers were normal and later had an affected daughter.

Opitz et al. (1987) suggested that Sedaghatian syndrome is an autosomal recessive trait with a high gene frequency in Iranians. Their reasons were that affected children of both sexes had been born to normal first-cousin parents and that the disorder appeared to be confined to Iranians.

Peeden et al. (1992) noted that an earlier-reported Iranian infant with a similar perinatal course and similar radiographs had been born to first-cousin parents. This suggested an autosomal recessive single-gene defect.

Smith et al. (2014) found that the disorder in 2 families was associated with biallelic GPX4 mutations, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Smith et al. (2014) performed whole-exome sequencing in a girl with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia and her unaffected parents. They found 2 rare variants in the GPX4 gene (138322): c.587+5G-A, inherited from the mother, and c.588-8_588-4del, which had arisen de novo (NM_001039848.1). Both variants were predicted to affect splicing. In vitro studies showed that the 2 splice mutations caused splicing out of part of exon 4 and skipping of exon 5, respectively. Both resulted in a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4.

Smith et al. (2014) then identified a second child with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. DNA from the child was not available. Sanger sequencing showed that both unaffected parents carried the same heterozygous stop mutation in exon 3 of GPX4, c.381C-A, which predicts a tyr127-to-ter (Y127X) substitution (NM_001039848.1).

### Pathogenesis

Sedaghatian (1980) attributed the low calcium, high phosphorus, and high alkaline phosphatase levels in one affected infant to a renal defect. The author stated that it was unclear how these findings related to the skeletal and cardiac abnormalities. ⚠︎
