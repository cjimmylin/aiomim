### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. It is characterized by severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. Affected children show progressive neurologic impairment, sometimes with seizures. The disorder is often lethal in the first weeks of life.

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) studied a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates. Some members of this family had a rare autosomal recessive syndrome characterized by severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The authors noted that the clinical presentation overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with cases carrying occludin mutations, but that massive intracranial hemorrhage distinguished these patients.

Akawi et al. (2013) reported 4 affected children from 3 families. In family 1, the parents were second cousins of Turkish origin. Their affected daughter had low birth weight and tonic seizures that were resistant to treatment. Physical examination showed a head circumference of 30 cm, a sloping forehead, a prominent bulging anterior fontanelle, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Neuroimaging showed subependymal calcification, multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage, and posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus. Her platelet levels were normal, and she died at 2 months of age. ⚠︎

Family 2 of Akawi et al. (2013) was a nonconsanguineous Afghani family with 2 affected brothers. The first boy had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth, along with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. Brain MRI at 15 days of age showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities in both cerebral hemispheres, and multiple subependymal cysts. Renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys with an echogenic cortex and altered corticomedullary differentiation. He had progressive neurologic deterioration and died at 2 weeks of age. His brother had bilateral cataracts at birth. His tone and reflexes were normal and there was no clinical evidence of seizures. However, an EEG on the third day of life showed bilateral epileptiform activity. Cranial ultrasound showed periventricular echogenicity and extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage. He also had a drop in hemoglobin, respiratory failure requiring ventilation, and pelvicalyceal dilatation of the right kidney. He died on the fifth day of life. ⚠︎

In family 3 of Akawi et al. (2013), the parents were first cousins of Moroccan origin and had had a total of 5 pregnancies. The index patient was a girl with bilateral central and nuclear cataracts, irritability, a bulging anterior fontanelle, macrocephaly (+4 SD), and prominent scalp veins. Head CT showed multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, and diffusely hypodense brain parenchyma. Other neuroimaging findings were dilated lateral ventricles, intraventricular hemorrhage, periventricular cysts, cerebral edema, cystic changes of the brain parenchyma, and a thin corpus callosum. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus. She had severe anemia with normal platelet levels and died at 39 days of age.

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that all affected children had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, and some had seizures. Neuroimaging showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with brain destruction and subependymal calcification. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement were more variable. The disorder was lethal in the first weeks of life in 7 of 10 cases, and survivors were severely retarded, with spasticity and seizures. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Mochida et al. (2010) described HDBSCC in a consanguineous family as an autosomal recessive syndrome. Affected members carried a homozygous JAM3 mutation. In the 3 families reported by Akawi et al. (2013), affected children were homozygous for JAM3 mutations and the parents were heterozygous carriers, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Mochida et al. (2010) used homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates to map the disease locus to chromosome 11q25. The maximum multipoint lod score was 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

Mochida et al. (2010) sequenced genes in the candidate interval on chromosome 11q25 in affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with HDBSCC. They identified a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice-donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene. RT-PCR in a patient lymphoblast cell line showed that the mutation caused abnormal splicing, leading to a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) identified 3 novel homozygous mutations in the JAM3 gene in 3 families with HDBSCC, of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin. The mutations were c.656G-A (C219Y) in exon 6, c.346G-A (E116K) in exon 4, and c.2T-G (M1R). In each family the parents were heterozygous carriers and the mutation segregated with the disease. None of the mutations was present in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes database. In transfected HeLa cells, the C219Y mutant was retained in the endoplasmic reticulum. This mutation disrupts a disulfide bond in the Ig-like C2-type domain. The E116K mutant trafficked normally to the plasma membrane, like wildtype, and the authors suggested that it may lose function through impaired interaction with a partner. The M1R mutation alters the predicted first methionine of the JAM3 protein, and the authors noted that such mutations almost invariably interfere with translation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Akawi et al. (2013) observed that missense mutations affecting different domains of JAM3 produced a similar phenotype. They suggested that the disorder is clinically and molecularly homogeneous, likely because of total loss of protein function.

### Pathogenesis

Mochida et al. (2010) suggested that, in humans, JAM3 is essential for maintaining the integrity of the cerebrovascular endothelium and for normal lens development.
