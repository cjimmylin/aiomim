### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is caused by homozygous or biallelic mutation in the COQ7 gene (601683) on chromosome 16p12.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive distal hereditary motor neuronopathy-9 (HMNR9) is a length-dependent pure motor axonal neuropathy that begins in childhood and causes progressive walking difficulties. Patients have distal weakness and atrophy, affecting the lower limbs more than the upper limbs, and pes cavus. Sensation is preserved (Jacquier et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023). In 1 family, pyramidal signs suggested upper motor neuron involvement (Jacquier et al., 2023). In another family, upper motor neuron signs were not present (Smith et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported 3 sibs, 2 brothers and a sister, born to nonconsanguineous healthy Portuguese parents. The index patient (II-2) was a 36-year-old man. At 12 years of age he developed walking difficulties, with a sensation of stiffness in the lower limbs and difficulty walking on his heels. He had distal weakness of the lower limbs and hands, amyotrophy of the lower limbs and hands, and pes cavus. Deep tendon reflexes were brisk in all 4 limbs, except the Achilles reflexes, which were abolished. He had a Hoffman sign, but no Babinski sign or spasticity. Sensory examination was normal, and he had no fasciculations or cognitive disorder. Electroneuromyography (ENMG) showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy with fibrillations and a neurogenic pattern. His sister (II-3), aged 25 years, had normal early development. At 9 years of age she developed walking difficulties with fatigability and foot drop. She had distal lower limb weakness, hand weakness, pes cavus, and a positive Gowers maneuver. She reported difficulty feeling cold and warm temperatures on her feet. Deep tendon reflexes were normal and there was no Hoffman sign, but a Babinski sign was present on the left foot. ENMG showed a pure motor axonal neuropathy. The eldest brother (II-1), aged 42 years, noticed walking and biking difficulties with foot drop at 10 years of age. On examination he had severe distal lower limb weakness, mild proximal lower limb weakness, hand weakness, and severe amyotrophy of the lower limbs, with a distal predominance, and of the hand muscles. He also had pes cavus, absent Achilles reflexes, and a positive Gowers maneuver. Neither of these 2 sibs had a cognitive disorder. The authors characterized the disorder as a length-dependent pure motor axonal neuropathy with childhood onset and progressive walking difficulties, and suggested that the pyramidal features indicated upper motor neuron involvement. In 1 patient, brain and spinal MRI, echocardiography, ECG, and audiometry were normal, and CPK was mildly elevated. ⚠︎

Liu et al. (2023) reported distal hereditary motor neuropathy in 2 Chinese families.

Smith et al. (2023) reported 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous Syrian family: a 24-year-old woman (sib 1), a 23-year-old man (sib 2), and a 12-year-old boy (sib 3). All 3 developed distal weakness by 10 to 14 years of age, with muscle atrophy of the distal lower limbs more than the upper limbs, and sensation was preserved. Nerve conduction studies showed reduced distal motor amplitudes with preserved sensory responses. In sib 1, EMG showed distal chronic reinnervation and deep tendon reflexes were preserved. Muscle MRI in sibs 1 and 2 showed distal fat infiltration, and in sib 1 the calves were most affected. A biopsy of the left vastus lateralis muscle in sib 2 showed neurogenic atrophy, with wide variation in fiber size and shape and fiber type grouping, and no ragged red fibers. Sib 1 had no developmental delay or cognitive impairment. The authors noted that, unlike previously reported patients with COQ7-related CoQ10 deficiency, their patients had a pure length-dependent motor neuropathy without sensory involvement, demyelination, developmental delay, or upper motor neuron signs. Sibs 2 and 3 and a fourth sib had congenital deafness. The deafness was explained by a homozygous GJB2 variant and did not segregate with the neuropathy: sib 1 was not deaf and sib 4 had no neuropathy. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

In patient fibroblasts, Jacquier et al. (2023) found decreased CoQ10 (12.5 vs 71.5 pmol/mg protein) and accumulation of 6-demethoxy-CoQ10 (6-DMQ), which is the substrate of COQ7. 6-DMQ was also detected in patient plasma. The CoQ10 precursor 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid slightly increased CoQ10 and normalized 6-DMQ in fibroblasts.

Smith et al. (2023) found reduced CoQ10 and accumulation of DMQ10 in fibroblasts from affected sibs. In sib 2, muscle CoQ10 was 67% lower than in an unrelated human donor (63 vs 192.7 ng/mg protein), and muscle showed reduced maximal respiration and reduced complex IV activity.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HMNR9 in the Portuguese family reported by Jacquier et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The affected sibs had nonconsanguineous healthy parents, and the mutation was homozygous in the affected sibs and heterozygous in both parents. In the consanguineous Syrian family reported by Smith et al. (2023), the COQ7 variant was homozygous in the 3 affected sibs, and the parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous carriers.

### Molecular Genetics

Whole-exome sequencing by Jacquier et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.3G-T mutation in the start codon of COQ7 isoform 1 (p.Met1?) in 3 Portuguese sibs with HMNR9. The mutation segregated with the disorder: the unaffected parents were heterozygous. It was present once, in the heterozygous state, in the gnomAD database. Alternative isoforms 2 and 3 use other start codons and were predicted to be unaffected. In transfected SH-EP neuroblastoma cells, the mutation reduced COQ7 protein levels by 85% without changing mRNA levels or subcellular localization. COQ7 protein was barely detectable in patient fibroblasts, consistent with a defect in translation initiation. A FLAG-tagged isoform 2 localized to mitochondria, suggesting partial compensation.

Liu et al. (2023) identified biallelic variants in the COQ7 gene in 2 Chinese families with distal hereditary motor neuropathy.

Duo-exome sequencing by Smith et al. (2023) in sibs 1 and 2 of a consanguineous Syrian family with HMNR9 identified a shared homozygous c.1A-G variant in the COQ7 gene. Affected sib 3 was also homozygous for the variant, and the parents and an unaffected sib (sib 4) were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from gnomAD. It was predicted to abolish the initiating methionine, so that translation would start at methionine-39, removing the cleavable mitochondrial targeting sequence and 2 additional amino acids. Fibroblasts from affected sibs had severely reduced COQ7 protein. The 3 sibs with congenital deafness (sibs 2, 3, and 4) were also homozygous for a GJB2 variant (c.35delG; p.Gly12ValfsTer2), which accounted for their deafness.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jacquier et al. (2023) compared the pure distal hereditary motor neuropathy in their patients with the severe multisystem phenotypes of COQ10 deficiency caused by COQ7 mutations in the catalytic domain, which affect all isoforms. They speculated that their patients' milder phenotype arose because the start-codon mutation affects only isoform 1 and does not completely prevent CoQ10 synthesis.

In 5 new patients with COQ7-related primary CoQ10 deficiency, Wongkittichote et al. (2023) described 2 distinct presentations. The first had neonatal onset with severe neuromuscular, cardiorespiratory, and renal involvement. The second had late onset with progressive neuropathy, lower extremity weakness, abnormal gait, and variable developmental delay. The authors noted an emerging genotype-phenotype correlation.

### Pathogenesis

Jacquier et al. (2023) showed that patient fibroblasts had reduced mitochondrial ATP production with a compensatory increase in glycolysis. Basal and maximal respiration were reduced, and CoQ10 supplementation rescued them. Proliferation in galactose medium was reduced and was partially rescued by 10 microM CoQ10.

Smith et al. (2023) found that fibroblasts from affected sibs had severely reduced COQ7 protein, 87% lower CoQ10, and accumulated DMQ10. DMQ10 was absent in cells from the carrier father and a carrier sib. Muscle and fibroblasts showed impaired maximal respiration. Muscle also showed increased H2O2 emission and fewer supercomplexes containing complex I and complex IV.

### Clinical Management

Jacquier et al. (2023) reported that 1 patient had stabilization of symptoms between 15 and 18 years of age while taking CoQ10 (30 mg/day), and symptoms worsened again after she stopped treatment. The authors proposed oral CoQ10 supplementation as a potential therapy. They also suggested that detecting 6-DMQ accumulation in plasma could confirm the pathogenicity of COQ7 variants.

### Animal Model

Wongkittichote et al. (2023) found that a yeast strain lacking CAT5, the ortholog of COQ7 (cat5-delta), could not grow on oxidative carbon sources. Wildtype CAT5 fully rescued this defect, but CAT5 carrying the equivalents of human pathogenic variants did not. The equivalents of the human R54Q, R107W, and I66N variants and of the complex allele [T103M;L111P] partially rescued growth, indicating that these are hypomorphic alleles. Supplementation with 2,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid (2,4-diHB) rescued the growth defect of both leaky and severe mutants. Overexpression of COQ8 together with 2,4-diHB supplementation restored oxidative growth and corrected the respiratory defect, and the 2 acted synergistically.
