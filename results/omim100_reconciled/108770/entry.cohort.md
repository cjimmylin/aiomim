### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that atrial standstill-1 (ATRST1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN5A gene (600163) on chromosome 3p22.2, in association with homozygosity for rare regulatory polymorphisms in the GJA5 gene (121013) on chromosome 1q21.2 (digenic inheritance). ⚠︎

### Description

Atrial standstill is an extremely uncommon arrhythmia that rarely appears familial. It has been associated with congenital heart disease, valvular heart disease, conduction disturbances, Brugada syndrome, myocardial infarction, and amyloidosis (Fazelifar et al., 2005). In affected individuals, P waves are absent on the surface ECG and the atrium cannot be captured by pacing (Makita et al., 2005). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Allensworth et al. (1969) reported persistent atrial standstill in a family with myocardial disease.

Harrison and Derrick (1969) presented 2 familial cases of atrial standstill.

Williams et al. (1972) reported a family with familial atrial cardiomyopathy and heart block.

Amat-y-Leon et al. (1974) described a family with atrial dysrhythmia and atrioventricular block, and reported intracellular microelectrode, clinical electrophysiologic, and morphologic observations.

Shah et al. (1992) reported a family with total atrial standstill.

Groenewegen et al. (2003) studied a large family in which 1 deceased and 3 living relatives were unambiguously affected by atrial standstill.

Fazelifar et al. (2005) reported a family with atrial standstill associated with syncope, dilated cardiomyopathy, and sudden cardiac death.

Makita et al. (2005) reported an 11-year-old boy with congenital atrial standstill. He had no P waves on surface ECG, and his right atrium could not be captured by pacing. His father, who carried the same SCN5A mutation, was in normal sinus rhythm, and his mother was asymptomatic.

### Inheritance

Groenewegen et al. (2003) and Makita et al. (2005) reported digenic inheritance of atrial standstill, involving a heterozygous SCN5A mutation together with polymorphisms in the GJA5 (Cx40) gene. In the family reported by Groenewegen et al. (2003), 5 unaffected relatives also carried the SCN5A mutation, and only the affected members had coinherited both the SCN5A mutation and the rare Cx40 genotype. In the family reported by Makita et al. (2005), the proband's father carried the SCN5A mutation but was in normal sinus rhythm. Makita et al. (2005) concluded that genetic defects in SCN5A most likely underlie atrial standstill, and that coinheritance of Cx40 polymorphisms is a possible genetic factor modifying the clinical manifestation, which may explain incomplete penetrance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a large family with atrial standstill, Groenewegen et al. (2003) carried out candidate gene screening and identified a novel mutation in the SCN5A gene (D1275N) in all 3 affected living relatives and in 5 unaffected relatives; the deceased affected relative was an obligate carrier. Eight relatives were homozygous for 2 closely linked regulatory polymorphisms in the GJA5 (Cx40) gene (-44G-A and +71A-G), 3 of whom were affected. The 3 living patients exclusively coinherited both the rare Cx40 genotype and the SCN5A D1275N mutation. Homozygosity for both Cx40 polymorphisms was found in only about 7% of control subjects. In functional studies, D1275N channels showed a small depolarizing shift in activation compared with wildtype channels, and the rare Cx40 genotype caused reduced reporter gene expression compared with the more common Cx40 genotype. Groenewegen et al. (2003) proposed that, although the functional effect of each genetic change is relatively benign, their combined effect eventually progresses to total atrial standstill.

In an 11-year-old boy with congenital atrial standstill, Makita et al. (2005) identified a heterozygous novel mutation in the SCN5A gene (L212P), inherited from his father, who was in normal sinus rhythm. Recombinant L212P channels showed a large hyperpolarizing shift in the voltage dependence of both activation (wildtype -48.1 mV vs L212P -63.5 mV) and inactivation (wildtype -86.6 mV vs L212P -95.6 mV), and delayed recovery from inactivation. Further screening for genetic variations that might mitigate L212P dysfunction showed that the proband, but not his father, carried Cx40 polymorphisms inherited from his asymptomatic mother. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Harrison and Derrick (1969) reported the use of epicardial pacing in 1 of 2 familial cases of atrial standstill.
