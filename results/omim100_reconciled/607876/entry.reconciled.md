### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by heterozygous expansion of an intronic ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat in the STARD7 gene (616712) on chromosome 2q11.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is an autosomal dominant familial cortical reflex myoclonus. It presents as an action-induced shivering movement of the hands and jerking of the upper limbs (cortical tremor). These are variably associated with focal and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. In the commonest form, a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor occurs together with subtle proximal myoclonus. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures are uncommon and tend to occur around sleep onset. A minority of patients have borderline or moderately impaired cognition, and the myoclonus often worsens in advanced age (De Fusco et al., 2014; Crompton et al., 2012).

### Nomenclature

Guerrini et al. (2001) called the disorder in their family autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME). Other reports of families linked to the same region of chromosome 2 used the terms benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME; de Falco et al., 2003; Madia et al., 2008), familial adult myoclonic epilepsy (FAME; Labauge et al., 2002; Crompton et al., 2012) and familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy (FCMTE; Suppa et al., 2009). De Fusco et al. (2014) suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME might be distinct clinical entities.

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) reported a pedigree with 8 affected individuals. Their disorder was nonprogressive, with onset between the ages of 12 and 50 years. All patients had predominantly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 also had complex partial seizures. Most individuals had rarely had seizures and had a normal cognitive level. However, the 3 individuals with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All patients had frontotemporal as well as generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Electrophysiologic studies showed an enhanced C-reflex at rest and high-amplitude somatosensory and visual evoked potentials. On transcranial magnetic stimulation, the resting motor threshold was significantly reduced. The post-MEP silent period was significantly shortened (101 ms vs 137 ms in controls). In 5 patients, EEG-EMG coherence at 8 to 25 Hz over the contralateral rolandic area, with EEG preceding EMG by 8 to 15 ms, suggested that the myoclonus was of cortical origin. Ipsilateral coherence in 2 patients suggested transcallosal spread. The authors suggested diffuse cortical hyperexcitability, particularly involving frontotemporal circuits.

Labauge et al. (2002) reported a 4-generation European family in which 10 living and 3 deceased relatives had the clinical characteristics of familial adult myoclonic epilepsy, including adult-onset myoclonus of the extremities. Among the 10 living patients, the mean age at onset was 41 years (range, 30-60 years). Eight of the 13 affected subjects had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed the diagnosis in the 5 patients studied.

de Falco et al. (2003) reported a clinical and electrophysiologic study of 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy.

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus and epilepsy. Eleven members, aged 24 to 56 years (mean, 39.2 years), had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 of them had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study showed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected family members. Three presymptomatic members had giant somatosensory-evoked potential components and an enhanced long loop reflex I. All 3 developed hand tremor and upper-limb myoclonus after 1.5 years of follow-up.

Madia et al. (2008) studied 5 southern Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy, 1 of which had not been reported before. DNA from 53 affected individuals with cortical tremor, myoclonus and epilepsy was genotyped. ⚠︎

Suppa et al. (2009) reported an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy. Affected members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neurophysiologic findings suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex with normal sensorimotor integration. On neuropsychological testing, patients had visuospatial impairment, which the authors suggested might be due to temporal lobe dysfunction.

Crompton et al. (2012) reported a 6-generation kindred of European descent, ascertained in New Zealand and Australia. The 55 affected members had fine hand tremor, with onset typically in adolescence (median age, 15 years; range, 4-60 years). Proximal myoclonus was present in 44 of 55 (80%) and arose later than the hand tremor (median age, 17 years; range, 5-60 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range, 18-76 years). Neurophysiologic testing confirmed features of cortical reflex myoclonus. The most common phenotype was a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, combined with subtle proximal myoclonus. Seizures were uncommon and occurred around sleep onset after severe generalized myoclonus.

De Fusco et al. (2014) studied 2 families from Tuscany. Family A was a 5-generation family with an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, previously mapped to chromosome 2. Its clinical features closely resembled those of family B. In family B, all patients had postural myoclonus of the hands and upper limbs. Cortical tremor was the presenting symptom in all affected individuals and appeared between 18 and 50 years of age. The affected members were: - I:2, a 70-year-old woman with generalized tonic-clonic seizures. - II:4, an 84-year-old man with onset at age 49 years and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. - III:2, a 55-year-old woman with onset at age 28 years, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, drug-resistant focal seizures and a borderline cognitive level. - III:5, a 45-year-old man with onset at age 27 years, generalized tonic-clonic seizures and drug-resistant focal seizures. - IV:1, a 29-year-old woman with onset at age 18 years and no seizures. Epilepsy occurred in all affected individuals except IV:1. Everyone except III:2 had normal intelligence. Brain MRI was normal in II:4, III:2 and III:5. None of the patients had evidence of cardiovascular disease. Two of the oldest mutation carriers developed age-related dementia.

Corbett et al. (2019) tabulated the clinical features of 22 FAME2 families: - Families 2 to 15 and family 22 were Italian. - Family 1 was Australian/New Zealand of European ancestry. - Family 16 was Iraqi of Sephardic Jewish ancestry. - Family 17 was Israeli of Sephardic Jewish ancestry. - Family 18 was South African of European ancestry. - Family 19 was French/Spanish. - Family 20 was French. - Family 21 was a single Syrian patient. Myoclonus/cortical tremor was present in all or nearly all studied affected members of each family. The proportion with tonic-clonic seizures varied widely, from 8 of 55 (15%) in family 1 to all affected members in several smaller families. Focal seizures were infrequent: they occurred in 2 of 55 members of family 1, 2 of 4 in family 3, 3 of 11 in family 4, 2 of 4 in family 15 and 2 of 10 in family 16, and were absent in many families. Mean or median ages at onset also varied, from 12 years in family 6 to 41 years in family 19. In family 1, the authors found evidence of anticipation. Median age at onset was 30 years in generation III (range, 14-60 years; n = 6), 17 years in generation IV (range, 8-50 years; n = 30) and 12 years in generation V (range, 4-19 years; n = 16).

### Other Features

Corbett et al. (2019) noted that increased cerebellar choline on proton MR spectroscopy had previously been found in 11 individuals from 3 Italian families. They showed that all 3 families carried the STARD7 ATTTC expansion. Because STARD7 transfers phosphatidylcholine, they suggested that this finding may be specific to FAME2.

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) found that the disorder in their family was inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with high penetrance. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also observed in the families reported by Labauge et al. (2002), Madia et al. (2008) and Crompton et al. (2012). ⚠︎

### Mapping

Guerrini et al. (2001) performed genome-wide linkage analysis in their family with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy. They identified a 12.4-cM critical region on 2p11.1-q12.2, between D2S2161 and D2S1897. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for D2S2175, and the multipoint lod score around D2S2175 was 3.74.

de Falco et al. (2003) found linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy.

Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p (maximum lod score of 5.9) in a 5-generation Italian family.

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the 2p11.1-q12.2 locus for cortical tremor associated with epilepsy and excluded candidate genes in the region.

Madia et al. (2008) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 5 southern Italian families. The maximum cumulative lod score was 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388.

In an Italian family, Suppa et al. (2009) excluded linkage to the 8q24 locus. Affected members shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the pericentromeric region of chromosome 2.

Using genetic mapping in a large 6-generation family, Crompton et al. (2012) narrowed the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

### Heterogeneity

Guerrini et al. (2001) excluded linkage of the disorder in their family to the familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus on chromosome 8q23.3-q24. They concluded that the centromeric chromosome 2 localization and the different phenotype defined a new epilepsy syndrome.

Labauge et al. (2002) excluded linkage to the FAME/BAFME locus on 8q23.3-q24.1 in a 4-generation European family. They concluded that the disorder is genetically heterogeneous and that their family extended the occurrence of FAME to non-Japanese patients.

de Falco et al. (2003) noted that BAFME had been mapped to chromosome 8q24. They concluded that their findings in Italian families supported genetic heterogeneity and suggested possible allelism with ADCME.

De Fusco et al. (2014) found no ADRA2B mutations in BAFME pedigrees from southern Italy that mapped to the ADCME locus. Southern blot analysis excluded large insertions or deletions. The authors suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME might be distinct clinical entities, although they could not exclude variants in noncoding regulatory regions.

### Molecular Genetics

De Fusco et al. (2014) studied a 5-generation Tuscan family with ADCME (family A) that had previously been mapped to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2. In this family, they identified a heterozygous in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (104260): c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG; p.His225_Leu229delinsGlnPheGlyArg. The change replaced 5 amino acids (HGGAL) with 4 new ones (QFGR). The variant segregated completely with the disease. It was not found in 575 Tuscan controls or in public databases. Next-generation sequencing of the critical region in 2 affected members found no other relevant change. The same indel was found in a second Tuscan ADCME family (family B). The 2 families had no known kinship but shared the same disease haplotype across the entire critical region, suggesting a common ancestor. In functional studies, the mutant receptor failed to form a stable complex with spinophilin, and the authors proposed a gain-of-function effect on calcium signaling. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) performed whole-genome sequencing in affected members of 3 FAME families and analyzed the data with ExpansionHunter and exSTRa. Within the FAME2 interval on chromosome 2, they identified an expansion of the endogenous ATTTT repeat with an inserted ATTTC repeat in the first intron of the STARD7 gene. The ATTTC repeat was absent from whole-genome sequencing data of 69 controls. Corbett et al. (2019) then developed a repeat-primed PCR assay. With it, they identified the expansion in all 137 affected individuals from 16 independently reported FAME2 families worldwide. Overall, the expansion segregated in 158 of 158 affected individuals from 22 FAME pedigrees. Two of 24 tested individuals without a FAME diagnosis carried the expansion and were likely presymptomatic. The expansion was not amplified in 28 control DNA samples. Among family members with an uncertain diagnosis of FAME, usually tremor alone, 8 of 13 carried the expansion. In family 1, 2 tested-negative individuals with an uncertain diagnosis did not share the affected haplotype. The 20 familial cases and 52 singleton cases screened included 52 unrelated patients with generalized epilepsy and tremor or adult-onset myoclonic epilepsy. All 20 familial cases were positive, but only 1 of the 52 singleton cases was. Long-read sequencing by Corbett et al. (2019) showed that the pathogenic allele had the structure (AAATG)n(AAAAT)n. In 1 patient, a SMRT read contained 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats, while a nanopore read contained 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats, suggesting somatic variation in repeat size. In another patient (family 5, III-37), a single nanopore read spanned the expanded repeats, with 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats and a total length of 4,645 bp. Two of the families with the STARD7 expansion (families 4 and 15) also carried the ADRA2B variant previously reported by De Fusco et al. (2014). Corbett et al. (2019) concluded that this strongly suggested that the ADRA2B allele is not causative. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In preliminary analyses, Madia et al. (2008) found only minor clinical differences among patients carrying the shared disease haplotype. They suggested that expressivity of the founder mutation is not markedly influenced by other factors.

### Pathogenesis

De Fusco et al. (2014) studied the variant ADRA2B receptor (alpha-2B-AR) in transfected HeLa cells. Like the wildtype receptor, the mutant localized normally to the plasma membrane and was internalized in an arrestin-3-dependent manner. In coimmunoprecipitation studies, however, it failed to form a stable complex with spinophilin. After 10 minutes of epinephrine stimulation, spinophilin binding to the mutant fell back to baseline, whereas binding to wildtype remained elevated. In Xenopus oocytes, spinophilin increased the EC50 for epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling through the wildtype receptor but not through the mutant. The authors concluded that the mutation increases the intensity of Ca(2+) signaling, a gain-of-function effect caused by loss of the spinophilin interaction. They proposed that the resulting cortical hyperexcitability might stem from impaired GABAergic inhibition. This ADRA2B variant was later considered not causative by Corbett et al. (2019).

Corbett et al. (2019) found that STARD7 transcript and protein levels were not altered in patient-derived fibroblasts. No RNA-seq reads containing ATTTC repeats were detected. They suggested that the repeat sequence alone is pathogenic, independent of any effect on the host gene.

### Diagnosis

Corbett et al. (2019) reported that a repeat-primed PCR assay for the ATTTC repeat was diagnostic for FAME. It had 100% sensitivity in families linked or suggestively linked to chromosome 2. They noted that FAME2 is the most frequently linked locus in Caucasian patients.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found that a haplotype ('5332') of the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113 and D2S2175 segregated with the disease in all 5 southern Italian families studied. This indicated that the same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, segregated in these families.

**.0002 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2**  
STARD7, (ATTTC)n EXPANSION, INTRON 1

Corbett et al. (2019) identified an expansion of the endogenous ATTTT repeat, with an inserted ATTTC repeat, in the first intron of the STARD7 gene. They found it by whole-genome sequencing in affected members of 3 FAME families, and it was absent from 69 control genomes. By repeat-primed PCR, the expansion segregated in 158 of 158 affected individuals from 22 FAME pedigrees. These pedigrees were of Italian, Australian/New Zealand and South African European, Sephardic Jewish (Iraqi and Israeli), French/Spanish and French ancestry. The expansion was also found in 1 Syrian singleton patient, but not in 28 control DNA samples. Long-read sequencing showed that the expanded allele had the structure (AAATG)n(AAAAT)n. In 1 patient (family 5, III-37), the allele contained 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats, a total length of 4,645 bp. In another patient, reads differed in repeat number, suggesting somatic variation. STARD7 transcript and protein levels were not altered in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎
