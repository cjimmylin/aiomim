### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene (613469) on chromosome 10q24.2.

### Description

Urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its cardinal features are an abnormal, 'inverted' facial expression, in which patients appear to cry when they try to smile, and congenital obstructive uropathy (Wang et al., 1997; Derbent et al., 2009). The bladder fails to empty completely even though there is no anatomic outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage. The resulting neurogenic bladder with voiding dysfunction leads to enuresis, urinary tract infection and hydronephrosis (Wang et al., 1997; Daly et al., 2010). Affected individuals often have reflux of infected urine to the upper renal tract, which puts the kidneys at risk of damage and renal failure (Daly et al., 2010). Some severely affected patients become hypertensive and progress to end-stage renal disease (Derbent et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Elejalde (1979) used the designation 'the Ochoa syndrome' for this disorder. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) proposed the term urofacial syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families, one of them consanguineous (first cousins). The patients had peculiar facies and gestures when smiling and crying. Their urinary findings included hydronephrosis, hydroureter and intravesical stenosis of the ureter. Other findings were an abnormal caliber of the urethra in its prostatic and membranous portions, urethral valves, and an abnormal bladder with trabeculation and diverticula, associated with severe hypertrophy of the mucosa with sclerotic changes.

Between 1965 and 1986, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) saw 36 children who had enuresis and urinary tract infection together with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic work-up showed mild neuropathic bladder in all cases and severe urinary tract damage in most. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation.

Wang et al. (1997) studied 31 patients with urofacial syndrome. The patients had enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and voiding dysfunction due to neurogenic bladder. ⚠︎

Chauve et al. (2000) reported a French family with urofacial syndrome, described as the first European cases of the disorder.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European cases of urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported the first published case of urofacial syndrome in Turkey. The patient had been followed with a diagnosis of recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux, and urofacial syndrome was diagnosed at 16 years of age.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 Turkish families, who had uropathy and facial abnormalities. Median follow-up was 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months). ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Elejalde (1979) analyzed 3 families, one of them consanguineous (first cousins). He concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance.

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) disagreed and concluded that inheritance is autosomal recessive. Their reasons were the occurrence of the disorder in multiple sibs with normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio.

Homozygosity mapping supported autosomal recessive inheritance (Wang et al., 1997). Daly et al. (2010) later identified homozygous mutations in the HPSE2 gene in affected individuals. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) performed a genome screen combining homozygosity mapping and DNA pooling in 20 patients with urofacial syndrome. Marker D10S677 was linked to and associated with the disorder, with excess homozygosity in patients (P less than 10(-6)). On haplotype analysis, all 31 patients were homozygous for D10S1726 and D10S198, which lie about 5 cM telomeric to D10S677, compared with 12% of unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-19)). Recombinations placed the gene within a 1-cM interval between D10S1433 and D10S603 on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) built a physical map of the UFS critical region on 10q23-q24 and identified new polymorphic markers in it. Haplotype analysis with these markers placed the UFS gene within a single 1,410-kb YAC clone.

Chauve et al. (2000) compared haplotypes in a French family with haplotypes previously described in patients from Colombia and America. The results were compatible with the same 10q23-q24 critical region.

Wang et al. (2003) built a genomic sequence map covering the whole UFS interval on chromosome 10q23-q24. They narrowed the disease interval from about 360 kb to a 220-kb region containing the ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene.

Pang et al. (2010) noted that they had previously localized the urofacial syndrome gene to chromosome 10q24 by homozygosity mapping. Daly et al. (2010) performed whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual and defined a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24 that contained the HPSE2 gene.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (1999) used homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage and found that the syndrome mapped to the same 10q23-q24 region as in the Colombian families, showing genetic homogeneity. Chauve et al. (2000) found results in a French family that also favored genetic homogeneity. Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q is responsible for the disorder in all UFS patients from multiple ethnic groups.

In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome, which indicated genetic heterogeneity.

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) found that the glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase gene (GOT1) lay within the refined UFS critical region and worked out its genomic structure. Mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) analyzed candidate genes in the 220-kb critical interval, covering the coding regions, 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, promoter regions and exon/intron junctions. They found no pathogenic mutation in patients with urofacial syndrome.

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all patients with urofacial syndrome studied, who came from Colombia, the United States and France.

Daly et al. (2010) used whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual to define an autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24. Within this region they identified a homozygous 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene. In 5 further unrelated families they found homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations and frameshift mutations in HPSE2, which confirmed HPSE2 as the causative gene for urofacial syndrome. They found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome.

### Pathogenesis

Pang et al. (2010) noted that HPSE2 encodes a 592-amino acid protein with a domain homologous to the glycosyl hydrolase motif of heparanase (HPSE). Because patients with urofacial syndrome have complete loss of HPSE2 function, they suggested that HPSE2 may be important for coordinating the muscles involved in facial expression and urine voiding.

Daly et al. (2010) showed that HPSE2 is expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system, where it might be involved in controlling facial expression and urinary voiding. They also found it expressed in bladder smooth muscle, consistent with a role in the structure and function of the renal tract.

### Diagnosis

Elejalde (1979) proposed that the syndrome reflects altered development of the facial and urinary developmental fields. He noted that the peculiar facies allows the condition to be recognized early, which can help early assessment and treatment and may improve prognosis.

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) stated that, because inverted facial expression is associated with neuropathic bladder and the resulting genitourinary damage, individuals with 'inversion' of facial expression should have a urologic evaluation.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) suggested that cases of urofacial syndrome outside Colombia may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing is not recognized. They emphasized that early diagnosis has important consequences for the management and prognosis of the urinary problems.

Derbent et al. (2009) likewise noted that the diagnosis may be missed for these reasons. They recommended considering the syndrome in any patient with dysfunctional voiding, particularly in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage.

### Clinical Management

Aydogdu et al. (2010) stressed that early diagnosis of urofacial syndrome is crucial for the management and prognosis of urinary problems caused by the dysfunctional bladder.

### Population Genetics

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) noted that urofacial syndrome was originally described in Colombian patients and that very few cases had been reported from other countries.
