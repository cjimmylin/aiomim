### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications (SENEBAC) is caused by homozygous or other biallelic mutation in the NRROS gene (615322) on chromosome 3q29.

### Description

Early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications (SENEBAC) is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder. Development is normal or mildly delayed at first. Refractory seizures begin in infancy or early childhood and are followed by developmental regression. Brain imaging shows intracranial calcification, white matter abnormalities and progressive atrophy. In the patients reported by Smith et al. (2020), death occurred in early childhood.

### Clinical Features

Smith et al. (2020) reported 3 children from 2 families. Patients 1 and 2 were girls, the first and third children of nonconsanguineous parents of Maori descent (family F1442). Patient 3 was a boy, the first child of South Asian parents who were first cousins (family F2382). All 3 children were born after a normal pregnancy and had unremarkable early development. In the second year of life, they developed refractory seizures and neurodegeneration, and they died between 27 and 36 months of age. Imaging first showed fine calcification at the depths of the cerebral gyri with normal white matter. Later imaging showed more calcification, severe atrophy and diffuse white matter signal changes. CT scans of patient 1 at 9 months of age, patient 2 at 17 months and patient 3 at 14 months showed widespread spot and linear calcification in the deep and subcortical white matter. Patient 1 had progressive cortical and subcortical atrophy and patient 2 had mild cerebral volume loss. Patient 3 had diffuse high signal in the white matter, which patient 1 did not. In patient 3, neuropathologic examination showed widespread neuronal loss with reactive gliosis throughout the gray matter. The most striking finding was an accumulation of foamy macrophages throughout the white matter, mainly around blood vessels. Myelin basic protein expression in the white matter was reduced compared with age-matched controls, although U fibers were preserved, and there were occasional axonal spheroids. ⚠︎

Dong et al. (2020) reported 6 individuals from 4 families with a neurodegenerative disease. Development was normal or mildly delayed at first, and seizures began within the first year of life. Developmental regression followed. All affected individuals had refractory epilepsy, developmental regression, and reduced white matter volume with delayed myelination. Three of the individuals had intracranial calcification.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SENEBAC is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Smith et al. (2020) found homozygous NRROS mutations in 2 affected sibs born to nonconsanguineous parents and in a child of first-cousin parents. Dong et al. (2020) found biallelic NRROS variants in affected individuals from 4 families.

### Molecular Genetics

Smith et al. (2020) performed exome sequencing on the affected children in 2 families with SENEBAC and found homozygous truncating mutations in the NRROS gene. The 2 affected sibs in a Maori family (F1442) carried a c.1777C-T transition that results in a gln593-to-ter (Q593X) substitution. The affected child of consanguineous South Asian parents (F2382) carried a 1-bp deletion (c.1257del) that results in a frameshift and premature termination (Gly420AlafsTer14). The authors noted that the Q593X variant had not been recorded before and that the frameshift variant was present on only 1 of 251,438 alleles in the gnomAD database. No cellular material was available for functional studies.

Dong et al. (2020) found biallelic loss-of-function variants in the NRROS gene in 6 individuals with SENEBAC from 4 families. The variants affected two significant functional domains of NRROS. In HEK293T cells, confocal microscopy showed that both wild-type and mutant NRROS proteins colocalized with latent TGF-beta-1 inside the cell. However, flow cytometry showed that, unlike wild-type NRROS, the mutant proteins failed to anchor latent TGF-beta-1 at the cell surface. Wild-type NRROS rescued this defect in the disease-associated mutants. Dong et al. (2020) suggested that loss of NRROS function causes a severe childhood-onset neurodegenerative condition with features suggestive of a disordered response to inflammation.

### Pathogenesis

By mining transcriptomic datasets, Smith et al. (2020) found that NRROS expression was more than 50-fold higher in human microglia than in whole brain. In mouse brain, Nrros expression was similarly enriched in microglia and was higher in CNS perivascular macrophages than in parenchymal microglia. In the brain of an affected patient, the foamy white matter macrophages were positive for CD68, MHC class II and p22phox. They did not react with CD163, IBA1, NRROS, P2Y12 or TMEM119. The loss of P2Y12 staining and weak TMEM119 staining suggested marked abnormalities of parenchymal microglia. The authors suggested that the upregulated p22phox indicated that loss of NRROS might increase p22phox-NOX2 binding and superoxide formation. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Wong et al. (2017) found that mice lacking Nrros, a myeloid-expressed transmembrane protein of the endoplasmic reticulum, developed spontaneous neurologic disorders with defects in motor function and died before 6 months of age. Nrros -/- mice had astrogliosis and lacked normal CD11b-hi/CD45-lo microglia. No demyelination or neuronal loss was detected; instead, perivascular macrophage-like myeloid cells populated the CNS. Using Cx3cr1-driven deletion of Nrros, Wong et al. (2017) showed that Nrros has a crucial role in establishing microglia during early embryonic stages. Nrros was required for normal expression of Sall1 and other microglial genes important for microglial development and function.

Smith et al. (2020) noted that the features of their patients recapitulated those of Nrros-deficient mice. They described these mice as showing progressive neurologic decline from 2 to 3 months of age and death by 6 months. They also described neuronal loss, demyelination, axonal pathology, astrogliosis and foamy macrophages in the mice, without immune-mediated inflammation. Dong et al. (2020) noted that the features of their patients were consistent with those of the Nrros knockout mouse. They also overlapped with those of the human condition associated with TGF-beta-1 deficiency.
