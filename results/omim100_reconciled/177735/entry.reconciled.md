### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant pseudohypoaldosteronism type I (PHA1A) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding the mineralocorticoid receptor (NR3C2; 600983) on chromosome 4q31.23.

### Description

Pseudohypoaldosteronism type I (PHA1) is characterized by neonatal renal salt wasting with dehydration, hypotension, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis, even though aldosterone levels are elevated (Geller et al., 1998). The autosomal dominant or sporadic form is milder than the autosomal recessive form and remits with age (Geller et al., 1998; Viemann et al., 2001). Viemann et al. (2001) contrasted it with the autosomal recessive form, which is caused by mutations in the amiloride-sensitive sodium channel.

### Clinical Features

Cheek and Perry (1958) described a salt-wasting syndrome in infancy.

Donnell et al. (1959) described pseudohypoadrenalocorticism, a disorder of renal sodium loss, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia. They attributed it to renal tubular insensitivity to mineralocorticoids.

Armanini et al. (1985) described pseudohypoaldosteronism as a salt-wasting syndrome with failure to thrive that usually presents in infancy. Features include high urinary sodium despite hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hyperreninemia, and elevated aldosterone levels. They studied a 28-year-old index case in Melbourne (patient 1) and 2 sibs in Munich, aged 8 and 2 years (patients 2 and 3), whose parents were first cousins. Clinically, patient 3, a boy, was less severely affected than his sister.

Hanukoglu (1991) reported a kindred (kindred I) with the renal form of PHA I. The propositus presented in infancy with renal salt wasting, vomiting, failure to thrive, short stature, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia. He responded dramatically to a high-salt diet (2.5 g/day) and no longer needed sodium supplementation after age 2 years. In 7 additional members across 3 generations, clinical expression ranged from asymptomatic to moderate. In the affected members (the propositus, his mother, and 2 brothers), hyperaldosteronism persisted over 13 years, but plasma renin activity (PRA) fell gradually to near normal values. The author took this to indicate tertiary hyperaldosteronism from an autonomously functioning zona glomerulosa.

Geller et al. (1998) studied 4 dominant kindreds and 1 sporadic kindred with PHA1. The disorder was characterized by neonatal renal salt wasting with dehydration, hypotension, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis despite elevated aldosterone levels. ⚠︎

Tajima et al. (2000) studied a Japanese family with a renal form of PHA1, which they described as mineralocorticoid resistance.

Viemann et al. (2001) studied a sporadic patient with PHA1 and 5 affected patients from 2 families with autosomal dominant PHA1. The family members of the sporadic patient were asymptomatic.

Sartorato et al. (2003) studied 14 families with autosomal dominant or sporadic PHA1. They described the disorder as a rare form of mineralocorticoid resistance characterized by neonatal renal salt wasting and failure to thrive.

Riepe et al. (2006) studied 6 patients with PHA1 and renal salt loss.

### Inheritance

Armanini et al. (1985) suggested that pseudohypoaldosteronism may be transmitted as an autosomal recessive trait. They based this on an affected sib pair whose parents were first cousins and had normal monocyte aldosterone receptor levels.

By contrast, Hanukoglu (1991) found that the pedigree of a kindred with renal PHA I was consistent with autosomal dominant transmission with variable expression. Geller et al. (1998) identified heterozygous mineralocorticoid receptor mutations that segregated with PHA1 in 4 dominant kindreds.

### Heterogeneity

Hanukoglu (1991) concluded that type I PHA includes 2 clinically and genetically distinct entities. One is a renal form with autosomal dominant transmission. The other is a multiple target organ form with autosomal recessive inheritance, which he observed in a girl from a consanguineous family who had severe salt wasting and increased salivary and sweat electrolytes.

Geller et al. (1998) found no ENaC gene mutations in 6 dominant and 7 sporadic PHA1 kindreds, which pointed to mutations in other genes. They found mineralocorticoid receptor mutations in only 5 of these 13 kindreds.

Viemann et al. (2001) found no mutation in the hMR gene in 2 kindreds with autosomal dominant PHA1. They concluded that autosomal dominant or sporadic PHA1 is genetically heterogeneous and involves other, as yet unidentified, genes.

Sartorato et al. (2003) found MR mutations in 70% of their familial cases of autosomal dominant PHA1. The absence of mutations in some families suggested that other genes may be involved.

Pujo et al. (2007) found that a genocopy might exist in 3 PHA1 kindreds without MR mutations. They suggested that other patients without MR mutations might have different disorders that resemble PHA1 in the neonatal period.

### Molecular Genetics

In 1 sporadic and 4 dominant PHA1 kindreds, Geller et al. (1998) identified heterozygous mutations in the mineralocorticoid receptor gene (MLR; NR3C2). The mutations were 2 frameshift mutations (1 of them de novo), 2 premature termination codons, and 1 splice donor mutation. The mutations segregated with PHA1 and were not found in unaffected subjects. The authors concluded that heterozygous MLR mutations cause PHA1 and stated that this underscores the role of mineralocorticoid receptor function in regulating salt and blood pressure homeostasis in humans.

In all affected members of a Japanese family with renal PHA1, Tajima et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous missense mutation in the MR gene (L924P) by PCR and direct sequencing. In transfected COS-1 cells with a reporter assay, aldosterone increased reporter activity with wildtype receptor but had no effect with the L924P mutant. The authors suggested that this mutation caused the mineralocorticoid resistance in the family and noted that it was the first missense MR mutation reported in renal PHA1.

In a sporadic patient with PHA1, Viemann et al. (2001) identified a heterozygous frameshift mutation (Ins2871C) in exon 9 of the hMR gene. Asymptomatic family members did not carry the mutation. The authors found no hMR mutation in 2 kindreds with autosomal dominant PHA1. ⚠︎

Sartorato et al. (2003) analyzed the hMR gene in 14 families with autosomal dominant or sporadic PHA1 and detected 6 heterozygous mutations. These were 2 frameshift mutations in exon 2 (insT1354 and del8bp537), a nonsense mutation in exon 4 (C645X), and 3 missense mutations (G633R, Q776R, and L979P). The 3 missense mutations affected hMR function differently in functional studies.

Riepe et al. (2006) identified 6 heterozygous NR3C2 mutations in 6 patients with PHA1. These were 2 frameshift mutations (c.1131dupT, previously reported, and c.2871dupC), 2 novel nonsense mutations predicted to produce a truncated receptor, and 2 missense mutations (S818L and E972G). The c.2871dupC, S818L, and E972G mutants were defective in functional studies.

Pujo et al. (2007) screened the MR gene in PHA1 patients for nucleotide substitutions in coding regions, intron-exon junctions, and untranslated exons, and for large deletions. They found 22 MR gene abnormalities in 33 patients. They concluded that MR mutations are extremely frequent in PHA1 patients classified by aldosterone and potassium levels.

### Pathogenesis

Armanini et al. (1985) found high-affinity [3H]aldosterone binding (Kd about 3 nM; 150 to 600 sites per cell) in control monocytes. No binding was found in monocytes from 2 patients with pseudohypoaldosteronism. In a third patient, binding was 62 sites per cell, more than 2 SD below control values. The authors concluded that the disorder appeared to be caused by a type I (mineralocorticoid) receptor defect that may be complete or partial.

Sartorato et al. (2003) found that the L979P mutant MR had a transdominant negative effect on wildtype MR activity. By contrast, the G633R and Q776R mutations probably resulted in haploinsufficiency.

### History

Raine and Roy (1962) described a salt-losing syndrome in infancy, which they called pseudo-hypoadrenocorticalism.

Asymptomatic family members did not carry the mutation. ⚠︎

In 2 patients with PHA1 and renal salt loss, Riepe et al. (2006) identified 2 novel heterozygous nonsense mutations in the NR3C2 gene. Both mutations generate a truncated receptor protein.

**.0016 PSEUDOHYPOALDOSTERONISM, TYPE I, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
NR3C2, SER818LEU [p.S818L]

In a patient with PHA1 and renal salt loss, Riepe et al. (2006) identified a heterozygous ser818-to-leu (S818L) mutation in the NR3C2 gene. They noted that it had been reported recently without complete in vitro data. The S818L mutant did not bind aldosterone, activate transcription, or translocate into the nucleus. Three-dimensional modeling showed major displacement of several residues involved in aldosterone binding. ⚠︎

**.0017 PSEUDOHYPOALDOSTERONISM, TYPE I, AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT**  
NR3C2, GLU972GLY [p.E972G]

In a patient with PHA1 and renal salt loss, Riepe et al. (2006) identified a novel heterozygous glu972-to-gly (E972G) mutation in the NR3C2 gene. The mutant receptor had significantly lower ligand-binding affinity and only 9% of wildtype transcriptional activity, which the authors attributed to major changes in receptor conformation.
