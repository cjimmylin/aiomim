### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SLC5A1 gene (182380) on chromosome 22q13.1.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disorder that appears within the first weeks of life. The intestine selectively fails to absorb dietary glucose and galactose. This leads to severe diarrhea and dehydration, which are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991). The disorder is caused by a defect in the intestinal Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1 (Martín et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

Anderson et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption, her family, and 16 normal volunteers. The proband's jejunal mucosa was unable to concentrate glucose throughout a 60-minute incubation period, but intestinal fructose accumulation was normal. She also had a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption. Both parents and a half sister had intermediate impairment of glucose transport, whereas an asymptomatic brother had normal transport.

Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in patients with glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported familial glucose-galactose malabsorption in which the glucose intolerance went into remission.

Martín et al. (1996) studied 30 new patients with glucose-galactose malabsorption. They described the phenotype as diarrhea beginning in the neonatal period that results in death unless glucose and galactose are removed from the diet.

In a separate report, Martín et al. (1996) described 2 sibs with GGM from a large consanguineous family. The disorder presented with severe osmotic diarrhea shortly after birth. ⚠︎

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 Arab children with glucose-galactose malabsorption from 8 different families. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. They were first seen for chronic watery diarrhea, present since birth, and failure to thrive. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another child was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All affected individuals presented as newborns with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. Increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite their illness, irritability, and apathy were also noted at initial presentation. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) showed that glucose transport by normal human jejunal mucosa was concentrative, saturable, and dependent on sodium and energy, and that it showed competitive inhibition. In the proband, absent intestinal glucose transport was accompanied by partial impairment of renal glucose transport. Fructose accumulation was similar in all subjects. In the jejunal mucosa of 3 heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired, while glucose efflux was the same as in normal controls. Kinetic analysis showed reduced transport capacity (Vmax) with normal affinity (Km), suggesting that heterozygotes can be detected.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Both parents and a half sister of an affected girl had glucose transport values between those of normal controls and those of the proband.

Autosomal recessive inheritance was also supported by Turk et al. (1991) and by Martín et al. (1996), who studied a consanguineous family. Xin and Wang (2011) found homozygous SLC5A1 variants that cosegregated with the disorder in a large Amish pedigree.

### Mapping

The SGLT1 gene, which is mutated in glucose/galactose malabsorption, had previously been assigned to 22q11.2-qter. Using fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe, Turk et al. (1993) refined its location to chromosome 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 (SLC5A1) cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family affected with GGM. They found a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. In Xenopus oocytes injected with the mutant cRNA, Na(+)-dependent glucose transport was completely lost.

Turk et al. (1993) noted that a missense mutation in SGLT1 caused autosomal recessive glucose/galactose malabsorption in 2 patients. In vitro, the resulting asp28-to-asn (D28N) change abolished SGLT1 transport activity.

Martín et al. (1996) screened the SGLT1 gene in 30 new patients with glucose-galactose malabsorption. They used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype and found that defects in SGLT1 trafficking and function caused the disorder.

Martín et al. (1996) noted that 2 sibs with GGM from a large consanguineous family had previously been shown to carry a D28N missense mutation in SGLT1, which accounted for the defect in sugar absorption.

In 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree, Xin and Wang (2011) found 4 homozygous missense variants in the SLC5A1 gene: c.152A-G (N51S), c.1231G-A (A411T), c.1673G-A (R558H), and c.1845C-G (H615Q). All 4 variants cosegregated with the GGM phenotype in all affected individuals.

### Diagnosis

Martín et al. (1996) carried out prenatal screening in 2 pregnancies in a large consanguineous GGM family. They PCR-amplified exon 1 of SGLT1 and tested for the D28N mutation by EcoRV restriction digestion. The proband's sib was heterozygous and a cousin was not a carrier. Both children were healthy without diarrhea at 2 years of age.

Abdullah et al. (1996) confirmed the diagnosis of glucose-galactose malabsorption in 8 Arab children by laboratory investigations, including small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay. The mean age at presentation was 10.6 months.

### Clinical Management

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported that all 8 Arab children with glucose-galactose malabsorption responded clinically to a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported that when patients were diagnosed correctly and managed adequately, the diarrhea stopped at once, the patients rehydrated quickly, and normal growth and development followed. When the disease was not recognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested that the SLC5A1 mutations associated with GGM in the Old Order Amish reflect a founder effect. They concluded that population-specific genetic testing is needed to make the early diagnosis that is critical for a favorable outcome.
