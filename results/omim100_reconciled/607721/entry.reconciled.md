### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is characterized by facial features that resemble but are not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, together with a behavioral phenotype, growth hormone (GH) deficiency, and a distinctive combination of ectodermal abnormalities, including darkly pigmented, hairless skin and loose anagen hair (Mazzanti et al., 2003). Affected individuals show reduced growth, often associated with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, distinctive hyperactive behavior, hypernasal voice, cardiac anomalies, and easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair (Cordeddu et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed that the combination of a facial appearance not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, a behavioral phenotype, GH deficiency, darkly pigmented hairless skin, and loose anagen hair represented a distinct, previously unreported syndrome, which they named 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.'

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. She had short, blond, easily pluckable hair that had never been cut. The trichogram showed an absolute prevalence of abnormally shaped anagen bulbs lacking inner and outer root sheaths, and scalp biopsy showed cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reviewed 19 patients diagnosed with loose anagen hair (14 children and 5 adults). Associated conditions included Noonan syndrome in a 5-year-old boy. In all cases, the trichogram showed a predominance of anagen hairs (80 to 100%) devoid of sheaths. The authors concluded that loose anagen hair may be sporadic, may occur together with developmental or acquired conditions, or, less commonly, may be familial.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children with short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), mild dilatation of the pulmonary root (in 2 of them), and a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities: darkly pigmented, hairless skin and loose anagen hair.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) described 25 unrelated, sporadic subjects with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. They exhibited reduced growth, which was frequently associated with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most subjects, hypernasal voice, and hair anomalies including easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair; loose anagen hair was confirmed microscopically in 12. Some had darkly pigmented skin, eczema, or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies included mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects, which were overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population. Other features in some subjects included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, short webbed neck, and pectus anomalies. Referring pediatricians felt that several subjects had features suggestive of Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome as newborns or young infants. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All showed short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six showed severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 showed leukocytosis. The authors concluded that the clinical features of SHOC2 mutation-positive patients partly overlap with those of typical Noonan or CFC syndrome, and that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair is distinctive in these patients.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a de novo SHOC2 S2G mutation who presented with fetal distress requiring premature delivery at 32 weeks. After birth he was noted to have dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography revealed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, which progressed rapidly, leading to discharge for palliative care. The authors noted that his cardiac findings and other features were not typical of patients previously reported with this mutation and concluded that the case expanded the clinical phenotype.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 males with the SHOC2 S2G mutation, 4 of whom had initially been suspected of having Costello syndrome. All had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay, and all 4 with available data had a structural cardiac anomaly. The light-colored, slow-growing scalp hair with dolichocilia resembled the hair seen in Costello syndrome caused by HRAS G13C. Brain imaging showed relative megalencephaly, enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a small posterior fossa with a vertical tentorium. Including previously reported patients, Chiari 1 malformation was present in 3 of 8 SHOC2 mutation-positive patients with MRI data; the authors attributed it to the combination of relative megalencephaly with a small posterior fossa and noted that it can develop after birth.

Patient 1 of Gripp et al. (2013) was born after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and cystic hygroma and had macrosomia, pyloric stenosis, severe failure to thrive requiring a gastrostomy tube, mild pulmonic valve stenosis, low axial tone, small optic nerves, delayed motor development, macrocephaly (OFC +3 SD) with a large anterior fontanel, sparse thin hair, ptosis, hepatosplenomegaly, hemangiomata, and fine wrinkling of the hands. Brain imaging showed enlarged subarachnoid spaces, a vertical tentorium with small posterior fossa, mild cerebellar tonsillar ectopia, and a thin corpus callosum. He developed primary myelofibrosis, with blasts in peripheral blood and a markedly hypercellular marrow with increased reticulin staining, and died at age 2.5 years after splenectomy in preparation for a bone marrow transplant. Patient 2, born to a 21-year-old mother and a 40-year-old father after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios, had increased birth weight, macrocephaly, ptosis, posteriorly angulated ears, increased palmar wrinkling, loose skin, bilateral inguinal hernias, and hypotonia, prompting consideration of Costello syndrome. He had severe feeding difficulties, a subdural effusion requiring drainage, delayed bone age, and nonverbal intellectual disability; at age 9 5/12 years his height was at the 3rd to 5th centile, and he had acanthosis nigricans, eczema, and dry skin. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, hypogenesis of the corpus callosum, decreased white matter volume, an abnormally vertical tentorium, and cerebellar tonsillar ectopia. ⚠︎

Patient 3 of Gripp et al. (2013) was born after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and had macrosomia, tracheostenosis, chronic lung disease, and a neonatal seizure. He had right bundle branch block and mild to moderate biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy at age 6 months, as well as aortic insufficiency and mild pulmonic stenosis. He required a gastrostomy for failure to thrive. He had developmental delay and aggressive behavior and was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome at 9 years. At age 9 years his height was 125 cm (10th centile), weight 29.4 kg (50th centile), and OFC 60 cm (+5 SD). Other features included coarse facial features, mild ptosis, eczema, a cafe-au-lait lesion, deep palmar creases, osteoporosis, and strabismus; brain imaging showed enlarged arachnoid spaces and decreased white matter volume. Patient 4, of Mexican ancestry, was born at 37 weeks' gestation weighing 2.9 kg to a 32-year-old mother and a 41-year-old father. He had failure to thrive, elevated PT/PTT, rectal prolapse, strabismus, nystagmus, chronic ear infections, atrial septal defect, trivial mitral regurgitation, severe eczema, sparse, thin, easily pluckable blond hair, long eyelashes, mild to moderate intellectual disability, short stature, webbed neck, pectus excavatum, and widely spaced nipples, but no macrocephaly. Brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly and periventricular nodular heterotopia; MRIs at 6 years, performed because of headaches, revealed development of a Chiari 1 malformation in the context of platybasia and basilar invagination, and fusion of the C3 and C4 vertebrae. Patient 5 had neonatal hypotonia and feeding difficulties requiring a nasogastric tube until about age 1 year. A brain MRI at 3 weeks showed a relatively small cerebellar vermis and hemispheres with prominent fluid spaces and increased extra-axial fluid spaces. He had a patent ductus arteriosus, an abnormal mitral valve with 3 papillary muscles, a duplicated renal collecting system, short stature, macrocephaly (OFC +2 SD), hemangiomata, dry hyperpigmented skin, slow-growing hair that was not easily pluckable, fine motor delay, and attention deficit disorder; he was last reported at 8 3/12 years.

Hannig et al. (2014) reported a 5-year-old girl of European descent, born to nonconsanguineous parents, who was referred for right optic nerve hypoplasia, bilateral nystagmus, speech delay, poor hair growth, and hyperactive behavior. She had average stature (47th percentile), macrocephaly (98th percentile), sparse and slow-growing hair that was not loose, normal skin pigmentation, bilateral ptosis, wide-spaced nipples, and clubbed nails; MRI showed irregularities in the midbrain, pons, and medulla. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair. Her father, who carried the same SHOC2 variant, was 6 feet 3 inches tall and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. Echocardiograms in both were normal.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a patient with a SHOC2 mutation (Q269_H270delinsHY) associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous SHOC2 mutations identified in sporadic patients by Cordeddu et al. (2009) were shown to be de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available. Hannig et al. (2014) reported transmission of a heterozygous SHOC2 variant from a mildly affected father to his affected daughter. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent pro49-to-arg (P49R) mutation in the PPP1CB gene who had sparse, thin, slow-growing hair similar to that seen in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, as well as craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair is very similar to the SHOC2-related disorder. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified SHOC2 as a candidate gene by in silico protein network analysis. In 4 of 96 unrelated Noonan syndrome patients without mutations in known genes, they identified a heterozygous A-to-G transition at position 4 of the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. Analysis of 410 further mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or related phenotypes identified the same change in 21. The mutation was de novo in 3 of the 4 subjects of the first cohort and in the 12 families of the second cohort for which parental DNA was available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found, suggesting a specific pathogenetic role for S2G. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1, and found the S2G mutation in 8. They found no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a premature male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) detected a de novo heterozygous S2G mutation in the SHOC2 gene. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the heterozygous SHOC2 S2G (c.4A-G) mutation in 5 patients. In patient 1, the mutation was found by postmortem research testing of stored buccal cell-derived DNA and was not present in either parent; in patient 2, it was also de novo.

In a 5-year-old girl with a RASopathy phenotype, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The variant was inherited from her father, who also had mild features. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the M173I variant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation only about 10% above the basal level, whereas wildtype SHOC2 rescued it fully. The variant did not affect protein half-life, subcellular localization, or binding to MRAS and RAF1, but the mutant failed to precipitate endogenous PP1c effectively and supported only a mild increase in RAF1 ser338 phosphorylation after EGF stimulation. The authors concluded that reduced binding of SHOC2 to PP1c caused ERK1/2 pathway dysregulation, making the variant loss-of-function.

In a patient with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Motta et al. (2019) identified a c.807_808delinsTT mutation in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a gln269_his270delinsHisTyr substitution. The mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade, indicating that the mutation was activating. Structural analyses suggested a possible effect on the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains of SHOC2.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the phenotype of patients with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed significantly from that of patients with the S2G mutation, in that they lacked short stature, cardiac anomalies, and loose anagen hair. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype SHOC2 was distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus in starved cells and was restricted to the nucleus after EGF stimulation; growth factor-stimulated nuclear translocation of endogenous SHOC2 was confirmed in primary skin fibroblasts. The S2G substitution created an N-myristoylation site: SHOC2 S2G incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype and S2A did not, and the mutant was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane and failed to translocate to the nucleus upon EGF stimulation. Membrane localization was reduced by the NMT inhibitor 2-hydroxymyristic acid. SHOC2 S2G did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2, excluding a dominant-negative effect, and its expression enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells, indicating cell type-specific MAPK activation.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported myelofibrosis in a 2-year-old with the SHOC2 S2G mutation, the first hematologic neoplasm in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation. Because the patient lacked the JAK2 V617F mutation, the authors suggested that the SHOC2 mutation may have predisposed to myelofibrosis through activation of the RAS/MAPK pathway, and proposed testing JAK2-negative myelofibrosis cases for SHOC2 S2G.

Young et al. (2018) found that MRAS interacts with SHOC2 and protein phosphatase-1 (PP1) to form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site of RAF kinases (S259 on CRAF, S365 on BRAF). MRAS and SHOC2 act as PP1 regulatory subunits that make the complex highly specific for RAF, and MRAS membrane localization is needed for efficient RAF dephosphorylation and ERK pathway regulation. The predicted structure of SHOC2 closely resembles the A subunit of PP2A. Noonan syndrome mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB all promoted formation of the complex among these 3 proteins but did not necessarily increase binding to other interactors, and the authors concluded that Noonan syndrome in individuals with these mutations is likely driven at the biochemical level by enhanced ternary complex formation.

Motta et al. (2019) showed that, unlike the recurrent S2G mutant, SHOC2 Q269_H270delinsHY was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane, and concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 functional dysregulation converge toward upregulation of MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

In all patients with loose anagen hair studied by Tosti et al. (1997), the trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) lacking sheaths. Pathologic study of hair from 5 patients found no morphologic abnormalities of the hair follicles apart from frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath, and the authors concluded that pathology is of little diagnostic value.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid melting curve analysis method to detect the SHOC2 S2G mutation and proposed it as a useful screening tool for this apparently common mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) stated that the overlap of the SHOC2-associated phenotype with Costello syndrome supports testing patients with a RASopathy phenotype using a gene panel.

### Population Genetics

Pooling 2 earlier case series, Gripp et al. (2013) estimated that SHOC2 mutations occur in about 6% (12 of 188) of patients with Noonan syndrome who lack a mutation in other known genes, corresponding to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome.

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that in C. elegans, human wildtype SHOC2, but not S2G, rescued sur-8 function. Expression of SHOC2 S2G or myristoylation-tagged wildtype SHOC2 at early L3 caused protruding vulva, egg-laying defects, and a bag-of-worms phenotype through abnormal vulval morphogenesis, without altering vulval cell fate specification.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G]

In 25 unrelated, sporadic patients with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation was found in 4 of 96 mutation-negative Noonan syndrome patients and in 21 of 410 further mutation-negative subjects, and was de novo in 3 of the 4 subjects of the first cohort and in 12 families of the second cohort with available parental DNA. The substitution created an N-myristoylation site; the mutant incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane, and failed to translocate to the nucleus upon EGF stimulation. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who lacked mutations in other known genes. All 8 had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin, and 6 had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo heterozygous S2G mutation in a male infant delivered at 32 weeks because of fetal distress, who had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the heterozygous S2G mutation in 5 males with NSLH1, including a boy who developed myelofibrosis and died at age 2.5 years; the mutation was de novo in that patient and in a second patient.

**.0002 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old girl of European descent with optic nerve hypoplasia, nystagmus, speech delay, sparse slow-growing hair, macrocephaly, and hyperactive behavior, but without short stature, cardiac anomaly, or loose anagen hair, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The variant was inherited from her father, who had intellectual disability, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. The mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation only about 10% above basal level in SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells and failed to precipitate endogenous PP1c effectively, and the authors considered it a loss-of-function variant.

**.0003 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, GLN269_HIS270DELINSHISTYR [c.807_808delinstt; p.Q269]

In a patient with a RASopathy associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Motta et al. (2019) identified a c.807_808delinsTT mutation in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a gln269_his270delinsHisTyr substitution. The mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased MAPK signaling, and, unlike S2G, was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.
