### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene (604618) on chromosome 1q23.3.

### Description

Brain small vessel disease-4 is an autosomal recessive disorder. Rutten et al. (2024) described it as a cerebral small vessel disease with a triad of movement disorders, massively dilated basal ganglia perivascular spaces, and intracerebral hemorrhage. Rendu et al. (2019) had earlier described the disorder as a metabolic disorder that combines neurologic signs, cavitating (cystic) lesions of the basal ganglia, and a positive urine sulfite test.

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) reported a family in which 3 brothers had a familial deep cavitating state. The index patient (II.4) was admitted to the emergency ward at age 34 years with a sudden left hemiparesis. He had a history of nonsevere mental retardation. Two years earlier he had been admitted to a psychiatry unit for an acute psychotic episode, which was followed by generalized seizures. Brain imaging showed bilateral small cystic lesions of the basal ganglia. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and cardiac echocardiography were normal. A dipstick urine test for sulfites was positive. Over a 20-year period his condition progressively worsened, with severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia. He died in 2011, and no autopsy was performed. In 2014 his brother (II.5), then 58 years old, was admitted to intensive care for a sudden coma with cardiovascular collapse. He had a history of diabetes mellitus and had led an active professional life. He had basal ganglia cystic lesions and a positive urine sulfite test. He progressively recovered but showed neuropsychological deterioration (BREF, 14/18; MMSE, 22/30). A third brother (II.7), aged 60 years, lived in a psychiatric institution and had been diagnosed with schizophrenia 40 years earlier. His clinical examination was normal, but CT scan and 3-T MRI showed the same brain lesions, and his urine sulfite test was positive.

Rutten et al. (2024) reported 7 patients with biallelic NIT1 variants: 3 sibs and 4 single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous and massively dilated basal ganglia perivascular spaces. Three of the 7 patients had a nonlobar intracerebral hemorrhage between 45 and 60 years of age, which was fatal in 2. Brain autopsy showed large electron-dense deposits in the vessel walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries.

### Biochemical Features

Using GC/MS, Rendu et al. (2019) found a deaminated glutathione (dGSH) derivative in the urine of the affected brothers. It was not found in their unaffected sibs or their mother. Its mass spectrum resembled that of the compound seen in Nit1-knockout mice, and the authors suggested that dGSH probably caused the positive urine sulfite test. In patient fibroblasts, mitochondrial MnSOD activity was increased twofold, whereas cytoplasmic Cu/Zn SOD activity was unchanged. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of BSVD4 is autosomal recessive. In the family reported by Rendu et al. (2019), affected brothers were compound heterozygous for NIT1 mutations, and one of the mutations was inherited from their asymptomatic mother. Rutten et al. (2024) found homozygous or compound heterozygous NIT1 variants in their patients.

### Molecular Genetics

Rendu et al. (2019) performed exome sequencing on 2 of the affected brothers (II.5 and II.7) from the family with a familial deep cavitating state. Both were compound heterozygous for 2 rare variants in the NIT1 gene: a c.457G-A transition predicting a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution, and a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA) predicting a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41). The c.670dupA variant was inherited from the asymptomatic mother, and molecular cloning showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles. Asymptomatic sibs carried neither variant. The c.457G-A variant affects the last nucleotide of exon 4, and it disrupted splicing in transcripts from patient skin. NIT1 protein could not be detected by Western blot in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎

In 7 patients with brain small vessel disease, Rutten et al. (2024) identified biallelic mutations in the NIT1 gene. Three sibs were compound heterozygous for a c.727C-T transition, predicting an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and a 2-bp deletion (c.198_199del), predicting a premature stop (Ala68Ter). Four single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees were homozygous for the R243W mutation. Western blot showed no NIT1 protein in patient fibroblasts, and metabolic analysis of urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function. These results were consistent with loss-of-function variants.

### Diagnosis

Rendu et al. (2019) noted that, before their report, sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency were the only conditions known to combine brain damage with a positive urinary sulfite test. Both were excluded in their family. They suggested that the sulfite test could be used to screen psychiatric patients for the disorder.
