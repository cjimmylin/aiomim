### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13. ⚠︎

### Description

NEDHLSS is a CACNA1C-related neurologic disorder with variable developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, epilepsy, hypotonia, and ataxia (Rodan et al., 2021). Many patients also have orthopedic abnormalities such as clubfoot, hip dysplasia, syndactyly, and joint hypermobility. Unlike in Timothy syndrome, significant cardiac abnormalities and the classic Timothy syndrome features are usually absent.

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She did not have QT prolongation.

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (patient 1) of English maternal and Ukrainian paternal ancestry born to nonconsanguineous, unaffected parents. Her seizures began within a few hours of birth, with numerous subtle tonic seizures and prolonged tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures every day. She also had epileptic tonic spasms and focal seizures with eye blinking and facial twitching. Her epileptic encephalopathy was drug-resistant, and EEG showed diffuse excessive fast activity. She had profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, made no developmental progress, and could not visually track or fixate. Other findings included neonatal hypoglycemia, short length at birth, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubfeet, bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, camptodactyly, micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, very scant hair, inverted nipples, and mild optic nerve hypoplasia. She had severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, anal stenosis, and severe dysphagia requiring gastrostomy, as well as a disturbed sleep-wake cycle and central and obstructive sleep apnea. Brain MRI, echocardiogram, and Holter monitoring were normal. She was last reported at 18 months of age. The authors noted that she had many hallmark features of Timothy syndrome, including cutaneous syndactyly, dysmorphic features, and sleep disturbance, but no cardiac abnormalities. She differed from previously reported individuals in having severe refractory neonatal-onset epilepsy, no neurodevelopmental progress, and a recognizable EEG pattern of diffuse fast activity.

Bozarth et al. (2018) also reported a nonconsanguineous family of Irish and German ancestry. The proband (patient 2), a girl, had tetralogy of Fallot that was surgically repaired at 2 months of age. She also had left ventricular noncompaction and acute severe biventricular failure. She had mild developmental delay at 8 months, and by 12 months she spoke 10 words and first walked. Neurologic examination showed mild diffuse hypotonia. She had a flat nasal bridge, low-set ears, cupped superior helices, deep-set eyes with shallow orbits, a thin vermilion border of the upper lip, a round face, and mild bilateral 2-3-4 toe syndactyly. Her father (patient 3) had a congenital ventricular septal defect and aortic valve stenosis, repaired at 2.5 months of age. He had learning disabilities requiring an individualized education program and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and by 18 years he had developed focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. His craniofacial features resembled his daughter's: low-set ears, a thin upper lip vermilion, premaxillary underdevelopment, and a round face.

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants. Significant cardiac abnormalities and classic Timothy syndrome features were absent in all but one; that patient (P13) had a prolonged QT interval. All 14 individuals with de novo nontruncating variants had developmental delay or intellectual disability. Nine of the 14 were nonverbal, 12 had hypotonia, and 10 had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years of age. Twelve had orthopedic abnormalities, including clubfoot, hip dysplasia, syndactyly, and joint hypermobility. Seizure types included: - absence and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (P1) - fever-sensitive focal motor and absence seizures (P2) - focal seizures (P3) - West syndrome/infantile spasms (P6) - myoclonic and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (P7) - fever-sensitive generalized epilepsy (P8) - behavioral arrest spells with or without head or limb jerking (P9) - febrile seizures (P11) - myoclonic seizures evolving to generalized epilepsy (P12) - infantile spasms evolving to Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (P13) - generalized epilepsy beginning at 2 months (P14) P4, P5, and P10 had no seizures. Movement abnormalities included action tremor of the arms that resolved (P4), intention tremor of the arms with head tremor and ataxia (P5), and leg spasticity (P10). Several patients were nonambulatory. Skeletal findings were: - pes planovalgus (P1, P5) - congenital hip dysplasia (P2, P14) - hip dislocation and subluxation (P4) - joint hypermobility (P3, P4, P5) - bilateral clubfoot with adducted thumbs (P7, P10), with camptodactyly in P10 - kyphosis (P8) - tapered fingers and small-appearing feet (P9) - 2-3 toe and 3-4 finger syndactyly with congenital torticollis (P11) - kyphoscoliosis and coxa valga (P12) - 2-3 toe syndactyly and bilateral adducted thumbs (P14) Ocular findings included strabismus, hyperopia, myopia, esotropia, and cortical visual impairment. P4 had a small interatrial communication with left-to-right shunt, and P12 had a possible ventricular conduction delay. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating CACNA1C variants, whose deficits were milder and more selective. Ten of the 11 had delayed expressive language, early motor development was essentially normal in all but 1, 5 had autism, and only 1 had seizures. - P15, a 15-year-old girl, had language delay, autism, ADHD, Chiari I malformation, and scoliosis, with IQ in the low-normal range. - In family 16, a 33-year-old man (P16b) had language delay and a learning disability with normal IQ. His 6-year-old son (P16a) had motor delay, hypotonia, poor balance and coordination, high-frequency tremor, language delay with only 2 words, autism, mild to moderate intellectual disability, and large ears. - P17, a 14.5-year-old girl, had language delay, borderline intellectual disability, and dysmorphic features. - P18, a 16-year-old boy, had moderate intellectual disability, ADHD, poor balance and coordination, language delay, pes planus, genu valgum, mildly broad thumbs and halluces, and hypoplastic fifth fingernails and fifth toes. - P19, a 6-year-old boy, had motor and language delay, poor coordination, ADHD, and dysmorphic features. - P20, a 4-year-old girl, had language delay, autism, moderate global developmental delay, and dysmorphic features. - P21, a 40-year-old woman, walked at 26 months and had language delay, moderate intellectual disability, and strabismus. - In family 22, a 32-year-old man (P22c) had a learning disability and a history of seizures; he was seizure-free without treatment. His 2 sons both had hypotonia, autism, and dysmorphic features, and spoke only a few words. The 2-year-old (P22a) also had metatarsus adductus, and the 3-year-old (P22b) had intention tremor.

### Inheritance

NEDHLSS is transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait. Most mutations reported by Kosaki et al. (2018), Bozarth et al. (2018), and Rodan et al. (2021) occurred de novo. Rodan et al. (2021) also found inherited truncating variants that segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in families. In family 16, a de novo nonsense variant in the father was transmitted to his son. In family 22, a nonsense variant was transmitted from the father to his 2 sons. Penetrance and expressivity among family members appeared to vary. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the CACNA1C gene, arg1024-to-gly (R1024G), in a girl with syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension.

Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.4087G-A mutation in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a val1363-to-met (V1363M) substitution, in a girl with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy (patient 1). She also carried a de novo terminal deletion of exons 11 to 16 of the MSH2 gene, which was not considered causal. On a predicted 3D structure of Cav1.2, V1363 lies in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV and faces the pore-forming sixth helix. It is in direct contact with A1473G, a Timothy syndrome variant. The authors suggested that the larger methionine residue likely disrupts the sixth helix and affects channel gating or calcium transmission. ⚠︎

In a girl with tetralogy of Fallot, left ventricular noncompaction, mild developmental delay, and hypotonia (patient 2), Bozarth et al. (2018) identified a heterozygous splice site variant in the CACNA1C gene (c.3717+1_3717+2insA) inherited from her affected father (patient 3). She also carried a heterozygous ALPK3 splice variant (c.5105+2dupT) and a heterozygous TPM1 missense variant (c.389T-C; I130T), which was absent from her father. The significance of the CACNA1C variant was considered uncertain.

Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous CACNA1C variants in 25 individuals from 22 families. These comprised 13 de novo missense variants, 1 de novo in-frame deletion, and truncating variants in 11 individuals from 8 families. None of the variants were present in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or the Exome Variant Server. The truncating variants were de novo in P15, P16b, P19, P20, and P21 and were paternally inherited in P16a, P17, P22a, and P22b. In P18, the splice site variant was not inherited from the father, and a maternal sample was unavailable. One truncating variant occurred in the last exon (exon 45 of 45) and might escape nonsense-mediated decay. Rodan et al. (2021) studied 4 missense variants by patch clamp in HEK293 cells: - L1408V reduced current density to about 30% of wildtype, with unchanged protein expression. - L657F increased both current and protein level about 2-fold. - L614P caused a modest, nonsignificant increase in current. - L614R had no significant effect.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kosaki et al. (2018) noted that their patient was the first with syndactyly and a CACNA1C mutation who lacked QT prolongation, a feature long considered obligatory for Timothy syndrome.

Rodan et al. (2021) observed that individuals with truncating CACNA1C variants had milder, more selective deficits than those with nontruncating variants. Ten of 11 with truncating variants had delayed expressive language, early motor development was essentially normal in all but 1, 5 of 11 had autism, and only 1 had seizures. By contrast, all 14 individuals with de novo nontruncating variants had developmental delay or intellectual disability, 9 were nonverbal, 12 had hypotonia, and 10 had epilepsy.

### Pathogenesis

Rodan et al. (2021) suggested that CACNA1C may be haploinsufficient, citing a gnomAD pLI of 1.0. They proposed that a dominant-negative effect would be better supported if patients with missense loss-of-function variants proved to have a more severe phenotype than patients with whole-gene or exonic deletions. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

In their patient with the V1363M mutation, Bozarth et al. (2018) found that seizures did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs. Topiramate and valproic acid gave a small reduction. Clobazam reduced seizures from numerous per day to fewer than 10 per day and improved sleep.
