### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene (138140) on chromosome 1p34.2.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis (sdCHC) is a rare form of stomatocytosis. Its red cells leak cations when cold, and patients have hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly. It is also associated with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder (Flatt et al., 2011). Flatt et al. (2011) found that the disorder was associated with mutations in SLC2A1, which caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak.

### Clinical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased permeability of the erythrocyte membrane to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis.

Fricke et al. (2004) described 5 cases of stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis that differed in how the passive K+ leak depended on temperature. In 3 cases, the leak varied with temperature in a simple monotonic way. In the other 2, the leak was lowest at about 20 to 25 degrees C and the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, a phenotype known as cryohydrocytosis. The authors stated that these 2 patients were the only known cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Both had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts, and massive hepatosplenomegaly. The authors suggested that this probably defined a new haemato-neurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) restudied the 2 sdCHC cases that had been reported earlier. They summarized the phenotype as stomatocytosis with a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) reported an infant who presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, and microcephaly. Seizures developed later, and developmental milestones were delayed. MRI and CT scans of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. The child had visual impairment due to both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. The authors noted that this was the fourth pedigree described with the syndrome. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) used SDS gel electrophoresis to separate erythrocyte membrane band 7 into 3 components of about 30, 28, and 26 kD. The 28-kD component was decreased in both a patient with hereditary stomatocytosis and a patient with cryohydrocytosis. On 2-dimensional electrophoresis, the 28-kD component separated into 2 proteins, 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. Protein 22 x 8 was completely absent in both patients, whereas 60 x 8 was present as usual.

Using Western blotting, Fricke et al. (2004) found that red cell membranes were deficient in stomatin in all of their cases of stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis. Immunocytochemistry showed that the deficiency was incomplete. On blood films, some red cells were stomatin-positive and most were stomatin-negative, although in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive.

Bawazir et al. (2012) found that the proband's red cells were extremely leaky to cations, which caused hemolysis. The cells also lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis, Flatt et al. (2011) found that the disorder was associated with mutations in the SLC2A1 gene; 2 mutations were found. In expression studies in Xenopus oocytes, the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Based on a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, the authors proposed possible mechanisms for the phenotypes of the 2 mutations.

In an infant with pseudohyperkalemia, hemolysis, seizures, cataracts, and developmental delay, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene. The deletion removes isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1.

### Pathogenesis

Lande et al. (1982) found that all band 7 proteins, including 22 x 8, were present in every normal subject and every reticulocytosis control they examined. They suggested that absence of this protein might be responsible for the increased Na and K permeability in hereditary stomatocytosis and cryohydrocytosis.

Flatt et al. (2011) found that erythroid cells lost stomatin during reticulocyte maturation through a process involving endocytosis.

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the multisystem pathology probably reflects 2 combined effects. The first is deficient glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The second is the harmful osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 STOMATIN-DEFICIENT CRYOHYDROCYTOSIS WITH NEUROLOGIC DEFECTS**  
SLC2A1, 2 MUTATIONS (NOT SPECIFIED)

Flatt et al. (2011) found 2 SLC2A1 mutations in the 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. The available evidence does not give the specific changes or their zygosity. The patients had stomatocytosis with a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, hepatosplenomegaly, cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder. In expression studies in Xenopus oocytes, the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak.

**.0002 STOMATIN-DEFICIENT CRYOHYDROCYTOSIS WITH NEUROLOGIC DEFECTS**  
SLC2A1, 3-BP DEL, ILE435DEL OR ILE436DEL

Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that removes isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1. The patient was an infant with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, microcephaly, seizures, delayed development, periventricular calcification, cataracts, and retinal dysfunction. Zygosity was not stated. The proband's red cells were extremely leaky to cations and lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which explained the pseudohyperkalemia.
