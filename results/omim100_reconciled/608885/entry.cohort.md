### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene (138140) on chromosome 1p34.2.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects is a rare form of stomatocytosis. Its features are a cold-induced red cell cation leak, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder (Flatt et al., 2011). Bawazir et al. (2012) described an infant with the disorder whose red cells lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which produced pseudohyperkalemia. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased permeability of the erythrocyte membrane to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis.

Fricke et al. (2004) described cases of stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis that differed in how the passive K+ leak depended on temperature. In 3 cases the leak followed a simple monotonic temperature dependence. In the other 2 cases the leak was lowest at around 20 to 25 degrees C, and the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, giving the phenotype known as cryohydrocytosis. The authors stated that these were the only 2 known cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Both patients had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts and massive hepatosplenomegaly. Fricke et al. (2004) suggested that this probably defined a new haemato-neurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) restudied the 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis that their group had reported earlier. They described the disorder as stomatocytosis with a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) reported an infant who presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction and microcephaly. Seizures developed later, and developmental milestones were delayed. Magnetic resonance imaging and CT scans of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. Visual impairment resulted from both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. The authors noted that this was the fourth pedigree described with the syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) used SDS gel electrophoresis to separate erythrocyte membrane band 7 into 3 components of about 30, 28 and 26 kD. The 28-kD component was decreased in a patient with hereditary stomatocytosis and in a patient with cryohydrocytosis. On 2-dimensional electrophoresis, the 28-kD component separated into 2 proteins, 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. Protein 22 x 8 was completely absent in both patients, whereas 60 x 8 was present as usual.

Fricke et al. (2004) showed by Western blotting that red cell membranes were deficient in stomatin in all of their cases. Immunocytochemistry showed that the deficiency was incomplete. On blood films, some red cells were stomatin-positive and most were stomatin-negative, although in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive.

Bawazir et al. (2012) found that the proband's red cells were extremely leaky to cations, which caused hemolysis. The cells lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis, Flatt et al. (2011) found that the disorder was associated with mutations in the SLC2A1 gene. Expression studies in Xenopus oocytes showed that the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Using a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, the authors proposed possible mechanisms for the phenotypes of the 2 mutations.

In an infant with SDCHCN, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deletes isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1.

### Pathogenesis

Lande et al. (1982) found all band 7 proteins, including 22 x 8, in every normal subject and every reticulocytosis control they surveyed. They suggested that absence of this protein might be responsible for the increased Na and K permeability in hereditary stomatocytosis and cryohydrocytosis.

Flatt et al. (2011) found that erythroid cells lose stomatin during reticulocyte maturation, through a process that involves endocytosis.

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the multisystem pathology probably reflects 2 combined effects. The first is deficient glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The second is the harmful osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 STOMATIN-DEFICIENT CRYOHYDROCYTOSIS WITH NEUROLOGIC DEFECTS**  
SLC2A1, 2 MUTATIONS (NOT SPECIFIED IN EVIDENCE)

In 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis, Flatt et al. (2011) found 2 mutations in the SLC2A1 gene. The patients had stomatocytosis with a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, hepatosplenomegaly, cataracts, seizures, mental retardation and movement disorder. Expression studies in Xenopus oocytes showed that the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. ⚠︎

**.0002 STOMATIN-DEFICIENT CRYOHYDROCYTOSIS WITH NEUROLOGIC DEFECTS**  
SLC2A1, 3-BP DEL, ILE435DEL OR ILE436DEL

In an infant with SDCHCN, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deletes isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1. The infant had pseudohyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hemolysis, hepatic dysfunction and microcephaly. Seizures and developmental delay followed, along with periventricular calcification, cataracts and retinal dysfunction. The proband's red cells were extremely leaky to cations and lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way.
