### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10 (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene (612453) on chromosome 5q23.

### Description

The mild variant of congenital myopathy-10 (CMYO10B) is an autosomal recessive myopathy. Onset is usually in infancy or childhood, and some cases begin in adulthood. Features include axial and proximal muscle weakness, facial weakness, contractures, scoliosis, hypernasal speech and respiratory insufficiency that develops later than in the classic early-onset form. Muscle biopsy often shows minicores. Reported patients have mostly carried missense mutations, and the disease is milder than in patients with null mutations in MEGF10. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) studied a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an undiagnosed autosomal recessive myopathy that resembled multiminicore disease in some respects. Onset was by 12 months of age with neck weakness, and neck contractures were present in infancy. The weakness was mainly axial and proximal, and the patients also had facial weakness. They had contractures of the elbows, knees and heel cords, and serum creatine kinase was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion surgery. Beginning at age 10 to 11 years, all 3 developed respiratory problems that were treated with bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation at night. All 3 had hypernasal speech, 1 had a cleft palate, and some had neck muscle atrophy. Muscle biopsy showed fatty replacement, increased endomysial connective tissue, minicores, variation in fiber size and type I fiber predominance. There were no rimmed vacuoles, nemaline rods, central cores or ragged red fibers.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with a milder, minicore myopathy-like phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness and dysphagia. In his fifties, while still ambulant, he developed respiratory failure that required ventilation. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins. Harris et al. (2018) concluded that their findings extended the phenotype of MEGF10 mutations to include onset in the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to healthy consanguineous parents with no family history of neuromuscular disease. From the first months of life he had weak suction and axial hypotonia. Motor development was not delayed and he did not have intellectual disability. He developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, elbow and ankle contractures, equinovarus foot stance, scoliosis and hyperlordosis. He also had rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement that required nocturnal noninvasive ventilation, with recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration. Examination showed generalized muscular hypotrophy, cleft palate and global areflexia. There was no facial weakness and sensation was not impaired. Nerve conduction studies showed reduced amplitude of muscle evoked potentials. Muscle biopsy showed fiber size variation, internal nuclei, fibrosis, whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions and minicores. His first muscle biopsy showed only nonspecific changes, and the myofibrillar changes with minicores were seen in a later biopsy. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CMYO10B is autosomal recessive. In family 1030, reported by Boyden et al. (2012), compound heterozygous MEGF10 mutations in trans segregated with the myopathy in all 6 family members. In the patient reported by Croci et al. (2022), who was born to consanguineous parents, the homozygous mutation was carried in the heterozygous state by both healthy parents.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) performed genomewide linkage analysis in family 1030 under a recessive model and found positive LOD scores at 16 loci. MEGF10 lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible LOD score of 1.329.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 3 affected sibs of family 1030, Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: c.976T-C (C326R) and c.2320T-C (C774R). MEGF10 was the sole remaining candidate gene in the linkage regions. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans in the affected sibs and segregated with the myopathy in all 6 family members. The mutations were absent from 715 and 645 control subjects, respectively, and both affected cysteine residues were conserved in all 43 species examined.

Boyden et al. (2012) also screened the entire MEGF10 open reading frame in 190 patients with unexplained myopathies and selected exons in another 82. Only 1 patient carried a mutation: a heterozygous c.211C-T (R71W) change. She was a Caucasian girl of distant Portuguese ancestry who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease and was the only affected member of her family. She presented at several weeks of age with hypotonia, a weak neck and a floppy head. She also had a markedly high arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, hyperflexible joints, frequent pulmonary infections in childhood, mild scoliosis, facial weakness, and mild nonprogressive proximal weakness. Her serum CK was mildly elevated (302 U/L). Muscle biopsy showed minicores, confirmed by electron microscopy, together with internalized nuclei and marked variation in fiber size. The variant was also present in the heterozygous state in an unaffected parent, so it had not arisen de novo, and no second mutation was found.

In a Japanese patient with a milder, minicore myopathy-like phenotype, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a c.2981-2A-G splice site mutation and a novel missense mutation, cys810-to-tyr (C810Y).

Using exome sequencing, Harris et al. (2018) identified 2 novel mutations in the MEGF10 gene in a 66-year-old man with adult-onset disease: an essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a c.352T-C transition resulting in a cys118-to-arg (C118R) substitution.

Using exome sequencing in a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) identified a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion (NM_001256545.2) in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the patient was homozygous and that both healthy parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that their patients with missense mutations had a milder phenotype than that previously reported in patients with MEGF10 nonsense and frameshift mutations. Minicores were prominent in their patients but had not been noted in the families with null mutations. They suggested that residual MEGF10 function might explain the milder phenotype, although genetic background or other factors could also contribute.

Takayama et al. (2016) studied 2 Japanese patients. One, with the classic early-onset myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress and dysphagia (EMARDD) phenotype, had a novel homozygous frameshift mutation (c.131_132del). The other, with the milder phenotype, had compound heterozygous mutations that included the novel missense mutation C810Y. Takayama et al. (2016) took these findings as evidence of a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy and suggested that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain of MEGF10 may be responsible for the milder phenotype.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed all 20 reported cases of MEGF10 myopathy and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. Patients with at least 1 null mutation developed respiratory dysfunction in the first year of life. Patients with 2 missense mutations developed it at 10 years of age or much later.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that their patient had early-onset myopathy with a progressive course even though he carried a homozygous missense mutation affecting a cysteine in an EGF-like domain. They suggested that classic EMARDD and the mild variant form a continuum rather than 2 separate forms.

### Pathogenesis

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that the C326R and C774R mutations replace conserved cysteines in atypical EGF-like domains and are likely to disrupt disulfide bonds. They hypothesized that the resulting loss of MEGF10 function could cause excessive differentiation of satellite cells, depleting the self-renewing cells needed for muscle regeneration.

Holterman et al. (2007) knocked down Megf10 with siRNA in activated satellite cells on isolated mouse myofibers. Knockdown reduced cell numbers through myogenin activation and precocious differentiation, and it depleted the self-renewing Pax7+/MyoD- population. The authors concluded that Megf10 acts on Notch signaling to regulate the balance between satellite cell proliferation and differentiation.

Croci et al. (2022) proposed that minicores, which mark the later-onset group, may result from a long-standing myofibrillar disease process that starts in infancy. They based this on the nonspecific changes in their patient's first muscle biopsy and the myofibrillar changes with minicores in a later biopsy.

### Diagnosis

Croci et al. (2022) suggested that MEGF10 should be tested in patients with suspected myofibrillar myopathy who have childhood-onset progressive myopathy, scoliosis, contractures and respiratory involvement.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish using 4 splice-blocking morpholinos. The morphants had curled or bent tails, impaired swimming, unhatched eggs, disorganized somites and reduced birefringence. Immunostaining and electron microscopy showed gaps between myofibers and myofibrillar disorganization, consistent with a myopathic process. ⚠︎

In 3 affected sibs from a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) with CMYO10B, Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene.
