### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.1.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Seizures begin in the first months of life and are mainly epileptic spasms, myoclonic seizures, or focal motor seizures, often with a suppression-burst EEG pattern. Affected individuals have severe developmental delay and abnormal muscle tone. Some also have non-neural features, including cleft palate, omphalocele, talipes equinovarus, joint contractures, skeletal abnormalities, and dysmorphic features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with a neonatal-onset syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. In all 11 patients, seizures began in the first 2 months of life, mainly as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. Seven of the 8 patients who had neonatal EEGs showed a suppression-burst or burst attenuation pattern, and the 2 patients first recorded later showed hypsarrhythmia. The authors described increased muscle tone as a core feature, often together with axial hypotonia.

In family A of Chatron et al. (2020), the 2 affected children were born to healthy Algerian first-cousin parents. The older child, a boy, presented to the neuropediatric unit at 5 months of age with developmental regression and loss of visual contact, opisthotonus, and axial hypotonia. He had a cleft soft palate, epileptic spasms, and hypsarrhythmia. At 6 years of age he could not sit unassisted, had spastic tetraparesis with limited extension of the knees and elbows, and had no verbal communication. He also had scoliosis and profound intellectual disability. His brain MRI was normal. His sister also had a cleft soft palate. At 2 days of age she was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit with episodes of eye revulsion, cyanosis, and spasm-like movements, and her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern. At 26 months of age she had severe developmental delay, axial hypotonia, no head control, segmental hypertonia with limited extension of the knees, no visual contact with abnormal eye movements, and no verbal communication.

Family B of Chatron et al. (2020) was a consanguineous Egyptian family with 2 affected sibs. In the girl, prenatal scanning showed an omphalocele containing liver, intestinal atresia, and right talipes equinovarus, and there was polyhydramnios. At birth she had an omphalocele and a cleft soft palate. Seizures began at 2 weeks of age as eye twitches and epileptic spasms. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern that evolved to hypsarrhythmia. She had severe developmental delay and spasticity. Brain imaging showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum, cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, and enlarged lateral ventricles. Her dysmorphic features included hypertelorism, downslanting palpebral fissures, microretrognathia, low-set abnormally shaped ears, and neck webbing. She also had multiple skin hemangiomas, limited range of movement in all joints, camptodactyly, rocker-bottom feet, scoliosis, and a large clitoris with hypoplastic labia. She died at 2 years of age from failure to thrive and respiratory infection. Her similarly affected brother had an omphalocele and bilateral talipes equinovarus at birth. He also had arthrogryposis, failure to thrive, global developmental delay, and dysmorphic features similar to his sister's, but no cleft palate. His seizures also began at 2 weeks of age as eye twitches and epileptic spasms. He died at 4 years of age from pulmonary infection and severe growth retardation.

Family C of Chatron et al. (2020) was a Turkish family with first-cousin parents. The proband, a 12-year-old girl, developed eyelid myoclonia at 2 weeks of age, followed 1 week later by myoclonic seizures of the upper limbs. She went on to have generalized tonic-clonic seizures and refractory epilepsy. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern and photosensitivity. She had profound intellectual disability, no speech, generalized hypotonia in infancy, and tetraparesis with increased muscle tone. She also had a cleft palate, left-sided talipes, conductive hearing loss, and recurrent episodes of fever without signs of infection. Her brain MRI was normal. Her brother had a similar phenotype, with refractory myoclonic, tonic, and tonic-clonic seizures from the first day of life, a suppression-burst EEG, and a cleft palate. His seizures worsened on a ketogenic diet, and he died from refractory seizures at 2 years of age. At that time he had profound intellectual disability and tetraparesis with increased muscle tone.

Family D of Chatron et al. (2020) was an Iranian family with second-cousin parents and a family history of epilepsy and developmental delay. In the male proband, myoclonic seizures were noted within 1 day of birth and later evolved into epileptic spasms; his EEG showed dysrhythmia. He had profound developmental delay and was unable to walk. Imaging showed prominent inner liquor spaces. He initially had reduced muscle tone and later developed spasticity, dystonia, and scoliosis. He had low-set ears, a broad nasal bridge, mild hypertelorism, and a thin upper lip. His sister had epileptic spasms and myoclonic seizures from 7 days of age. Her EEG initially showed burst attenuation and later scattered sharp waves. At 15 months of age she could roll onto her side but could not sit unsupported, and her head circumference was 44 cm (1.2 cm below the 3rd centile). She also had axial hypotonia and mild dystonia. The authors considered a cervical notch seen on her brain MRI to be probably unrelated to GAD1.

Family E of Chatron et al. (2020) was a consanguineous Turkish family. At 2 months of age the male proband was referred for facial dysmorphism, developmental delay, cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, and pes equinovarus. Epileptic spasms were seen on examination and had in retrospect been present since 1 month of age; his EEG showed hypsarrhythmia. His other features included flexion contractures of the elbows and knees, upslanting palpebral fissures, hypertelorism, microretrognathia, low-set ears, cervical and axillary webbing, sparse hair and eyebrows, umbilical hernia, hepatomegaly, hydrocephalus detected in utero, and an atlantoaxial anomaly. On eye examination he had nystagmus, alternating esotropia, and bilateral pale optic discs. His audiogram was normal at birth, but he failed a hearing test at 1 year of age. He also had recurrent aspiration pneumonia, an atrial septal defect, nephrocalcinosis, no head control or eye contact, axial hypotonia, and limb spasticity. His older brother was born at 29 weeks' gestation. He had cleft palate, short neck, narrow thorax, myoclonic seizures, and congenital heart disease (a large patent ductus arteriosus, a small atrial septal defect, and moderate pulmonary hypertension). He also had abdominal distension and pes equinovarus, and he died of sepsis on postnatal day 9.

Family F of Chatron et al. (2020) was a Brazilian family of Caucasian and African ancestry with first-cousin parents. At 7 days of age the proband, a girl, had fast facial twitches with blinking followed by staring. Her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern with multifocal discharges, and she went on to have focal seizures and epileptic spasms. Imaging showed mild cerebral atrophy with ventricular dilation. She had profound developmental delay and intellectual disability, no speech, choreoathetosis, and lower-limb spasticity with brisk reflexes and a Babinski sign. At 6 years of age she had inverted nipples, hirsutism, and severe scoliosis, but no dysmorphic facial features.

Neuray et al. (2020) described a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy in 6 patients from 6 unrelated families. Five of the families were consanguineous. Seizures began at 2 to 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures. The patients had severe developmental delay and reduced muscle tone. Some also had variable non-CNS features, including skeletal abnormalities, dysmorphic features, and cleft palate. Three of the 6 patients became seizure-free, yet all had severe intellectual disability, and the authors suggested that GAD1 defects affect development and cognition independently of seizure control. ⚠︎

In the series of Neuray et al. (2020), the proband of family A was a girl born to consanguineous Persian parents. She had seizure onset at 2 months of age, with focal seizures with impaired awareness and a burst-suppression EEG at onset. At 10 years and 3 months of age she had global developmental delay and moderate intellectual disability. She also had high myopia, dysmorphic facial features with thick eyebrows and protruding ears, clinodactyly, pes planus, scoliosis, and mild hypotonia. Her renal findings were hydronephrosis, nephrocalcinosis, and bilateral kidney stones. Her brain MRI was normal. The proband of family B was a boy born to consanguineous Pakistani parents. His seizures began at 6 months of age and were focal motor seizures with impaired awareness and oral automatisms, as well as bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. His EEG showed multifocal and generalized epileptogenic activity, and MRI showed a prominent ventricular space. At 7 years of age he had delayed milestones and severe intellectual disability. He also had arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, brisk deep tendon reflexes, and stereotypic hand movements. His slight dysmorphic features were a wide mouth, thin upper lip, bitemporal narrowing, and retrognathia. ⚠︎

The proband of family C of Neuray et al. (2020) was an African American boy. His seizures began at 2 months of age as focal seizures with impaired awareness and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, and his epilepsy was refractory. His EEG showed burst suppression at onset and later slowing with multifocal epileptic discharges. At 22 months of age he had no head control, could not sit, and had no speech. He had severe intellectual disability, mild hypotonia, spasticity of the lower extremities, and oropharyngeal dysphagia requiring a nasogastric tube. His brain MRI was normal. The proband of family D was a boy born to consanguineous Sudanese parents. He developed epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia at 2 months of age. At 18 months he had severe developmental delay with poor head control and inability to sit, severe intellectual disability, and severe hypotonia. He also had dysphagia due to a floppy epiglottis, short arms, and diastasis recti. His severe dysmorphic features were infraorbital creases, a severely depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, and prominent nasolabial folds. His brain MRI was normal.

The proband of family E of Neuray et al. (2020) was a girl born to consanguineous Egyptian parents. Seizure onset was at 6 months of age, with bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, refractory epilepsy, and generalized epileptogenic EEG activity. At 4 years of age she was bedridden, with severe delay in all milestones and severe intellectual disability. She had severe hypotonia, hyporeflexia, dysphagia with intermittent dependence on a nasogastric tube, moderate hearing impairment, and dysmorphic facial features. She was the only patient in this series born with a cleft palate, and she also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with shallow acetabula and talipes equinovarus. Brain imaging showed moderate global brain atrophy. A similarly affected sib died without genetic testing. The proband of family F was born to consanguineous Turkish parents. Seizure onset was at 2 months of age, with focal-to-bilateral motor seizures with impaired awareness and hypsarrhythmia. At 3 years of age this child had severe delay in all milestones, could not sit or crawl, and had severe intellectual disability, high myopia, and severe hypotonia. Brain MRI was normal.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported a patient with GAD1 deficiency.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 in the families reported by Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Most affected individuals were born to consanguineous parents and carried homozygous GAD1 mutations. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Chatron et al. (2020) noted that the gnomAD pRec score of 0.934 for GAD1 was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance of loss-of-function variants.

### Molecular Genetics

In 11 patients from 6 consanguineous families with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified homozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. In family A, the 2 affected sibs were homozygous for a splice site mutation, c.1414-1G-C. In cDNA analysis this mutation abolished normal splicing: no normal mRNA product was detected, and there were 3 aberrant isoforms, with skipping of exon 15, retention of 76 bases of intron 14, or deletion of the first 7 bases of exon 15. The 2 affected sibs in family B were homozygous for an in-frame deletion, c.695_697delAGA, which deletes lys232 (K232del). The 2 affected sibs in family C were homozygous for a 5-bp deletion, c.812_816delTTAAG, which causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Affected members of 2 families were homozygous for a nonsense mutation, c.1591C-T, which causes an arg531-to-ter substitution (R531X): the Iranian family D and the Turkish family E. The proband of family F was homozygous for a missense mutation, c.1525G-A, which causes a glu509-to-lys substitution (E509K).

In a cell-free expression system, the GAD67 protein carrying the K232del mutation was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. Chatron et al. (2020) noted that this result did not exclude a splicing effect predicted in silico. They considered the variant pathogenic because family B reproduced the cleft palate and omphalocele of the knockout mouse, although loss of function was not formally shown. In structural modeling, the E509K mutation affected a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. It was predicted to disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389, so that the mutant residue would no longer fit into the domain core. Four of the 6 families carried homozygous truncating variants, which the authors considered supportive of a complete loss-of-function mechanism. ⚠︎

In 6 patients from 6 unrelated families with DEE89, Neuray et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify biallelic mutations in the GAD1 gene. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation consistent with recessive inheritance. Five probands carried homozygous mutations: N564S (c.1691A-G) in a Persian family, F324C (c.971T-G) in a Pakistani family, T347M (c.1040C-T) in a Sudanese family, Y29X (c.87C-G) in an Egyptian family, and c.568delC (Gln190SerfsTer11) in a Turkish family. The African American proband of family C was compound heterozygous for R531X (c.1591C-T) and c.670delC (Leu224SerfsTer5). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) observed phenotypic variability between 2 families carrying the same R531X mutation. Pes equinovarus, joint contractures, and clefting were present in family E but absent in family D. They suggested that genetic background or environmental factors that modulate movement in utero might explain the difference.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) proposed that the cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures in GAD1-related disease result from reduced fetal movements. They suggested that these non-neural features are probably secondary to severe CNS involvement, as in Gad1-null mice.

Neuray et al. (2020) noted that 4 of the variants they identified affected the PLP-binding domain and that 4 were null alleles. In silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability for 2 of the missense variants and disrupted PLP binding for another. Together, these findings supported a loss-of-function mechanism.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a good seizure response to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 treated patients. They cautioned that the response may reflect seizure type, since patients with epileptic spasms seemed to benefit most. They called for validation of vigabatrin as a targeted treatment and for early genomic sequencing in patients with early-onset seizures. In 1 of their patients, seizures worsened on a ketogenic diet.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported treating a patient with GAD1 deficiency with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet, which they described as a rational therapy.

### Population Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) found the R531X mutation in both an Iranian family and a Turkish family. Given the relative geographic proximity of the 2 families, they suggested that the variant may lie on a common haplotype.

Neuray et al. (2020) noted that, according to gnomAD, GAD1 is moderately intolerant to missense variants (Z-score 2.32) and to predicted loss-of-function variants (9 observed vs 33.1 expected).

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking Gad67. The mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn mutant mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% of normal and GABA content to 7% of normal, but the brain showed no discernible defects. The authors stated that this was the first demonstration of a role for GAD67-derived GABA in the development of nonneural tissue. By contrast, their Gad65-null mice had normal brain GABA content and normal behavior, apart from a slight increase in susceptibility to seizures. ⚠︎

### History

Neuray et al. (2020) questioned the pathogenicity of a homozygous GAD1 S12C variant previously reported in spastic cerebral palsy by Lynex et al. (2004). They pointed out that pathogenicity predictions for the variant conflict and that other candidate genes in the linked 2q24-q31.1 region had not been investigated. They proposed that pathogenic GAD1 variants cause a distinctive neurodevelopmental disorder rather than spastic cerebral palsy.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 sibs with DEE89, born to Algerian first-cousin parents (family A), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1414-1G-C splice site mutation in the GAD1 gene. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Both sibs had a cleft soft palate and neonatal or infantile epileptic spasms, and both had severe or profound developmental impairment. In cDNA analysis no normal mRNA product was detected, and there were 3 aberrant isoforms, with skipping of exon 15, retention of 76 bases of intron 14, or deletion of the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delaga; p.K232del]

In 2 sibs with DEE89 from a consanguineous Egyptian family (family B), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous in-frame 3-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene, c.695_697delAGA, which deletes lys232 (K232del). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Both sibs had an omphalocele, talipes equinovarus, and epileptic spasms from 2 weeks of age. The girl also had a cleft soft palate, but her brother did not. In a cell-free expression system the mutant protein was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this did not exclude a splicing effect predicted in silico. They considered the variant pathogenic because the family reproduced the knockout mouse phenotype of cleft palate and omphalocele, although loss of function was not formally demonstrated.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, NT812 [c.812_816delttaag; p.V271D]

In 2 sibs with DEE89, born to Turkish first-cousin parents (family C), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 5-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene, c.812_816delTTAAG, which causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. Both sibs had refractory myoclonic and tonic-clonic seizures beginning in the neonatal period, a suppression-burst EEG, cleft palate, profound intellectual disability, and tetraparesis with increased muscle tone. The brother died from refractory seizures at 2 years of age.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; g.171715383C>T; p.R531*]

In affected members of 2 consanguineous families with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, which causes an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution. Family D was Iranian, and family E was Turkish. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. In family D, 2 sibs had neonatal-onset myoclonic seizures and epileptic spasms with profound developmental delay but no clefting or limb contractures. In family E, the proband had epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia from 1 month of age, cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, and multiple dysmorphic and other congenital anomalies. His older brother had cleft palate, myoclonic seizures, congenital heart disease, and pes equinovarus, and died on postnatal day 9. The authors suggested that, given the relative geographic proximity of the 2 families, the variant may lie on a common haplotype.

In an African American boy with DEE89 (family C), Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: R531X and a 1-bp deletion, c.670delC, which causes a frameshift (Leu224SerfsTer5). His mother carried the c.670delC mutation, and his father was not available for testing. The boy had refractory focal and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures from 2 months of age, a burst-suppression EEG at onset, and severe intellectual disability.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.E509K]

In a Brazilian girl with DEE89, born to first-cousin parents (family F), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, which causes a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disease. The mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that it would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389 and that the mutant residue would no longer fit into the domain core. The patient had seizures from 7 days of age, a suppression-burst EEG, epileptic spasms, and profound developmental delay, but no dysmorphic facial features.

**.0006 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ASN564SER [c.1691A>G; g.171716298A>G; p.N564S]
