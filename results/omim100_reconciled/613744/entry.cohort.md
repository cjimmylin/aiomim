### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.2.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder. Patients have microcephaly and infantile hypotonia that progresses to spastic paraplegia or spastic tetraplegia, severe intellectual disability, and absent or very limited speech. Most never walk independently. Other features include stereotypic laughter, drooling, lack of sphincter control, and, in some patients, seizures and cerebral and cerebellar atrophy. The disorder belongs to a group of conditions caused by deficiency of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs, a woman (IV-4) aged 23 years and a man (IV-5) aged 22 years, from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. The sister had microcephaly (-3 SD) and hypotonia at birth. By the first year of life, the hypotonia had progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia. She never developed speech or independent walking. She had been wheelchair-bound since 5 years of age, which was also when generalized tonic-clonic seizures began. She had profound intellectual disability, drooling, stereotypic laughter, a Babinski sign, and lack of sphincter control. Her dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face, a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum, a full everted upper vermilion, a prominent antihelix, and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was most pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing. Eye evaluation and hearing were normal.

Her brother, also reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), had microcephaly (-3 SD), neonatal hypotonia, and developmental delay. He acquired some dysarthric words but never normal speech. He walked with support, but increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic seizures at age 15. On examination at 22 years, he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia, hyperreflexia, and a Babinski sign. He also had outbursts of stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, severe intellectual disability, and lack of sphincter control. Brain imaging showed moderate ventriculomegaly and prominent cisterns. Hearing was normal.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families with mutations in AP4 subunit genes; 1 of the families carried an AP4E1 mutation. The patients had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, a shy character, stereotypic laughter, and muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia. Other features were microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls (P1 and P2), born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, who were 3 years old at last report. Both had intellectual disability, microcephaly (head circumference 46 cm (-2 SD) in P1 and 45.5 cm (-2.5 SD) in P2), and early infantile hypotonia. They developed spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities. Neither twin ever walked or spoke. Both had absent sphincter control, stereotypic laughter, drooling, short stature, and low body weight. They shared a discreet but remarkable facial gestalt with a prominent bulbous nose, a wide mouth, and coarse features. CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis and cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. Both twins had astigmatism. P1 also had myopia and left-sided conductive hearing loss. Neither had seizures, hyperreflexia, a Babinski sign, or contractures. At 9 months of age, both developed unilaterally enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph nodes (BCG lymphadenitis). Lymphocyte subsets, immunoglobulins, complement, the IL-12/IFN-gamma circuit, and oxidative burst were all normal, and no immunologic abnormality was found to explain the mycobacterial disease. The authors proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) restudied these Moroccan twin sisters at 8 years of age. They noted that the twins had hereditary spastic paraplegia due to the homozygous AP4E1 mutation and had suffered neonatal asphyxia at delivery. The twins also had Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease, with BCG disease, enlarged inflamed axillary lymph nodes, and acid-fast bacilli on lymph node biopsy. Kong et al. (2018) attributed the mycobacterial disease to a homozygous SPPL2A mutation rather than to the AP4E1 mutation. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

In the consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), the mother was a heterozygous carrier of the deletion. SNRPN methylation analysis showed that the probands had inherited chromosome 15 from both parents, so the father was inferred to be a carrier. These findings were consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) described the disorder in consanguineous families as autosomal recessive. In the consanguineous Moroccan family reported by Kong et al. (2013), both affected twins were homozygous for the AP4E1 mutation, both parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was wildtype.

### Heterogeneity

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) found mutations in 3 different AP4 subunit genes (AP4S1, AP4B1, and AP4E1) in 3 consanguineous families. They concluded that deficiency of the AP4 complex causes a clinically recognizable syndrome, supporting a crucial role for AP4-mediated trafficking in brain development and function.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous deletion of chromosome 15q21.2 that removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes (V1). The deletion was initially estimated at 139 kb (minimum) to 222 kb (maximum). High-resolution arrays defined it as 192 kb, and FISH confirmed it. The mother and an older sister were heterozygous carriers, and a younger sib did not carry the deletion. The authors could not completely exclude a contribution from loss of SPPL2A, but they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main disease mechanism.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families with intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia. They used autozygosity mapping combined with either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing, and found 1 mutation in each of 3 genes encoding AP4 subunits. In 1 family, the mutation was a splice site mutation in AP4E1 (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA; V4) that resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20). The other 2 families had a nonsense mutation in AP4S1 and a frameshift mutation in AP4B1.

Kong et al. (2013) studied Moroccan identical twins, born to first-cousin parents, who had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia and BCG lymphadenitis. Using whole-exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in AP4E1 (R1105X; V5). The parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib was wildtype. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or 69 Moroccan controls. Patient EBV-B cells had normal levels of AP4E1 mRNA. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta were also reduced. In patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells, the amount of assembled AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower than in controls. The AP-4 binding partner tepsin was not detectable in patient fibroblasts.

In the same Moroccan twins, Kong et al. (2018) confirmed by Sanger sequencing that both also carried a homozygous splice donor site mutation in intron 6 of SPPL2A (c.733+1G-A). Its segregation in the family was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The authors attributed the twins' mycobacterial disease to this SPPL2A mutation and their hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that their family with the AP4E1 deletion closely resembled a family with a homozygous AP4M1 mutation. They proposed that loss of any one of the 4 AP-4 subunits disrupts the whole complex, causing a distinct autosomal recessive 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.'

Kong et al. (2013) found that the AP4E1 R1105X mutation did not change AP4E1 mRNA levels, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable. Levels of other AP-4 subunits were reduced, and the assembled AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower than in controls. The authors suggested that the mutant transcript might escape nonsense-mediated decay and produce a truncated protein.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) studied 2 sibs with SPG51 from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. In both, they identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion of chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171; hg18) that removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The deletion was defined using high-resolution arrays and confirmed by FISH. The mother and an older sister were heterozygous carriers, and the father was inferred to be a carrier. The sibs had microcephaly, neonatal hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraplegia, severe to profound intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, seizures, and stereotypic laughter. The authors proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main disease mechanism, although they could not completely exclude a contribution from loss of SPPL2A.

**.0002 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 4-BP DEL, NT542+1 [c.542+1_542+4delgtaa; p.E181G]

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) used autozygosity mapping and sequencing to study 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia. In 1 of the families, they identified a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The mutation resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20).

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105*]

Kong et al. (2013) studied identical twin girls with SPG51 and BCG lymphadenitis, born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. Using whole-exome sequencing, they identified a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene. The parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib was wildtype. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or 69 Moroccan controls. AP4E1 mRNA levels were not changed in patient cells, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable. The amount of assembled AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower than in controls.

Kong et al. (2018) restudied the same twins at 8 years of age. They attributed the twins' hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation and their mycobacterial disease to a coexisting homozygous SPPL2A splice site mutation. ⚠︎
