### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is a severe neurologic disorder caused by deficiency of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4). Neonatal or early infantile hypotonia progresses to spasticity, either spastic paraplegia or spastic tetraplegia. Affected individuals have microcephaly, severe to profound intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech and inability to walk, and many show stereotypic laughter, drooling and lack of sphincter control. Some patients have seizures, and brain imaging may show ventriculomegaly and cortical and cerebellar atrophy. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs, a female (IV-4) and a male (IV-5), from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. The female, examined at 23 years of age, had microcephaly (-3 SD) and hypotonia at birth. By the first year of life these had progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia, and she also had a Babinski sign. She never developed speech or independent walking. She had used a wheelchair since age 5 years, when generalized tonic-clonic seizures began. She had profound intellectual disability, drooling, stereotypic laughter and lack of sphincter control. Dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face, a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum, an everted full upper vermilion, a prominent antihelix and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was more pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing. Eye evaluation and hearing were normal.

Her brother, examined at 22 years of age, had microcephaly (-3 SD), neonatal hypotonia and developmental delay. He acquired some dysarthric words but never normal speech. He could walk with support, but increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. On examination he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia, hyperreflexia and a Babinski sign, outbursts of stereotypic laughter, drooling and jerky nystagmus. He also had severe intellectual disability and lack of sphincter control. Brain imaging showed moderate ventriculomegaly and prominent cisterns. Hearing was normal (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011).

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families with AP-4 deficiency. Each family carried a mutation in a different AP-4 subunit gene, one of which was AP4E1. The patients had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, a shy character, stereotypic laughter, and muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia. Other features were microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls (P1 and P2) born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. When examined at 3 years of age, both had intellectual disability, microcephaly (head circumference 46 cm (-2 SD) and 45.5 cm (-2.5 SD), respectively), early infantile hypotonia and spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities. Neither had ever walked or spoken, and both lacked sphincter control. Both twins showed stereotypic laughter, drooling, short stature and low body weight. They shared a discreet but remarkable facial gestalt with a prominent bulbous nose, a wide mouth and coarse features. CT scans showed atrophy of the inferior vermis and cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. Both twins had astigmatism. P1 also had myopia and left-sided conductive hearing loss, with normal hearing on the right. Neither twin had seizures, hyperreflexia, a Babinski sign or contractures. At 9 months of age, both twins developed unilaterally enlarged, inflammatory axillary lymph nodes, diagnosed as BCG lymphadenitis. Kong et al. (2013) found no immunologic abnormality to explain the mycobacterial disease: lymphocyte subsets, immunoglobulins, complement, the IL-12/IFN-gamma circuit and oxidative burst were all normal. They proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) restudied the same Moroccan twins at 8 years of age. Neonatal asphyxia had been noted at delivery. Kong et al. (2018) attributed the twins' hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 mutation. In contrast to the earlier proposal, they attributed the BCG disease, which involved enlarged inflammatory axillary lymph nodes with acid-fast bacilli on biopsy, to Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease caused by a separate homozygous mutation in the SPPL2A gene. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 in the family reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Both affected sibs were homozygous for the deletion, and FISH showed that the mother was a heterozygous carrier. SNRPN methylation analysis showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15 in the probands, so the father was inferred to be a carrier. In the consanguineous Moroccan family reported by Kong et al. (2013), both affected twins were homozygous for the mutation and both parents were heterozygous.

### Heterogeneity

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) found mutations in 3 different genes encoding AP-4 subunits (AP4S1, AP4B1 and AP4E1) in 3 consanguineous families. They concluded that deficiency of the AP-4 complex causes a clinically recognizable syndrome, and that this supports a crucial role for AP-4-mediated trafficking in brain development and function.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous deletion of chromosome 15q21.2 that removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The deletion was first estimated at 139 kb (minimum) to 222 kb (maximum). High-resolution arrays then defined it as 192 kb, and FISH confirmed it. The mother and an older sister were heterozygous carriers, and a younger sib did not carry the deletion. Although they could not completely exclude a contribution from deletion of SPPL2A, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main mechanism.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families with AP-4 deficiency, using autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. They identified one mutation in each of 3 genes encoding AP-4 subunits. In AP4E1, the mutation was a splice site change (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA) that deleted r.421_542 and caused a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20).

In Moroccan identical twins with complex hereditary spastic paraplegia and BCG lymphadenitis, born to first-cousin parents, Kong et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X). The parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib did not carry the mutation. The mutation was absent from 1,050 CEPH controls and 69 Moroccan controls.

In the same twins, Kong et al. (2018) confirmed by Sanger sequencing that both girls also carried a homozygous splice donor site mutation in intron 6 of the SPPL2A gene (c.733+1G-A). Within the consanguineous kindred, this mutation segregated in a pattern consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The authors attributed the twins' mycobacterial disease to the SPPL2A mutation and their hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that their family with AP4E1 deletion closely resembled a previously reported family with a homozygous AP4M1 mutation. They proposed that loss of any one of the 4 AP-4 subunits disrupts the whole complex, causing a distinct autosomal recessive 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.'

In EBV-B cells from the twins, Kong et al. (2013) found that the R1105X mutation did not change AP4E1 mRNA levels. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta were also reduced. In patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells, the amount of assembled AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower than in controls. The AP-4 binding partner tepsin could not be detected in patient fibroblasts. Kong et al. (2013) suggested that the mutant transcript may escape nonsense-mediated decay and produce a truncated protein.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with SPG51, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion of chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48,835,480-49,028,171; hg18). The deletion removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. It was defined with high-resolution arrays and confirmed by FISH. The mother and an older sister were heterozygous, and the father was inferred to be a carrier. The patients had microcephaly, neonatal hypotonia that progressed to spastic tetraplegia, severe to profound intellectual disability, absent or minimal speech, seizures and stereotypic laughter. Although the authors could not completely exclude a contribution from loss of SPPL2A, they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main mechanism.

**.0002 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 4-BP DEL, 542+1GTAA [c.542+1_542+4delgtaa; p.E181G]

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 3 consanguineous families with AP-4 deficiency, a syndrome of severe intellectual disability, absent speech, stereotypic laughter, and hypotonia progressing to spastic paraplegia. Using autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing, they identified a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). This mutation resulted in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). The other 2 families carried mutations in different AP-4 subunit genes.

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105*]

In Moroccan identical twin girls with SPG51 and BCG lymphadenitis, born to first-cousin parents, Kong et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene. The parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was wildtype. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells, AP4E1 mRNA levels were unchanged, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable and levels of AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta were reduced. Assembled AP-4 complex in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells was more than 95% lower than in controls, and tepsin could not be detected in patient fibroblasts.

Kong et al. (2018) found that the same twins also carried a homozygous splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene. They attributed the twins' mycobacterial disease to the SPPL2A mutation and their hereditary spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.
