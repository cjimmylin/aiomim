## MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME; MTCHRS

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene (612659) on chromosome 6q22.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder. Its features are neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, intestinal atresia (duodenal and/or jejunal), and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder. Many affected infants have died in the first year of life (Mitchell et al., 2004). Some patients with RFX6 mutations have a milder course, with diabetes beginning in childhood rather than the neonatal period, no liver disease, and longer survival (Sansbury et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Smith et al. (2010) proposed the name Mitchell-Riley syndrome after the 2 clinicians who first described the disorder. They distinguished it from Martinez-Frias syndrome, whose original patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias without diabetes; in 1 of those patients, no RFX6 mutation was found. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Martinez-Frias et al. (1992) studied 2 sibs born to consanguineous parents. Both had a multiple congenital anomaly pattern of low birthweight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. The authors considered this a previously unreported autosomal recessive syndrome. A computerized search of the Spanish Collaborative Study of Congenital Malformations (ECEMC) identified 3 other unrelated infants with intestinal atresias, hypospadias, and low birth weight. The authors suggested that these infants might represent a milder expression of the same syndrome.

Anneren et al. (1998) described a lethal syndrome of intrauterine growth retardation, intra- and extrahepatic biliary atresia, and esophageal and duodenal atresia. They considered it autosomal recessive.

Gentile and Fiorente (1999) reported a patient with esophageal, duodenal, rectoanal, and biliary atresia, intestinal malrotation, malformed/hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. They presented this as further evidence of a new distinct syndrome. ⚠︎

Mitchell et al. (2004) reported 5 infants: 2 sib pairs from 2 families and 1 isolated case. One sib pair was born to consanguineous parents. The infants presented with neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia. Four of the children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. One patient, with a milder form, was surviving free of insulin. On pancreatic immunohistochemistry, there were a few scattered chromogranin-A-positive cell clusters but a complete absence of insulin, glucagon, and somatostatin.

Galan-Gomez et al. (2007) described a female infant born to consanguineous parents. She had low birth weight, intrauterine growth retardation, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and intestinal malrotation, and she died 60 days after birth. Her chromosomes were normal (46,XX) at the 550- to 600-band level. The authors considered the case further evidence of the syndrome described by Martinez-Frias et al. (1992).

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient born to consanguineous parents who had neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis. This was the first reported case of neonatal diabetes in the syndrome without a demonstrable structural pancreatic abnormality, which the authors took to show that a deficit in pancreatic function is involved. They proposed that previously reported cases with overlapping features, such as malrotation, biliary atresia, and pancreatic hypoplasia (e.g., those described by Martinez-Frias), may represent variations of the same syndrome.

Martinovici et al. (2010) reported a newborn from consanguineous parents who had multiple congenital anomalies and neonatal hemochromatosis. The anomalies were intrauterine growth retardation, intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus, which fit the diagnosis of Martinez-Frias syndrome. The authors suggested that neonatal hemochromatosis may be part of the syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) described infants with neonatal diabetes and small bowel obstruction due to bowel atresia. Pancreatic size was somewhat reduced, but there was no exocrine enzyme deficiency. The infants also had a hypoplastic gallbladder and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement. Two cases came to autopsy, including their proband 1, a girl born to first cousins who died in the first few months of life. Both showed a normal-appearing exocrine pancreas containing clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells, but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. Of their probands with RFX6 mutations, 3 died in the first few months of life. Proband 2 was alive at 4.5 years. Proband 3 was alive at 9 years and was intermittently off insulin.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 double first cousins of Turkish origin from a single family, both aged 9 years at the time of the report. The first was a girl born at 32 weeks' gestation with a birth weight of 1.65 kg (42nd centile). She had duodenal atresia, jejunal web, and Meckel diverticulum, and her gallbladder was absent. Hyperglycemia was noted at 2 years of age, and she presented with diabetic ketoacidosis at 3 years. Insulin and C-peptide levels were low, and islet autoantibodies were absent. She had gastrointestinal bleeding, and gastric heterotopia was found in the small bowel. Her liver function tests were normal. The second patient was a boy born at 34 weeks' gestation with a birth weight of 1.70 kg (7th centile). He had duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation. Asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia was noted at 5 years, and diabetes was diagnosed at 6 years when he presented with hyperglycemia (16.0 mmol/l). He had no islet autoantibodies. His past history included chronic iron deficiency anemia. No structural abnormality of the hepatobiliary tract was identified, and his liver function tests had always been normal. Sansbury et al. (2015) also reviewed 8 previously reported cases. All had duodenal atresia, 7 of 8 had gallbladder agenesis, and 7 of 8 had intrahepatic cholestasis. Five of the 8 children had died, all within the first year of life.

### Inheritance

Martinez-Frias et al. (1992) suggested autosomal recessive inheritance because the syndrome occurred in 2 sibs born to consanguineous parents. Mitchell et al. (2004) concluded that the combination of neonatal diabetes, pancreatic hypoplasia, intestinal atresia, and gallbladder hypoplasia probably represents a new autosomal recessive syndrome. They based this on affected sib pairs, one of them born to consanguineous parents. Chappell et al. (2008) and Galan-Gomez et al. (2007) also considered the disorder autosomal recessive. ⚠︎

Smith et al. (2010) found that affected individuals were homozygous or compound heterozygous for RFX6 mutations inherited from carrier parents, which confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. In the family studied by Sansbury et al. (2015), heterozygous carriers of RFX6 mutations, including several relatives with adult-onset diabetes, did not differ from noncarriers in C-peptide or HbA1c levels. The authors concluded that their data did not support heterozygosity as a risk factor for diabetes. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) performed homozygosity mapping in 2 probands, who were the offspring of first and second cousins, respectively. They identified 3 shared regions of homozygosity totaling 24 Mb, at 2p15-p16, 6q21-q22, and 6q23. Of the 194 genes in these regions, only RFX6, located in the largest segment at 6q21-q22, had pancreas-enriched expression.

### Molecular Genetics

In 1 affected infant from the consanguineous family they studied, Mitchell et al. (2004) found no duplication or uniparental isodisomy of PLAGL1 at 6q24 and no contiguous gene deletion involving the glucokinase gene. They also found no mutation in the coding sequences or splice sites of PDX1.

In their patient, Chappell et al. (2008) found no mutations in genes with a recognized role in monogenic diabetes, including KCNJ11, ABCC8, GCK, IPF1, HNF1B, NEUROD1, and TCF7L2. They also found no mutation in the candidate gene HNF6, which is involved in hepatobiliary and pancreatic development.

Smith et al. (2010) used targeted capture and deep sequencing of exons in the shared homozygous regions in proband 2, the offspring of second cousins. The only nonsynonymous novel variant they found was a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) substitution in the RFX6 gene. They then sequenced RFX6 directly and identified mutations in 5 of 6 probands. Proband 1 was homozygous for loss of the splice donor site in intron 2 (IVS2+2T-C). Proband 3 was compound heterozygous for loss of the splice donor site in intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G) and disruption of the splice acceptor site in intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G). Proband 4 was homozygous for an out-of-frame deletion in exon 7. Proband 5 was homozygous for an arg181-to-gln (R181Q) substitution at a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain. All mutations were inherited from carrier parents. Homozygous severe mutations were associated with death in early infancy, whereas milder genotypes were associated with survival. In functional studies, R181Q completely abrogated DNA binding, whereas S217P only modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation. In autopsy pancreas from proband 1, properly spliced RFX6 transcript was not detected. The authors found no RFX6 mutation in proband 6. Because DNA from this proband was not available, they sequenced both parents and found no point mutation in RFX6 or NEUROG3, and long-range PCR showed no deletion of RFX6. They could not rule out a de novo mutation but considered this case most likely a phenocopy. ⚠︎

In the 2 Turkish double first cousins with childhood-onset diabetes and intestinal atresia, Sansbury et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene: c.2176C-T (R726X) in exon 17 and c.2596C-T (R866X) in exon 18. Both fathers were heterozygous for R726X, and both mothers were heterozygous for R866X. The R866X mutation lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary and is predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay. It would produce a truncated protein that keeps the full DNA-binding domain and possibly some function. R726X is predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay. The authors suggested that partial retention of function by the R866X protein might explain the milder phenotype and later onset of diabetes.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) found that homozygous severe RFX6 mutations were associated with death in early infancy, whereas milder genotypes were associated with survival. ⚠︎

Sansbury et al. (2015) reviewed 8 previously reported molecularly confirmed cases of Mitchell-Riley syndrome. In those cases, the mutations either disrupted splicing or the reading frame in exons 2 to 8 or were missense changes clustered at the DNA-binding domain, and diabetes began within the first 2 weeks of life. In contrast, their 2 patients had 3-prime truncating mutations, diabetes beginning in childhood, no liver disease, and longer survival.

### Pathogenesis

Galan-Gomez et al. (2007) suggested that the syndrome most probably has a blastogenetic origin, mainly affecting the midline developmental duodenal-biliary-pancreatic junction.

### Diagnosis

Sansbury et al. (2015) recommended considering RFX6 genetic testing in patients with intestinal atresias even when neonatal diabetes is absent, particularly when autosomal recessive inheritance is suspected.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) found that Rfx6-null mice were born at mendelian ratios but failed to feed. They had gross bowel distension due to small bowel obstruction and died within 2 days of birth. Some had a reduced pancreas size. The mutant pancreata contained many endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin, whereas pancreatic polypeptide-expressing cells were increased.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 1, a girl with Mitchell-Riley syndrome born to first cousins. She was homozygous for a T-to-C transition at position +2 of intron 2 of the RFX6 gene (IVS2+2T-C), which causes loss of the splice donor site. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. The patient had neonatal diabetes and died in the first few months of life. At autopsy, the exocrine pancreas appeared normal and contained chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. Properly spliced RFX6 transcript was not detected in her autopsy pancreas.

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [p.S217P]

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 2, a child with Mitchell-Riley syndrome who was the offspring of second cousins. By targeted capture and deep sequencing of the shared homozygous regions, they identified a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) substitution in the RFX6 gene, and direct sequencing confirmed it. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. The child was alive at 4.5 years of age. In functional studies, S217P only modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation.

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 3, a patient with Mitchell-Riley syndrome who was alive at 9 years of age and intermittently off insulin. The patient was compound heterozygous for 2 mutations in the RFX6 gene, both inherited from carrier parents. One was a T-to-G transversion at position +2 of intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G), which causes loss of the splice donor site. The other was an A-to-G transition at position -12 of intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G), which disrupts the splice acceptor site.

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.R726*]

Sansbury et al. (2015) studied 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome. Both had duodenal atresia and developed diabetes in childhood, at 3 and 6 years of age. They were compound heterozygous for 2 nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene. One was a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution. The other was a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution. Both fathers were heterozygous for R726X, and both mothers were heterozygous for R866X. The R726X mutation is predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay.
