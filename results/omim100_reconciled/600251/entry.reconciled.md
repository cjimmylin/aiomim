## FACIAL CLEFTING, OBLIQUE, 1; OBLFC1

*Alternative titles; symbols*

OCULOMAXILLOFACIAL DYSPLASIA WITH OBLIQUE FACIAL CLEFTS

### TEXT

This entry describes oblique facial clefting-1 (OBLFC1). These are rare facial clefts that include types 2 through 6 of Tessier's classification.

### Description

Oblique facial clefts are rare and include types 2 through 6 of Tessier's classification (Richieri-Costa and Gorlin, 1994). Saadi et al. (2011) described oblique facial clefts as a unique class of human facial malformations.

### Nomenclature

Tessier (1976) proposed a new anatomical classification of facial, craniofacial, and laterofacial clefts based on his own observations. The classification used the orbit as the main structure of reference and distinguished 15 locations for clefts, and Tessier described in detail the course of each cleft through soft tissue and bone. He also discussed combinations of several cleft types and associated malformations. He suggested that the new classification did away with confusing older terminology and made it easier to record malformations and plan treatment.

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (1988) reported an 8-month-old girl born with symmetric bilateral oblique facial clefts and a calcaneovarus foot deformity. CT scan of the head showed severe bilateral ocular hypoplasia and normal brain parenchyma.

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) reported 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts and a strikingly similar facial appearance. Three of the 4 patients had consanguineous parents. The authors suggested that the pattern of facial involvement, the consanguinity, and the overall clinical picture pointed to a unique dysmorphogenetic process. They proposed that in some cases this could represent an oculomaxillofacial dysostosis.

### Cytogenetics

In the girl with bilateral oblique facial clefts reported by Dasouki et al. (1988), a peripheral blood karyotype showed a de novo balanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 22. The authors stated that they could not rule out a submicroscopic imbalance resulting from the translocation. They recommended chromosome studies in children with such rare malformations.

### Molecular Genetics

Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in the SPECC1L gene are pathogenic for oblique facial clefts. In mammalian cells, SPECC1L colocalized with both tubulin and actin. SPECC1L deficiency led to defective reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and to abnormal cell adhesion and migration. The authors concluded that SPECC1L functions in actin-cytoskeleton reorganization and is required for proper facial morphogenesis.

### Diagnosis

Dasouki et al. (1988) suggested that the pattern of anomalies would help to distinguish oblique facial clefts from amniotic band disruption.

### Animal Model

Saadi et al. (2011) found that during mouse embryogenesis, Specc1l is expressed in cell populations of the developing facial primordia, which proliferate and fuse to form the face. Knockdown of a SPECC1L homolog in zebrafish produced a faceless phenotype with loss of jaw and facial structures. Knockdown of the SPECC1L homolog in Drosophila phenocopied mutants in the integrin signaling pathway, which show defects in cell migration and adhesion.
