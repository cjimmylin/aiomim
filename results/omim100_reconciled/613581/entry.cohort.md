### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-56 (RP56) is caused by homozygous mutation in the IMPG2 gene (607056) on chromosome 3q12.

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-56 is an autosomal recessive retinal dystrophy caused by mutation in the IMPG2 gene, which encodes interphotoreceptor matrix proteoglycan-2. Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) reported that most affected individuals had early-onset retinitis pigmentosa with progressive visual field loss and deterioration of visual acuity.

### Clinical Features

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) studied 2 families with retinitis pigmentosa, each with 3 affected sibs: one family was of Iraqi Jewish ancestry and the other was Dutch. They also studied 10 additional index cases of Dutch, Israeli, Italian, and Pakistani origin. Most patients with IMPG2 mutations had an early-onset form of retinitis pigmentosa with progressive visual field loss and deterioration of visual acuity. One patient, who carried a missense mutation, was instead diagnosed with maculopathy.

### Inheritance

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) established autosomal recessive inheritance of RP56. In 2 families, each with 3 affected sibs, homozygous IMPG2 mutations cosegregated with the disease. Further patients carried homozygous IMPG2 mutations within homozygous regions that encompassed the gene. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) used autozygosity mapping to identify 2 families with retinitis pigmentosa, one Iraqi Jewish and one Dutch, each with 3 affected sibs. The affected individuals shared large overlapping homozygous regions on chromosome 3 that contained the IMPG2 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) sequenced the IMPG2 gene in the index cases of 2 families with autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa, each with 3 affected sibs. They found homozygous mutations that cosegregated with the disease in each family. The 3 affected sibs of Iraqi Jewish ancestry carried a homozygous nonsense mutation. The 3 affected sibs in the Dutch family carried a homozygous 1.8-kb genomic deletion that removes exon 9 and results in the absence of 7 amino acids in a conserved SEA domain of the IMPG2 protein. In transiently transfected COS-1 cells, a construct expressing the wildtype IMPG2 SEA domain was targeted to the plasma membrane. In contrast, the mutant construct lacking the 7 amino acids appeared to be retained in the endoplasmic reticulum.

Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010) also analyzed 10 additional index cases of Dutch, Israeli, Italian, and Pakistani origin who had homozygous regions encompassing IMPG2. They found 5 additional mutations: 4 nonsense mutations and 1 missense mutation affecting a highly conserved phenylalanine residue. The patient with the missense mutation was diagnosed with maculopathy.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Among the patients with IMPG2 mutations studied by Bandah-Rozenfeld et al. (2010), most had early-onset retinitis pigmentosa with progressive visual field loss and deterioration of visual acuity. The patient who carried a missense mutation affecting a highly conserved phenylalanine residue was diagnosed with maculopathy instead.

Meunier et al. (2014) screened 49 families with vitelliform macular dystrophy who did not carry a BEST1 or PRPH2 mutation. In 1 family, 2 patients with autosomal dominant adult-onset vitelliform dystrophy carried a heterozygous c.3230G-T mutation in the IMPG2 gene, resulting in a cys1077-to-phe (C1077F) substitution. ⚠︎
