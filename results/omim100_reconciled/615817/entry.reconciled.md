### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the WASHC4 gene (615748) on chromosome 12q23.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is a disorder of intellectual disability. It was first reported as nonsyndromic intellectual disability. Later reports described motor problems, including clumsiness, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and dysarthria, and syndromic presentations with dysmorphic features, macrocephaly or microcephaly, skeletal anomalies, and skeletal muscle involvement. Assoum et al. (2020) concluded that the phenotype is heterogeneous. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic intellectual disability.

Assoum et al. (2020) reported 3 patients with syndromic intellectual disability. Two sisters, aged 4 and 5.5 years, both had learning disabilities, macrocephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, and subependymal heterotopic nodules. The younger sister also had congenital absence of the right internal carotid artery and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. The third patient was 34 years old and had mild intellectual disability, short stature, and microcephaly. Assoum et al. (2020) concluded that the gene is responsible for a heterogeneous autosomal recessive intellectual disability phenotype and that more case descriptions would be needed to refine it.

Courtland et al. (2021) retrospectively analyzed clinical findings from 7 children who were homozygous for the WASHC4 P1019R mutation. These were the patients originally reported by Ropers et al. (2011), and their data had been obtained in 2010 at the Royal Hospital in Muscat, Oman. Developmental delay or learning difficulties were the main reason for medical evaluation, but all patients also had motor symptoms (mean age, 10.4 years). On clinical examination their movements were described as 'clumsy,' with notable fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. The children were by then an average of 21 years old, and recent communication with their parents revealed no notable worsening of symptoms.

Gangfuß et al. (2022) reported 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype defined by dysmorphisms, intellectual disability, profound developmental disorder, and skeletal muscle involvement. The authors stated that this expanded the clinical spectrum of the disorder and showed skeletal muscle involvement in patients for the first time.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MRT43 is autosomal recessive. Ropers et al. (2011) found a homozygous mutation in affected members of a consanguineous family. Assoum et al. (2020) found compound heterozygous mutations in which each variant had been inherited from a different parent.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability, Ropers et al. (2011) combined homozygosity mapping, targeted exon enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing. After SNP filtering, only 2 changes remained. One was a homozygous nonsynonymous change in the SWIP (WASHC4) gene, which the authors considered causative because of its high pathogenicity and conservation scores and on functional grounds. The mutation significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex, which is involved in actin polymerization and endosomal transport. Courtland et al. (2021) identified this mutation as c.3056C-G (P1019R) and noted that cell lines from the patients had decreased amounts of WASH proteins.

In 2 sisters with syndromic intellectual disability, Assoum et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the WASHC4 (KIAA1033) gene: a stop-gain mutation (Q442X) and a missense mutation (D1048G). Each mutation was inherited from one parent. In an unrelated 34-year-old patient, they found compound heterozygous missense mutations in WASHC4, K1079R and H503R, with one inherited from each parent.

Courtland et al. (2021) studied the 7 children with the homozygous WASHC4 c.3056C-G (P1019R) mutation originally reported by Ropers et al. (2011). The substitution lies in a region of SWIP thought to be critical for binding to the WASH component Strumpellin. In HEK cells, mutant SWIP co-immunoprecipitated significantly less Strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype SWIP (54.8% and 41.4% of wildtype, respectively), indicating that the mutation hinders formation of the WASH complex.

In 2 sisters with syndromic intellectual disability and skeletal muscle involvement, Gangfuß et al. (2022) identified a homozygous variant in the WASHC4 gene. Proteomic profiling of fibroblasts from one patient showed dysregulation of proteins relevant to maintaining the neuromuscular axis. Immunostaining of the same patient's muscle biopsy confirmed dysregulation of proteins needed for normal muscle function.

### Pathogenesis

Ropers et al. (2011) showed that the WASHC4 (SWIP) mutation they identified significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex.

Courtland et al. (2021) proposed a mechanistic model based on their findings in a mouse model (see ANIMAL MODEL). In this model, SWIP P1019R causes loss of WASH complex function, which disrupts the endo-lysosomal system. Over time this leads to accumulation of neurodegenerative markers, including unfolded protein response modulators, lysosomal enzymes, lipofuscin, and cleaved caspase-3, resulting in cognitive deficits and progressive motor impairment. The authors noted that the human P1019R mutation has not been classified as a neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL). However, findings in the mutant mice, including lipofuscin accumulation, suggested that loss of WASH complex function leads to phenotypes strongly resembling the NCLs. In mouse brain, they identified a neuronal WASH proteome of 175 significantly enriched proteins using in vivo BioID with a WASH1 probe. It included all 5 WASH complex components, 13 known WASH interactors, and proteins with endosomal, endocytic, cytoskeletal, and postsynaptic functions, supporting a major role for the WASH complex in endosomal trafficking in brain.

Gangfuß et al. (2022) reported that histologic studies and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering microscopy supported a functional role for WASHC4 in protein processing and clearance in humans.

### Animal Model

Courtland et al. (2021) used CRISPR to introduce a CCT-to-CGT point mutation into exon 29 of mouse Washc4, modeling the human c.3056C-G (P1019R) mutation. In whole-brain lysates from homozygous mutant mice, levels of the WASH complex members Strumpellin and WASH1 were markedly reduced (3.8% and 1.1% of wildtype, respectively), phenocopying the human patient data.

Quantitative spatial proteomics showed that 23 of 49 protein covariation modules differed between wildtype and mutant brain. The WASH/endosomal module was significantly decreased, whereas a lysosomal module was significantly increased; it contained cathepsins, hydrolases such as HEXA, GBA, GLB1, MAN2B1, and MAN2B2, and progranulin. Compared with wildtype neurons, cultured cortical neurons from mutant mice had larger somatic EEA1-positive early endosomal puncta with no change in their number, and fewer but larger cathepsin D-positive lysosomal puncta.

At 10 months of age, mutant mice had significantly increased cleaved caspase-3 staining, selectively in motor cortex (44.01% vs 25.38% of nuclei); this increase was not present at adolescence. Electron microscopy of motor cortex from 7-month-old mutant mice showed electron-dense lipofuscin deposits that were increased in size and number. Behaviorally, mutant mice performed normally in novel object recognition and the Y-maze. However, they had a persistent deficit in cued fear memory recall at both adolescence and adulthood, with intact responses to footshock and tones. They also had progressive motor dysfunction: rotarod retention was reduced by nearly 50% at both ages, and gait abnormalities (longer swing time, longer strides, and reduced homologous coupling) emerged in adulthood.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE 43**  
WASHC4, PRO1019ARG [c.3056C>G; p.P1019R]

In affected members of a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic intellectual disability, Ropers et al. (2011) used homozygosity mapping, targeted exon enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsynonymous change in the WASHC4 (SWIP) gene. The mutation significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex.

Courtland et al. (2021) identified this mutation as a homozygous c.3056C-G transversion in the WASHC4 gene, resulting in a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution, and studied 7 children from the family reported by Ropers et al. (2011), whose data had been obtained in Oman. All had developmental delay or learning difficulties and motor symptoms, including clumsiness, fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. In HEK cells, mutant SWIP co-immunoprecipitated significantly less Strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype SWIP. Mice carrying the corresponding mutation had markedly reduced brain levels of Strumpellin and WASH1, endo-lysosomal abnormalities, cognitive deficits, and progressive motor dysfunction. ⚠︎
