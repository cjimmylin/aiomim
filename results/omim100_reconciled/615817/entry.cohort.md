### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the WASHC4 gene (615748) on chromosome 12q23. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is characterized by intellectual disability or learning difficulties. It was first described as nonsyndromic (Ropers et al., 2011). Later reports described motor problems, including clumsiness, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria (Courtland et al., 2021). Some patients have a syndromic presentation with dysmorphic features, macrocephaly or microcephaly, skeletal anomalies, subependymal heterotopic nodules, or skeletal muscle involvement (Assoum et al., 2020; Gangfuss et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic intellectual disability.

Assoum et al. (2020) reported 3 patients with syndromic intellectual disability. Two sisters, aged 4 and 5.5 years, both had learning disabilities, macrocephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, and subependymal heterotopic nodules. The younger sister also had congenital absence of the right internal carotid artery and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. The third patient was 34 years old and had mild intellectual disability, short stature, and microcephaly. Assoum et al. (2020) concluded that the disorder has a heterogeneous phenotype and that more case descriptions are needed to refine it.

Courtland et al. (2021) retrospectively reviewed clinical findings from 7 Omani children who were homozygous for the WASHC4 c.3056C-G mutation. Developmental delay or learning difficulties had been the main reason for medical evaluation, but all of the patients also had motor symptoms (mean age, 10.4 years). Their movements were described as clumsy, and clinical examination showed notable fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. The patients were then an average of 21 years old, and recent communication with their parents revealed no notable worsening of symptoms.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) reported 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype consisting of dysmorphic features, intellectual disability, profound developmental disorder, and skeletal muscle involvement. The authors stated that this was the first report of skeletal muscle involvement in the disorder.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MRT43 in the family reported by Ropers et al. (2011) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Autosomal recessive inheritance was further supported by the homozygous or compound heterozygous WASHC4 mutations found in later patients (Assoum et al., 2020; Courtland et al., 2021; Gangfuss et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability, Ropers et al. (2011) used homozygosity mapping, targeted exon enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing. After filtering for SNPs, only 2 changes remained. One was a homozygous nonsynonymous change in the SWIP (WASHC4) gene, which the authors considered causative based on high pathogenicity and conservation scores and functional considerations. The mutation led to significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex, which is involved in actin polymerization and endosomal transport. Courtland et al. (2021) later identified this mutation as c.3056C-G (P1019R) and noted that cell lines from the patients showed decreased abundance of WASH proteins. ⚠︎

In 2 sisters with syndromic intellectual disability, Assoum et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the WASHC4 gene: a nonsense mutation (Q442X) and a missense mutation (D1048G). Each mutation was inherited from one parent. In a third, unrelated patient, they found compound heterozygous missense mutations in WASHC4 (K1079R and H503R), one inherited from each parent.

Courtland et al. (2021) showed that the P1019R substitution lies in a region of SWIP thought to be critical for binding to the WASH component Strumpellin. In HEK cells, mutant SWIP co-immunoprecipitated significantly less Strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype SWIP (54.8% and 41.4% of wildtype, respectively), indicating that the mutation interferes with WASH complex formation.

In 2 sisters with syndromic intellectual disability and skeletal muscle involvement, Gangfuss et al. (2022) identified a homozygous variant in the WASHC4 gene.

### Pathogenesis

Courtland et al. (2021) proposed that SWIP P1019R causes loss of WASH complex function. In their model, this loss disrupts the endo-lysosomal pathway and leads to gradual accumulation of neurodegenerative markers, including unfolded protein response modulators, lysosomal enzymes, lipofuscin, and cleaved caspase-3, which results in cognitive deficits and progressive motor impairment. The human mutation has not been classified as a neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL). However, the authors noted that features of their mutant mouse model, including lipofuscin accumulation, closely resembled the NCLs.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) performed proteomic profiling of fibroblasts from a WASHC4-deficient patient and found dysregulation of proteins important for maintaining the neuromuscular axis. Immunostaining of the same patient's muscle biopsy confirmed dysregulation of proteins needed for normal muscle function. Histologic studies and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering microscopy supported a role for WASHC4 in protein processing and clearance in humans.

### Animal Model

Courtland et al. (2021) used CRISPR to introduce a CCT-to-CGT point mutation into exon 29 of mouse Washc4, creating a model of the human c.3056C-G mutation. In whole-brain lysate from homozygous mutant mice, the WASH complex members Strumpellin and WASH1 were markedly reduced (3.8% and 1.1% of wildtype, respectively), matching findings in the human patients. Quantitative spatial proteomics showed that 23 of 49 protein covariation modules differed between mutant and wildtype brain. A WASH/endosomal module was significantly decreased, and a lysosomal module containing cathepsins, hydrolases, and progranulin was significantly increased.

Cultured cortical neurons from the mutant mice had larger EEA1-positive early endosomal puncta in the soma, with no change in their number. They also had fewer but larger cathepsin D-positive lysosomal puncta (Courtland et al., 2021). At 10 months of age, but not in adolescence, mutant mice had significantly increased cleaved caspase-3 staining selectively in the motor cortex (44.01% vs 25.38% of nuclei). Electron microscopy of motor cortex from 7-month-old mutant mice showed larger and more numerous electron-dense lipofuscin deposits.

On behavioral testing, the mutant mice performed normally on novel object recognition and the Y-maze (Courtland et al., 2021). However, they had a persistent deficit in cued fear memory recall in both adolescence and adulthood, despite intact responses to footshock and tones. Motor function was impaired and worsened over time: rotarod retention was reduced by nearly 50% at both ages, and gait abnormalities (longer swing time, longer strides, and reduced homologous coupling) appeared in adulthood.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE 43**  
WASHC4, PRO1019ARG [c.3056C>G; p.P1019R]

In affected members of a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic intellectual disability, Ropers et al. (2011) used homozygosity mapping, targeted exon enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsynonymous change in the SWIP (WASHC4) gene. The mutation led to significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the WASH complex. Courtland et al. (2021) later identified this mutation as c.3056C-G (P1019R). ⚠︎

Courtland et al. (2021) re-evaluated 7 Omani children who were homozygous for a c.3056C-G transversion in the WASHC4 gene, resulting in a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution. The children had developmental delay or learning difficulties and motor symptoms, including clumsiness, fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. In HEK cells, the mutant protein co-immunoprecipitated less Strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype (54.8% and 41.4% of wildtype, respectively). Mice homozygous for the corresponding mutation had markedly reduced brain levels of Strumpellin and WASH1, endo-lysosomal abnormalities, a cued fear memory deficit, and progressive motor dysfunction.
