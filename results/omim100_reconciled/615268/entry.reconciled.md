### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene (605870) on chromosome 13q12.

### Description

Onat et al. (2013) described cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium syndrome as a rare and heterogeneous condition. CAMRQ4 is an autosomal recessive disorder with onset at birth or in infancy. Its main features are cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and cerebellar atrophy, and some patients walk on all fours (quadrupedal locomotion). Severity varies: some patients have severe developmental delay, hypotonia, and involuntary movements, while others have congenital ataxia with near normal cognition. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family with 4 affected individuals. The affected individuals had cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium, with cerebellar atrophy and quadrupedal locomotion.

Mohamadian et al. (2020) reported 3 affected sibs born to healthy first-cousin parents of Arab descent in an Iranian family. The proband, a 9-year-old girl, first showed severe neonatal hypotonia, followed by delay in all psychomotor and developmental milestones. She was wheelchair-bound and could not walk, sit, or hold her head, and she had had feeding difficulties since infancy. She did not have seizures, optic atrophy, or hearing impairment, and her metabolic investigations were normal. Her affected brother and sister were 17 and 15 years old and had features similar to the proband's. All 3 patients had limited intellectual function with IQ levels from 20 to 40, delayed speech and language, dysarthria, and absent deep tendon reflexes. Brain MRI showed cerebellar atrophy, and hearing examination showed no significant impairment.

Damásio et al. (2021) described 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who learned to walk independently by age 4 years. After 16 years of follow-up, they had near normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported 2 Japanese sibs who developed athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. They also had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, and severely delayed development. One of them had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. Over time, both developed areflexia and flattened auditory brainstem responses. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or control their head, but some nonverbal communication remained. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

CAMRQ4 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Onat et al. (2013) found a homozygous mutation in affected members of a consanguineous Turkish family. Mohamadian et al. (2020) showed that a homozygous mutation segregated with the disorder in a consanguineous Iranian family: the affected sibs were homozygous, while the parents and 2 healthy sibs were heterozygous. Narishige et al. (2022) found compound heterozygous mutations in affected sibs. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Onat et al. (2013) noted that ATP8A2 had been disrupted by a de novo balanced t(10; 13) translocation in an unrelated individual with mental retardation and severe hypotonia.

### Mapping

Onat et al. (2013) performed homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Turkish family with 4 affected individuals. They found several shared homozygous regions, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Ozcelik et al. (2008) studied 4 large consanguineous Turkish kindreds with quadrupedal gait (Unertan syndrome), a rare phenotype associated with dysarthric speech, mental retardation, and varying degrees of cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia. In 2 of the families, the trait mapped to chromosome 9p24 and was caused by mutations in the VLDLR gene. In the other 2 families, the phenotype was not linked to chromosome 9p. The authors concluded that hereditary disorders with quadrupedal gait in humans are genetically heterogeneous. In a reply to Herz et al. (2008) and Humphrey et al. (2008), Ozcelik et al. (2008) discussed the genetic heterogeneity of cerebellar hypoplasia with quadrupedal locomotion.

Gulsuner et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous Turkish family with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation, and cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia, and linked the disorder to a 7.1-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 17p13.1-p13.3. In this family, they found a homozygous P856L mutation in the WDR81 gene that segregated with the disorder. They stated that WDR81 was the third gene implicated in human quadrupedal locomotion, after VLDLR and CA8. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Onat et al. (2013) studied the consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4. They performed targeted next-generation sequencing in an affected individual and followed it with segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction analyses. This identified a homozygous I376M mutation in the ATP8A2 gene, located in the shared homozygous region on chromosome 13q12. The variant lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain. ⚠︎

Mohamadian et al. (2020) studied 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4 and identified a homozygous c.1339G-A transition in ATP8A2, resulting in a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disorder: it was homozygous in the 3 affected sibs, heterozygous in both parents and 2 healthy sibs, and absent from 1 healthy sib. It was not found in public databases or in 100 ethnically matched controls, and the authors classified it as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. Gly447 lies in a highly conserved part of the cytoplasmic N (ATP-binding) domain. Homology modeling predicted that the substitution greatly reduces protein stability and alters the ligand-binding pocket.

Damásio et al. (2021) used exome sequencing in 2 sibs with congenital ataxia and identified a homozygous c.1580-18C-G variant in intron 17 of ATP8A2. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the variant caused use of an alternative acceptor splice site. The resulting aberrant transcript kept 17 bp of intron 17, which inserted 4 amino acids and led to a premature stop codon.

Narishige et al. (2022) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4 and identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in ATP8A2: c.1741C-T (R581X) and c.2158C-T (R720X).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Damásio et al. (2021) concluded that their findings in 2 sibs with congenital ataxia, near normal cognition, and a homozygous ATP8A2 splice site mutation expand the ATP8A2-related disorders to include less severe forms of congenital ataxia.

### Pathogenesis

Onat et al. (2013) noted that ATP8A2 is expressed mainly in the brain and during development, particularly in the cerebellum. They suggested that ATP8A2 helps form the cerebro-cerebellar structures that humans need for posture and gait.

### Diagnosis

Narishige et al. (2022) noted that CAMRQ4 symptoms do not progress early in development, which makes the disorder hard to distinguish from dyskinetic cerebral palsy, especially in solitary cases. They suggested that visual and hearing impairment combined with involuntary movements and severe developmental delay may be a clue to suspect CAMRQ4.
