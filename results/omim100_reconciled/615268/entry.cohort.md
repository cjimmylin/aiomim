### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene (605870) on chromosome 13q12.

### Description

Cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is an autosomal recessive disorder. It is characterized by cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, dysequilibrium, and cerebellar atrophy, and some patients walk on all fours (quadrupedal locomotion) (Onat et al., 2013). Onat et al. (2013) described cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium syndrome as rare and genetically heterogeneous. Severity varies. Some patients have severe hypotonia, involuntary movements, and severe developmental delay (Mohamadian et al., 2020; Narishige et al., 2022). Others have congenital ataxia with near normal cognition (Damasio et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family in which 4 individuals had cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, dysequilibrium, cerebellar atrophy, and quadrupedal locomotion. ⚠︎

Mohamadian et al. (2020) reported 3 affected sibs of Arab descent from an Iranian family. Their parents were healthy first cousins. The proband was a 9-year-old girl. Her first noticeable sign was severe neonatal hypotonia, and all her psychomotor and developmental milestones were then delayed. She used a wheelchair and could not walk, sit, or hold her head up, and she had had feeding difficulties since infancy. Her affected brother and sister, aged 17 and 15 years, had features similar to the proband's. All 3 patients had IQs of 20 to 40, delayed speech and language, dysarthria, absent deep tendon reflexes, and cerebellar atrophy on MRI. Hearing examination showed no significant impairment. The proband had normal metabolic investigations, no seizures, and no optic atrophy.

Damasio et al. (2021) described 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who learned to walk independently by age 4 years. After 16 years of follow-up, both had near normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported 2 Japanese sibs who developed athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. They also had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, and severely delayed development. One of them had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. Over time both lost their tendon reflexes (areflexia) and developed flattened auditory brainstem responses. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or control the head, although some nonverbal communication remained.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMRQ4 in the families reported by Onat et al. (2013), Mohamadian et al. (2020), and Narishige et al. (2022) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the family reported by Mohamadian et al. (2020), the affected sibs were born to first-cousin parents, both of whom were heterozygous carriers of the mutation. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Onat et al. (2013) noted that ATP8A2 had previously been found disrupted by a de novo balanced t(10; 13) translocation in an unrelated individual with mental retardation and severe hypotonia.

### Mapping

Onat et al. (2013) performed homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Turkish family with 4 affected individuals. Several homozygous regions were shared by the patients, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Ozcelik et al. (2008) studied 4 large consanguineous Turkish kindreds with quadrupedal gait. In 2 of the families the trait mapped to chromosome 9p24 and was caused by mutations in the VLDLR gene. In the other 2 families the phenotype was not linked to chromosome 9p. The authors concluded that hereditary disorders with quadrupedal gait in humans are genetically heterogeneous. In a reply to Herz et al. (2008) and Humphrey et al. (2008), Ozcelik et al. (2008) again addressed the genetic heterogeneity of cerebellar hypoplasia with quadrupedal locomotion.

Gulsuner et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous Turkish family with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation, and cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia. The disorder was linked to a 7.1-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 17p13.1-p13.3. Diffusion-weighted imaging and fiber tractography showed abnormalities of the cerebellum and corpus callosum, in particular atrophy of the superior, middle, and inferior cerebellar peduncles. Morphometric abnormalities of the precentral gyrus and of Brodmann areas BA6, BA44, and BA45 were also reported. The authors sequenced the whole homozygous region in 3 affected individuals and 2 obligate carriers. This identified a private homozygous P856L missense mutation in the WDR81 gene (614218), which cosegregated with the disorder in the extended family. The mutation lies in a highly conserved region of WDR81, between the N-terminal BEACH domain and the C-terminal WD40 beta-propeller domains. WDR81 was highly expressed in the cerebellum and corpus callosum, especially in the Purkinje cell layer. Gulsuner et al. (2011) stated that WDR81 was the third gene implicated in human quadrupedal locomotion, after VLDLR and CA8.

### Molecular Genetics

Onat et al. (2013) performed targeted next-generation sequencing of 1 affected member of a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4. This identified a novel homozygous missense variant, I376M, in the ATP8A2 gene. It lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain. The variant was supported by segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction programs. ⚠︎

Mohamadian et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1339G-A transition in the ATP8A2 gene in 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4. The mutation results in a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution. Both parents and 2 healthy sibs were heterozygous, and 1 healthy sib carried 2 normal copies of the gene. The variant was not found in public databases or in 100 ethnically matched controls, and it was classified as likely pathogenic by ACMG criteria. Gly447 lies in a highly conserved part of the cytoplasmic N (ATP-binding) domain. Homology modeling predicted that the substitution greatly reduces protein stability and changes the ligand-binding pocket.

Damasio et al. (2021) used exome sequencing in 2 sibs with congenital ataxia and identified a novel homozygous intronic variant in the ATP8A2 gene (c.1580-18C-G, intron 17). In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the variant caused use of an alternative acceptor splice site. The resulting aberrant transcript kept 17 bp of intron 17, which inserted 4 amino acids and led to a premature stop codon.

Narishige et al. (2022) used whole-exome sequencing in 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4 and identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the ATP8A2 gene: c.1741C-T (R581X) and c.2158C-T (R720X).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Damasio et al. (2021) concluded that their 2 sibs, who had a homozygous ATP8A2 splice variant, congenital ataxia, and near normal cognition, extend the range of ATP8A2-related disorders to include less severe forms of congenital ataxia.

### Pathogenesis

Onat et al. (2013) noted that ATP8A2 is expressed mainly in the brain and during development, particularly in the cerebellum. They suggested that ATP8A2 is involved in the development of the cerebrocerebellar structures that humans need for posture and gait.

### Diagnosis

Narishige et al. (2022) noted that the symptoms of CAMRQ4 did not progress in early development, which can make the disorder hard to distinguish from dyskinetic cerebral palsy, especially in patients with no affected relatives. They suggested that visual and hearing impairments, together with involuntary movements and severe developmental delay, may be a clue to CAMRQ4.
