### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene (602242) on chromosome 19q13.32.

### Description

Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (FHH3), also called the Oklahoma variant of familial benign hypercalcemia (FBHOk), is an autosomal dominant disorder of calcium homeostasis marked by lifelong hypercalcemia with low urinary calcium excretion. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that its clinical features differ from those of FHH1 and FHH2 and include increased serum PTH concentrations, hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) named this form of familial benign hypercalcemia the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok). Lloyd et al. (1999) called it FBHOk.

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) studied 46 members of a large 5-generation kindred with familial benign hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FBH) and found 19 of them affected. In the 15 hypercalcemic members examined biochemically, serum immunoreactive PTH, measured in 3 separate immunoassays, became supranormal by about age 30 years and appeared to rise further after that. Serum alkaline phosphatase activity and creatinine were normal, but inorganic phosphate levels were lower than in unaffected members of the kindred. Radiographs of 3 of 5 affected adults older than 40 years showed changes suggestive of osteomalacia. In one of them, a 51-year-old woman, iliac crest biopsy confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. McMurtry et al. (1992) concluded that the disorder in this kindred may not be totally benign, because osteomalacia, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia, can develop in adulthood. They raised the possibility that they had observed an unusual variant of FBH. ⚠︎

Trump et al. (1995) described a 5-generation kindred from Oklahoma, USA, with 19 affected members, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected members. In this kindred, FBH was associated with a developmental elevation in serum PTH levels.

Hannan et al. (2010) included 34 members of the 5-generation Oklahoma kindred in genetic analysis: 19 affected members, 13 unaffected members, and 2 unaffected spouses. Affected status was based on hypercalcemia with a ratio of calcium clearance to creatinine clearance below 0.01. Elevated serum PTH had been seen in some affected individuals older than 30 years (5 individuals), and 3 adults older than 40 years had evidence of osteomalacia.

Nesbit et al. (2010) reported a second FHH3 kindred. The proband was a 43-year-old woman with a corrected serum calcium of 2.74 mmol/liter (normal, 2.15-2.55), a serum PTH of 47 pg/ml (normal, 10-65), and a urinary calcium clearance:creatinine clearance ratio of 0.006. Sixteen members of the kindred were hypercalcemic, with normal or elevated serum PTH and mild hypophosphatemia.

Nesbit et al. (2013) studied 2 FHH3 kindreds. The FHHOk kindred included 13 affected females and 3 affected males, and the FHHNI kindred included 11 affected females and 5 affected males. Affected members had hypercalcemia and low urinary calcium excretion. The authors also studied 11 unrelated probands with FHH who did not have CASR mutations and whose age at diagnosis ranged from 8 days to 78 years. Some of these probands had affected relatives. ⚠︎

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese female infant whose hypercalcemia was found on a routine laboratory test done because of poor weight gain on breast feeding. At 49 days of age, her serum calcium (adjusted by Payne's formula) was 13.1 mg/dL, intact PTH was 27 pg/mL, and urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg, indicating hypercalciuria.

Howles et al. (2016) described 3 unrelated probands with FHH3 and symptomatic hypercalcemia. A 34-year-old woman with a 12-year history of hypercalcemia had fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. A 22-year-old man had hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. A 52-year-old woman with an approximately 20-year history of hypercalcemia had headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy.

### Inheritance

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that heterozygous AP2S1 mutations cosegregated with the disorder in 2 multigenerational kindreds. In the infant reported by Fujisawa et al. (2013), the heterozygous AP2S1 mutation was presumably de novo.

### Mapping

Trump et al. (1995) used 17 polymorphic markers in the 5-generation Oklahoma kindred. They excluded linkage to the FBH loci on 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3, to 11q13 (the MEN1 locus), and to 11p15 (the PTH locus), with lod scores below -2.00 at theta = 0.05 to 0.25. Multipoint crossover analysis and LINKMAP confirmed the exclusion.

Lloyd et al. (1999) performed a genomewide search in the Oklahoma family and found linkage to 8 loci on chromosome 19q13. The maximum lod score was 6.67 at theta = 0.0 for D19S606. Recombinants placed the locus in an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866.

Hannan et al. (2010) typed 24 polymorphic loci in the Oklahoma kindred and found that the disorder segregated with a chromosome 19q13 haplotype, with a peak lod score of 6.92 at zero recombination with D19S606. Recombinants in individuals II.2, III.3, and III.4 refined the FBHH3 locus to a 4.1-Mb interval flanked by D19S112 and rs245111. This interval contains 135 protein-coding genes and 24 predicted genes. The syntenic region on mouse chromosome 7 contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs) that have no human homologs. Comparative analysis showed that 2 conserved segments were inverted and that an 11.6-Mb region containing 35 murine genes, including the 4 Gprc2a-rs genes, is absent from the human genome. FBHH3 therefore cannot be attributed to these genes.

In 24 members from 3 generations of a second FHH kindred whose proband had no CASR mutation, Nesbit et al. (2010) found that FHH cosegregated with chromosome 19q13.3 markers, with a peak lod score of 5.98 at 0% recombination with D19S412. Analysis of recombinants placed the locus in a 3.46-Mb interval between SNP rs1990932 on the centromeric side and D19S604 on the telomeric side.

### Heterogeneity

Trump et al. (1995) found that the Oklahoma variant was not linked to markers that segregate with FBH, MEN1, and PTH, and concluded that their results indicated further genetic heterogeneity and the presence of a third locus for FBH. Lloyd et al. (1999) noted that not all patients with familial benign hypercalcemia have CASR mutations and that another locus had been mapped to 19p13.3. Because the Oklahoma variant was excluded from 3q and 19p and mapped to 19q13, it represented a third locus.

Nesbit et al. (2010) noted that FHH is genetically heterogeneous, with 3 types: FHH1 (3q21.1), FHH2 (19p), and FHH3 (19q13). FHH1 is caused by CASR mutations and accounts for more than 65% of patients. They suggested that FHH3 may explain the disorder in FHH patients without CASR mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Lloyd et al. (1999) found no abnormalities in the coding region, 5-prime UTR, or part of the promoter of the calmodulin III gene, which lies within the candidate interval, in an individual with FBHOk. They concluded that this gene is unlikely to be involved.

In an affected individual from the Oklahoma kindred, Hannan et al. (2010) sequenced the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries of 12 candidate genes from the FBHH3 interval that are expressed in parathyroid and/or kidney. These included PTH2, PTGIR, GNG8, EMP3, SPHK2, TULP2, NUCB1, HRC, RCN3, CYTH2, RRAS, and ATF5. No abnormalities were found, and RT-PCR excluded splicing defects.

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in one affected individual from each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds (FHHOk and FHHNI). Within the 3.46-Mb FHH3 interval on 19q13.3, they found a single shared variant: a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene predicting an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with the disorder in members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds (32 affected and 23 unaffected). The authors then sequenced AP2S1 in 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. They found 11 heterozygous missense mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 R15C, 3 arg15-to-his (R15H), and 4 arg15-to-leu (R15L). They suggested that AP2S1 arg15 mutations may cause FHH in more than 20% of patients without CASR mutations and that arg15 is a likely mutational hotspot. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, all 3 arg15 mutants shifted the concentration-response curve to the right, with a significantly higher EC50 than wildtype, indicating reduced sensitivity to extracellular calcium. With the cys15 mutant, the EC50 was higher than with vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect.

In a Japanese infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria, Fujisawa et al. (2013) found no pathologic mutation in CASR or GNA11. They identified a heterozygous R15L mutation in the AP2S1 gene, which was presumably de novo.

Howles et al. (2016) found that each of 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic hypercalcemia had a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation causing an R15C, R15H, or R15L substitution.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that patients with the different AP2S1 arg15 mutations, aged 8 days to 78 years, had similar elevations in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium excretion. They concluded that the specific mutation did not correlate with the clinical phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that, after calcium stimulation, cells transfected with wildtype AP2S1 lost more cell-surface CaSR than cells transfected with the R15C mutant, indicating reduced CaSR endocytosis with the mutant. Arg15 contacts the glutamine at position -4 of dileucine motifs. Mutating the C-terminal dileucine motif of CaSR (leu-leu1013-1014 to ala-ala) also increased the EC50, suggesting that the AP2S1 mutants act through loss of interaction with this motif.

### Diagnosis

McMurtry et al. (1992) noted that, in their kindred, FBH in adults could be especially difficult to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism because serum PTH levels may be elevated.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) concluded that the absence of hypocalciuria does not necessarily argue against an AP2S1 mutation. They suggested that their patient's hypercalciuria was partly due to her early infantile age.

### Clinical Management

Fujisawa et al. (2013) found that their infant with FHH3 showed catch-up growth on a low-calcium formula (2.6 mg/dL calcium), with lower serum calcium and a lower urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio. A 2:1 mixture of low-calcium and standard formula kept serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without a marked rise in PTH. The authors suggested that low-calcium formula is worth trying in infants with FHH.

Howles et al. (2016) treated 3 unrelated probands with FHH3 due to AP2S1 arg15 mutations with oral cinacalcet (30 to 60 mg daily) for 33 to 45 months. Serum calcium fell by more than 20% and symptoms abated. Serum phosphate increased and PTH decreased, both remaining in the normal range. No nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia developed, although the authors noted that long-term surveillance would be needed. In vitro, cinacalcet (10 nM) corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in cells expressing CaSR together with the cys15, his15, or leu15 AP2-sigma mutants.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15CYS/ARG15HIS/ARG15LEU [p.R15C; p.R15H; p.R15L]

Nesbit et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution, by exome sequencing in affected members of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, FHHOk and FHHNI. The mutation cosegregated with the disorder in members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds (32 affected and 23 unaffected). Affected members had hypercalcemia and low urinary calcium excretion. In HEK293 cells expressing CaSR, the R15C mutant gave a higher EC50 than wildtype, and also a higher EC50 than vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. Cells transfected with the mutant showed less calcium-stimulated reduction of cell-surface CaSR than cells transfected with wildtype, indicating reduced CaSR endocytosis.

Among 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations, Nesbit et al. (2013) found heterozygous AP2S1 arg15 mutations in 11 probands. Four probands had R15C (families 02/08, 02/10, 03/96, and 07/98). Four had an arg15-to-leu (R15L) substitution resulting from a CGC-to-CTC change (families 13/07, 11/02, 04/10, and 19/07). Three had an arg15-to-his (R15H) substitution resulting from a CGC-to-CAC change (families 04/04, 23/08, and 11/10). All 3 mutants caused a rightward shift in the CaSR concentration-response curve, with a significantly higher EC50 than wildtype. Patients with the different mutations, aged 8 days to 78 years, had similar biochemical features.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a presumably de novo heterozygous R15L mutation in AP2S1 in a Japanese female infant. She had hypercalcemia, poor weight gain, and hypercalciuria, and no pathologic mutation in CASR or GNA11.

Howles et al. (2016) found an AP2S1 R15C, R15H, or R15L mutation in each of 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic hypercalcemia. In cells expressing the mutants, cinacalcet corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium.
