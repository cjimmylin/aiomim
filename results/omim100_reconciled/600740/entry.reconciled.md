### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene (602242) on chromosome 19q13.32.

### Description

Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (FHH3) is an autosomal dominant disorder of calcium homeostasis characterized by hypercalcemia with a low ratio of calcium clearance to creatinine clearance. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that FHH3 differs clinically from FHH1 and FHH2: patients can have increased serum PTH concentrations, hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia.

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) named this form of familial benign hypercalcemia (FBH) the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok), after the Oklahoma kindred in which it was first defined. Lloyd et al. (1999) referred to it as FBHOk, and Hannan et al. (2010) used the designation FBHH3. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) studied 46 members of a large 5-generation kindred with familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia and found that 19 were affected. In the 15 hypercalcemic members examined biochemically, serum immunoreactive PTH was measured in 3 separate immunoassays. Levels became supranormal by about age 30 years and appeared to increase further thereafter. Serum alkaline phosphatase activity and creatinine were normal, but serum inorganic phosphate was lower than in unaffected members of the kindred. Three of 5 affected adults older than 40 years who were studied radiographically had changes suggestive of osteomalacia. In one of them, a 51-year-old woman, iliac crest biopsy confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. McMurtry et al. (1992) concluded that the disorder in this kindred may not be totally benign, because osteomalacia, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia, can develop during adulthood. They raised the possibility that they had observed an unusual variant of FBH.

Trump et al. (1995) described the same kindred, from Oklahoma, USA, as a 5-generation family with 19 affected members, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected members. In this family FBH was associated with a developmental elevation in serum PTH. ⚠︎

Hannan et al. (2010) included 34 members from 5 generations of the Oklahoma kindred in their genetic analysis: 19 affected members, 13 unaffected members, and 2 unaffected spouses. Affected status was based on hypercalcemia together with a low ratio (less than 0.01) of calcium clearance to creatinine clearance. Elevated serum PTH had been found in some affected individuals older than 30 years (5 individuals), and 3 adults older than 40 years had evidence of osteomalacia.

Nesbit et al. (2010) reported a second FHH3 kindred, in which 24 members from 3 generations were studied. The proband was a 43-year-old woman with no CASR mutation. She had a corrected serum calcium of 2.74 mmol/liter (normal, 2.15-2.55), a serum PTH of 47 pg/ml (normal, 10-65), and a urinary calcium clearance:creatinine clearance ratio of 0.006. Sixteen members of the family were hypercalcemic, with normal or elevated serum PTH and mild hypophosphatemia.

Nesbit et al. (2013) studied the previously reported Oklahoma kindred (FHHOk) and a second kindred, FHHNI, which had 16 affected members (11 females and 5 males). They also studied 11 unrelated probands with FHH who lacked CASR mutations and carried AP2S1 mutations. Some of these probands had affected relatives. Age at diagnosis ranged from 8 days to 78 years. All patients were hypercalcemic, and urinary calcium excretion was low in those in whom it was reported. ⚠︎

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese girl in whom hypercalcemia was found on routine laboratory testing for poor weight gain on breastfeeding. At 49 days of age, her serum calcium (adjusted by Payne's formula) was 13.1 mg/dL, intact PTH was 27 pg/mL, and the urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg, indicating hypercalciuria.

Howles et al. (2016) reported 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic hypercalcemia. A 34-year-old woman with a 12-year history of hypercalcemia had fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. A 22-year-old man had hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. A 52-year-old woman with an approximately 20-year history of hypercalcemia had headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of FHH3 was shown by the multigenerational Oklahoma kindred reported by Trump et al. (1995) and Hannan et al. (2010). Nesbit et al. (2013) demonstrated cosegregation of heterozygous AP2S1 mutations with the disorder in this kindred and in a second kindred. Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a presumably de novo heterozygous AP2S1 mutation in an affected infant. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Trump et al. (1995) studied the 5-generation Oklahoma kindred with 17 polymorphic markers. They excluded linkage to the FBH loci on 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3, to the MEN1 locus on 11q13, and to the PTH locus on 11p15, with lod scores below -2.00 at theta = 0.05-0.25. Multipoint crossover analysis and LINKMAP confirmed these exclusions.

Lloyd et al. (1999) performed a genomewide search in the Oklahoma family and found linkage to 8 loci on chromosome 19q13. The maximum lod score was 6.67 at theta = 0.0 with D19S606. Recombinants placed the locus in an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866.

Hannan et al. (2010) performed linkage studies with 24 polymorphic loci in the Oklahoma kindred and obtained a peak lod score of 6.92 at zero recombination with D19S606. Recombinants in 3 individuals refined the FBHH3 locus to a 4.1-Mb interval on 19q13 flanked by D19S112 and rs245111. This interval contained 135 protein-coding genes and 24 predicted genes. The syntenic region on mouse chromosome 7 contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs), but these have no human homologs. Comparative analysis showed that 2 conserved segments were inverted and that an 11.6-Mb region containing 35 murine genes, including the 4 Gprc2a-rs genes, is absent from the human genome. FBHH3 therefore cannot be attributed to these genes.

In a second FHH kindred whose proband had no CASR mutation, Nesbit et al. (2010) found cosegregation of the disorder with chromosome 19q13.3 markers, with a peak lod score of 5.98 at 0% recombination with D19S412. Analysis of recombinants placed the locus in a 3.46-Mb interval between SNP rs1990932 on the centromeric side and D19S604 on the telomeric side.

### Heterogeneity

Trump et al. (1995) concluded that their exclusion of known loci in the Oklahoma kindred indicated further genetic heterogeneity and a third locus for FBH. Lloyd et al. (1999) noted that not all patients with familial benign hypercalcemia have CaSR mutations and that another locus had been mapped to 19p13.3. The Oklahoma variant, excluded from 3q and 19p, mapped to 19q13, establishing a third locus.

Nesbit et al. (2010) noted that FHH is genetically heterogeneous, with 3 types: FHH1 (3q21.1), FHH2 (19p), and FHH3 (19q13). FHH1 is caused by CASR mutations and accounts for more than 65% of patients. They suggested that FHH3 may account for the disorder in FHH patients without CASR mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Lloyd et al. (1999) found no abnormalities in the coding region, the 5-prime UTR, or part of the promoter of the calmodulin III gene, which lies within the candidate interval, in an individual with FBHOk. They concluded that this gene is unlikely to be involved.

In an affected member of the Oklahoma kindred, Hannan et al. (2010) sequenced the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries of 12 candidate genes from the FBHH3 interval that are expressed in parathyroid and/or kidney, including PTH2, PTGIR, GNG8, EMP3, SPHK2, TULP2, NUCB1, HRC, RCN3, CYTH2, RRAS, and ATF5. No abnormalities were found, and RT-PCR excluded splicing defects.

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in an affected member of each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, FHHOk and FHHNI. Within the 3.46-Mb FHH3 interval on 19q13.3, they found a single shared variant: a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, predicting an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. Nesbit et al. (2013) then sequenced AP2S1 in 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. They found 11 heterozygous missense mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 R15C, 3 arg15-to-his (R15H), and 4 arg15-to-leu (R15L). They suggested that AP2S1 arg15 mutations may cause FHH in more than 20% of patients without CASR mutations and that arg15 is a likely mutational hotspot. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, all 3 arg15 mutants shifted the concentration-response curve to the right, with a significantly higher EC50 than wildtype, indicating reduced sensitivity to extracellular calcium. The EC50 for the cys15 mutant was higher than that for vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect.

In a Japanese infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria, Fujisawa et al. (2013) found no pathologic mutation in CASR or GNA11. They identified a presumably de novo heterozygous R15L mutation in the AP2S1 gene.

Howles et al. (2016) found that each of their 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic hypercalcemia had a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation causing an R15C, R15H, or R15L substitution.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that patients with the different AP2S1 arg15 mutations, aged 8 days to 78 years, had similar elevations in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium excretion. They concluded that the specific mutation did not correlate with the clinical phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

After calcium stimulation, Nesbit et al. (2013) found a greater reduction in cell-surface CaSR in cells transfected with wildtype AP2S1 than in cells transfected with the R15C mutant, indicating reduced CaSR endocytosis. Arg15 contacts the glutamine at position -4 of dileucine motifs. Mutating the C-terminal dileucine motif of CaSR (leu-leu1013-1014 to ala-ala) also increased the EC50. This suggested that the AP2S1 mutants act through loss of interaction with this motif.

### Diagnosis

McMurtry et al. (1992) noted that in their kindred, FBH in adults could be especially difficult to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism because serum iPTH levels may be elevated.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) concluded that the absence of hypocalciuria does not necessarily argue against an AP2S1 mutation. They suggested that their patient's early infantile age contributed to her hypercalciuria.

### Clinical Management

Fujisawa et al. (2013) found that their infant with FHH3 showed catch-up growth on a low-calcium formula (2.6 mg/dL calcium), with lower serum calcium and a lower urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio. A 2:1 mixture of low-calcium and standard formula kept serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without a marked rise in PTH. The authors suggested that low-calcium formula is worth trying in infants with FHH.

Howles et al. (2016) treated 3 unrelated probands with FHH3 due to AP2S1 arg15 mutations with oral cinacalcet (30 to 60 mg daily) for 33 to 45 months. Serum calcium fell by more than 20%, and symptoms abated. Serum phosphate increased and PTH decreased, with both remaining in the normal range. No nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia developed, although the authors noted that long-term surveillance would be required. In vitro, cinacalcet (10 nM) corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in cells expressing CaSR together with the cys15, his15, or leu15 AP2-sigma mutants.

In affected members of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, FHHOk (the Oklahoma kindred) and FHHNI, Nesbit et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, predicting an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. In 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations, Nesbit et al. (2013) found further heterozygous mutations at the same codon. Four probands carried R15C, some with affected relatives. Four carried an arg15-to-leu (R15L) substitution (CGC-to-CTC), and 3 carried an arg15-to-his (R15H) substitution (CGC-to-CAC). In HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR, all 3 mutants shifted the calcium concentration-response curve to the right, with a higher EC50 than wildtype. The cys15 mutant gave an EC50 higher than vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. The R15C mutant also reduced calcium-stimulated CaSR endocytosis. ⚠︎

In a Japanese infant who had hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, and poor weight gain, Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a presumably de novo heterozygous R15L mutation in the AP2S1 gene. No pathologic mutation was found in CASR or GNA11.

Howles et al. (2016) found a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation causing an R15C, R15H, or R15L substitution in each of 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic FHH3. Their symptoms improved with cinacalcet treatment.
