### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene (112264) on chromosome 8p21.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII is an autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder characterized by recurrent fractures, which may begin prenatally or at birth, or in childhood and mainly affect the lower limbs. Reported bone mineral density has varied: it was increased in some patients, normal in one, and decreased in others. Some severely affected patients also had marked short stature, bone deformities, joint hyperextensibility and umbilical hernia. The disorder results from deficient procollagen type I C-propeptide cleavage by BMP1. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Munns et al. (2004) described 3 unrelated children with an apparently novel bone fragility disorder associated with an idiopathic mineralization defect. Recurrent lower limb fractures started with weight bearing. On x-rays the children had increased metaphyseal trabeculation and normal to increased cortical thickness, with no evidence of rickets. Peripheral quantitative CT (pQCT) showed elevated radial cortical thickness and volumetric bone mineral density. Iliac bone biopsy showed a paucity of the birefringent lamellar bone pattern. Quantitative histomorphometry showed osteomalacia, with a prolonged mineralization lag time and a decreased mineral apposition rate. There was no biochemical evidence of abnormal calcium or phosphate metabolism. Munns et al. (2004) concluded that the disorder was a bone fragility disorder of moderate severity that tended to cause lower extremity fractures and was associated with osteoid accumulation due to an intrinsic mineralization defect. The patients lacked the phenotypic, radiologic and histomorphometric features of osteogenesis imperfecta, idiopathic juvenile osteoporosis and mild autosomal dominant osteopetrosis. ⚠︎

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) reported 2 affected sibs born to first-cousin Egyptian parents. The proband (II-3), a 15-year-old girl, was referred with a history of recurrent fractures and bone deformities, beginning with a tibial fracture at birth. On examination she had a triangular face, broad forehead, wide palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, faint blue sclerae, long philtrum, thin lips and prominent ears. Her gross motor development was delayed, but her mental development was normal and she did not have dentinogenesis imperfecta or hearing loss. Other findings were a large umbilical hernia, generalized hypotonia, muscle wasting, nocturnal enuresis and generalized hirsutism, without skin hyperextensibility or bruising. She had a bowed humerus, radius and ulna, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible elbows, wrists and interphalangeal joints, limited knee movement, kyphoscoliosis and pectus carinatum. On x-rays she had platyspondyly, generalized decreased bone density and wormian bones. She had severe short stature (-11.5 SD), low weight and microcephaly (-3.0 SD), and her alkaline phosphatase was slightly elevated. Her 5-year-old brother (II-6) had bilateral femoral bowing on fetal ultrasound, and both femora were fractured at birth. He had recurrent fractures, generalized bone deformities, joint hyperextensibility, arachnodactyly and a large umbilical hernia, with normal mentality and no dentinogenesis imperfecta or hearing loss. An x-ray survey showed wormian bones of the skull, deformed long bones with lack of modeling, fractures and callus formation, scoliosis, platyspondyly and a wide symphysis pubis. He had short stature (-7.2 SD) and slightly elevated alkaline phosphatase. At 5 years, DEXA showed borderline osteoporosis at the spine (Z-score -1.63) and osteoporosis at the left femur (Z-score -2.75).

Asharani et al. (2012) studied a consanguineous family affected by increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures.

Valencia et al. (2014) described a patient with osteogenesis imperfecta and normal bone density.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) studied 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder characterized by lower extremity fractures and a mineralization defect in bone tissue. On pQCT, all the children had elevated volumetric cortical bone mineral density. Iliac bone histomorphometry and quantitative backscattered electron imaging showed that the onset of mineralization at bone formation sites was delayed but that the mineralized matrix was hypermineralized. The mineralization defect involved bone tissue but not the growth plate.

Cho et al. (2015) reported a Korean patient with osteogenesis imperfecta who had an umbilical hernia, frequent fractures, markedly short stature, delayed motor development, scoliosis, and dislocation of the radial head with a bowed radius and ulna.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of OI13 is autosomal recessive. In the consanguineous Egyptian family reported by Martínez-Glez et al. (2012), both affected sibs were homozygous for a BMP1 mutation, while their unaffected parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous. Asharani et al. (2012) found a homozygous BMP1 mutation in another consanguineous family.

### Mapping

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) carried out homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Egyptian family with autosomal recessive osteogenesis imperfecta. They excluded the known loci for autosomal recessive OI and found 2 shared homozygous regions. One was a 10.27-Mb region on chromosome 8p containing 84 genes, including BMP1.

### Molecular Genetics

Munns et al. (2004) found no type I collagen mutations in their 3 unrelated children with bone fragility and an intrinsic mineralization defect.

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with OI13, Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in the BMP1 gene, resulting in a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disorder: the parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous. It was not found in 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin or in 884 European control chromosomes. In NIH3T3 fibroblasts, overexpression of wildtype mTLD (the BMP1 isoform mammalian Tolloid) led to C-terminal processing of most secreted pro-alpha-1(I) chains, whereas the F249L mutant did not enhance processing. Only wildtype mTLD rescued the processing defect in patient fibroblasts.

In a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures, Asharani et al. (2012) used whole-exome sequencing combined with filtering for homozygous stretches. They identified a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene. The mutation lies within the BMP1 signal peptide and led to impaired secretion and altered posttranslational modification.

In a patient with osteogenesis imperfecta and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) found the same homozygous c.34G-C mutation in BMP1 that Asharani et al. (2012) had reported. The patient's dermal fibroblasts had decreased levels of both alternatively spliced BMP1 proteins and abnormal cleavage of the C-terminal propeptide of type I procollagen.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 4 unrelated children with bone fragility and a bone tissue mineralization defect. Three were homozygous for a c.*241T-C variant in BMP1. This variant affects the polyadenylation signal of the transcript encoding the short isoform, BMP1-1. The fourth child was compound heterozygous for c.*241T-C and a c.2107G-C variant in exon 15, which affected splicing of both BMP1-1 and the long isoform, BMP1-3. Skin fibroblasts from the homozygous children had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein. Both the homozygous and the compound heterozygous genotypes were associated with impaired cleavage of the procollagen type I C-propeptide. The authors concluded that isolated lack of BMP1-1 causes bone fragility in children.

By whole-exome sequencing in a Korean patient with OI, Cho et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous variants in the BMP1 gene. One was a c.808A-G transition resulting in a met270-to-val (M270V) substitution. The other was a c.1297G-T transversion that caused skipping of exon 10. In a zebrafish assay, both variants were hypofunctional.

### Pathogenesis

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) studied fibroblasts from patients homozygous for the F249L mutation. These cells had decreased pN-alpha-1(I) relative to pC-alpha-1(I) and reduced free C-propeptide in the culture supernatant, indicating diminished procollagen I C-propeptidase activity, although some activity remained. Activity was slightly impaired in heterozygous cells. Collagen was not overmodified, and BMP1/mTLD protein levels were unchanged.

Valencia et al. (2014) examined cultured fibroblasts from their patient with homozygous c.34G-C mutation and from a previously reported patient with homozygous c.747C-G mutation in BMP1. Fluorescence and electron microscopy showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix. The authors concluded that BMP1 is essential for type I collagen fibrillogenesis in humans.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) found low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein in skin fibroblasts from patients homozygous for c.*241T-C. Fibroblast supernatants from both homozygous and compound heterozygous patients contained less free procollagen type I C-propeptide, indicating impaired C-propeptide cleavage.

### Animal Model

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) noted that the human phenotype differed from that of Bmp1-null mice, which die perinatally without gross skeletal abnormalities. However, Bmp1-null mice consistently show herniation of the gut, as their patients showed umbilical hernias. ⚠︎

Asharani et al. (2012) characterized a zebrafish bone mutant with lesions in bmp1a, showing that the role of BMP1 in osteogenesis is conserved across species. Genetic, biochemical and histologic analyses showed that Bmp1a is critically required to generate mature collagen in bone, downstream of osteoblast maturation.
