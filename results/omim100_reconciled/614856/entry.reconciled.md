### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene (112264) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is an autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder caused by deficient BMP1 activity. It is characterized by recurrent fractures, which may be present at birth or begin with weight bearing and mainly affect the lower limbs. Some patients also have bone deformities, severe short stature, joint hyperextensibility, and umbilical hernia. Bone mineral density has been increased in some patients and normal or decreased in others. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Munns et al. (2004) described 3 unrelated children with an apparently new bone fragility disorder associated with an idiopathic mineralization defect. Recurrent lower limb fractures began with weight bearing. Radiographs showed increased metaphyseal trabeculation, normal to increased cortical thickness, and no evidence of rickets or osteomalacia. Peripheral quantitative computed tomography (pQCT) showed elevated radial cortical thickness and volumetric bone mineral density (BMD). Iliac bone biopsy showed a paucity of the birefringent lamellar bone pattern. On quantitative histomorphometry, there was osteomalacia with a prolonged mineralization lag time and a decreased mineral apposition rate. There was no biochemical evidence of abnormal calcium or phosphate metabolism. Munns et al. (2004) concluded that this was a bone fragility disorder of moderate severity that tends to cause lower extremity fractures and is associated with osteoid accumulation due to an intrinsic mineralization defect. The patients lacked the phenotypic, radiologic, and histomorphometric features of osteogenesis imperfecta, idiopathic juvenile osteoporosis, and mild autosomal dominant osteopetrosis. ⚠︎

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) reported 2 affected sibs born to Egyptian parents who were first cousins and unaffected. The proband (II-3) was a 15-year-old girl referred with a history of recurrent fractures and bone deformities; she had had a tibial fracture at birth. On examination she had a triangular face, broad forehead, wide palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, faint blue sclerae, long philtrum, thin lips, and prominent ears. She had delayed gross motor development but normal mental development, and no dentinogenesis imperfecta. Other findings included a large umbilical hernia, generalized hypotonia, muscle wasting, nocturnal enuresis, and generalized hirsutism, without skin hyperextensibility or bruising. Skeletal findings included bowing of the humerus, radius, and ulna, arachnodactyly, hyperextensibility of the elbow, wrist, and interphalangeal joints, limited knee movement, kyphoscoliosis, and pectus carinatum. Radiographs showed platyspondyly, generalized decreased bone density, and wormian bones. She had short stature (-11.5 SD), low weight, and microcephaly (-3.0 SD). Alkaline phosphatase was slightly elevated, and hearing was normal.

Her 5-year-old younger brother (II-6) had bilateral femoral bowing detected in utero by fetal ultrasound, and fractures of both femora were noted at birth. He had recurrent fractures, generalized bone deformities, joint hyperextensibility, arachnodactyly, and a large umbilical hernia. He had normal mentality and no dentinogenesis imperfecta. X-ray survey showed wormian bones of the skull, deformed long bones with lack of modeling, fractures and callus formation, scoliosis, platyspondyly, and a wide symphysis pubis. He had short stature (-7.2 SD). Bone densitometry (DEXA) at 5 years showed borderline osteoporosis at the spine (Z-score -1.63) and osteoporosis at the left femur (Z-score -2.75). Alkaline phosphatase was slightly elevated, and hearing was normal (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012).

Asharani et al. (2012) studied a consanguineous family in which affected individuals had increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures.

Valencia et al. (2014) reported a patient with osteogenesis imperfecta and normal bone density.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) studied 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder characterized by lower extremity fractures. On pQCT, all 4 individuals had elevated volumetric cortical BMD. Iliac bone histomorphometry and quantitative backscattered electron imaging showed that mineralization began late at bone formation sites, but that the mineralized matrix was hypermineralized. Fahiminiya et al. (2015) noted that the mineralization defect affected bone tissue but not the growth plate.

Cho et al. (2015) reported a Korean patient with OI who had an umbilical hernia, frequent fractures, markedly short stature, delayed motor development, scoliosis, and dislocation of the radial head, with a bowed radius and ulna.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of OI13 is autosomal recessive. In the consanguineous Egyptian family reported by Martinez-Glez et al. (2012), the 2 affected sibs were homozygous for a BMP1 mutation, and their unaffected parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous.

### Mapping

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) performed homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Egyptian family with autosomal recessive OI. They excluded the known autosomal recessive OI loci and identified 2 homozygous regions shared by the affected sibs. One was a 10.27-Mb region on chromosome 8p containing 84 genes, among them BMP1.

### Molecular Genetics

Munns et al. (2004) found no type I collagen mutations in 3 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder associated with an intrinsic mineralization defect.

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with autosomal recessive OI, Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in the BMP1 gene, resulting in a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution. Both parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous for the mutation. The mutation was not found in 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin or in 884 European control chromosomes. In NIH3T3 fibroblasts, overexpression of wildtype mTLD led to C-terminal processing of most secreted proalpha-1(I), but the F249L mutant did not enhance processing. Only wildtype mTLD rescued the processing defect in patient fibroblasts.

In affected members of a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures, Asharani et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene. They found the mutation by whole-exome sequencing combined with filtering for homozygous stretches. The mutation lies within the BMP1 signal peptide and resulted in impaired secretion and altered posttranslational modification.

In a patient with OI and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) identified homozygosity for the recurrent c.34G-C mutation in BMP1. The patient's dermal fibroblasts showed decreased protein levels of both alternatively spliced BMP1 products and abnormal cleavage of the C-terminal propeptide of type I procollagen.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder characterized by lower extremity fractures and a mineralization defect in bone tissue. Three of the children were homozygous for a c.*241T-C variant in BMP1. This variant affects the polyadenylation signal of the transcript encoding the short isoform of BMP1 (BMP1-1). The fourth child was compound heterozygous for the c.*241T-C variant and a c.2107G-C variant in exon 15. The c.2107G-C variant affected splicing of both BMP1-1 and the long isoform, BMP1-3. Skin fibroblasts from the homozygous individuals had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein. Both the homozygous and the compound heterozygous genotypes were associated with impaired cleavage of the procollagen type I C-propeptide. Fahiminiya et al. (2015) concluded that isolated lack of BMP1-1 causes bone fragility in children.

By whole-exome sequencing in a Korean patient with OI, Cho et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous variants in the BMP1 gene. One was a c.808A-G transition resulting in a met270-to-val (M270V) substitution. The other was a c.1297G-T transversion that caused skipping of exon 10. In a zebrafish assay, both variants were hypofunctional.

### Pathogenesis

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) found that, in supernatants of fibroblasts from their patients, pN-alpha-1(I) was decreased relative to pC-alpha-1(I) and free C-propeptide was reduced. This indicated diminished procollagen I C-propeptidase activity, although some activity remained. Cells heterozygous for the mutation were slightly impaired. There was no collagen overmodification, and BMP1/mTLD protein levels were unchanged.

Using fluorescence and electron microscopy, Valencia et al. (2014) showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix of cultured fibroblasts from their patient and from a previously reported patient homozygous for the BMP1 c.747C-G mutation. They concluded that BMP1 is essential for human type I collagen fibrillogenesis.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) found low BMP1-1 transcript and protein levels in skin fibroblasts from homozygous patients. Fibroblast supernatants from both homozygous and compound heterozygous patients contained less free procollagen type I C-propeptide, indicating impaired C-propeptide cleavage.

### Population Genetics

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) did not find the BMP1 c.747C-G mutation in 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin or in 884 European control chromosomes.

### Animal Model

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) noted that the phenotype of their patients differed from that of Bmp1-null mice, which die perinatally and have no gross skeletal abnormalities. However, Bmp1-null mice consistently show herniation of the gut, similar to the umbilical hernias in their patients.

Asharani et al. (2012) characterized a zebrafish bone mutant with lesions in bmp1a and showed that the role of BMP1 in osteogenesis is conserved across species. Genetic, biochemical, and histologic analyses showed that Bmp1a is critically required to generate mature collagen in bone, downstream of osteoblast maturation.
