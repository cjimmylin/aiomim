### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PRPH2 gene (179605) on chromosome 6p21.1. A digenic form of RP7 is caused by double heterozygous mutation in the PRPH2 gene and the ROM1 gene (180721). ⚠︎

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-7 is a form of retinitis pigmentosa caused by mutation in the gene encoding peripherin/RDS (PRPH2), a photoreceptor-specific glycoprotein. It is usually transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait. Severity is often moderate, and the phenotype varies widely, even within one family. Some family members have widespread RP, while others have pericentral RP, pattern macular dystrophy, or fundus flavimaculatus. A digenic form occurs in individuals who are double heterozygotes for mutations in PRPH2 and ROM1. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Kajiwara et al. (1991) studied 3 separate families with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP). In each family, a mutation in the RDS gene cosegregated with the disease.

Farrar et al. (1991) and Jordan et al. (1992) studied a large pedigree of Irish origin with late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa.

Weleber et al. (1993) described a family in which a 75-year-old woman, her 2 daughters (aged 44 and 50 years), and her 49-year-old son all carried the same peripherin/RDS deletion. The mother presented at age 63 years with a profoundly abnormal electroretinogram (ERG) and adult-onset retinitis pigmentosa. Her disease progressed dramatically over 12 years, with marked loss of peripheral visual field. One daughter developed pattern macular dystrophy at age 31 years. At age 44, her ERG was moderately abnormal, but her clinical disease was limited to the macula. The other daughter presented at age 42 years with macular degeneration and over 10 years developed the clinical picture of fundus flavimaculatus. Her peripheral visual field was preserved, but her ERG was moderately abnormal. The son had onset of macular degeneration at age 44 years. He had pericentral scotomas and a markedly abnormal ERG, and fluorescein angiography showed punctate pigment epithelial transmission defects. ⚠︎

Wells et al. (1993) searched for RDS mutations in families with autosomal dominant retinal dystrophies. They identified 1 family in which retinitis pigmentosa was associated with a cysteine deletion at codon 118/119.

Kikawa et al. (1994) reported autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa associated with bull's-eye maculopathy.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with retinitis pigmentosa in which only individuals who were double heterozygotes for mutations in ROM1 and peripherin/RDS developed the disease.

Wang et al. (2013) described 3 patients with early-onset retinal dystrophy who were homozygous for PRPH2 mutations, each with onset within the first year of life. Patient 1318 had Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA), with visual impairment and nystagmus. The ERG was nondetectable or reduced. A fundus photograph taken at age 29 years showed a prominent multilobulated central atrophic maculopathy surrounded by concentric rings of yellow deposits, vessel narrowing, and fine diffuse peripheral retinal changes. Patient 3256 also had LCA, with nystagmus and a nondetectable or reduced ERG. Fundus photographs taken at age 66 years showed peripheral and macular pigment deposits, choroidal sclerosis, vessel narrowing, optic disc pallor, and a central extensive atrophic maculopathy. Patient 741 had juvenile RP with a nondetectable or reduced ERG and visual acuity of 20/40. A fundus photograph at age 30 years showed diffuse retinal dystrophy with retinal vessel narrowing, retinal pigment epithelium mottling and loss, and a multilobulated maculopathy. There was also perifoveal hyperautofluorescence.

Manes et al. (2015) characterized 32 French adRP families with PRPH2 mutations. PRPH2-associated adRP was of moderate severity: 78% of eyes had visual acuity of 1-0.5, and 52% retained more than 50% of the visual field. Some patients characteristically showed vitelliform deposits or macular involvement. In some families, some members had pericentral RP or macular dystrophy while other members had widespread RP. The authors suggested that mild cases could be underdiagnosed.

### Inheritance

Kajiwara et al. (1991) and Farrar et al. (1991) showed that RP7 is transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait, with RDS mutations cosegregating with the disease in the families they studied. ⚠︎

Kajiwara et al. (1994) established digenic inheritance of RP. In 3 families, retinitis pigmentosa developed only in individuals who were double heterozygotes for mutations in the unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes ROM1 and peripherin/RDS.

Wang et al. (2013) reported homozygous PRPH2 mutations in patients with LCA or juvenile RP. In 2 of the families, the parents were heterozygous carriers. The heterozygous carriers had no symptoms but showed macular pattern dystrophy.

### Mapping

Farrar et al. (1991) typed members of an Irish family with late-onset autosomal dominant RP with a series of markers from chromosome 6p. Two-point lod scores were positive with several markers, including D6S105 (Z = 6.081 at theta = 0.00) and RDS (Z = 5.376 at theta = 0.00). Multipoint analysis gave a maximum lod score of 6.6 at HLA-DRA. The authors concluded that their data provided direct evidence for an additional adRP locus. They stated that the data strongly implicated peripherin-rds, the human equivalent of the mouse retinal degeneration slow (rds) gene, as a candidate gene. Farrar et al. (1991) also reported that the 6p locus in this pedigree was tightly linked to the gene for peripherin, a photoreceptor cell-specific glycoprotein.

Jordan et al. (1992) further studied the large Irish family with late-onset adRP linked to 6p, a locus they referred to as RP6. An intragenic marker derived from the peripherin-RDS gene cosegregated with the disease locus with zero recombination (lod score 5.46 at theta = 0.00). They mapped the peripherin-RDS gene against other 6p markers using the CEPH reference panel. In multipoint analysis, the lod score reached 8.21 and peaked at the peripherin-RDS locus.

### Heterogeneity

Farrar et al. (1991) noted that autosomal dominant RP is genetically heterogeneous, with loci mapped to chromosome 3q near the rhodopsin gene, to chromosome 8p, and to chromosome 6p.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) concluded that the allelic and nonallelic heterogeneity of monogenic RP is further complicated by interactions between unlinked mutations that cause digenic RP.

### Molecular Genetics

Kajiwara et al. (1991) identified 3 mutations in the human homolog of the mouse rds gene (RDS; PRPH2). Each mutation cosegregated with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa in a separate family. They concluded that some cases of adRP are due to mutations at the RDS locus.

In a large Irish pedigree with adRP linked to 6p, Farrar et al. (1991) identified a heterozygous 3-bp deletion in the peripherin-RDS gene. The deletion results in loss of one of a pair of highly conserved cysteine residues in the predicted third transmembrane domain of peripherin. It segregated with the disease phenotype and was not present in unaffected controls. ⚠︎

Jordan et al. (1992) concluded that their linkage results gave strong evidence for a role of peripherin-RDS, a photoreceptor-specific protein, in the cause of one form of adRP.

In a family with variable retinal phenotypes, Weleber et al. (1993) found a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene. The deletion was present in the affected mother and in her 3 affected children. ⚠︎

In a family with adRP, Wells et al. (1993) found a heterozygous deletion of a cysteine codon at codon 118/119 of the RDS gene. ⚠︎

In patients with adRP associated with bull's-eye maculopathy, Kikawa et al. (1994) used nonradioactive single-strand conformation polymorphism (SSCP) analysis to identify an asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene.

In 3 families with retinitis pigmentosa, Kajiwara et al. (1994) found heterozygous mutations in both ROM1 and peripherin/RDS. Only double heterozygotes developed RP.

Wang et al. (2013) used targeted next-generation sequencing in a cohort of patients with LCA and juvenile RP and identified homozygous PRPH2 missense mutations in 3 patients. In patient 1318, who had LCA, they found a homozygous c.637T-C transition, resulting in a cys213-to-arg (C213R) substitution. Both parents and the patient's son were heterozygous, and an unaffected brother was homozygous wildtype. Patient 3256, who also had LCA, was homozygous for a c.554T-C transition, resulting in a leu185-to-pro (L185P) substitution. Parental DNA was not available. The same homozygous L185P mutation was found in patient 741, who had juvenile RP, and both parents were heterozygous. L185P had previously been reported in digenic RP with a ROM1 null mutation. The authors concluded that homozygous mutations in PRPH2 cause early-onset retinal dystrophy with LCA/juvenile RP phenotypes.

Manes et al. (2015) screened PRPH2 in French families with adRP and identified 15 mutations in 32 families, 6 of them novel. A recurrent novel mutation, leu254-to-gln (L254Q), led to protein aggregation when expressed in yeast, suggesting abnormal folding.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Weleber et al. (1993) concluded that a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene can produce clinically disparate phenotypes even within one family. In the family they studied, these phenotypes were retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus.

Wells et al. (1993) found that RDS mutations caused either retinitis pigmentosa or macular dystrophy. Three families with similar macular dystrophy had mutations at codon 172: arg172 to trp in 2 families and arg172 to gln in 1. A stop codon at codon 258 was found in a family with adult vitelliform macular dystrophy. The authors suggested that certain amino acids in peripherin-RDS may have different functional significance in cones and rods.

Wang et al. (2013) found that heterozygous carriers of PRPH2 mutations in the families of their homozygous patients had no symptoms but showed macular pattern dystrophy. One example was the 56-year-old father of a patient homozygous for L185P, who had macular pattern dystrophy with normal acuity. The authors suggested a dose-dependent effect. They proposed that heterozygous L185P carriers in digenic RP families may have a subclinical phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Manes et al. (2015) screened PRPH2 in 310 French families with autosomal dominant RP and identified 15 mutations (6 novel) in 32 families, a prevalence of 10.3%. This made PRPH2 the second most frequent adRP gene after RHO in their series.

### Animal Model

Kajiwara et al. (1991) noted that the mouse retinal degeneration slow (rds) mutation is semidominant. In rds mice, rod and cone photoreceptors develop abnormally and then slowly degenerate, which made the mouse a possible model for retinitis pigmentosa.
