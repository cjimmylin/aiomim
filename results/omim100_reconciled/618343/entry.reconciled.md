### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (PMGEDSV) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL3A1 gene (120180) on chromosome 2q32.2.

### Description

Polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic COL3A1 mutations. The brain phenotype consists of bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone variant, cerebellar microcysts, and white matter abnormalities, and resembles that of individuals with recessive GPR56 mutations. Some affected individuals also have a severe Ehlers-Danlos syndrome phenotype with vascular fragility. The phenotype varies among patients (Horn et al., 2017; Vandervore et al., 2017). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Plancke et al. (2009) studied a young girl with recessive Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) whose parents were consanguineous and healthy. She fulfilled the criteria of vascular EDS for laboratory testing. Type III collagen was absent in the proband.

Jørgensen et al. (2015) reported 2 sibs with vascular EDS. The proband (III-2), a boy, was born with a right-sided clubfoot and developed a unilateral hydrocele of the testis. He had crowded teeth, retrognathia, long slender fingers, and pale, translucent skin with visible subcutaneous vessels. His facial features were characteristic of EDS type IV, with a small lower jaw, prominent eyes, a pinched nose, and a thin upper lip. He died at 15 years of age. Autopsy showed 3 dissections: a transverse dissection of nearly the whole circumference of the thoracic aorta at the level of the ligamentum arteriosus, and separate dissections of the right renal artery and the left femoral artery. There was cystic medial necrosis of the aortic wall, and no macroscopic brain anomalies were found. His sister (III-3) was evaluated at 19 years of age. She had cerebral cortical dysplasia with bilaterally thickened frontoparietal cortices and small gyri, findings consistent with pachy micropolygyria. Her cognitive functioning was normal, a recent neurologic examination was normal, and she had never had epileptic seizures. MR arteriography and CT angiography showed a stenotic process in the right internal carotid artery, which ultrasonography showed to be a dissection. The left internal carotid artery was ectatic, and the right external iliac artery was stenotic. She had the same skin and facial features as her brother, with spontaneous ruptures of superficial vessels, long slender fingers, hypermobility of the small joints of the hands, and crowded teeth. Other findings included rupture of the medial meniscus and anterior cruciate ligament, intermittent abdominal pain, diarrhea, and blood in the stool with increased wall thickness of the transverse colon, migraine with aura, and minimal mitral leakage. She did not have aortic dilatation or varicose veins. Heterozygous carriers in the family varied markedly. The father, who carried a nonsense variant, had no abnormal findings at age 63, and paternal relatives with the same variant had no features of vascular EDS. The mother, who carried a missense variant, had thin skin, visible vessels, and reduced elasticity of the ascending aorta, but by age 60 she did not have the complete picture of vascular EDS.

Vandervore et al. (2017) studied a family with 2 affected and 2 unaffected sibs. Brain MRI in the affected sibs showed a cobblestone-like cortical malformation, white matter changes, and cerebellar cysts. The authors also reviewed brain imaging of 2 previously reported individuals with biallelic COL3A1 mutations. The brain phenotype in all of these patients was similar to that associated with GPR56 mutations, and all 3 families reported to date had a cobblestone-like malformation. ⚠︎

Horn et al. (2017) reported a 3-year-old girl with developmental delay who was born with bilateral clubfoot, joint laxity, and dysmorphic facial features. At 2 years of age she had an aneurysmal brain hemorrhage. Cerebral MRI showed profound abnormalities, including bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone variant. In a second family, 2 affected sibs had a cobblestone-like cortical malformation, cerebellar cysts, white matter abnormalities, developmental delay, and seizures. Horn et al. (2017) also considered 3 previously reported families. All families with biallelic COL3A1 variants had specific structural brain anomalies, and some had a severe EDS phenotype. The brain phenotype consisted of bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone variant, cerebellar microcysts, and white matter abnormalities, and resembled that of individuals with recessive GPR56 mutations.

### Biochemical Features

Jørgensen et al. (2015) found that cultured dermal fibroblasts from the compound heterozygous patient produced a reduced amount of type III procollagen. All of its chains had abnormal electrophoretic mobility, consistent with overmodification. Fibroblasts from the mother, who was heterozygous for the missense variant, produced mostly normal type III procollagen with a small amount of abnormal procollagen. Fibroblasts from the father, who was heterozygous for the nonsense variant, produced a reduced amount of apparently normal type III procollagen.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of PMGEDSV is autosomal recessive. Plancke et al. (2009) reported a homozygous COL3A1 mutation in a girl born to healthy consanguineous parents, each of whom carried the mutation. Jørgensen et al. (2015) found compound heterozygous COL3A1 variants in 2 affected sibs; the variants were in trans, one inherited from each parent. Vandervore et al. (2017) and Horn et al. (2017) reported affected sibs who were homozygous for a COL3A1 mutation. In the family reported by Jørgensen et al. (2015), heterozygous carriers showed marked clinical variability.

### Molecular Genetics

Plancke et al. (2009) sequenced the entire COL3A1 cDNA of a girl with recessive vascular EDS, born to consanguineous parents. They identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.479dupT) predicted to cause a frameshift and premature termination codon (Lys161GlnfsTer45). Studies of genomic DNA showed that the mutation was inherited from each parent. RT-PCR, quantitative PCR, immunohistochemistry, and Western blot analysis showed that the mutation caused strong mRNA decay and absence of type III collagen in the proband.

In 2 sibs with vascular EDS, one of whom also had bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria, Jørgensen et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous variants in the COL3A1 gene: a c.1786C-T transition resulting in an arg596-to-ter (R596X) substitution, and a c.3851G-A transition resulting in a gly1284-to-glu (G1284E) substitution. The father was heterozygous for the nonsense variant and the mother for the missense variant, confirming that the variants were in trans. An unaffected brother carried neither variant. The G1284E substitution was predicted to alter a highly conserved residue in the C-propeptide, within a calcium-binding region and next to a cysteine involved in interchain disulfide bonding, and to slow trimer assembly.

By exome analysis in a family with 2 affected sibs who had a cobblestone-like cortical malformation, Vandervore et al. (2017) identified a homozygous c.145C-G transversion in exon 2 of the COL3A1 gene, resulting in a pro49-to-ala (P49A) substitution.

In a 3-year-old girl with developmental delay, clubfoot, joint laxity, aneurysmal brain hemorrhage, and cobblestone-type bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria, Horn et al. (2017) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL3A1 gene. One was a c.1282C-T transition resulting in an arg428-to-ter (R428X) substitution, and the other was a 1-bp deletion (c.2057delC) resulting in a frameshift (Pro686LeufsTer105). Both mutations were novel. In a second family, Horn et al. (2017) found that 2 affected sibs were homozygous for the COL3A1 P49A missense variant.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Schwarze et al. (2001) studied 4 probands whose presenting feature was vascular aneurysm or rupture. They identified 3 heterozygous frameshift mutations in the COL3A1 gene (1832delAA, 413delC, and 555delT), which led to premature termination codons in exons 27, 6, and 9, respectively. A fourth patient carried a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the final exon (4294C-T; R1432X). The mRNAs from the frameshift alleles were transcribed efficiently but degraded rapidly, presumably by nonsense-mediated decay. The mRNA from the final-exon mutation was stable, but the truncated protein it encoded was not incorporated into mature type III procollagen molecules. The authors concluded that, unlike null mutations in COL1A1 and COL2A1, null mutations in COL3A1 produce phenotypes that overlap with vascular EDS.

In contrast, Plancke et al. (2009) noted that the expected COL3A1 haploinsufficiency in the healthy heterozygous relatives of their patient did not cause vascular EDS. They suggested a stochastic effect of COL3A1 haploinsufficiency, which might be explained by the relationship between the efficiency of nonsense-mediated decay and dominant-negative effects, depending on the position of the mutation and/or on modifying factors.

Vandervore et al. (2017) noted that the phenotype varied among patients with biallelic COL3A1 mutations. They suggested that genetic changes in different domains of type III procollagen can lead to different outcomes.

### Pathogenesis

Jeong et al. (2012) found no obvious defects in meningeal fibroblasts from Col3a1-null mice, and expression and glycosylation of alpha-dystroglycan were unchanged. They suggested that collagen III, a ligand of GPR56, regulates cortical development independently of the dystroglycan pathway. They also suggested that mutations in both COL3A1 alleles might be associated with a lethal form of cobblestone lissencephaly similar to Walker-Warburg syndrome, and that connective tissue diseases and brain malformations might share a common pathologic pathway.

Jørgensen et al. (2015) suggested that the interaction of type III collagen with GPR56 defines a pathway in frontal brain formation; mutations in GPR56 cause recessive bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria. They also suggested that a recessive syndromic form of vascular EDS includes both tissue fragility and cerebrocortical anomalies. However, they could not exclude that the brain finding was coincidental or due to variants in a second gene. ⚠︎

Vandervore et al. (2017) noted that type III procollagen is the ligand of GPR56 and that biallelic GPR56 mutations cause a similar cobblestone-like malformation. They concluded that the cobblestone-like malformation in patients with biallelic COL3A1 mutations highlights the critical role of the type III collagen-GPR56 axis and the pial membrane in brain development and cortical lamination. Horn et al. (2017) concluded that, consistent with findings in knockout mice, collagen III has a role in regulating cortical development in addition to its function in connective tissue formation.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (1997) inactivated the Col3a1 gene in mouse embryonic stem cells by homologous recombination. About 10% of homozygous mutant mice survived to adulthood, but they lived much shorter lives than wildtype mice. The main cause of death was rupture of the major blood vessels, similar to patients with type IV EDS. Ultrastructural analysis of tissues from the null mice showed that type III collagen was essential for normal collagen I fibrillogenesis in the cardiovascular system and other organs.

Jeong et al. (2012) found that Col3a1-null mice had a cobblestone-like cortical malformation, characterized by breakdown of the pial basement membrane, neuronal overmigration, abnormal attachment of radial glial endfeet, and marginal zone heterotopias. Ectopias were seen in 23 of 28 null embryos and in none of the wildtype or heterozygous embryos. The pial basement membrane formed normally at embryonic day (E) 10.5. Regional breakdown of the membrane, together with neurons migrating past it, appeared in about half of E11.5 embryos and in all embryos older than E12.5. The ectopias contained cortical neurons from both deep and superficial layers, Cajal-Retzius cells, and interneurons.
