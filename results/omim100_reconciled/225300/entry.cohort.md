### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene (601906) on chromosome 12q13.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is an autosomal recessive limb malformation. It involves central reductions of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands, and expression varies within families. Several families with affected sibs born to consanguineous parents have been reported. SHFM6 is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split foot in 2 sibs.

Verma et al. (1976) reported an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. Both sibships were born to first-cousin parents.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Hennekam and Lommen (1988) reported ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split hand/foot malformation.

Gul and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 cases of split hand/split foot malformation in an inbred kindred. Expression of the malformation varied within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM and 13 affected members. Twelve had central reductions of the feet, with or without involvement of the hands. The remaining patient had the mildest phenotype, which the authors classified as atypical SHFM.

Blattner et al. (2010) reported a patient with sporadic SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with split-hand/foot malformation, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. The patient and all of her family members had normal bone density, and none were obese. The authors noted that some patients with WNT10B variants have been obese. They suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated findings of SHFM6.

### Inheritance

Freire-Maia (1971) described a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it meant for genetic counseling. Verma et al. (1976) reported affected sibships born to first-cousin parents. They concluded that their report and earlier ones further showed that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Mufti and Wood (1987) concluded that autosomal recessive inheritance was likely in their family with affected sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) discussed the possibility of a rare autosomal recessive form of split hand/foot malformation. They also proposed an alternative two-locus model, in which a dominant mutation at a second, controlling locus would cause nonpenetrance of a dominant split hand/foot mutation. Gul and Oktenli (2002) discussed autosomal recessive inheritance and a two-locus model for their inbred kindred. They contrasted this with the autosomal dominant inheritance seen in most familial cases.

In their kindred, Ugur and Tolun (2008) found an asymptomatic female who was homozygous for the WNT10B mutation. They proposed 2 explanations: either a second locus contributes to manifestation of the SHFM phenotype, or a suppressor locus prevented the trait from appearing in this nonpenetrant female. They suggested that inheritance was complex.

Blattner et al. (2010) concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait and may be inherited in a recessive manner. In that case, the recurrence risk is 25% for sibs. For offspring of patients, the risk is very low unless there is consanguinity.

### Mapping

Ugur and Tolun (2008) performed homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. They identified a novel SHFM locus at 12q13.11-q13, with a maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47.

### Heterogeneity

Elliott and Evans (2006) noted that split-hand/foot malformation is genetically heterogeneous, with at least 5 loci identified. They also noted that it occurs both as an isolated malformation and as part of syndromes, and shows variable expressivity and reduced penetrance.

In their kindred, Ugur and Tolun (2008) excluded linkage to 4 of the 5 known SHFM loci. At the TP63 gene (603273), the SHFM4 locus, they found a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) in most affected subjects. This variant lies in the alternate promoter used to transcribe the N-terminally truncated p63 isoform.

### Molecular Genetics

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. They identified a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene, arg332-to-trp (R332W), in all affected members except the 1 atypical case. An asymptomatic female was also homozygous for the mutation. In most affected subjects they also detected a rare TP63 insertion variant (rs34201045) at the alternate promoter for the N-terminally truncated p63 isoform.

In a patient with sporadic SHFM, Blattner et al. (2010) identified a homozygous 4-bp duplication in the WNT10B gene that results in a premature termination codon. Nine relatives who were heterozygous for the duplication showed no sign of SHFM.

In an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows, Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a novel homozygous 3-bp deletion in the WNT10B gene (c.695_697delACA), which removes asn232 (N232del). The deletion lies at a critical position in the protein structure. Protein modeling predicted that it was likely to severely affect protein structure and function. The authors predicted that it would probably shorten the protein's lifetime and prevent binding to its receptors and other ligands.

### Diagnosis

Blattner et al. (2010) recommended testing patients with sporadic SHFM for WNT10B mutations to determine the recurrence risk.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPLIT-HAND/FOOT MALFORMATION 6**  
WNT10B, ARG332TRP [p.R332W]

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. In this kindred they identified a homozygous arg332-to-trp (R332W) mutation in the WNT10B gene in 12 affected members, who had central reductions of the feet with or without involvement of the hands. The mutation was absent in 1 affected member with the mildest, atypical phenotype. It was also found in the homozygous state in an asymptomatic female, which led the authors to suggest complex inheritance involving a second locus or a suppressor locus. The mutation was found by homozygosity mapping to 12q13.11-q13 (maximum multipoint lod score, 5.47). ⚠︎
