### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN9A gene (603415) on chromosome 2q24.3.

### Description

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEPD), previously known as familial rectal pain, is an inherited condition characterized by paroxysms of rectal, ocular, or submandibular pain with flushing (Fertleman et al., 2006). Onset is in the neonatal period or infancy. Autonomic manifestations such as skin flushing, harlequin color change, tonic attacks, and syncope with bradycardia predominate early, and attacks of excruciating deep burning pain follow later. The disorder persists throughout life (Fertleman et al., 2007).

### Nomenclature

Fertleman and Ferrie (2006) and Fertleman et al. (2007) proposed that the disorder previously called familial rectal pain syndrome be renamed paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.

### Clinical Features

Hayden and Grossman (1959) reported a family with rectal, ocular, and submaxillary pain. They described it as a familial autonomic disorder related to proctalgia fugax.

Dugan (1972) reported on familial rectal pain.

Fertleman et al. (2006) studied 11 families and 2 sporadic cases with PEPD. They described the condition as an inherited disorder with paroxysms of rectal, ocular, or submandibular pain with flushing.

Fertleman et al. (2007) identified 77 individuals from 15 families. Onset was in the neonatal period or infancy, and the disorder persisted throughout life. Autonomic manifestations predominated at first: all patients had skin flushing, and most had harlequin color change and tonic attacks. Dramatic syncopes with bradycardia, and sometimes asystole, were common. Later in life, the disorder was characterized by attacks of excruciating deep burning pain, often in the rectal, ocular, or jaw areas, but also diffuse. Attacks of pain were triggered by factors such as defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion.

Meglič et al. (2014) described a 3-year-old girl with a rare clinical picture of PEPD: she had had extremely painful voiding since birth. Her father had occasionally had some pain in his jaw while yawning since childhood.

### Inheritance

Fertleman et al. (2007) described paroxysmal extreme pain disorder as an autosomal dominant sodium channelopathy involving SCN9A. Meglič et al. (2014) found transmission of a heterozygous SCN9A mutation from an affected father to his affected daughter, which is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Fertleman et al. (2006) performed a genome-wide linkage search in families with PEPD and followed it with mutation analysis of the candidate gene SCN9A.

### Molecular Genetics

In 11 families and 2 sporadic cases with PEPD, Fertleman et al. (2006) identified 8 heterozygous missense mutations in the SCN9A gene, which encodes the sodium channel Nav1.7. They found the gene by a genome-wide linkage search followed by mutation analysis of SCN9A as a candidate gene. In vitro functional analysis of 3 of the mutant channels showed reduced fast inactivation, which led to a persistent sodium current. ⚠︎

In a 3-year-old girl with PEPD who had extremely painful voiding, Meglič et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.554G-A mutation in the SCN9A gene, resulting in an arg185-to-his (R185H) substitution. The mutation was inherited from her father, who had occasional jaw pain while yawning.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Meglič et al. (2014) noted that the SCN9A R185H mutation found in their patient and her father had also been reported in patients with small fiber sensory neuropathy. They suggested that this could be explained by the intra- and interfamilial phenotypic variability associated with gain-of-function Nav1.7 variants.

### Pathogenesis

Fertleman et al. (2006) found by in vitro functional analysis that 3 PEPD-associated mutant Nav1.7 channels had reduced fast inactivation, which led to a persistent sodium current. By contrast, primary erythermalgia mutations cause more negative activation thresholds. They concluded that PEPD and primary erythermalgia are allelic variants with distinct biophysical mechanisms.

### Diagnosis

Fertleman et al. (2007) noted that the disorder is most likely to be misdiagnosed as epilepsy and, particularly in infancy, as hyperekplexia and reflex anoxic seizures.

### Clinical Management

Fertleman et al. (2006) noted that carbamazepine is effective in PEPD but not in primary erythermalgia. In vitro, carbamazepine selectively blocked the persistent current of PEPD mutant channels but did not affect the negative activation threshold of a primary erythermalgia mutant.

Fertleman et al. (2007) found that carbamazepine was effective in almost all patients who tried it, but the response was often incomplete.

Meglič et al. (2014) reported that carbamazepine significantly reduced the pain in a 3-year-old girl with PEPD who had extremely painful voiding.
