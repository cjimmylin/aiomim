### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cataract-2 of multiple types (CTRCT2), with or without microcornea, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gamma-C-crystallin gene (CRYGC; 123680) on chromosome 2q33.3. ⚠︎

### Description

CTRCT2 is an autosomal dominant congenital lens opacity. Families with CRYGC mutations have shown different types of cataract: Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus, highly variable zonular pulverulent cataract, and nuclear congenital cataract. In some families the nuclear cataract was accompanied by microcornea, nystagmus, and amblyopia. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family with hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus, which occurred in heterozygotes.

Scott et al. (1994) described a 7-generation family with autosomal dominant congenital cataract of the pulverulent type. Of 138 individuals, 48 were known to be affected. Of the 53 individuals examined, 28 had congenital cataracts: 19 had unilateral cataract (8 right eyes and 11 left eyes) and 9 had bilateral cataract. The cataract appeared seemingly at random as unilateral or bilateral. Including historic data, there were 48 affected members: 28 with unilateral cataract, 17 with bilateral cataract, and 3 obligate carriers who were not examined. In patients with unilateral cataract, detailed slit-lamp examination of the clinically unaffected eye showed no lens opacity. Severity ranged from a subtle unilateral zonular cataract with 20/20 visual acuity, through bilateral inner fetal nuclear pulverulent opacities with 20/16 visual acuity in both eyes, to dense unilateral and bilateral nuclear cataracts that required early surgical removal. ⚠︎

Ren et al. (2000) studied a previously described 7-generation family in which 30 members had highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) described a family with autosomal dominant primary nuclear congenital cataract.

Zhang et al. (2009) studied a 4-generation family from northern China in which 6 members had nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The affected individuals had bilateral congenital nuclear cataracts, with a central nuclear opacity affecting the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nucleus of the lens. They also had congenitally small eyeballs and corneas with a diameter of approximately 9 to 10 mm. Every affected individual had the same poor visual acuity of hand movement in front of the eye, and all had nystagmus and amblyopia.

Li et al. (2012) studied a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract.

Guo et al. (2012) described a 4-generation Chinese family with isolated bilateral autosomal dominant nuclear cataracts and microcornea. All affected individuals had bilateral nuclear cataracts and microcornea with a diameter of approximately 9 mm. The opacities involved the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nuclei. All patients had nystagmus and amblyopia, with normal intraocular pressure and no systemic abnormalities. The proband (II:7), a man, was diagnosed with bilateral nuclear cataract and microcornea at age 8 years. He underwent iridectomy on both eyes at age 8, and phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation in the left eye at age 38. Individual I:2 was examined at age 68 years. Individual II:2 was diagnosed at age 13 years. Individual III:3 had cataract extraction at age 6 years. Individuals III:6 and IV:1 were first diagnosed at ages 6 months and 1 year, respectively.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CTRCT2 is autosomal dominant. Lubsen et al. (1987) observed Coppock-like cataract in heterozygotes. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also described by Scott et al. (1994), Ren et al. (2000), Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007), Zhang et al. (2009), Li et al. (2012), and Guo et al. (2012).

### Mapping

Lubsen et al. (1987) found that polymorphic TaqI sites in the gamma-crystallin gene cluster could define 8 possible haplotypes. Only 3 of these (Q, R, and S) were found in the Dutch and English population. A fourth haplotype (P) was found in a family with hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus. Haplotype P was present only in family members with cataract, and all family members with cataract had haplotype P. The cataract locus was closely linked to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, with a maximum lod score of 7.58 at theta = 0. Lubsen et al. (1987) concluded that the primary cause of a human cataract could possibly be a lesion in a crystallin gene.

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) mapped the polymorphic TaqI sites closely linked to the Coppock-like cataract locus. These sites defined a 15-kb region encompassing the gamma-D and pseudo-gamma-E crystallin genes.

Scott et al. (1994) reported that work was under way to determine whether the causative gene in their 7-generation family was linked to previously defined cataract loci on chromosomes 1, 2, or 16.

Ren et al. (2000) mapped the cataract in a 7-generation family to a 19-cM interval on chromosome 2q33-q35 that includes the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. The maximum lod score was 4.56 (theta = 0.02) with D2S157, and the lod score with CRYG was 3.57 (theta = 0.052).

Zhang et al. (2009) found linkage of autosomal dominant nuclear cataract with microcornea in a Chinese family to chromosome 2q32.3-q35. The maximum 2-point lod score was 2.29 at marker D2S325 (theta = 0.0). Haplotype analysis placed the locus in a 19.04-cM region between D2S117 and D2S2382 that contains the gamma-crystallin gene cluster.

### Molecular Genetics

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) cloned the gamma-D and pseudo-gamma-E crystallin genes from the Coppock-like cataract chromosome. The gamma-D gene was functionally equivalent to the wildtype allele. The pseudo-gamma-E gene had a cluster of sequence changes in and around its TATA box. Together these changes increased promoter activity 10-fold, raising its expression to 30% of that of gamma-D.

In the original Coppock-like cataract family, Héon et al. (1999) identified a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The mutation was not seen in a large control population. They also found that the change in the CRYGE pseudogene previously reported as the Coppock-like cataract mutation was a polymorphism present in 23% of their control population. They noted that, to date, there was no direct evidence that up-regulation of a pseudogene causes cataracts. Héon et al. (1999) concluded that their findings were the first evidence that CRYGC is involved in human cataract formation.

In a 7-generation family with autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. (2000) identified a 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The duplication was found in 1 allele of each affected family member and was absent from unaffected family members and unaffected controls. Ren et al. (2000) found that activation of the pseudo-gamma-E-crystallin gene was unlikely to cause the cataracts in this family. Base changes in its TATA box were found in unaffected controls, so they could be excluded as a sole cause. The CRYGD gene showed no abnormalities.

Santhiya et al. (2002) reported novel mutations in the gamma-crystallin genes that caused autosomal dominant congenital cataracts.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) sequenced the CRYGA-D gene cluster in affected members of a family with autosomal dominant nuclear congenital cataract. They identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The mutation segregated with the cataract. Healthy family members and 170 unrelated controls had a normal sequence. Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) concluded that R168W is the actual disease-causing mutation and should no longer be considered a SNP, as it was listed in the NCBI database.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.470G-A transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with cataract in all affected members and was absent from unaffected members and 100 controls. W157 is a highly conserved tryptophan. The mutation was predicted to truncate 17 amino acids from the C terminus of gamma-C-crystallin and disrupt the fourth Greek key motif, possibly altering folding, stability, and/or solubility. ⚠︎

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) sequenced candidate genes and identified a heterozygous c.385G-T transversion in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. Molecular modeling and spectroscopy showed that the mutation impaired the tertiary structure of gamma-C-crystallin by modifying the H-bonding network in the C-terminal domain. The mutation also dramatically decreased the thermal stability of the protein and increased its propensity to aggregate under high concentration, heat, and UV irradiation stresses. ⚠︎

In affected members of a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) identified a c.471G-A transition (TGG-TGA) in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. The mutation was predicted to truncate 18 C-terminal amino acids and disrupt the fourth Greek key motif. It cosegregated with the disease, was absent from unaffected family members, and was not found in 100 controls.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) noted that the CRYGC R168W mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract, whereas their family had nuclear congenital cataract. They described this as the first case of phenotypic heterogeneity in primary congenital cataract specifically associated with R168W.

Guo et al. (2012) noted that the same W157X protein change, resulting from a different nucleotide substitution (c.470G-A), had previously been reported in an unrelated Chinese family with a similar phenotype of nuclear cataract and microcornea. They concluded that W157X is disease-causing rather than a polymorphism.

### Pathogenesis

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) noted that the predicted protein product of the pseudo-gamma-E gene was a 6-kD N-terminal gamma-crystallin fragment. They suggested that reactivation of the pseudogene, with the resulting overexpression of this fragment, could cause Coppock-like cataract. Héon et al. (1999) later found that the pseudogene change was a common polymorphism in controls (see Molecular Genetics).

Ren et al. (2000) predicted that the CRYGC 5-bp duplication disrupts the reading frame and produces an unstable gamma-C-crystallin. The predicted protein would contain 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids. They suggested that mutant beta-gamma-crystallins may not need to associate properly into oligomers to cause cataracts.

Zhang et al. (2009) suggested that mutations disrupting lens biochemistry early in development may cause microphakia and secondary microcornea.

### Animal Model

Li et al. (2012) found that expressing the gamma-C-crystallin G129C mutant in zebrafish caused vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens, recapitulating the human cataract phenotype.

### History

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) suggested that activation of the gamma-E-crystallin pseudogene, caused by sequence changes in and around its TATA box, could cause the Coppock-like cataract in the family studied by Lubsen et al. (1987). Héon et al. (1999) found that this pseudogene change was a polymorphism present in 23% of controls. In the same family, they identified a missense mutation in the CRYGC gene. Ren et al. (2000) likewise found TATA box changes in the pseudogene in unaffected controls. ⚠︎

**.0002 CATARACT 2, ZONULAR PULVERULENT**

In a 7-generation family in which 30 members had highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. The duplication was found in 1 allele of each affected family member and was absent from unaffected family members and unaffected controls. It was predicted to disrupt the reading frame and produce an unstable protein containing 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids.

**.0003 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR CONGENITAL**  
CRYGC, ARG168TRP [c.502C>T; p.R168W]

In affected members of a family with autosomal dominant nuclear congenital cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. It was not found in healthy family members or in 170 unrelated controls. Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) noted that this mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract and should no longer be considered a SNP.

The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was absent from unaffected members and 100 controls. In affected members of a 4-generation Chinese family with bilateral congenital nuclear cataract, microcornea, nystagmus, and amblyopia, Guo et al. Guo et al. (2012) noted that the same protein change, resulting from the c.470G-A substitution, had been reported in an unrelated Chinese family with a similar phenotype.

**.0005 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR CONGENITAL**  
CRYGC, GLY129CYS [c.385G>T; p.G129C]

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al.
