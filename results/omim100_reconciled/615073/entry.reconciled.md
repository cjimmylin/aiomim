### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant form of primary torsion dystonia. In the families reported by Fuchs et al. (2013), onset was usually in adulthood and most often in the neck. The dystonia stayed focal in some patients and spread to segmental or generalized dystonia in others. Cranial and speech involvement were common, but arm involvement was less frequent.

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 individuals from 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected, with a mean age at onset of 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 28 mutation carriers with definite dystonia from 8 families. Mean age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of patients, and 93% had neck involvement at their last examination, but only 46% still had focal dystonia. Cranial involvement was seen in 57% and speech involvement in 44%. No patient had onset in the arms, and only 32% developed arm involvement. The authors noted that this feature distinguished GNAL dystonia from DYT6.

Family D1 of Fuchs et al. (2013) was of Caucasian mixed European ancestry and included 7 affected individuals (4 females and 3 males), with onset between 7 and 50 years of age. The male proband had onset in the legs at age 7 years and had generalized dystonia at age 69. The other 6 affected members had segmental dystonia. In all 6 it began in the neck, together with the larynx and trunk in 1 and the face in another.

Family P of Fuchs et al. (2013) was of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite ancestry and included 6 affected individuals, with onset between 25 and 48 years of age. The female proband had onset in the leg at age 25 years and had generalized dystonia at age 47. One affected male had generalized dystonia that began in the neck at age 35. Of the other 4 affected members, 3 had focal dystonia and 1 had segmental dystonia, all with neck onset.

The other 6 families reported by Fuchs et al. (2013) were of Caucasian mixed European ancestry. In family S, 2 males had neck-onset dystonia at ages 18 and 17 years. The disease was focal in one and segmental in the other. Family D2 had 4 affected females. The proband had segmental dystonia that began in the jaw at age 39 years. Two relatives had segmental neck-onset dystonia beginning at ages 33 and 18 years. A fourth relative had focal dystonia that began in the tongue at age 11 years. In family H, 3 individuals had focal neck dystonia, with onset at 47, 38, and 31 years. In family B, a male had focal neck dystonia from age 54 years and the female proband had segmental neck-onset dystonia from age 36 years. In family T, 2 males had focal neck dystonia from ages 25 and 42 years, and a female had segmental dystonia that began in the larynx at age 25 years. Family N was represented by a single male proband with focal neck dystonia that began at age 20 years.

### Inheritance

Fuchs et al. (2013) showed autosomal dominant inheritance of dystonia-25. Heterozygous GNAL mutations segregated with the disease in all families in which DNA from multiple affected individuals was available. Dystonia was inherited equally from the maternal and paternal sides.

### Mapping

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that the GNAL gene lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci. They suggested that loss of GNAL may contribute to the dystonia seen in patients with 18p deletions.

### Heterogeneity

Bressman et al. (1994) used linkage data to exclude the DYT1 region on chromosome 9q34 in a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset torsion dystonia that mainly began in the neck. They concluded that a gene other than DYT1 causes some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with primary torsion dystonia and identified heterozygous mutations in the GNAL gene. Family P carried a nonsense mutation (S293X) and family D1 carried a missense mutation (V137M). In family D1, V137M was among the 20 missense variants shared by all 4 sequenced members, and it segregated with the disease. Sanger sequencing of 39 additional multiplex primary torsion dystonia families that were negative for DYT1 and DYT6 identified GNAL mutations in 6 more families (19%). These were a missense mutation (E155K), 2 frameshift insertions (S95fsX110 and R198fsX210), a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C), a nonsense mutation (R21X), and an in-frame deletion (P102-V104del). None of the variants were present in dbSNP135, about 3,500 European exomes, or 572 control chromosomes. Segregation analysis confirmed that the mutations were transmitted with the disease in all families for which DNA from multiple affected individuals was available.

Fuchs et al. (2013) tested the mutants in a BRET assay of Galpha-olf coupling to the D1 dopamine receptor (D1R). The S293X mutant failed to support any D1R-driven responses. The E155K and V137M mutants showed intermediate phenotypes, largely consistent with impaired association with G-beta-gamma subunits. A control variant, V16F, behaved like wildtype. The authors suggested that the results pointed to a loss-of-function mechanism.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no correlation between genotype and phenotype by mutation type, and phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation. They saw no apparent phenotypic difference by parental origin of the mutation, but said further study was needed to determine whether parental origin affects penetrance or expression.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that Galpha-olf replaces Galpha-s in striatal medium spiny neurons. There it couples D1 dopamine and A2A adenosine receptors to adenylyl cyclase type 5. They suggested that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1R and/or A2AR transmission, located postsynaptically in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.
