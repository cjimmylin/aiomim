### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), also called tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) deficiency, is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13. ⚠︎

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), or TYK2 deficiency, is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency. The first reported patient had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome and showed susceptibility to viral, fungal, and mycobacterial infections together with atopic dermatitis and elevated serum IgE (Minegishi et al., 2006). Patients reported later did not have the features of hyper-IgE syndrome, and Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infections.

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient showed unusual susceptibility to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE. The patient's cells had defective signaling for type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, born in 1992, who was the third child of healthy first-cousin parents. He was vaccinated with BCG at 2 months of age, and at 8 months left axillary lymphadenopathy was found. He developed disseminated BCG infection with generalized fistulous lymphadenopathy and fever. He also had neurobrucellosis with a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, severe sensorineural hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, and lacunar infarcts on MRI. He had pneumonia and herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve. Serum IgE measured at 2, 11, 16, and 18 years of age was normal or slightly high (80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml, respectively), and serum IgG, IgM, and IgA were normal. His PPD skin test was negative, and his antibody response to tetanus toxoid was low. He had no atopy or eczema, staphylococcal skin infections, skeletal or dental abnormalities, or candidiasis. Kilic et al. (2012) noted that, unlike the first Japanese TYK2-deficient patient, their patient had none of the cardinal features of hyper-IgE syndrome. They suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in patients with BCG disease, particularly when severe herpesvirus infections are also present, even without features of hyper-IgE syndrome.

Kreins et al. (2015) restudied the Japanese patient (P1) reported by Minegishi et al. (2006), a man born to consanguineous parents. He had had atopic dermatitis since 1 month of age and recurrent bacterial and viral infections since 1 year of age. These included recurrent Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, BCGitis, nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis with sepsis, parainfluenza virus 3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV infections, molluscum contagiosum, mild oral candidiasis, pneumonia without pneumatocele, otitis, and sinusitis. He also had ocular sarcoidosis. Repeated studies showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE (1,430-2,100 IU/ml).

Kreins et al. (2015) also studied 7 newly identified TYK2-deficient patients from 5 kindreds originating from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. P2 was the Turkish man, born to first-cousin parents, who had been reported by Kilic et al. (2012). According to Kreins et al. (2015), he had disseminated BCG lymphadenitis at 21 months of age and severe bacterial meningitis at 8 years, with CSF cultures positive for Brucella. At 11 years he was diagnosed with shingles of a branch of the trigeminal nerve. He had no atopy, high serum IgE, or staphylococcal infections.

In kindred C, a Moroccan family with consanguineous parents, P3 was a girl who was hospitalized at 13 years of age with suspected abdominal tuberculosis (Kreins et al., 2015). Her abdominal complaints persisted despite antimycobacterial treatment, and a few months later she developed ascites and a psoas abscess. Treatment was restarted, but she was readmitted with bacterial meningitis and died 15 months after symptom onset. Her younger brother (P4) was hospitalized at 8 months of age for meningitis of unknown etiology. He had recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels, but no high serum IgE. He was 15 years old, without treatment, at the time of the report.

In kindred D, an Iranian family with consanguineous parents, P5 was a 5-year-old boy who was vaccinated with BCG at birth (Kreins et al., 2015). He developed right axillary lymphadenitis at 2 months and multiple cervical lymphadenitis at 4 months. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with fistulization and pulmonary involvement. His 2-year-old younger sister (P6), also vaccinated with BCG at birth, had BCG lymphadenopathy for 1 month after vaccination. At 1 year of age she was hospitalized for 1 week for an unknown viral infection of the skin.

P7, from kindred E, was a 9-year-old Iranian girl born to consanguineous parents (Kreins et al., 2015). She had pneumonia at 8 months of age and later miliary tuberculosis. Spiral lung CT showed diffuse bilateral micronodular infiltrates, hilar and mediastinal adenopathies, and cavitary consolidation in the right upper lobe. P8, from kindred F, was an Argentinian boy. He had herpes gingivostomatitis at 18 months of age, then aseptic meningitis (fever, vomiting, irritability, and altered mental state or reduced consciousness) and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions at 24 months. He also had exanthema subitum. He had mild mental disability, and it was not clearly established whether it was congenital or occurred after the meningitis.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that none of the 7 newly identified patients had features of hyper-IgE syndrome (atopy, high serum IgE, or staphylococcal disease), and none had fungal infections or mucocutaneous candidiasis. Four of the 7 had a pure phenotype of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (P4 and P5) or of tuberculosis (P3 and P7). The authors concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infections, and they suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in children with severe tuberculosis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels above 1000 U/mL. The patient had no viral or mycobacterial infections. NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, were normal.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy, aged 1 year and 11 months, who was the second child of young nonconsanguineous parents. He was born in 2016 and vaccinated with BCG on the third day of life. He developed an abscess and ulceration at the BCG vaccination site and BCG-associated tuberculosis of the axillary lymph nodes. He also had recurrent respiratory tract infections, bacterial pneumonia, diarrhea, Salmonella infection, Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, and influenza virus infection. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal illness, or lymphopenia. The percentage of B cells was increased and that of NK cells was decreased. His motor development was normal, but he did not say simple words until 3 years and 4 months of age, and he had a small head circumference. Wu et al. (2020) also reviewed 12 patients with TYK2 deficiency. Of these, 58% had intracellular bacterial infections, 58% had recurrent viral infections, and 25% had markedly increased serum IgE. None had the facial, skeletal, dental, or vascular features of STAT3-related hyper-IgE syndrome.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 TYK2-deficient patients who presented with or without elevated IgE and atopy and had distinct profiles of pathogen infection. The patients' cells showed heterogeneous responses to IFN-alpha, IFN-beta, IFN-gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23, and lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted. The authors noted that TYK2-deficient patients do not necessarily present with features of hyper-IgE syndrome.

Zhang et al. (2022) studied an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. Three of them were unrelated Turkish children with TYK2 deficiency, each born to a different set of consanguineous parents. P2, a 2-year-old girl, was hospitalized for moderate COVID-19 pneumonia but did not need oxygen therapy. Her earlier history included hospitalization for sepsis as a neonate, 2 ICU admissions for fever and dyspnea before 5 months of age, a diagnosis of Kawasaki disease at 11 months, and hospitalization for infection with the VZV vaccine at 2 years. P3, a 4-year-old boy, and P4, a 9-year-old boy, were both admitted to the ICU for critical COVID-19 pneumonia and needed high-flow oxygen therapy. P3 had been hospitalized for neonatal sepsis and admitted to the ICU for influenza pneumonia at 3 years. P4 had recurrent bronchitis requiring hospital admission and inhaler therapy. Neither boy had an adverse reaction to MMR vaccination. All 3 survived. P3's 3-year-old younger sister, who carried the same genotype, had a history of critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and ventilation and of disseminated varicella. She had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and remained seronegative. Zhang et al. (2022) found critical COVID-19 pneumonia in these unrelated patients surprising, because TYK2 deficiency impairs but does not abolish cellular responses to type I IFNs. They noted that more infected TYK2-deficient patients would be needed to estimate penetrance, which may be incomplete.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Affected individuals were homozygous for TYK2 mutations, and most were born to consanguineous parents (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2022). In the kindreds studied by Kreins et al. (2015), clinical disease was seen in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. In the family reported by Wu et al. (2020), the parents of the affected boy were heterozygous for the mutation. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis.

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells had defects in several cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. Transduction of intact TYK2 restored the signaling defects.

In a Turkish boy with TYK2 deficiency born to consanguineous parents, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del), which created a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein could be detected by Western blotting in the patient's EBV-B cells.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that the Japanese patient (P1) reported by Minegishi et al. (2006) was homozygous for a 4-bp deletion in exon 4 of TYK2 (C70HfsX21). They described the mutation in P2, the patient of Kilic et al. (2012), as a 9-bp deletion in exon 16, c.2303_2311del (L767X). In 7 newly identified patients from 5 kindreds, they found 5 different homozygous null mutations in TYK2: c.3318_3319insC (T1106HfsX4) in sibs P3 and P4, c.462G-T (E154X) in sibs P5 and P6, c.149delC (S50HfsX1) in P7, and c.1912C-T (R638X) in P8. All 5 mutations were predicted to cause premature stop codons, none was found in public databases, and TYK2 protein could not be detected in the patients' cells.

In a Chinese boy with TYK2 deficiency born to nonconsanguineous parents, Wu et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.2395G-A missense mutation in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. In the patient's cells, TYK2 mRNA expression was decreased and no TYK2 protein was detected on Western blot. In transfected HEK293T cells, the mutation also decreased mRNA and protein expression of FLAG- and GFP-tagged TYK2. The patient was also heterozygous for variants in CFH (c.2089C-T, L697F), LRRC8A (c.1250G-A, R417Q), and NIPBL (c.5575-27_c.5575-26delCT), which the authors considered of uncertain significance.

In 5 TYK2-deficient patients, Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations and confirmed their pathogenic effects using qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays.

In 3 unrelated Turkish children born to consanguineous parents, found in a cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified the same homozygous frameshift variant in TYK2 (P216Rfs*14). This variant had previously been shown to cause loss of expression, and the authors confirmed that it was loss of function. The younger sister of one patient carried the same homozygous genotype. ACE2-transduced SV40 fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for the variant did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally and were not protected by IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment. These cells also failed to induce ISGs in response to infection or to IFN-alpha-2b. Zhang et al. (2022) proposed autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency as a new genetic cause of COVID-19 pneumonia.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that TYK2 deficiency probably accounted for the complex clinical picture of their patient, including impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation. They noted that TYK2 has obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals in human innate and acquired immunity, which differs substantially from its function in mice.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that the absence of TYK2 caused defective IL-12 signaling and impaired IFN-gamma production, leading to susceptibility to BCG and Brucella. They suggested that the patient's viral illnesses were probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that cells from all 8 TYK2-deficient patients had impaired, but not abolished, responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta, and IL-10. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda, and LIF were normal. Surface expression of IFNAR1, IL10R2, and IL12RB1 was reduced, reflecting the scaffolding role of TYK2, and was rescued by expression of wildtype TYK2. Cells from the 7 newly identified patients responded normally to IL-6, but cells from P1, the original patient with hyper-IgE syndrome, were hyporesponsive. Neither expression of wildtype TYK2 nor silencing of TYK2 rescued the IL-6 hyporesponsiveness in P1 cells. The authors suggested that impaired IL-6 responses and hyper-IgE syndrome are not intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency, and that P1 may carry a variant in another, unknown component of the IL-6 pathway. Ex vivo, the proportions of IL-17-positive T cells and the numbers of central and effector memory T cells were normal, although IL-17 production by naive CD4 T cells was profoundly impaired in vitro. The authors suggested that residual IL-23 responses explain the absence of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) found that a TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in a monocyte assay. IL-10 signaling was partly reduced, along with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, whereas IL-6 signaling was not reduced. Type III IFN signaling was almost normal, and virus control was only minimally impaired in a TYK2-deficient human cell line. The authors suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may help protect TYK2-deficient patients against viruses, leading to a surprisingly mild human phenotype.

Lv et al. (2022) found heterogeneous responses of patient PBMCs to IFN-alpha, IFN-beta, IFN-gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. They proposed that TYK2 acts as a multi-tasker that orchestrates various cytokine signaling pathways, and that different combinations of defects account for the different clinical manifestations.

### Diagnosis

Fuchs et al. (2016) established a simple diagnostic monocyte assay that detects loss of the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in TYK2 deficiency.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) found no individuals homozygous for TYK2 stop mutations in any database, and only 84 heterozygous individuals.

Wu et al. (2020) noted that the c.2395G-A (G799R) variant was present in the gnomAD database, suggesting that it was not a private mutation.
