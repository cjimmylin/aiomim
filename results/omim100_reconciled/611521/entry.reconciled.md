### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.2.

### Description

TYK2 deficiency is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that its core clinical phenotype is mycobacterial and/or viral infection, including disease caused by BCG vaccine and tuberculosis. The first reported patient had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES), with atopic dermatitis and elevated serum IgE (Minegishi et al., 2006). Most patients reported later had none of the cardinal features of HIES (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. He showed unusual susceptibility to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, born in 1992, who was the third child of healthy first-cousin parents. He was vaccinated with BCG at 2 months of age. At 8 months, left axillary lymphadenopathy was detected, and he went on to develop disseminated BCG infection with generalized fistulous lymphadenopathy and fever. He later had neurobrucellosis, a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, severe sensorineural hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, lacunar infarcts on MRI, pneumonia, and herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve. Serum IgE concentrations measured at ages 2, 11, 16, and 18 years were normal or slightly high (80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml, respectively). His PPD skin test was negative and his antibody response to tetanus toxoid was low. He had no atopy or eczema, staphylococcal skin infections, skeletal or dental abnormalities, or candidiasis, and his serum IgG, IgM, and IgA were normal. Kilic et al. (2012) noted that, unlike the first Japanese patient with TYK2 deficiency, their patient had none of the cardinal features of HIES. They suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in patients with BCG disease, particularly when accompanied by severe herpesvirus infections, even without features of HIES.

Kreins et al. (2015) restudied the Japanese patient (P1) originally reported by Minegishi et al. (2006). He was born to consanguineous parents, had atopic dermatitis from 1 month of age, and had recurrent bacterial and viral infections from 1 year of age. His infections included recurrent Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, BCGitis, nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis with sepsis, parainfluenza virus 3 pneumonia, recurrent oral herpes simplex virus infections, molluscum contagiosum, mild oral candidiasis, pneumonia without pneumatocele, otitis, and sinusitis. He also had ocular sarcoidosis. Repeated immunologic studies showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE concentrations (1,430-2,100 IU/ml). He was 30 years old at the time of the report.

Kreins et al. (2015) also reported 7 further TYK2-deficient patients from 5 kindreds originating from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. P2, from kindred B, was the Turkish man previously reported by Kilic et al. (2012), born to consanguineous first-cousin parents. In this report he had disseminated BCG lymphadenitis at 21 months of age, severe bacterial meningitis with CSF cultures positive for Brucella at 8 years, and shingles involving a branch of the trigeminal nerve at 11 years. He had no atopy, high serum IgE, or staphylococcal infections.

In kindred C, which was Moroccan and consanguineous, the girl P3 was hospitalized at 13 years of age with suspected abdominal tuberculosis. Despite antimycobacterial treatment her abdominal complaints persisted, and she developed ascites and a psoas abscess within a few months. Treatment was restarted, but she was readmitted with bacterial meningitis and died 15 months after symptom onset. Her younger brother, P4, was hospitalized at 8 months of age for meningitis of unknown etiology. He also had recurrent otitis, urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels, but not high serum IgE. At age 15 years he was receiving no treatment (Kreins et al., 2015).

In kindred D, P5 was a boy born to consanguineous Iranian parents and vaccinated with live BCG at birth. He developed right axillary lymphadenitis at 2 months of age and multiple cervical lymphadenitis at 4 months. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with fistulization and pulmonary involvement. His 2-year-old younger sister, P6, was also vaccinated with BCG at birth and afterward had BCG lymphadenopathy lasting 1 month. At 1 year of age she was hospitalized for 1 week for an unknown viral infection of the skin (Kreins et al., 2015).

P7, from kindred E, was a 9-year-old Iranian girl born to consanguineous parents. She had miliary tuberculosis, and spiral lung CT showed diffuse bilateral micronodular infiltrations, hilar and mediastinal adenopathies, and cavitary consolidation in the right upper lobe. She had also had pneumonia at 8 months of age. P8, from kindred F, was an Argentinian boy. He had herpes gingivostomatitis at 18 months of age, then aseptic meningitis and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions at 24 months, and he also had exanthema subitum. He had mild mental disability, and it was not clearly established whether this was congenital or followed the meningitis (Kreins et al., 2015).

Kreins et al. (2015) found that none of the 7 newly identified patients had features of hyper-IgE syndrome (atopy, high serum IgE, or staphylococcal disease), and none had fungal infections or mucocutaneous candidiasis. Four of the 7 had a pure phenotype of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease or of tuberculosis. The authors concluded that the core clinical phenotype of TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infection, and suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in children with severe tuberculosis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient who had eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels above 1000 U/mL, without viral or mycobacterial infections. NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, were normal.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy, aged 1 year and 11 months, who was the second child of young nonconsanguineous parents. He was born in 2016 and vaccinated with BCG on the third day after birth. He developed an abscess with ulceration at the BCG vaccination site and BCG-associated tuberculosis of the axillary lymph nodes. He also had recurrent respiratory tract infections, bacterial pneumonia, diarrhea, Salmonella infection, Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, and influenza virus infection. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal illness, or lymphopenia. He spoke his first simple words at 3 years and 4 months, with normal motor development, and examination showed a small head circumference. The percentage of B cells was increased and the percentage of NK cells decreased. In a review of 12 patients with TYK2 deficiency, Wu et al. (2020) found intracellular bacterial infections in 58%, recurrent viral infections in 58%, and markedly increased serum IgE in 25%. None had the facial, skeletal, dental, or vascular features of STAT3-related hyper-IgE syndrome.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 further TYK2-deficient patients, with or without hyper-IgE levels or atopy, who had recurrent infections with differing pathogen profiles. The patients had heterogeneous responses to IFN-alpha, IFN-beta, IFN-gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23, and their lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted. ⚠︎

In an international cohort of children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated TYK2-deficient children (P2, P3, and P4), aged 2, 4, and 9 years, each born to a different set of consanguineous Turkish parents. P2, a girl, was hospitalized for moderate COVID-19 pneumonia but did not need oxygen therapy. She had been hospitalized for sepsis as a neonate, was admitted to the ICU twice for fever and dyspnea before 5 months of age, was diagnosed with Kawasaki disease at 11 months, and was hospitalized at 2 years for infection with the varicella-zoster virus (VZV) vaccine. P3 and P4, both boys, were admitted to the ICU for critical COVID-19 pneumonia requiring high-flow oxygen therapy. P3 had been hospitalized for neonatal sepsis and was admitted to the ICU for influenza pneumonia at 3 years. P4 had recurrent bronchitis requiring hospital admission and inhaler therapy. Neither boy had an adverse reaction to MMR vaccination. The 3-year-old younger sister of P3, who had the same genotype, had a history of critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and ventilation and of disseminated varicella, but had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and remained seronegative. The authors found critical COVID-19 pneumonia in unrelated TYK2-deficient patients surprising, because TYK2 deficiency impairs but does not abolish cellular responses to type I IFNs. They noted that more infected TYK2-deficient patients were needed to estimate penetrance, which may be incomplete.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of TYK2 deficiency is autosomal recessive. Most patients were born to consanguineous parents and were homozygous for TYK2 mutations (Kilic et al., 2012; Kreins et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2022). In the kindreds studied by Kreins et al. (2015), clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. In the patient of Wu et al. (2020), both nonconsanguineous parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis.

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells had defects in several cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I interferon, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. Transduction of intact TYK2 restored the signaling defects. Kreins et al. (2015) later characterized this patient's mutation as a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 (C70HfsX21).

In a Turkish boy with disseminated BCG infection and neurobrucellosis, born to first-cousin parents, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del). The deletion created a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein was detectable by Western blotting in the patient's EBV-B cells.

Kreins et al. (2015) identified 5 different homozygous null mutations in the TYK2 gene in 7 patients from 5 kindreds originating from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. All 5 mutations were predicted to cause premature stop codons, none was present in public databases, and TYK2 protein was undetectable in the patients' cells. In the Turkish patient P2, previously reported by Kilic et al. (2012), they designated the 9-bp exon 16 deletion as c.2303_2311del (L767X). The other mutations were a 1-bp insertion in exon 23 (c.3318_3319insC; T1106HfsX4) in Moroccan sibs P3 and P4; a c.462G-T transversion in exon 5 resulting in a glu154-to-ter (E154X) substitution in Iranian sibs P5 and P6; a 1-bp deletion in exon 3 (c.149delC; S50HfsX1) in Iranian patient P7; and a c.1912C-T transition in exon 13 resulting in an arg638-to-ter (R638X) substitution in Argentinian patient P8.

In a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and elevated IgE, Fuchs et al. (2016) found that the TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in a monocyte assay. The mutation partly reduced IL-10 signaling, together with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not reduce IL-6 signaling.

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent infections, Wu et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.2395G-A transition in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution. His parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The mutation decreased TYK2 mRNA expression in patient-derived cells, and no TYK2 protein was detected by Western blot. In transfected HEK293T cells, the mutant also reduced mRNA and protein expression of FLAG- and GFP-tagged TYK2. The patient also carried heterozygous variants of uncertain significance in the CFH, LRRC8A, and NIPBL genes.

Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations in 5 TYK2-deficient patients. They confirmed the pathogenic effects of the mutations by qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays. The authors proposed that TYK2 acts as a multitasker coordinating several cytokine signaling pathways, and that differing combinations of defects account for the clinical manifestations.

In an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated Turkish children, born to consanguineous parents, who were homozygous for the same TYK2 frameshift variant (P216Rfs*14). The variant had previously been shown to cause loss of expression, and the authors confirmed that it was loss of function. Autosomal recessive inheritance was confirmed by family segregation analysis, and the younger sister of one patient carried the same genotype. ACE2-transduced SV40 fibroblasts from a homozygous patient did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally and were not protected by IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment. These cells also failed to induce interferon-stimulated genes in response to infection or IFN-alpha-2b. Zhang et al. (2022) implicated autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency as a new genetic etiology of COVID-19 pneumonia.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Lv et al. (2022) noted that TYK2-deficient patients do not necessarily present with features of hyper-IgE syndrome; their patients had elevated IgE and atopy in some cases but not in others.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that TYK2 deficiency likely accounted for their patient's complex clinical picture, including impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation. They noted that in humans TYK2 has obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals of innate and acquired immunity, which differs substantially from its function in mice.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that absence of TYK2 caused defective IL-12 signaling and impaired IFN-gamma production, resulting in susceptibility to BCG and Brucella. They considered the viral illnesses probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that cells from all 8 TYK2-deficient patients had impaired but not abolished responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta, and IL-10. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda, and LIF were normal. Surface expression of IFNAR1, IL10R2, and IL12RB1 was reduced, reflecting the scaffolding role of TYK2, and was rescued by expression of wildtype TYK2. Cells from the 7 newly identified patients responded normally to IL-6, whereas cells from the original patient with HIES (P1) were hyporesponsive. This IL-6 hyporesponsiveness was rescued neither by expressing wildtype TYK2 nor by silencing TYK2. The authors suggested that impaired IL-6 responses and HIES are not intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency, and that P1 may carry a variant in another, as yet unknown, component of the IL-6 pathway. Proportions of IL-17-positive T cells ex vivo were normal despite profoundly impaired IL-17 production by naive CD4 T cells in vitro, and central and effector memory T cell numbers were also normal. The authors suggested that residual IL-23 responses account for the lack of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) found almost normal type III IFN signaling and only minimal impairment of virus control in a TYK2-deficient human cell line. They suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may contribute to antiviral protection in TYK2 deficiency, leading to a surprisingly mild human phenotype.

### Diagnosis

Fuchs et al. (2016) established a simple diagnostic monocyte assay that detects loss of the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in TYK2 deficiency.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) found no individuals homozygous for TYK2 stop mutations in any database and only 84 heterozygous individuals.

Wu et al. (2020) noted that the c.2395G-A (G799R) variant was present in the gnomAD database, suggesting that it was not a private mutation.
