### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Pallister-Hall-like syndrome (PHLS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the SMO gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Pallister-Hall-like syndrome is a developmental disorder with a broad range of features. These can include hypothalamic hamartoma and microcephaly, atrioventricular septal defect, skeletal anomalies such as postaxial polydactyly, narrow chest, and shortening of the long bones, and aganglionosis of the enteric nervous system (Le et al., 2020).

### Nomenclature

Encha-Razavi et al. (1992) proposed the name 'congenital hypothalamic hamartoma syndrome' (CHHS) for a possibly familial entity of congenital hypothalamic hamartoma with skeletal dysplasia and orofacial abnormalities.

### Clinical Features

Marcuse et al. (1953) reported 2 cases of hamartoma of the hypothalamus with associated developmental defects.

Encha-Razavi et al. (1992) found congenital hypothalamic hamartomas at autopsy in 3 unrelated fetuses. In the first 2 fetuses, the tumor occurred with skeletal dysplasia only. In the third fetus, it was part of a nonrandom pattern of congenital malformations that suggested Meckel syndrome. In 1 family, a previous boy had died soon after birth with similar craniofacial and skeletal abnormalities. After analyzing reported cases of Pallister-Hall syndrome, the authors found that only skeletal dysplasia and orofacial abnormalities were constantly present. They proposed these as the minimum criteria for a distinct, possibly familial entity, which they called 'congenital hypothalamic hamartoma syndrome' (CHHS).

Rubino et al. (2019) reported 2 brothers with a familial syndrome of hypothalamic hamartomas and polydactyly. In 1 brother, a large hypothalamic hamartoma was biopsied and subtotally resected through an endoscopic endonasal approach.

Le et al. (2020) reported 7 individuals from 5 independent families. Their developmental anomalies varied widely and affected the brain (hypothalamic hamartoma and microcephaly), heart (atrioventricular septal defect), skeleton (postaxial polydactyly, narrow chest, and shortening of the long bones), and enteric nervous system (aganglionosis). ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Le et al. (2020) found biallelic SMO mutations in affected individuals from 5 families, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The 2 affected brothers reported by Rubino et al. (2019) carried germline mutations in the SMO gene. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Rubino et al. (2019) found germline mutations in the SMO gene in 2 brothers with hypothalamic hamartomas and polydactyly.

Le et al. (2020) identified biallelic loss-of-function mutations in the SMO gene in 7 individuals with PHLS from 5 independent families. Ciliogenesis was normal in cells from affected individuals, but hedgehog signal transduction was severely altered. This resulted either from altered trafficking through the primary cilium or from abnormal activation of the pathway downstream of SMO. These cells also accumulated GLI2 at the tip of the primary cilium independently of hedgehog signaling. The authors suggested that SMO may help regulate basal ciliary trafficking of GLI2 when the pathway is off.

Craig et al. (2008) studied 55 patients with sporadic, nonsyndromic hypothalamic hamartoma and intractable epilepsy, comparing peripheral blood with surgically resected hamartoma tissue. They found no causative germline GLI3 mutations. However, 8 of the 55 (15%) resected tissue samples had somatic chromosomal abnormalities that included the GLI3 locus. These comprised a somatic abnormality on chromosome 7p in 1 sample and loss of heterozygosity within or around GLI3 in 8 patients. The somatic changes appeared to be highly variable.
