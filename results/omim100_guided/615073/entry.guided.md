### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene on chromosome 18p.

### Description

Dystonia-25 (DYT25) is an autosomal dominant form of primary torsion dystonia, meaning dystonia is the only neurologic sign. Onset is usually in adulthood, but the age at onset varies widely. The dystonia most often starts in the neck and then often spreads to other sites, especially cranial muscles. Speech can be affected, but arm involvement is less common (Fuchs et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 individuals from 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected. Their mean age at onset was 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 28 individuals with definite dystonia and complete clinical information from 8 multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia, all of whom carried GNAL mutations. All were Caucasian of mixed European ancestry. The average age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7-54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82%, and 93% had neck involvement at final examination. Most patients had progressed to dystonia at other sites, and only 46% had focal dystonia at last examination. Cranial involvement was present in 57% of carriers, and 44% had speech involvement. No patient had onset in the arm, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement. Other sites of onset in individual patients were the legs, larynx, jaw, and tongue. Two patients had generalized dystonia with onset in the legs at ages 7 and 25 years. Dystonia was inherited equally often from the maternal and paternal side, and the phenotype did not appear to differ between maternally and paternally inherited cases. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DYT25 in the families reported by Fuchs et al. (2013) was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance.

### Heterogeneity

In a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset, mainly cervical-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia, Bressman et al. (1994) excluded linkage to the region containing the DYT1 locus on chromosome 9q34. This showed that a gene other than DYT1 causes some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. In one family (P) of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage, they found a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the GNAL gene (S293X). This family had previously been reported, and a genome scan had identified 3 regions of possible linkage, which contained the mutation. In the other family (D1), also previously reported, they found a heterozygous missense mutation (V137M). In both families the mutations segregated with the disorder. Neither mutation was found in about 3,500 European exomes in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project database, and V137M was not found in 572 control chromosomes. The authors then Sanger-sequenced GNAL in 39 additional multiplex families of mixed European origin who had no DYT1 or DYT6 mutations. They found 6 further heterozygous mutations in 6 families (19%): a nonsense mutation (R21X), 2 frameshift mutations (S95fsX110 and R198fsX210), a missense mutation (E155K), an in-frame 3-residue deletion (P102-V104del), and a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C). None of these variants was found in dbSNP135, about 3,500 European exomes, or 572 control chromosomes. In all families with DNA available from multiple affected members, the mutations segregated with the disorder. The V137 and E155 residues were completely conserved in GNAL orthologs. In a cell-based BRET assay, the mutant proteins were tested for coupling to the dopamine D1 receptor. S293X failed to support any D1 receptor-driven response. V137M and E155K gave intermediate results, largely consistent with impaired association with G-beta-gamma subunits. A V16F variant found in a single control behaved like wildtype. The authors concluded that the findings suggested a loss-of-function mechanism.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no genotype-phenotype correlation related to mutation type among 8 families with GNAL mutations. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation.
