### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene on chromosome 18p.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is a form of primary torsion dystonia, in which dystonia, with or without tremor, is the only neurologic sign. In families with GNAL mutations, the phenotype was mainly cervical dystonia. Age at onset varied widely, and in more than half of patients the dystonia spread to other muscles, especially the facial muscles. Speech involvement was also common (Fuchs et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 members spanning 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected, with a mean age at onset of 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported clinical features in 28 individuals from 8 families with GNAL mutations who had complete clinical information and definite dystonia. All carriers were Caucasian of mixed European ancestry. The average age at onset of dystonia was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of carriers, and 93% had neck involvement at final examination. However, most progressed to have dystonia at other sites, and only 46% had focal dystonia at last examination. Cranial involvement was present in 57% of carriers and speech involvement in 44%. No patient had onset in the arm, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement, which distinguished the GNAL phenotype from DYT6. Sites of onset other than the neck included the legs, jaw, tongue, and larynx. Some patients had segmental or generalized dystonia. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation.

### Inheritance

In the families studied by Fuchs et al. (2013), dystonia-25 was inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Mutations were transmitted from maternal and paternal parents equally, and the authors saw no obvious phenotypic difference between maternally and paternally inherited cases. They noted that further study was needed to establish whether the parent of origin affects penetrance or expression.

### Mapping

In a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset, mainly cervical-onset dystonia, Bressman et al. (1994) used linkage data to exclude the region on chromosome 9q34 containing the DYT1 locus.

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that the GNAL gene lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 locus for focal dystonia and the DYT15 locus for myoclonus-dystonia. A previous whole-genome scan of one of their families (family P) had identified 3 regions of possible linkage, with lod scores ranging from 1.5 to 2.1.

### Heterogeneity

Bressman et al. (1994) concluded that a gene other than DYT1 is responsible for some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. In family P, which had mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage, they identified a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the GNAL gene (S293X). This mutation was found within a region of possible linkage and cosegregated with dystonia in the family. In family D1, they identified a heterozygous missense mutation (V137M) that segregated with the disease and was not found in 572 control chromosomes. Neither variant was present in about 3,500 European exomes in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project database.

Fuchs et al. (2013) then Sanger sequenced the entire GNAL coding region in 39 additional multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia who were negative for DYT1 and DYT6 mutations. They found novel GNAL mutations in 6 of these families (19%): - a nonsense mutation (R21X) - 2 frameshift mutations - a missense mutation (E155K) - an in-frame deletion of 3 amino acids - a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C) None of the variants were found in dbSNP135, about 3,500 European exomes, or 572 control chromosomes. In all families with DNA available from multiple affected individuals, the mutations segregated with the disease. Residues V137 and E155 were completely conserved in GNAL orthologs. The authors could not test the effect of the splice site variant on splicing because GNAL expression was undetectable in patient lymphoblasts. In a cell-based BRET assay of G-alpha-olf coupling to the dopamine D1 receptor, the S293X mutant failed to support any receptor-driven response. The V137M and E155K mutants showed intermediate phenotypes, largely consistent with impaired association with G-beta-gamma subunits. A V16F variant found in a single control behaved like wildtype. The authors judged that the functional findings suggested a loss-of-function mechanism. They could not discern a genotype/phenotype correlation by mutation type.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that GNAL encodes G-alpha-olf, a stimulatory G-protein alpha subunit. In striatal medium spiny neurons, G-alpha-olf replaces G-alpha-s and couples dopamine D1 receptors and adenosine A2A receptors to activation of adenylyl cyclase type 5. G-alpha-olf is also expressed in striatal cholinergic interneurons. The authors suggested that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1 receptor and/or A2A receptor transmission as a possible cause of dystonia. In this model, the primary abnormality would be postsynaptic, in striatal dopaminoceptive neurons and/or cholinergic interneurons.
