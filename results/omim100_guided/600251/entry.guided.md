### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that oblique facial clefting-1 (OBLFC1) is caused by loss-of-function mutation in the SPECC1L gene.

### Description

Oblique facial clefts are a rare and distinct class of facial malformations. They include types 2 to 6 of the Tessier classification of facial clefts (Tessier, 1976; Richieri-Costa and Gorlin, 1994). Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in the SPECC1L gene, which encodes a cytoskeletal protein, cause oblique facial clefting. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Tessier (1976) proposed an anatomic classification of facial, craniofacial, and laterofacial clefts based on personal observations. The orbit served as the main reference structure, and the classification distinguished 15 cleft locations. Tessier (1976) described how each cleft runs through soft tissue and bone. Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) noted that oblique facial clefts include types 2 to 6 of this classification. They suggested that the condition might sometimes represent an oculomaxillofacial dysostosis.

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (1988) reported an 8-month-old girl born with symmetric bilateral oblique facial clefts and a calcaneovarus foot deformity. A CT scan of the head showed severe bilateral ocular hypoplasia and normal brain parenchyma. The authors suggested that the pattern of anomalies would help distinguish oblique facial clefts from amniotic band disruption.

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) reported 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts who had a strikingly similar facial appearance. Three of the patients had consanguineous parents. The authors concluded that the pattern of facial involvement, the consanguinity, and the overall clinical picture pointed to a unique dysmorphogenetic process.

### Cytogenetics

In a girl with bilateral oblique facial clefts, Dasouki et al. (1988) found a de novo balanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 22 in the peripheral blood karyotype. They could not rule out a submicroscopic imbalance caused by the translocation. They recommended chromosome studies in children with such rare malformations.

### Heterogeneity

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) considered their 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts to be evidence of clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity in this condition.

### Molecular Genetics

Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in the SPECC1L gene cause oblique facial clefts. In the mouse embryo, Specc1l was expressed in the cell populations of the developing facial primordia, which proliferate and fuse to form the face. In mammalian cells, SPECC1L colocalized with both tubulin and actin. SPECC1L deficiency caused defective reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and abnormal cell adhesion and migration. The authors concluded that SPECC1L functions in actin-cytoskeleton reorganization and is required for normal facial morphogenesis.

### Animal Model

Saadi et al. (2011) found that knockdown of a SPECC1L homolog in zebrafish produced a 'faceless' phenotype, with loss of the jaw and facial structures. In Drosophila, knockdown phenocopied mutants in the integrin signaling pathway, which show defects in cell migration and adhesion.
