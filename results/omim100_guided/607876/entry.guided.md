### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by an expanded ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat insertion in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene on chromosome 2.

### Description

Familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is an autosomal dominant disorder with a core triad of cortical tremor, multifocal myoclonus, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (Crompton et al., 2012). Cortical myoclonic tremor usually starts in the second decade of life, followed by overt myoclonic or generalized tonic-clonic seizures (Corbett et al., 2019). The course is nonprogressive (Guerrini et al., 2001). Expressivity varies within and between families, and the frequency of generalized tonic-clonic seizures ranges widely between families (Corbett et al., 2019). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Guerrini et al. (2001) called the disorder in their family 'autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy' (ADCME). They considered it a new epilepsy syndrome because its phenotype differed from that of familial adult myoclonic epilepsy and because it did not map to chromosome 8q23.3-q24. De Falco et al. (2003) suggested that benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME) linked to chromosome 2 might be allelic with ADCME. De Fusco et al. (2014) found no ADRA2B mutations in BAFME pedigrees mapping to the ADCME locus and suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME might be distinct clinical entities. Corbett et al. (2019) used the term FAME2 for chromosome 2-linked FAME.

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) described a pedigree with 8 affected individuals who had a nonprogressive disorder starting between the ages of 12 and 50 years. All patients had predominantly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 had complex partial seizures. Most patients rarely had seizures and had normal cognition, but 3 with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All patients had frontotemporal and generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Neurophysiologic study suggested that the myoclonus was of cortical origin. EEG activity over the contralateral rolandic area was coherent with EMG activity at 8 to 25 Hz and preceded it by 8 to 15 ms. The C-reflex at rest was enhanced, and somatosensory and visual evoked potentials were of high amplitude. Compared with controls, the resting motor threshold to transcranial magnetic stimulation was reduced and the post-motor evoked potential silent period was shortened. The authors concluded that these findings indicated diffuse cortical hyperexcitability.

Labauge et al. (2002) reported a 4-generation European kindred with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy. Ten living and 3 deceased relatives were affected. Mean age at onset in the 10 living patients was 41 years (range, 30-60 years). Eight of the 13 affected individuals had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Electrophysiologic studies in 5 patients confirmed the diagnosis.

De Falco et al. (2003) reported the clinical and electrophysiologic findings in 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy.

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. Eleven members, aged 24 to 56 years (mean, 39.2 years), had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 of them had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study showed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected members. Three presymptomatic members had giant somatosensory evoked potential components and an enhanced long-loop reflex I. These 3 developed hand tremor and myoclonus of the upper limbs after 1.5 years of follow-up.

Suppa et al. (2009) studied an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy. Affected members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neuropsychologic testing showed visuospatial impairment, possibly due to temporal lobe dysfunction. Neurophysiologic findings suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex with normal sensorimotor integration.

Crompton et al. (2012) reported a 6-generation kindred of European descent from New Zealand and Australia with 55 affected members. All had fine hand tremor, which typically began in adolescence (median age, 15 years; range, 4-60 years). Proximal myoclonus was present in 44 of 55 (80%) and started later than the hand tremor (median age, 17 years; range, 5-60 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range, 18-76 years). These seizures occurred around sleep onset after severe generalized myoclonus. Neurophysiologic testing confirmed cortical reflex myoclonus. The most common phenotype in this family was a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, combined with subtle proximal myoclonus.

De Fusco et al. (2014) described a second Tuscan family with ADCME (family B), a 4-generation kindred with 5 affected members. All had postural myoclonus of the hands and upper limbs. Cortical tremor was the presenting symptom in every case and began between 18 and 50 years of age. All but 1 had epilepsy, with rare generalized tonic-clonic seizures that were sometimes precipitated by visual stimuli. Two patients also had drug-resistant focal motor or complex partial seizures. One patient had borderline cognitive level. Generalized or focal paroxysmal EEG activity was found in all patients. Jerk-locked back-averaged EEG, somatosensory evoked potentials, and long-loop reflexes were consistent with cortical reflex myoclonus. Two of the oldest mutation carriers developed age-related dementia. De Fusco et al. (2014) noted that the myoclonus often worsened in advanced age. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) summarized 22 families with FAME2. In the large Australian/New Zealand family, they found evidence of anticipation in the median age at onset of any relevant symptom. Median onset was 30 years in generation III (range, 14-60 years; n = 6), 17 years in generation IV (range, 8-50 years; n = 30), and 12 years in generation V (range, 4-19 years; n = 16).

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) and Labauge et al. (2002) reported autosomal dominant inheritance in their families. Guerrini et al. (2001) noted that penetrance was high. Madia et al. (2008) also described the disorder as autosomal dominant.

### Mapping

Guerrini et al. (2001) performed a genome-wide linkage analysis in their family and found a 12.4-cM critical region on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2, between markers D2S2161 and D2S1897. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for marker D2S2175. The multipoint lod score reached 3.74 around D2S2175.

De Falco et al. (2003) showed that 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy were linked to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2.

Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p in their family, with a maximum lod score of 5.9.

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the 2p11.1-q12.2 locus for cortical tremor associated with epilepsy and excluded candidate genes.

Madia et al. (2008) genotyped 53 affected individuals from 5 families from southern Italy. They found significant linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2, with a maximum cumulative lod score of 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388.

Suppa et al. (2009) excluded the 8q24 locus in their Italian family. The patients shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the pericentromeric region of chromosome 2.

Crompton et al. (2012) used their large Australian/New Zealand kindred to narrow the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

### Heterogeneity

Guerrini et al. (2001) excluded linkage to the chromosome 8q23.3-q24 familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus in their family. Labauge et al. (2002) excluded linkage to the 8q23.3-q24.1 FAME/BAFME locus in a European family. They concluded that this established genetic heterogeneity of the disorder.

### Molecular Genetics

De Fusco et al. (2014) studied 2 Tuscan families with ADCME, including the family reported by Guerrini et al. (2001). They found a heterozygous in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG) that replaces 5 amino acids (HGGAL) with 4 new residues (QFGR) in the third intracellular loop. The variant was found by sequencing candidate genes in the critical region. It cosegregated with the disease and was absent from 575 Tuscan controls, dbSNP, the Exome Variant Server, and the 1000 Genomes Project. Targeted capture and next-generation sequencing of the critical region in 2 affected members of the first family found no other relevant variant. The 2 families shared the disease haplotype across the entire critical region. In transfected cells, the mutant receptor localized normally to the plasma membrane and was internalized normally in an arrestin-3-dependent manner. However, it did not form a stable complex with spinophilin after 10 minutes of epinephrine stimulation. In Xenopus oocytes, spinophilin increased the EC50 for epinephrine with the wildtype receptor but not with the mutant receptor. The authors concluded that the mutation had a gain-of-function effect and increased epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling. They found no ADRA2B mutations in BAFME pedigrees from southern Italy that mapped to the same locus. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from affected members of 3 families with FAME2. In all of them they found an expanded ATTTT repeat with an inserted ATTTC repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene. The ATTTC repeat was absent from whole-genome sequencing data of 69 controls. Using repeat-primed PCR, they detected the ATTTC expansion in all 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported families with FAME2. Two of 24 tested family members without a FAME diagnosis also carried it; both were from younger generations and likely presymptomatic. Among 72 additional individuals with clinical features similar to FAME, the expansion was found in 20 of 20 familial cases from 6 families and in 1 of 52 singleton cases. In total, the expansion segregated in 158 of 158 typically affected individuals from 22 pedigrees. It did not amplify in 28 unrelated control samples. Long-read sequencing in 1 patient showed 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats in one read and 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats in another, suggesting somatic variation in repeat size. A second patient had 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats, 4,645 bp in total. Corbett et al. (2019) also found the STARD7 expansion in the 2 families carrying the ADRA2B indel reported by De Fusco et al. (2014). They concluded that the ADRA2B allele was not causative.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In a preliminary analysis of 5 Italian families sharing a founder haplotype, Madia et al. (2008) found only minor clinical differences among patients carrying the disease haplotype. They suggested that other factors do not markedly influence the expressivity of the founder mutation.

### Pathogenesis

In fibroblasts from patients with FAME2, Corbett et al. (2019) found no change in STARD7 transcript levels by quantitative RT-PCR or in STARD7 protein levels by Western blot. RNA-seq showed no difference in STARD7 expression and no reads containing ATTTC repeats. The authors suggested that the repeat sequence itself is pathogenic, independent of any effect on the encompassing gene.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found that the haplotype '5332' of the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113, and D2S2175 segregated with the disease in 5 families from southern Italy. They concluded that the same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, was segregating in these families.

Corbett et al. (2019) found that the FAME2 locus was the linked region most frequently observed in Caucasian individuals with FAME. Families with the STARD7 expansion included Italian, Australian/New Zealand and South African families of European ancestry, Iraqi and Israeli families of Sephardic Jewish ancestry, French, French/Spanish, and Syrian families.

In affected members of 2 Tuscan families with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (FAME2), De Fusco et al. (2014) found a heterozygous in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG). It replaces 5 residues (HGGAL) with 4 new residues (QFGR) (p.H225_L229delinsQ225_F_G_R228) in the third intracellular loop. The variant cosegregated with the disease. It was absent from 575 Tuscan controls, dbSNP, the Exome Variant Server, and the 1000 Genomes Project. In functional studies, the mutant receptor localized and internalized normally but failed to form a stable complex with agonist-activated spinophilin, and it increased epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) found the STARD7 ATTTC repeat expansion in the 2 families carrying this ADRA2B variant. They concluded that the ADRA2B allele was not causative.

**.0002 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2**  
STARD7, (ATTTC)n INS/EXP, INTRON 1

In 158 individuals from 22 families with FAME2, including 16 previously reported families, Corbett et al. (2019) identified an expanded ATTTT repeat with an inserted, expanded ATTTC repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene. It was detected by whole-genome sequencing and repeat-primed PCR. The expansion segregated with the disease in all typically affected individuals. It was absent from whole-genome sequencing data of 69 controls and did not amplify in 28 control samples. Long-read sequencing showed expansions of 274 to 588 AAATG repeats and 340 to 390 AAAAT repeats. Expression of STARD7 mRNA and protein in patient fibroblasts was unaltered.
