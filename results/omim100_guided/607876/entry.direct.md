### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by an expanded intronic ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat insertion, together with expansion of the adjacent endogenous ATTTT repeat, in the first intron of the STARD7 gene on chromosome 2.

### Description

Familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is an autosomal dominant disorder. Its core triad is cortical tremor, multifocal myoclonus, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (Crompton et al., 2012). Symptoms usually begin in the second to third decade. Expressivity varies within and between families, and the frequency of generalized tonic-clonic seizures ranges from 15 to 100% across families (Corbett et al., 2019). Some patients also have complex partial or focal seizures. In 1 family with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME), 3 individuals with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation (Guerrini et al., 2001).

### Nomenclature

Guerrini et al. (2001) named the disorder in their family 'autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy' (ADCME). Because their family was excluded from linkage to the chromosome 8q23.3-q24 familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus, mapped to a different region, and had a different phenotype, they regarded it as a new epilepsy syndrome. De Falco et al. (2003) later reported Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME) that showed linkage to the same chromosome 2 region, and they suggested that BAFME and ADCME might be allelic. ⚠︎

De Fusco et al. (2014) found an ADRA2B variant in 2 ADCME families but no ADRA2B mutations in BAFME pedigrees from southern Italy that mapped to the same locus. On this basis, they suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME could be classified as 2 distinct clinical entities. Corbett et al. (2019), however, found the same STARD7 repeat expansion in all chromosome 2-linked families they studied. These included the 2 families with the ADRA2B variant as well as families reported as BAFME. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) described a pedigree in which 8 individuals had a nonprogressive disorder beginning between the ages of 12 and 50 years. All patients had predominantly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 had complex partial seizures. Most affected individuals rarely had seizures and had normal cognitive levels. However, 3 individuals with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All patients had both frontotemporal and generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Neurophysiologic study suggested that the myoclonus was cortical in origin. In 5 patients, EMG and EEG recorded over the contralateral rolandic area showed significant coherence at 8 to 25 Hz, with EEG activity preceding EMG activity by 8 to 15 ms. In 2 individuals there was also significant ipsilateral EEG-EMG coherence, consistent with transcallosal spread of myoclonic activity. The C-reflex at rest was enhanced, and somatosensory and visual evoked potentials were of high amplitude. Compared with controls, the resting motor threshold to transcranial magnetic stimulation was significantly reduced (38%; SD +/- 7; P = 0.01). The post-motor evoked potential silent period was significantly shortened (101 ms vs 137 ms in controls). The authors concluded that these findings suggested diffuse cortical hyperexcitability, particularly involving frontotemporal circuits.

Labauge et al. (2002) described a 4-generation European kindred with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy (FAME) that included 18 members. They examined 15 living members clinically and studied 5 patients electrophysiologically. Ten living and 3 deceased relatives had the clinical characteristics of FAME. Among the 10 living patients, mean age at onset was 41 years (range, 30-60 years). Eight of the 13 affected individuals had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed the diagnosis in all 5 patients tested. The authors noted that this family extended the occurrence of FAME to non-Japanese patients.

De Falco et al. (2003) reported clinical and electrophysiologic studies of 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy.

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. Eleven members aged 24 to 56 years (mean, 39.2 years) had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study confirmed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected members. Three presymptomatic members also had giant somatosensory evoked potential components and an enhanced long-loop reflex I. After 1.5 years of follow-up, these 3 members developed hand tremor and myoclonus of the upper limbs.

Madia et al. (2008) studied 5 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy, 1 of which had not been reported before. They described the disorder as shivering-like tremors of cortical origin, myoclonus, and epilepsy. In a preliminary genotype-phenotype analysis, patients carrying the shared disease haplotype showed only minor clinical differences. The authors suggested that expressivity of the founder mutation is not strongly influenced by other factors.

Suppa et al. (2009) studied an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy. Clinically affected members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neuropsychologic testing showed visuospatial impairment, which the authors suggested might reflect temporal lobe dysfunction. Neurophysiologic findings suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex with normal sensorimotor integration.

Crompton et al. (2012) studied a 6-generation FAME kindred of European descent ascertained in New Zealand and Australia. All 55 affected members had fine hand tremor, typically beginning in adolescence (median age, 15 years; range, 4-60 years). Proximal myoclonus was present in 44 of 55 (80%) and began later than the hand tremor (median age, 17 years; range, 5-60 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range, 18-76 years). Neurophysiologic testing confirmed features of cortical reflex myoclonus. In this family, the most common phenotype was a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, combined with subtle proximal myoclonus. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures were uncommon and occurred around sleep onset after severe generalized myoclonus.

De Fusco et al. (2014) described a second ADCME family from Tuscany (family B), a 4-generation kindred with 5 affected members (4 living). They also noted that the clinical features of the previously described family A closely resembled those of family B. All patients in family B had postural myoclonus of the hands and upper limbs. Cortical tremor was the presenting symptom in all affected individuals and appeared between the ages of 18 and 50 years. All but 1 affected individual had epilepsy, characterized by rare generalized tonic-clonic seizures that were at times precipitated by visual stimuli. Two patients had drug-resistant focal motor or complex partial seizures. One individual had borderline cognitive level, and the others had normal intelligence. All patients had generalized or focal paroxysmal EEG activity. Jerk-locked back-averaged EEG, somatosensory evoked potentials, and long-loop reflexes were consistent with cortical reflex myoclonus. No patient had evidence of cardiovascular disease. The 2 oldest mutation carriers developed age-related dementia. The authors noted that myoclonus often worsened at advanced age. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) summarized clinical data from 22 FAME2 families. Symptom onset occurred in the second to third decade, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 15 to 100% of affected members across families. Seizures were typically controlled with antiepileptic drugs for generalized epilepsies, although rare individuals had drug-resistant epilepsy.

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) found autosomal dominant inheritance with high penetrance in the family they reported. Labauge et al. (2002) also observed a pattern consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Crompton et al. (2012) and Madia et al. (2008) likewise described FAME2 as an autosomal dominant disorder.

### Mapping

Guerrini et al. (2001) performed genome-wide linkage analysis in a family with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy. They identified a 12.4-cM critical region between markers D2S2161 and D2S1897 on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for marker D2S2175, and multipoint lod scores reached 3.74 around D2S2175.

De Falco et al. (2003) found linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy.

In a 5-generation Italian family, Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p (maximum lod score of 5.9).

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the 2p11.1-q12.2 locus for cortical tremor associated with epilepsy and excluded candidate genes.

Madia et al. (2008) genotyped DNA from 53 affected individuals in 5 Italian families using microsatellite markers on chromosomes 2p11.1-q12.2 and 8q23.1-q24.11. They found significant linkage to 2p11.1-q12.2, with a maximum cumulative lod score of 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388.

In an Italian family, Suppa et al. (2009) excluded the 8q24 locus. Affected members shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the pericentromeric region of chromosome 2.

Crompton et al. (2012) studied a large New Zealand/Australian family and narrowed the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

### Heterogeneity

Guerrini et al. (2001) excluded linkage of their family to the familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus on chromosome 8q23.3-q24. Labauge et al. (2002) also excluded the 8q23.3-q24.1 FAME/BAFME locus in a European family, establishing genetic heterogeneity of the disorder.

Corbett et al. (2019) noted that FAME had been mapped to 4 distinct loci, on chromosomes 2, 3, 5, and 8. They reported that the chromosome 2 locus was the most frequently linked region in Caucasian individuals, whereas chromosome 8 linkage had so far been limited to Asian individuals.

### Molecular Genetics

De Fusco et al. (2014) studied the Tuscan family originally reported by Guerrini et al. (2001) (family A). Sequencing of genes in the ADCME critical region identified an in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG). The change replaces 5 amino acids (HGGAL) with 4 new residues (QFGR) in the third intracellular loop of the receptor. The variant cosegregated completely with the disease. It was absent from 575 unrelated controls from Tuscany and from dbSNP, the Exome Variant Server, and the 1000 Genomes Project, totaling 16,080 chromosomes. Targeted capture and next-generation sequencing of all coding regions in the critical region in 2 affected members of family A found the ADRA2B variant to be the only relevant change. The same variant was present in a second Tuscan ADCME family (family B), which shared the disease haplotype across the entire critical region. No ADRA2B mutations were found in BAFME pedigrees from southern Italy that mapped to the same locus. In transfected cells, the mutant receptor localized normally to the plasma membrane and was internalized in an arrestin-3-dependent manner. However, it did not form a stable complex with spinophilin after 10 minutes of epinephrine stimulation. In Xenopus oocytes, spinophilin increased the EC50 for epinephrine with the wildtype receptor but not with the mutant receptor. The authors concluded that the mutant receptor increased the intensity of calcium signaling, representing a gain-of-function effect. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 2 individuals from the Australian-New Zealand family reported by Crompton et al. (2012), 1 individual from an Italian family, and 3 individuals from a French-Spanish family. Using repeat expansion detection methods, they identified expansion of an ATTTT repeat and insertion of an ATTTC repeat in the first intron of the STARD7 gene in all FAME samples tested. The ATTTC repeat was absent from whole-genome sequencing data from 69 controls. The endogenous ATTTT repeat varied in length in the normal population but was not expanded to the same extent as in patients. Using a repeat-primed PCR assay, the authors detected the expansion in 137 of 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported FAME2 families. Of 24 tested family members without a FAME diagnosis, 2 carried the expansion; both were from younger generations and were likely presymptomatic. Among 72 additional individuals clinically similar to FAME, the expansion was found in 20 of 20 familial cases from 6 families and in 1 of 52 singleton cases. Eight of 13 family members with an uncertain diagnosis, usually tremor alone, carried the expansion. The expansion did not amplify in any of 28 control DNA samples. In all, 158 individuals from 22 pedigrees tested positive. Long-read sequencing in 1 individual from the Australian-New Zealand family showed 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats in an SMRT read, but 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats with some interruptions in a nanopore read, suggesting somatic variation in repeat size. In an individual from another family, a nanopore read contained 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats (4,645 bp in total). The ATTTC repeat primer detected FAME with a sensitivity of 100% in all families with linkage or suggestive linkage to chromosome 2. These included 2 families carrying the ADRA2B variant reported by De Fusco et al. (2014), which led Corbett et al. (2019) to conclude that the ADRA2B allele is very likely not causative. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In a preliminary genotype-phenotype analysis of 5 Italian families sharing a founder haplotype, Madia et al. (2008) found only minor clinical differences among patients carrying the disease haplotype.

Corbett et al. (2019) found evidence of anticipation in the large Australian/New Zealand family. Median age at onset was 30 years in generation III (range, 14-60 years; n = 6), 17 years in generation IV (range, 8-50 years; n = 30), and 12 years in generation V (range, 4-19 years; n = 16).

### Pathogenesis

In patient-derived fibroblasts, Corbett et al. (2019) found no significant change in STARD7 transcript levels by quantitative PCR or in STARD7 protein levels by Western blot. RNA-seq from 6 patient-derived fibroblast lines showed no difference in STARD7 expression along the entire length of the gene. Despite robust STARD7 expression, no reads containing ATTTC repeats were detected. The authors suggested that the repeat sequence itself is pathogenic, independent of any effect on the coding sequence of the gene that contains it.

### Diagnosis

Crompton et al. (2012) highlighted mild phenotypes of FAME2 that can be mistaken for other disorders. In their family, the most common presentation was a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, together with subtle proximal myoclonus. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) found that a repeat-primed PCR assay for the ATTTC repeat in STARD7 detected FAME with 100% sensitivity in families with linkage or suggestive linkage to chromosome 2. The naturally variable length of the endogenous ATTTT repeat made that sequence unsuitable for mutation screening.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found that the haplotype '5332', formed by the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113, and D2S2175, segregated with the disease in all 5 Italian families they studied. This indicated that the same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, was present in these families. De Fusco et al. (2014) reported that 2 Tuscan ADCME families with no known kinship shared the same disease haplotype across the entire chromosome 2 critical region.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2 (FAME2)**  
ADRA2B, 12-BP DEL/9-BP INS, NT675 [c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG; p.H225_L229delinsQ225_F_G_R228]

In affected members of 2 Tuscan families with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy, De Fusco et al. (2014) identified an in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG). The change replaces 5 residues (HGGAL) with 4 new residues (QFGR) in the third intracellular loop (p.H225_L229delinsQ225_F_G_R228). The variant cosegregated with the disease and was absent from 16,080 control chromosomes. The mutant receptor failed to form a stable complex with spinophilin after epinephrine stimulation, and it increased epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling in Xenopus oocytes.

Corbett et al. (2019) found that the 2 families carrying this ADRA2B variant also carried the expanded ATTTC repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene. They concluded that this finding strongly suggests the ADRA2B allele is not causative.

**.0002 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2 (FAME2)**  
STARD7, (ATTTC)n INS AND (ATTTT)n EXP, INTRON 1 [(AAATG)n[N](AAAAT)n]

In affected individuals from 22 FAME2 pedigrees, Corbett et al. (2019) identified an inserted ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat accompanied by expansion of the endogenous ATTTT repeat in the first intron of the STARD7 gene. The pedigrees included an Australian-New Zealand family, Italian families, a French-Spanish family, Sephardic Jewish families, and a South African family. The variant was detected by whole-genome sequencing and repeat-primed PCR, and its structure was characterized by long-read sequencing. On the forward strand of chromosome 2, the pathogenic repeat had the structure (AAATG)n[N](AAAAT)n. In 1 patient, an SMRT read contained 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats, and a nanopore read from another patient contained 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats. The expansion segregated with the disease in all 158 individuals who tested positive, including 137 of 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported families. It was absent from 69 control genomes and 28 control DNA samples. STARD7 mRNA and protein levels were not altered in patient fibroblasts.
