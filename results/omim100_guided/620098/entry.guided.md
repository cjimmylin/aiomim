### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental delay with variable intellectual disability and dysmorphic facies (DIDDF) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the JARID2 gene (601594) on chromosome 6p22.3.

### Description

Developmental delay with variable intellectual disability and dysmorphic facies (DIDDF) is a neurodevelopmental disorder. Features include developmental delay, cognitive impairment that ranges from borderline intellectual functioning to severe intellectual disability, hypotonia, autistic features, and behavioral abnormalities. Dysmorphic facial features include a high anterior hairline, broad forehead, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, depressed nasal bridge, bulbous nasal tip, and full lips (summary by Verberne et al., 2022). Palatal abnormalities and heart defects have also been reported, and expressivity is variable (Cadieux-Dion et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Barøy et al. (2013) reported 4 unrelated patients with de novo deletions of chromosome 6p22.3 that included JARID2. Patient 1 was a 15-year-old Norwegian girl with moderate intellectual disability. She walked independently at 3.5 years, had a vocabulary of 20 to 30 words, and had an unsteady, broad-based gait, hyperactivity, sleeping difficulties, and syncopal episodes suspected to be seizures. Patient 2 was a 4-year-old boy with moderate intellectual disability, severely delayed speech, a broad-based unsteady gait, and an atrial septal defect that closed spontaneously. Patient 3 was a 6.5-year-old Norwegian girl with severe intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, hyperactivity, neonatal hypotonia, and a large umbilical hernia. Patient 4 was a 6.5-year-old boy of Lebanese descent with borderline intellectual functioning (IQ 74), delayed language, early-onset hypotonia, and problems with balance and coordination. All 4 patients had normal muscle tone when last examined. All had similar facial features, including prominent supraorbital ridges, deep-set eyes, dark infraorbital circles, and midface hypoplasia, as well as a bulbous nasal tip, full lips, a marked Cupid's bow, and a prominent or pointed chin. Patients 1 and 4 had dyspraxia. All 4 had fifth-finger clinodactyly, and foot anomalies included pes planovalgus, toe syndactyly, and a sandal gap. When Barøy et al. (2013) reviewed the photographs of 7 of 10 previously described patients with larger deletions that included JARID2, they saw a similar facial pattern.

Verberne et al. (2021) reported 16 individuals from 15 families who had a deletion or a single-nucleotide variant in JARID2. All of them had developmental delay and/or intellectual disability. They shared some clinical characteristics, including facial features, with individuals who had larger deletions involving JARID2.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported 7 individuals from 5 families with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants. They highlighted palatal abnormalities and heart defects as part of the phenotype. In some families the variant was inherited from a mildly affected parent, which demonstrated variable expressivity.

Verberne et al. (2022) summarized the clinical findings in 8 patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants, some of whom had been reported before. All 8 had developmental delay. Intellectual functioning ranged from normal through borderline intellectual functioning (IQ 82), learning difficulties, and mild intellectual disability to moderate intellectual disability. Some patients had autistic features or a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder, behavioral abnormalities, hypotonia, or gait disturbance. Dysmorphic features varied between patients and included a broad forehead, high anterior hairline, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, midface hypoplasia, bulbous nasal tip, short philtrum, and full lips. Other findings, each seen in individual patients, included submucous cleft palate with bifid uvula, a small posterior fossa cyst or mega cisterna magna, an arachnoid cyst, cryptorchidism, kyphoscoliosis, and a supernumerary tooth.

### Inheritance

In most reported patients, the heterozygous JARID2 variants occurred de novo (Verberne et al., 2021; Cadieux-Dion et al., 2022). Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported JARID2 variants inherited from mildly affected parents. Verberne et al. (2022) reported a girl who inherited a JARID2 deletion from her mother, who had learning difficulties. These findings are consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with variable expressivity. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

In a cohort of about 3,600 patients ascertained for neurodevelopmental disorders, Barøy et al. (2013) used array CGH to identify 5 unrelated patients with de novo deletions in chromosome 6p22.3-p24.1. The deletions ranged in size from 0.1 to 4.8 Mb. The 189-kb deletion in patient 4 defined a smallest region of overlap (SRO I) that was deleted in patients 1 through 4 and contained the JARID2 and DTNBP1 genes. This deletion removed exons 3 through 18 of JARID2, exons 7 through 10 of DTNBP1, and the 3-prime UTR. A second region (SRO II), deleted in patients 1 through 3 and in patient 5, contained GMPR and ATXN1. Patient 5 had a 116-kb deletion of SRO II that did not include JARID2. She had mild intellectual disability and behavioral abnormalities, but no dysmorphic features were noted. Leukocytes from the 4 patients with JARID2 deletions showed significantly reduced JARID2 expression compared with controls (p less than or equal to 0.01). Barøy et al. (2013) suggested that haploinsufficiency of JARID2 was the main cause of the phenotype in patients 1 through 4. Patients 1 through 3 had deletions of both JARID2 and ATXN1 and had lower cognitive function than patients 4 and 5, possibly because both genes were haploinsufficient.

### Molecular Genetics

Using chromosomal microarray analysis, exome sequencing, and GeneMatcher, Verberne et al. (2021) identified deletions or single-nucleotide variants in JARID2 in 16 individuals from 15 families with DIDDF. Several of the variants were likely to cause haploinsufficiency through nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. Before this report, all published individuals with deletions encompassing JARID2 had also had a deletion of at least one adjoining gene.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants in 7 individuals from 5 families with DIDDF. Some of the variants were inherited from mildly affected parents. The authors noted that intragenic JARID2 copy number variants were common in this group, which they said made exon-level analysis important.

Verberne et al. (2022) studied 8 patients with DIDDF who carried pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants. Six had deletions of at least 1 exon of JARID2, 1 had a frameshift variant (c.2866dupG), and 1 had a splice site variant (c.2731+1G-C). In a mother and daughter, the deletion was inherited from the mother; the variants in the other patients had occurred de novo. Using peripheral blood DNA methylation profiling, the authors identified an episignature of 150 probes that clearly separated these 8 patients from 56 matched controls. They also tested 3 patients who carried JARID2 missense variants of uncertain significance (R788Q in 2 unrelated patients and E644K in 1). These patients clustered with the controls and had methylation variant pathogenicity scores near zero. The authors suggested that the missense variants might be benign.

Zahir et al. (2016) studied a copy number variant (CNV) involving only exon 6 of JARID2, which had previously been found to occur apparently de novo in 7 patients with intellectual disability. Exon 6 encodes one of a series of repeated motifs. After assessing the CNV in 2 cohorts, Zahir et al. (2016) concluded that it was benign, with a population frequency greater than 14%, although they suggested it could have a contributory effect.

### Diagnosis

Verberne et al. (2022) built a support vector machine classifier from a JARID2-specific DNA methylation episignature. All 8 patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants received methylation variant pathogenicity scores near 1. Control samples and samples from 57 other neurodevelopmental disorders received low scores. The authors concluded that the episignature could be used as a biomarker to diagnose the disorder and to reclassify JARID2 variants.

JARID2, 1-BP DUP, 2866G [NM_004973.4:c.2866dupG; p.(Glu956GlyfsTer72)]

Verberne et al. (2022) reported a 14-year-old boy with DIDDF (patient 4) who had a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication (c.2866dupG, NM_004973.4) in the JARID2 gene. The duplication was predicted to cause a frameshift and premature termination (Glu956GlyfsTer72). He had moderate intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, behavioral abnormalities, a high anterior hairline, short philtrum, full lips, and a small posterior fossa cyst or mega cisterna magna. He had previously been described in the literature. His blood DNA showed the JARID2-associated DNA methylation episignature.
