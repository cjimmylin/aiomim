### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding thrombopoietin (THPO) on chromosome 3.

### Description

Congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia-2 (CAMT2) is an autosomal recessive disorder. It is characterized by severe thrombocytopenia with markedly reduced or absent bone marrow megakaryocytes, which may progress to trilineage bone marrow failure in childhood. Unlike CAMT caused by MPL mutations, serum thrombopoietin levels are not raised. The bone marrow failure does not respond to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, but patients respond to treatment with thrombopoietin receptor agonists (Seo et al., 2017; Pecci et al., 2018; Capaci et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (2013) reported 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with idiopathic, autosomal recessive aplastic anemia. Both parents and a heterozygous sib had reduced platelet counts, whereas a sib with the wildtype sequence had a normal platelet count. Pecci et al. (2018), discussing this family, stated that bone marrow aplasia had been recognized at ages 16 and 28 years in the 2 investigated patients, and that 2 other sibs had died of aplastic anemia in adolescence. Pecci et al. (2018) noted that it was not known whether congenital thrombocytopenia had preceded the bone marrow aplasia in these patients.

Seo et al. (2017) reported 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families with severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to trilineage bone marrow failure. Four of the children received hematopoietic stem cell transplants. All 4 had poor graft function with persistent severe cytopenias, even after repeated transplants from different donors. According to Pecci et al. (2018), the 3 families were consanguineous and the patients presented with hypomegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia, with or without anemia or neutropenia, which progressed to trilineage bone marrow aplasia in early childhood. Pecci et al. (2018) also stated that 3 of these children died early after transplant.

Pecci et al. (2018) reported 3 sibs, born to Egyptian first-cousin parents, with congenital amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia. The proband, a boy, presented at age 3.5 years with ecchymotic patches. He also had preauricular skin tags and a left divergent squint. Blood counts showed very severe thrombocytopenia (platelets 3 x 10(9)/l), hyporegenerative anemia, and severe neutropenia, with normal blood cell morphology and normal platelet size. Bone marrow was hypocellular with very rare immature megakaryocytes, and the karyotype was normal. Fanconi anemia and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria were excluded, and idiopathic aplastic anemia was initially diagnosed. He went on to have recurrent major hemorrhages (epistaxis, gum, and rectal bleeding) and severe infections (febrile neutropenia or otitis media every 1 to 2 months), which needed multiple platelet and red cell transfusions. His brother had severe isolated thrombocytopenia at age 7 months, with markedly fewer bone marrow megakaryocytes, and had recurrent spontaneous bleeding from age 1.6 years. A younger sister developed a hematoma at a vaccination site at age 2 months and had anemia and severe thrombocytopenia. Pecci et al. (2018) concluded that the trilineage aplasia in the oldest sib suggested that the hematologic picture worsens with age, and that the clinical presentation could not be distinguished from CAMT caused by MPL mutations.

Capaci et al. (2023) reported a 3.5-year-old boy, born to first-cousin Muslim Palestinian parents, who presented with easy bruising and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 21 x 10(9)/L) with a normal mean platelet volume. He had bruised frequently all his life. His platelet count shortly after birth had been 70 x 10(9)/L. At age 2 years, when urethral stenosis was diagnosed, his platelet count was 20 x 10(9)/L. Apart from mild occasional neutropenia, he had no other cytopenias. Bone marrow biopsies showed normocellular marrow with severe megakaryocyte hypoplasia, a few small immature forms, and mild dyserythropoiesis. Chromosomal breakage studies excluded Fanconi anemia, and the bone marrow karyotype was normal. His heterozygous mother had mild thrombocytopenia (platelets about 80 x 10(9)/L) that had worsened during each pregnancy. A heterozygous younger sib had a platelet count of 130 x 10(9)/L around birth and 160 x 10(9)/L several weeks later.

### Biochemical Features

Seo et al. (2017) found that both of the THPO mutations they identified resulted in a lack of THPO in the patients' serum. In 2 affected sibs, Pecci et al. (2018) measured serum THPO levels before treatment of 10.9 and 14.1 pg/ml, which were within the normal range (6.9 to 54.4 pg/ml in 50 healthy subjects). Pecci et al. (2018) pointed out that serum THPO is markedly increased in all forms of bone marrow aplasia-hypoplasia, including CAMT caused by MPL mutations, and that these values were therefore inappropriately low. In the patient reported by Capaci et al. (2023), the serum THPO concentration was 126 pg/mL (laboratory reference range 15-80 pg/mL for children under 15 years of age). The authors considered this lower than expected for amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMT2 in the families reported by Dasouki et al. (2013), Seo et al. (2017), Pecci et al. (2018), and Capaci et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the families reported by Dasouki et al. (2013) and Capaci et al. (2023), heterozygous carriers had mild thrombocytopenia.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs from a Micronesian family with autosomal recessive aplastic anemia, Dasouki et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.112C-T mutation in the THPO gene. The mutation resulted in an arg38-to-cys substitution (R38C; R17C in the mature protein) in the receptor-binding domain. Both parents and a sib were heterozygous for the mutation and had reduced platelet counts. In functional assays, medium containing the mutant THPO was 2- to 3-fold less able than wildtype to sustain the THPO-dependent UT7-TPO cell line. In silico modeling predicted that the extra cysteine could disrupt normal disulfide bonding. Pecci et al. (2018) later showed that R38C THPO was stable and expressed in HEK293T cells but was poorly secreted, with reduced activity in stimulating MPL.

Using exome and targeted sequencing in 5 individuals from 3 unrelated families with severe thrombocytopenia that progressed to bone marrow failure, Seo et al. (2017) identified homozygous mutations in the THPO gene. Affected children in 2 families were homozygous for an R99W mutation, and the affected child in the third family was homozygous for an R157X mutation. Both mutations resulted in a lack of THPO in patient serum. Seo et al. (2017) concluded that biallelic loss-of-function THPO mutations cause bone marrow failure that does not respond to transplant because the mechanism is extrinsic to hematopoietic cells.

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family with CAMT, Pecci et al. (2018) found no variants in MPL but identified a homozygous c.355C-T mutation in the THPO gene, resulting in an arg119-to-cys (R119C) substitution. The methods section of the paper gives the change as c.335C-T. The parents were heterozygous, and the variant was absent from the dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, and ExAC databases. Residue R119 lies in helix C of the receptor-binding domain and is highly conserved. In HEK293T cells, the mutant protein was stable and expressed at least as well as wildtype, but its concentration in the culture supernatant was about 23-fold lower, indicating defective secretion. At equal THPO concentrations, R119C had about 50% of wildtype activity in stimulating proliferation of UT7-TPO cells. It also caused defective phosphorylation of STAT5, ERK1/2, and AKT downstream of MPL.

Capaci et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing in a Palestinian boy with CAMT, born to consanguineous parents. They identified a homozygous c.-323C-T substitution in the promoter region of the THPO gene (NM_000460), 45 nucleotides upstream of the transcription start site of transcript variant 1. The parents and a younger sib were heterozygous, and 2 healthy sibs were homozygous for the wildtype allele. The variant was in dbSNP (rs1208732776) but not in gnomAD, and the surrounding region was conserved across species. No pathogenic variants were found in MPL, MECOM, HOXA11, RBM8A, or genes associated with Fanconi anemia or other inherited bone marrow failure syndromes. In HEK293T cells, the variant reduced promoter-driven luciferase reporter activity by almost 80%. Chromatin immunoprecipitation showed that the variant reduced binding of the transcription factors ETS1 and STAT4 to the promoter. Knockdown of ETS1 and STAT4 in HepG2 liver cells reduced THPO mRNA and protein levels. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Pecci et al. (2018) and Capaci et al. (2023) suggested that measuring serum THPO could help distinguish patients with THPO mutations, in whom levels are within or near the normal range, from patients with MPL mutations or other bone marrow failure syndromes, in whom levels are markedly increased. Capaci et al. (2023) recommended that molecular analysis of THPO, including its regulatory regions, be part of the diagnostic workup in suspected CAMT.

### Clinical Management

In the 2 surviving patients reported by Seo et al. (2017), treatment with a THPO receptor agonist improved trilineage hematopoiesis. By contrast, hematopoietic stem cell transplants in 4 patients resulted in poor graft function.

Pecci et al. (2018) treated 3 affected sibs with romiplostim. The proband had not responded to cyclosporine A or oxymetholone. With romiplostim, starting at 1 microgram/kg/week and later given at 4 microgram/kg monthly, his pancytopenia improved across all 3 lineages, and he had no further spontaneous bleeding, infections, or transfusion requirement over a 6.5-year follow-up. His brother had a good platelet response with remission of bleeding and transfusion independence maintained over 6.3 years. The youngest sib had good platelet and hemoglobin responses. No side effects attributable to romiplostim were observed.

Capaci et al. (2023) treated their patient with eltrombopag, initially at 1.4 mg/kg/day and then at 2.8 mg/kg/day. His platelet count rose gradually to 125 x 10(9)/L, and his easy bruising resolved.
