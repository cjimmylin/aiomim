### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple types of cataract (CTRCT2), with or without microcornea, are caused by heterozygous mutation in the gamma-C-crystallin gene (CRYGC) on chromosome 2q33-q35. ⚠︎

### Description

CRYGC-related cataract is an autosomal dominant congenital lens opacity. The papers describe several morphologic types: Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic nucleus, variable zonular pulverulent cataract, lamellar cataract, and nuclear cataract. Some families also have microcornea (Heon et al., 1999; Ren et al., 2000; Gonzalez-Huerta et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family with hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus that occurred in heterozygotes.

Scott et al. (1994) described a 7-generation family in which 48 of 138 individuals were known to have autosomal dominant congenital pulverulent cataracts. Of 53 individuals examined, 28 had congenital cataracts: 19 unilateral (8 right eyes and 11 left eyes) and 9 bilateral. In patients with unilateral cataract, detailed slit-lamp examination showed no lens opacity in the clinically unaffected eye. Severity ranged from a subtle unilateral zonular cataract with 20/20 visual acuity, to bilateral inner fetal nuclear pulverulent opacities with 20/16 visual acuity in both eyes, to dense unilateral and bilateral nuclear cataracts that required early surgical removal. Counting historical data on unexamined members, there were 48 affected members: 28 unilateral, 17 bilateral, and 3 unexamined obligate carriers. The authors noted that hereditary cataracts are typically symmetric, so this apparently random unilateral or bilateral expression was unusual.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) described a family with several members affected by primary congenital nuclear cataract.

Zhang et al. (2009) studied a 4-generation Chinese family from a relatively isolated region of northern China, in which 6 members had congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea. Affected individuals had bilateral central nuclear opacities involving the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nucleus of the lens. They also had congenitally small eyeballs and corneas about 9 to 10 mm in diameter. Every affected individual had visual acuity of hand movements, together with nystagmus and amblyopia. There were no other ocular or systemic abnormalities.

Li et al. (2012) studied a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract.

Guo et al. (2012) described a 4-generation Chinese family in which 6 members had isolated bilateral nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The opacities involved the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nuclei, and corneal diameter was approximately 9 mm. Age at diagnosis ranged from 6 months to 13 years. The proband was diagnosed at age 8 years and had a best corrected visual acuity of 0.08 in the right eye. All patients had nystagmus and amblyopia, and intraocular pressure was normal.

### Inheritance

Scott et al. (1994), Ren et al. (2000), Zhang et al. (2009), Li et al. (2012), and Guo et al. (2012) reported autosomal dominant inheritance of CRYGC-related cataract. In the family reported by Zhang et al. (2009), male-to-male transmission occurred and penetrance appeared virtually complete. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Lubsen et al. (1987) found that within the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, polymorphic TaqI sites defined haplotypes, and a haplotype (P) occurred only in the family members with Coppock-like cataract. Every affected family member carried it. The cataract locus was closely linked to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, with a maximum lod score of 7.58 at theta = 0.

In the 7-generation family with variable zonular pulverulent cataract first reported by Scott et al. (1994), Ren et al. (2000) mapped the disorder to a 19-cM interval on chromosome 2q33-q35 that included the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. Maximum lod scores were 4.56 (theta = 0.02) with D2S157, 3.66 (theta = 0.12) with D2S72, and 3.57 (theta = 0.052) with CRYG. ⚠︎

In a 4-generation Chinese family with nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) obtained a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.29 at marker D2S325 (theta = 0.0). Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 19.04-cM region on 2q32.3-q35, bounded by D2S117 and D2S2382.

### Molecular Genetics

Heon et al. (1999) found that the sequence changes in the CRYGE pseudogene, previously proposed as the cause of Coppock-like cataract, were a polymorphism present in 23% of their control population. On further analysis of the original Coppock-like cataract family (Lubsen et al., 1987), they identified a missense mutation in CRYGC, in a highly conserved segment of exon 2. The mutation was not found in a large control population. Zhang et al. (2009) and Guo et al. (2012) later listed this mutation as thr5-to-pro (T5P), and Guo et al. (2012) gave its nucleotide change as c.13A-C.

In the family with variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataract reported by Scott et al. (1994), Ren et al. (2000) sequenced the CRYGC and CRYGD genes. CRYGD was normal. In every affected family member they found a heterozygous 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of CRYGC, which was absent from unaffected family members and controls. The duplication disrupts the reading frame and is predicted to produce an unstable gamma-C-crystallin made up of 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids. Ren et al. (2000) also found that the pseudo-gamma-E-crystallin promoter sequence made activation of the pseudogene an unlikely cause of cataract in this family. TATA-box changes were found in unaffected controls.

In a family with primary congenital nuclear cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) sequenced the CRYGA-CRYGD gene cluster and found a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in CRYGC, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disease and was not found in healthy family members or in 170 unrelated controls. The authors noted that this mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract but was listed as a SNP in the NCBI database. They concluded that R168W is disease-causing and that it shows phenotypic heterogeneity.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.470G-A change in exon 3 of CRYGC, predicted to produce a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution at a phylogenetically conserved residue. The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was not found in 100 normal controls. It was predicted to truncate 17 C-terminal amino acids and to disrupt the fourth Greek key motif. ⚠︎

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) sequenced candidate genes and identified a heterozygous c.385G-T mutation in CRYGC, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. In molecular modeling and spectroscopic studies, the mutation impaired the protein's tertiary structure by altering the hydrogen-bonding network in the C-terminal domain. The mutant protein had greatly reduced thermal stability. It was also much more prone to aggregate at high concentrations and under heat and UV-irradiation stress.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) screened 9 candidate genes. They identified a heterozygous c.471G-A transition (TGG-to-TGA) in exon 3 of CRYGC, predicted to cause a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was not found in 100 ethnically matched controls. This was the same protein change reported by Zhang et al. (2009), but caused by a different nucleotide substitution. Guo et al. (2012) noted that the phenotypes of the 2 families were similar. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Li et al. (2012) found that expressing the G129C mutant gamma-C-crystallin in zebrafish caused lens vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens, recapitulating the human cataract phenotype.

### History

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) cloned the CRYGD and pseudo-gamma-E (CRYGE) genes from the Coppock-like cataract chromosome in the family reported by Lubsen et al. (1987). The CRYGD gene was functionally equivalent to its wildtype allele. The CRYGE pseudogene, however, had a cluster of sequence changes within and around its TATA box. Together these changes increased promoter activity 10-fold, raising CRYGE expression to 30% of that of CRYGD. The authors proposed that reactivation of the pseudogene, with overexpression of a predicted 6-kD N-terminal gamma-crystallin fragment, could cause the cataract. Heon et al. (1999) later found that these CRYGE changes were a polymorphism present in 23% of controls. They identified a CRYGC missense mutation in the original family and noted that there was no direct evidence that upregulation of a pseudogene causes cataract. ⚠︎
