### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene on chromosome 10.

### Description

Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is an autosomal recessive disorder of CoQ10 biosynthesis that causes oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) deficiency (Mollet et al., 2007). The phenotype varies widely. Some patients have sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, with mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and cardiac valvulopathy. Others have a severe infantile multisystem disorder that includes encephalopathy, nephrotic syndrome or pulmonary hypertension, and early death (Nardecchia et al., 2021; Bellusci et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) found an inborn error of CoQ10 biosynthesis in a family (family 1) with OXPHOS deficiency. Nardecchia et al. (2021) summarized the clinical features of this family's 2 affected sibs, a male examined at 22 years and a female examined at 14 years. Both had mild intellectual disability, lactic acidosis, macrocephaly, peripheral neuropathy, muscle weakness or exercise intolerance, sensorineural deafness, optic atrophy, and cardiac valvulopathy, as well as obesity/livedo reticularis. In both sibs, sensorineural hearing loss came before optic atrophy.

Using targeted next-generation sequencing of patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders, Vasta et al. (2012) identified variants in several known pathogenic genes, including PDSS1. As summarized by Nardecchia et al. (2021), the patient with PDSS1 variants had developmental delay, failure to thrive, and leukoencephalopathy with a lactic acid peak on spectroscopy. This patient also had severe nephrotic syndrome, which led to kidney failure and death at 16 months of age.

Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported 2 sisters with COQ10D2 born to consanguineous Egyptian parents. The proband was 16 years old at the time of the report. She had severe language delay, and profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss was found at age 4 years. Mild intellectual disability was diagnosed later. Her visual acuity was normal at 11 years but had fallen to 1/10 bilaterally by 14 years, with red-green dyschromatopsia. Fundus examination showed optic atrophy that was most marked in the inferior temporal sector. Visual evoked potentials showed increased P100 latency and reduced amplitude, and optical coherence tomography showed reduced retinal nerve fiber layer thickness. She also had diffuse mild muscle weakness and a mild postural tremor of the upper limbs. Brain MRI was normal. Echocardiography showed a moderately dysplastic mitral valve with moderate insufficiency, tricuspid insufficiency, and a moderately dilated left atrium. Her 6-year-old sister had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. At age 6 she developed decreased visual acuity, with optic atrophy and divergent strabismus. Neither sister had lactic acidosis.

Bellusci et al. (2021) reported a boy with COQ10D2 who was born prematurely. At age 5 months he had failure to thrive, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, bilateral hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, and chronic distal phalangeal erythema. Laboratory studies showed anemia, lactic acidosis, 3-methylglutaconic and tricarboxylic aciduria, and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes. He developed a progressive multisystem disorder with severe developmental delay, pyramidal dysfunction, seizures, tremor, optic atrophy, nystagmus, severe visual and hearing impairment, and chronic heart failure. Brain MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy at 12 months and cystic white matter changes at 24 months. High-dose CoQ10 supplementation did not halt progression, and he died at 3 years of age.

### Biochemical Features

In family 1, Mollet et al. (2007) found that quinone-dependent OXPHOS activities were in the range of the lowest control values, while OXPHOS enzyme activities themselves were normal. Adding quinone restored normal OXPHOS activities, which confirmed CoQ10 deficiency.

### Inheritance

The homozygous and compound heterozygous PDSS1 mutations reported by Mollet et al. (2007), Nardecchia et al. (2021), and Bellusci et al. (2021) are consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the family studied by Nardecchia et al. (2021), both parents carried the mutation and an unaffected brother was heterozygous. ⚠︎

### Mapping

In family 1, Mollet et al. (2007) performed a genome-wide search for homozygosity and identified a region on chromosome 10 that contains the PDSS1 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a family with CoQ10 deficiency, Mollet et al. (2007) identified a homozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene (D308E) that changes a conserved amino acid. When the corresponding mutant yeast COQ1 gene was transformed into a yeast Delta-coq1 strain, the cells grew poorly on respiratory medium, indicating that the mutation causes the OXPHOS deficiency.

Using targeted next-generation sequencing, Vasta et al. (2012) identified PDSS1 variants in a patient with a suspected mitochondrial disorder. According to Nardecchia et al. (2021), this patient was compound heterozygous for a missense variant and a truncating variant. ⚠︎

Whole-exome sequencing in 2 Egyptian sisters with COQ10D2 born to consanguineous parents identified a homozygous missense mutation in the PDSS1 gene (Q245H; Nardecchia et al., 2021). The mutation was absent from gnomAD and lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity. It segregated with the disorder in the family.

In a boy with COQ10D2, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene, V239G and R395X.

### Clinical Management

The patient reported by Bellusci et al. (2021) received oral ubidecarenone, starting at 15 mg/kg/day and later increased to 30 mg/kg/day. The treatment did not halt the progression of his disease.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, ASP308GLU [c.924T>G; p.Asp308Glu; D308E]

In affected members of a family with CoQ10 deficiency (COQ10D2), Mollet et al. (2007) identified a homozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene that results in an asp308-to-glu (D308E) substitution at a conserved residue. Nardecchia et al. (2021) gave the nucleotide change as c.924T-G. When the corresponding mutant yeast COQ1 gene was transformed into a yeast Delta-coq1 strain, the cells grew poorly on respiratory medium.

**.0002 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, GLN245HIS [c.735G>T; p.Gln245His]

In 2 sisters with COQ10D2 born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, Nardecchia et al. (2021) identified a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene that results in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution at a highly conserved position. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and was absent from gnomAD. Both parents were heterozygous. Of the 2 unaffected sibs, one was heterozygous and the other was homozygous wildtype.

**.0003 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, VAL239GLY [NM_014317.3:c.716T>G; p.Val239Gly]

In a boy with severe COQ10D2 who died at age 3 years, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene. One was a c.716T-G transversion (NM_014317.3) that results in a val239-to-gly (V239G) substitution. The other was a c.1183C-T transition that results in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution.

**.0004 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, ARG395TER [NM_014317.3:c.1183C>T; p.Arg395Ter]

For discussion of the c.1183C-T transition (NM_014317.3) in the PDSS1 gene, which results in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution and was found in compound heterozygous state in a patient with COQ10D2 by Bellusci et al. (2021), see the V239G entry.
