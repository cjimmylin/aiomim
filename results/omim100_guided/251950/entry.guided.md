### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder. In the first patient with PNPLA8 mutations, reported by Saunders et al. (2015), it presented as progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia, seizures, poor weight gain, and lactic acidosis. Nakamura et al. (2024) reviewed current and previously reported patients and concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum. At the severe end is developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia, and at the milder end is childhood-onset neurodegeneration. A milder phenotype of adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy has also been described (Burnyte et al., 2023).

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) reported a 23-year-old man with mitochondrial myopathy. He had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy appeared. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and severe lactic acidosis developed at one stage of the illness. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and accumulation of neutral lipids, which was confirmed histochemically and by thin-layer chromatography.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl with a suspected mitochondrial myopathy. She was asymptomatic in her first year and met all developmental milestones, although her parents noted toe-walking. A positive Gowers sign was noted at age 2 years, and by age 3 years she could no longer walk. Her fine motor skills declined, and her speech became dysarthric. She had proximal muscle weakness, tight heel cords, and dysmetria, and her hypotonicity progressed to spasticity. Later she developed complex partial seizures with recurrent left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. She had a persistently very lean body habitus and significant lactic acidosis requiring bicarbonate supplementation. The anion gap had been elevated (17 mmol/L) since age 5. At age 6, cerebrospinal fluid showed deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. At age 7, blood lactic acid was 3.8 to 5.6 mmol/L (normal, 0.7-2.1) and pyruvic acid 0.18 to 0.32 mmol/L (normal, 0.08-0.16), with lactate:pyruvate ratios of 17.5 to 21.1. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle increased signal in the medial globus pallidus bilaterally, which was no longer seen 2 years later. A muscle biopsy at age 4 years showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic fibers and no ragged red fibers. On electron microscopy, mitochondria showed marked disarray of cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions, and there were subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes. Secondary lysosomes containing residual bodies that appeared to be derived from mitochondria were increased.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls. Patient 1, of Indian origin, was the second child of consanguineous parents. She had congenital microcephaly (head circumference 29 cm, -4 SD, at birth), clonic seizures beginning on the second day of life, and spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak.

Patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018), born to nonconsanguineous parents of Indian/Fijian origin, developed normally until about 13 months of age. She then regressed, with episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures. She developed acquired microcephaly (head circumference 49 cm, -4.7 SD, at 15 years of age). She remained severely delayed and nonverbal, needed a gastrostomy tube, and developed choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, and severe contractures. Over a decade, MRI showed progressive volume loss in the cerebellum, brainstem, and bilateral parietal and occipital lobes, with thinning of the corpus callosum. MRS showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. On electron microscopy, muscle showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria with surrounding membrane whorls. Blood lactate was elevated on several occasions, and at age 14 years growth and differentiation factor-15 was 1293 pg/ml (normal, less than 750). She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, severely reduced amplitudes with prolonged latencies on visual evoked potentials, and obstructive sleep apnea. ⚠︎

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset, severe, progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) reported 2 adult sibs with adolescence-onset ataxia, progressive sensorimotor polyneuropathy, and cognition that remained intact over time. The brother, now 49 years old, developed weakness and distal muscle atrophy of the lower limbs at age 14, together with ataxia and gait disturbance. He could not walk without assistance from age 17 and became nonambulant at age 44. Findings included tetraparesis, absent tendon reflexes in the lower limbs, bilateral Babinski signs, marked dysmetria, foot deformities, and sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. At age 45 he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, and his Mini-Mental State Examination score was 30/30. Brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. His sister developed walking difficulty and lower limb weakness at age 14 years. At age 21 she had distal limb muscle atrophy, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia, appendicular dysmetria, and axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, and her cognition was normal.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 had progressive microcephaly, infantile-onset seizures, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a distinct phenotype.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals from 12 unrelated families. Frequent features included central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). The 8 patients from Families 1 to 8 formed a severe group. Their features included congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7 of 7), peripheral spasticity (7 of 8), seizure onset within 7 months of age (8 of 8), a simplified gyral pattern (6 of 6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7 of 7), and severe progressive atrophy of the cerebral cortex (5 of 5). The 4 patients from Families 11 and 12 formed a mild group. All 4 learned to walk and later regressed, 3 had ataxia, and 3 had mild or no intellectual disability. They had little or no cerebral cortical volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Patients from Families 9 and 10 had intermediate severity. In skin fibroblasts from Patient 1, maximal mitochondrial respiration was significantly lower than in controls, but mitochondrial morphology was not altered.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015), muscle showed normal SDH and COX oxidative enzyme reactions and normal electron transport chain function. The authors suggested that, without an elevated lactate:pyruvate ratio, these results pointed to mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect. Shukla et al. (2018) likewise found normal activities of complexes I through IV in muscle from their patient 2.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MMLA in the family reported by Saunders et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance: the compound heterozygous PNPLA8 mutations were inherited from carrier parents. Shukla et al. (2018) and Nakamura et al. (2024) also reported autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 7-year-old girl with MMLA, Saunders et al. (2015) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4) (NM_001256011.1). Both mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with the disorder. Each parent was a carrier, and 2 unaffected sibs carried neither variant. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. On Western blot, patient muscle showed a dramatic decrease in the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 protein bands.

Shukla et al. (2018) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 unrelated girls with MMLA. Patient 1, born to consanguineous parents, was homozygous for a nonsense mutation in PNPLA8 (c.517G-T, Glu173Ter; NM_001256007.1), and her parents were heterozygous. Patient 2 was compound heterozygous for a frameshift mutation (c.2275_2276delCT, Leu759AlafsTer4) and a nonsense mutation (c.419C-A, Ser140Ter). Segregation analysis showed that the variants were inherited from carrier parents.

In a patient with prenatal-onset severe neurodegeneration, Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation (c.1874A-G) in the patatin domain of PNPLA8.

In 2 sibs with adolescence-onset ataxia and peripheral neuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in exon 11 of PNPLA8 (NM_001256007.3). One was a novel 1-bp deletion (c.2269del, Thr757GlnfsTer10) that was absent from the ExAC, 1000 Genomes Project, and gnomAD databases. The other was the previously reported c.2275_2276del (Leu759AlafsTer4) mutation, which was maternally inherited. Paternal DNA was unavailable, but whole-exome and Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles. Sanger sequencing confirmed both mutations in the affected sister.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing to identify PNPLA8 mutations in 3 patients from 2 families: a loss-of-function variant (c.1231C-T, Arg411Ter) in Family I and a missense variant (c.1559T-A, Val520Asp) in Family II.

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified biallelic PNPLA8 variants in 14 individuals from 12 unrelated families using proband-only or trio exome or genome sequencing. One of these families had previously been reported by Denommé-Pichon et al. (2022). There were 14 different variants, 12 of them novel: 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near an exon-intron boundary, and 2 missense. All had frequencies of less than 0.001% in population databases. In patient lymphoblastoid cells, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant in Patient 1 caused skipping of exons 6 to 8, and the patient's fibroblasts showed a significant reduction in the 77-kD PNPLA8 isoform. Fibroblasts from Patient 6, who carried a frameshift variant (Glu296LysfsTer6), also showed a null effect. Cerebral organoids derived from Patient 1 induced pluripotent stem cells had fewer basal radial glial cells and fewer SATB2-positive upper-layer neurons than control organoids. Adding lysophosphatidic acid partly restored the number of basal radial glial cells. Two further individuals from a non-neurologic cohort, one with optic atrophy and one with nonsyndromic hearing loss, were homozygous for N-terminal frameshift variants that affect only the 88-kD isoform. The authors considered the significance of these variants undetermined.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that the c.2275_2276del mutation had previously been reported in a compound heterozygous state, together with another truncating variant, in a patient with severe early-childhood neurodegeneration. In their sibs, the combination of c.2275_2276del with c.2269del gave a milder phenotype. Because both truncating variants lie in the last coding exon, the authors suggested that the transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and retain partial enzyme activity.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) suggested that the site of the variant, rather than its type, was strongly correlated with the age at disease onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) compared genotypes and phenotypes across their 14 patients and 11 previously reported patients. Biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were found specifically in the severe group, which included patients with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. All patients with the relatively mild phenotype of late-onset movement disorder carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (Thr757GlnfsTer10 or Leu759AlafsTer4). Fibroblasts from their Patient 12, who had the mild phenotype and carried Leu759AlafsTer4, showed weak expression of the 77-kD isoform, suggesting residual protein function. In contrast, the homozygous Val520Asp missense variant found in a previously reported patient with a severe phenotype gave protein expression similar to that of controls.

### Clinical Management

In the patient reported by Mastaglia et al. (1980), the myopathy improved markedly after treatment with prednisone and thiamine was started. The improvement was maintained after these drugs were withdrawn.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that Pnpla8-null mice had a markedly elevated hippocampal cardiolipin content with a shift toward shorter-chain molecular species, decreased plasmenylethanolamine, increased oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species, and increased ceramides. On electron microscopy, the hippocampus contained enlarged, heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. Proteomic analysis identified these structures as degenerating mitochondria. The authors concluded that loss of Pnpla8 function causes a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder characterized by degenerating mitochondria, autophagy, and cognitive dysfunction.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that the mitochondrial ultrastructural changes in their patient's muscle closely matched those seen in Pnpla8-null mice. They also noted that the null mice have impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, and a thin body habitus.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 334AATT [c.334_337delAATT; p.Asn112HisfsX29]

In a 7-year-old girl with mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA), Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT, NM_001256011.1) that causes a frameshift (Asn112HisfsX29). The other was a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG) causing Leu659AlafsX4. The mutations were found by exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and they were inherited from carrier parents. Neither was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot showed a dramatic decrease of PNPLA8 protein in patient muscle.

**.0002 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 1975AG [c.1975_1976delAG; p.Leu659AlafsX4]

In a girl with MMLA, Saunders et al. (2015) found a 2-bp deletion in the PNPLA8 gene (c.1975_1976delAG, NM_001256011.1) that causes a frameshift (Leu659AlafsX4). It was in compound heterozygosity with a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT). The mutation was inherited from a carrier parent and was absent from the Exome Sequencing Project database. Patient muscle showed near absence of PNPLA8 protein.

**.0003 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, GLU173TER [c.517G>T; p.Glu173Ter]

In a girl of Indian origin with congenital microcephaly, neonatal seizures, spasticity, and a hypoplastic cerebellum, born to consanguineous parents, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous c.517G-T transversion in the PNPLA8 gene (NM_001256007.1). The change results in a glu173-to-ter (E173X) substitution. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and both parents were heterozygous.

**.0004 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 2275CT [c.2275_2276delCT; p.Leu759AlafsTer4]

In a girl with MMLA who regressed after 13 months of age and had acquired microcephaly, dystonia, lactic acidosis, and progressive cerebellar atrophy, Shukla et al. (2018) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT, NM_001256007.1) causing a frameshift (Leu759AlafsTer4). The other was a c.419C-A transversion causing a ser140-to-ter (S140X) substitution. The mutations were found by whole-exome sequencing and were inherited from carrier parents.

In 2 sibs with adolescence-onset ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified the c.2275_2276del mutation (NM_001256007.3) in compound heterozygosity with a 1-bp deletion (c.2269del, Thr757GlnfsTer10) in exon 11 of PNPLA8. The c.2275_2276del mutation was maternally inherited.

Nakamura et al. (2024) described Leu759AlafsTer4 as a recurrent C-terminal frameshift variant. All patients with the relatively mild late-onset phenotype carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (Leu759AlafsTer4 or Thr757GlnfsTer10). Fibroblasts from their Patient 12, who carried Leu759AlafsTer4, showed weak expression of the 77-kD PNPLA8 isoform.
