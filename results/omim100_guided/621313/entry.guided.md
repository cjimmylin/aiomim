### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene.

### Description

Brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is an autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease that begins in mid-adulthood. Rutten et al. (2024) described a triad of movement disorders, numerous and massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and non-lobar intracerebral hemorrhage. Other reported features include neuropsychological deterioration and psychiatric illness. Rendu et al. (2019) reported a positive urine sulfite test, which they linked to urinary excretion of deaminated glutathione (dGSH). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) reported 3 brothers with bilateral small cystic lesions of the basal ganglia ('deep cavitating state') and a positive urine dipstick test for sulfites. The index patient was a man with nonsevere mental retardation. He had an acute psychotic episode followed by generalized seizures and, at age 34, presented with a sudden pure motor left hemiparesis. CT and MRI showed bilateral small cystic lesions affecting the basal ganglia. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and echocardiography were normal. Over a 20-year period he developed severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia, and he died at age 57. A brother with diabetes mellitus who had led an active professional life presented at age 58 with sudden coma and cardiovascular collapse. He recovered but had neuropsychological deterioration (BREF 14/18, MMSE 22/30). A third brother had been diagnosed with schizophrenia 40 years earlier and had a normal clinical examination. Both of these brothers had the same brain lesions on CT and 3-T MRI and a positive urine sulfite test. The mother and 3 other sibs had no symptoms and negative urine sulfite tests. The authors noted that the cavities differed from the cavitary lesions frequently reported in adults because of their basal ganglia location.

Rutten et al. (2024) studied 7 patients from 5 unrelated pedigrees: 3 sibs and 4 single cases. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous and massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia. Three patients had non-lobar intracerebral hemorrhage between ages 45 and 60, which was fatal in 2. On brain autopsy, large electron-dense deposits were seen in the vessel walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) performed qualitative urinary organic acid analysis by GC/MS. In the 3 affected brothers they identified a compound with a mass spectrum identical to a dGSH derivative, matching the compound found in the urine of Nit1-knockout mice. This compound was undetectable in the unaffected sibs and mother who were tested. The authors suggested that dGSH accumulation was probably responsible for the positive urine sulfite test. In patient fibroblasts, cytoplasmic (Cu/Zn) superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was unchanged, but mitochondrial SOD (MnSOD) activity was increased 2-fold compared with controls, which the authors interpreted as evidence of oxidative stress. ⚠︎

Rutten et al. (2024) found that metabolic analysis of urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzymatic function in their patients.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance of BSVD4 was proposed by Rendu et al. (2019) in a family with 3 affected brothers who were compound heterozygous for NIT1 mutations. Rutten et al. (2024) confirmed it with 4 additional patients from unrelated pedigrees who were homozygous for a NIT1 mutation. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Rendu et al. (2019) performed exome sequencing in 2 brothers with BSVD4. Both were compound heterozygous for 2 mutations in the NIT1 gene (NM_005600). The first was a c.457G-A transition, predicted to cause a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution at a highly conserved residue. Because it lies at the last nucleotide of exon 4, it was also predicted to affect splicing, and sequencing of NIT1 transcripts from a skin biopsy confirmed abnormal splicing. The second was a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA), predicted to cause a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41). The duplication was inherited from the asymptomatic mother; the father's DNA was unavailable. Molecular cloning confirmed that the 2 mutations were on different alleles. Asymptomatic sibs who were tested carried neither mutation. NIT1 protein was undetectable on Western blot of patient fibroblasts. The third affected brother had died, and his DNA was not available. ⚠︎

Rutten et al. (2024) performed exome sequencing in 7 patients with BSVD4 from 5 unrelated pedigrees. Three sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 NIT1 mutations: a c.727C-T transition, resulting in an arg243-to-trp (R243W) substitution, and a 2-bp deletion (c.198_199del), resulting in a premature stop (Ala68Ter). The 4 remaining patients were single cases from 4 unrelated pedigrees, and all were homozygous for the R243W mutation. Western blotting of patient fibroblasts showed absence of NIT1 protein.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 BRAIN SMALL VESSEL DISEASE 4**  
NIT1, GLY153ARG [NM_005600:c.457G>A; p.Gly153Arg]

In 2 brothers with BSVD4 and bilateral cystic lesions of the basal ganglia, Rendu et al. (2019) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene (NM_005600). One allele carried a c.457G-A transition, predicted to cause a gly153-to-arg (G153R) substitution at a highly conserved residue. This change lies at the last nucleotide of exon 4, and transcript sequencing from a patient skin biopsy showed that it affected splicing. The other allele carried a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA). Cloning showed that the 2 mutations were on different alleles, and unaffected sibs who were tested carried neither mutation. NIT1 protein was undetectable in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎
