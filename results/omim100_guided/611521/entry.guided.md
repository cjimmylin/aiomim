### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), also known as tyrosine kinase-2 (TYK2) deficiency, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency. Its core feature is susceptibility to intracellular bacteria, such as BCG and other mycobacteria, Salmonella, and Brucella, and/or to viruses, particularly herpesviruses. The first reported patient was diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES) because he had atopic dermatitis, high serum IgE, and staphylococcal infections (Minegishi et al., 2006). Most later patients have had no features of HIES (Kreins et al., 2015). Patient cells show impaired but not abolished responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta, and IL-10 (Kreins et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

TYK2 deficiency was first described in a patient diagnosed with autosomal recessive hyper-IgE syndrome (Minegishi et al., 2006). After further patients were reported without HIES, Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that impaired IL-6 responses and HIES do not appear to be intrinsic features of human TYK2 deficiency. Wu et al. (2020) noted that human TYK2 deficiency had been proposed to be a distinct primary immunodeficiency rather than a common cause of autosomal recessive HIES, and that the International Union of Immunological Societies (IUIS) had classified it under mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. He had unusual susceptibility to viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE. Kreins et al. (2015) gave further details of this patient (P1), a Japanese man born to consanguineous parents. He had atopic dermatitis from age 1 month and recurrent bacterial and viral infections from age 1 year. At 22 months, 6 months after vaccination, he developed BCGitis. He had repeated skin abscesses caused by S. aureus, pneumonia without pneumatocele formation, otitis, and sinusitis. At 15 years he developed sepsis after severe nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis. Viral infections included parainfluenza virus-3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV infections with tonsillitis, and molluscum contagiosum. He also had mild oral candidiasis. Investigations repeatedly showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE (1,430-2,100 IU/ml), and he had an NIH HIES score of 48. Kilic et al. (2012) noted that he had no bone fractures and that his dentition and facial appearance were normal.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy with IMD35 born to first-cousin parents. He received BCG vaccine at 2 months. Left axillary lymphadenopathy appeared at 8 months, and by 21 months he had generalized lymphadenopathy and fatigue. At 2 years he had fever and fistulous bilateral cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymphadenopathy, and M. bovis BCG was isolated from the draining cervical sinuses. At 8 years he developed Brucella meningitis (neurobrucellosis) with a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. This was followed by severe hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, and lacunar infarcts on MRI. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve. He had no atopy, staphylococcal infection, candidiasis, or skeletal or dental features of HIES. Serum IgE was 80, 115, 218, and 155 IU/ml at ages 2, 11, 16, and 18 years, respectively.

Kreins et al. (2015) reported 7 further TYK2-deficient patients (P2-P8) from 5 families. P2 was the Turkish patient reported by Kilic et al. (2012). P3, a Moroccan girl born to first-cousin parents, was hospitalized at 13 years with suspected abdominal tuberculosis and later had disseminated extrapulmonary tuberculosis with ascites and a psoas abscess. She died of bacterial meningitis 15 months after symptom onset. Her brother (P4) had meningitis of unknown etiology at 8 months, recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, eczema of the ear canal, and high eosinophil levels. P5, a boy born to consanguineous Iranian parents, had BCG lymphadenitis at 2 and 4 months. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with pulmonary involvement. His younger sister (P6) had BCG lymphadenopathy and, at 1 year, an unidentified viral skin infection. P7, a 9-year-old Iranian girl born to consanguineous parents, had miliary tuberculosis. P8, an 11-year-old boy from Argentina, had recurrent herpes gingivostomatitis, aseptic herpes meningitis, and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions. He also had mild mental disability that may have been congenital or postmeningitic. None of P2-P8 had documented staphylococcal or fungal infections, and serum IgE was never high. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) reported a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and IgE levels above 1000 U/mL, but no viral or mycobacterial infections. They described the phenotype as surprisingly mild.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy with IMD35 born to nonconsanguineous parents. After BCG vaccination on day 3 of life, he had repeated abscess and ulceration at the injection site, which healed by 10 months. At 14 months he developed BCG lymph node tuberculosis in the left axilla. From age 6 months he had recurrent respiratory tract infections and diarrhea. Pathogens found during his admissions included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and M. bovis BCG, and IgM antibodies to influenza A and B viruses were positive. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, or staphylococcal disease. He spoke his first simple words at 3 years 4 months, and his head circumference was small. Natural killer cells were reduced (3.6%) and CD19+ B cells were increased (26.6%).

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 TYK2-deficient patients with or without hyper-IgE levels and atopy, each with a distinct profile of infections. Lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted. ⚠︎

Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated Turkish children with COVID-19 pneumonia in an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for the disease. They were a 2-year-old girl and boys aged 4 and 9 years, each born to consanguineous parents. The girl had moderate pneumonia, and the 2 boys had critical pneumonia requiring ICU admission. Earlier, the girl had been hospitalized for infection from a live viral vaccine, the 4-year-old boy had been admitted to the ICU for influenza pneumonia at 3 years, and the 9-year-old boy had recurrent bronchitis. The 3-year-old sister of the 4-year-old boy carried the same genotype. She had had critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and disseminated varicella, but had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance of IMD35 was established by Kreins et al. (2015). In all 5 kindreds they studied, clinical disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. Wu et al. (2020) found that the parents of their patient were heterozygous carriers. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance by family segregation analysis in 3 patients. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient with IMD35 who had been diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. Transducing the intact TYK2 gene into the patient's cells restored the defective cytokine signals. Kreins et al. (2015) reported that this patient (P1) carries a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4. In a later tabulation, Wu et al. (2020) gave this mutation as c.550_553delGCTT (C70HfsX21).

In a Turkish boy with IMD35 born to first-cousin parents, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del), which creates a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein was detected by Western blotting in the patient's EBV-B cells. Kreins et al. (2015) stated that this mutation was identified by a genome-wide linkage study and Sanger sequencing, and designated it c.2303_2311del (L767X).

In 6 patients with IMD35 from 4 families, Kreins et al. (2015) identified 4 further homozygous TYK2 mutations by whole-exome sequencing or targeted next-generation sequencing. All were predicted to cause a premature stop codon. The mutations were c.3318_3319insC (T1106HfsX4) in Moroccan sibs, c.462G-T (E154X) in Iranian sibs, c.149delC (S50HfsX1) in an Iranian girl, and c.1912C-T (R638X) in an Argentinian boy. None of these mutations was present in an in-house exome database or in public databases. Western blotting with 4 antibodies against N- or C-terminal TYK2 epitopes detected no TYK2 protein in cells from any patient, indicating complete deficiency.

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient and found that the mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in a monocyte assay. In the literature review by Wu et al. (2020), this patient was listed as a Kurdish boy homozygous for c.647delC in exon 7 (P216RfsX14). ⚠︎

In a Chinese boy with IMD35, Wu et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.2395G-A transition in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous. TYK2 mRNA was decreased and TYK2 protein was undetectable in patient-derived cells. In HEK293T cells, the mutant reduced expression of FLAG- and GFP-tagged TYK2 at both mRNA and protein levels. The variant was present in gnomAD. Wu et al. (2020) also tabulated 2 sibs reported by Nemoto with compound heterozygous TYK2 mutations (c.209_212delGCTT and c.691C-T; C70SfsX21 and R231W), T-cell lymphopenia, and EBV-associated B-cell lymphoma.

Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations in 5 patients with TYK2 deficiency by high-throughput sequencing, with Sanger confirmation. They confirmed the pathogenic effects using qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays.

By exome or genome sequencing, Zhang et al. (2022) identified the same homozygous TYK2 variant, P216fs*14, in 3 unrelated Turkish children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. They confirmed that the variant is loss-of-function. Fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for P216fs*14 could not control SARS-CoV-2 replication and failed to induce interferon-stimulated genes, even after pretreatment with IFN-alpha-2b.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) found that cells from their patient had defects in several cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23. The patient also showed impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that cells from all 8 TYK2-deficient patients had impaired but not abolished responses to IL-12, IFN-alpha/beta, IL-23, and IL-10. They linked the IL-12 defect to mycobacterial infections and the IFN-alpha/beta defect to viral infections. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda, and LIF were normal. Proportions of circulating IL-17-positive T cells were normal, which may explain why the patients did not develop chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis. Surface expression of IFNAR1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1 was reduced and was rescued by wildtype TYK2. Leukocytes and fibroblasts from P2-P8 responded normally to IL-6, whereas P1 cells did not. Neither expression of wildtype TYK2 nor silencing of TYK2 changed the IL-6 hyporesponsiveness of P1 cells. The authors therefore concluded that this defect was independent of the TYK2 genotype and might underlie P1's HIES features. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) found that the patient's TYK2 mutation partly reduced IL-10 signaling, with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not affect IL-6 signaling. In a TYK2-deficient human cell line, type III IFN signaling was almost normal and virus control was only minimally impaired. NK-cell phenotype and function, including IL-12/IL-18 responses, were normal in the patient. The authors suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may contribute to antiviral protection.

Lv et al. (2022) found heterogeneous responses to IFN-alpha/beta/gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23 among their patients. They proposed that different combinations of cytokine signaling defects account for the divergent clinical features.
