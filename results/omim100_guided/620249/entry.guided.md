### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10B (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene on chromosome 5.

### Description

The mild variant of congenital myopathy-10B (CMYO10B) is a muscle disorder that is milder than the classic early-onset form. Features include axial and proximal muscle weakness, joint contractures, scoliosis, and respiratory insufficiency that begins in childhood or later. Muscle biopsy may show minicores along with dystrophic or myofibrillar changes (Boyden et al., 2012; Harris et al., 2018; Croci et al., 2022).

### Nomenclature

Boyden et al. (2012) described the disorder as a recessive congenital myopathy with minicores. Takayama et al. (2016) referred to it as the milder phenotype of minicore myopathy, distinct from early-onset myopathy, areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD). Croci et al. (2022) used the term 'mild variant EMARDD' (mvEMARDD).

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) reported a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had an unclassified myopathy. Weakness began by 12 months of age, first in the neck. It was mainly axial and proximal, included facial weakness, and stabilized in adolescence. Neck contractures appeared in infancy, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords developed by adulthood. Serum CK measured before age 10 years was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had childhood-onset scoliosis treated with spinal fusion. Beginning at age 10 to 11 years, all developed respiratory problems treated with nighttime bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation. One patient was born with cleft palate, a second had a midline ridge on her palate, and all 3 had hypernasal speech. Muscle biopsies showed dystrophic features, including fatty replacement, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers. They also showed moth-eaten fibers and minicores, a slight type I fiber predominance, and marked variation in fiber size.

Boyden et al. (2012) also reported a Caucasian female of distant Portuguese ancestry (subject 105-1) who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease. At several weeks of age she presented with hypotonia, a weak neck, and a floppy head. She also had a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. As a child she had frequent acute pulmonary infections but no chronic breathing problems. At age 10 years her serum CK was 302 U/L and she had mild scoliosis, which progressed to 20 degrees by age 12 years. Muscle biopsy showed fiber size variation, internalized nuclei, and slightly increased endomysial connective tissue. Electron microscopy confirmed minicores.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. He developed respiratory failure requiring ventilation in his 50s, while still ambulant. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents at 33 weeks' gestation. He had weak suction, and elbow contractures and scoliosis were present from the first months of life. Motor development was not delayed. He developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, and dysphagia. Respiratory involvement required nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years, and from age 17 years he had recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration. On examination he had axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy and weakness, cleft palate, global areflexia, contractures of the elbows and ankles, hyperlordosis, and an equinovarus foot stance. Serum CK was normal. A first muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: fiber size variation, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A later biopsy showed whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance was suggested by Boyden et al. (2012), who found compound heterozygous MEGF10 mutations that segregated with disease in a family with 3 affected sibs. It was further supported by Croci et al. (2022), who found a homozygous mutation in the son of consanguineous parents who were both carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) carried out SNP-based linkage analysis in family 1030 and obtained positive lod scores at 16 genomic loci. The MEGF10 gene lies within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible lod score of 1.329.

### Molecular Genetics

Boyden et al. (2012) performed whole-genome sequencing in an affected member of family 1030, filtered by linkage data. In the 3 affected sibs they identified compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: C326R and C774R. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans and segregated with the disorder in all 6 family members. Both mutations were absent from dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, and NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project data, and from more than 645 genotyped controls. Each substitution affected a cysteine of an atypical EGF-like domain that is conserved in 43 species. Boyden et al. (2012) then screened the MEGF10 open reading frame in 190 additional patients with unexplained myopathies. Subject 105-1 carried a heterozygous c.211C-T mutation predicting an arg71-to-trp (R71W) substitution in the emilin domain, inherited from an unaffected parent. No second mutation was found, including in RT-PCR products from muscle RNA. Boyden et al. (2012) suggested that this patient may carry a second MEGF10 mutation they were unable to detect. ⚠︎

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a splice site mutation, c.2981-2A-G, and a novel missense mutation, cys810-to-tyr (C810Y).

Using exome sequencing in a 66-year-old man with adult-onset disease, Harris et al. (2018) identified 2 novel mutations in the MEGF10 gene: an essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a missense mutation (c.352T-C; cys118-to-arg, C118R). ⚠︎

In an Italian man born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) identified a homozygous c.2096G-C mutation in the MEGF10 gene (NM_001256545.2) by exome sequencing. The mutation predicts a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed it, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD and affected a conserved residue in an EGF-like domain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that the C774R mutation found in their family 1030 had also been reported, in compound heterozygosity with a frameshift mutation, in a family with a more severe phenotype. They proposed that the milder disease in their patients was consistent with residual MEGF10 function from missense mutations, compared with total loss of MEGF10 expression in families with null mutations. They also noted that minicores were prominent in their patients but had not been reported in patients with null mutations.

Takayama et al. (2016) found a homozygous frameshift mutation in a Japanese patient with the severe EMARDD phenotype. A second Japanese patient with the milder phenotype carried a splice site mutation and a cysteine missense mutation. The authors concluded that these cases indicated a genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGF10 myopathy.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed 20 reported patients with MEGF10 mutations and confirmed a genotype-phenotype correlation. In patients with at least 1 null mutation, respiratory dysfunction began in the first year of life. In patients with 2 missense mutations, it began at 10 years of age or much later.

Croci et al. (2022) observed that their patient had a homozygous missense mutation but early-onset, progressive disease. Minicores appeared only on a later biopsy. The authors proposed that the minicores that mark the late-onset group may result from long-standing myofibrillar pathology that begins in infancy, and they suggested a phenotypic continuum rather than a dichotomy between the severe and mild forms.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish with 4 different splice-blocking morpholinos. The morphants had unhatched eggs, curled or bent tails, impaired swimming, and reduced motility in response to touch. They also showed decreased birefringence, disrupted somite boundaries, gaps between myofibers, and myofibrillar disorganization on electron microscopy, consistent with a myopathic process.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS326ARG [c.976T>C; p.C326R]

In 3 sibs from a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family with a congenital myopathy with minicores (CMYO10B), Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene. One allele was a c.976T-C transition in exon 10, resulting in a cys326-to-arg (C326R) substitution in EGF-like domain 6. The other was C774R. The mutations were found by whole-genome sequencing, confirmed in trans by Sanger sequencing, and segregated with the disorder. C326R was absent from 715 controls and public databases. The affected cysteine is conserved in 43 species and was predicted to form a disulfide bond.

**.0002 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS774ARG [c.2320T>C; p.C774R]

In 3 sibs with CMYO10B, Boyden et al. (2012) identified a c.2320T-C transition in exon 19 of the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys774-to-arg (C774R) substitution in EGF-like domain 16. The mutation was in compound heterozygosity with C326R. It was absent from 645 controls and public databases, and the affected cysteine is conserved in 43 species. Boyden et al. (2012) noted that the same mutation had been reported in compound heterozygosity with a frameshift mutation in a family with a more severe phenotype.

**.0003 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS810TYR [p.Cys810Tyr]

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype of MEGF10 myopathy (CMYO10B), Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a novel cys810-to-tyr (C810Y) missense mutation and a splice site mutation (c.2981-2A-G).

**.0004 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, 2981-2A-G [c.2981-2A>G]

In a Japanese patient with CMYO10B, Takayama et al. (2016) found a c.2981-2A-G splice site mutation in the MEGF10 gene, in compound heterozygosity with C810Y.

**.0005 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS118ARG [c.352T>C; p.Cys118Arg]

In a 66-year-old man with adult-onset MEGF10 myopathy (CMYO10B) and prominent respiratory failure, Harris et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify a novel c.352T-C transition in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys118-to-arg (C118R) substitution. He also carried a novel essential splice site mutation, c.1426+1G-T.

**.0006 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, 1426+1G-T [c.1426+1G>T]

In a 66-year-old man with CMYO10B, Harris et al. (2018) identified a novel essential splice site mutation in the MEGF10 gene, c.1426+1G-T, together with the missense mutation C118R.

**.0007 CONGENITAL MYOPATHY 10B, MILD VARIANT**  
MEGF10, CYS699SER [NM_001256545.2:c.2096G>C; p.Cys699Ser]

In a 22-year-old Italian man with a progressive form of mild variant EMARDD (CMYO10B), born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) identified a homozygous c.2096G-C transversion in the MEGF10 gene (NM_001256545.2), resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and both parents were heterozygous. It was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD and affected a conserved cysteine in an EGF-like domain.
