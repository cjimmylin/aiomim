### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that severe achondroplasia with developmental delay and acanthosis nigricans (SADDAN) is caused by mutation in the FGFR3 gene.

### Description

Severe achondroplasia with developmental delay and acanthosis nigricans (SADDAN) is a skeletal dysplasia. Its disturbance of endochondral bone growth approaches and overlaps that seen in thanatophoric dysplasia type I (TD1). It differs from TD1 in that most affected individuals survive beyond infancy, often into adulthood. Surviving patients develop progressive acanthosis nigricans, severe developmental delay, seizures, and structural anomalies of the central nervous system (Tavormina et al., 1999; Bellus et al., 1999).

### Nomenclature

Tavormina et al. (1999) named the phenotype caused by the lys650-to-met mutation in FGFR3 'severe achondroplasia with developmental delay and acanthosis nigricans' (SADDAN) because it differed significantly from the phenotypes caused by other known FGFR3 mutations.

### Clinical Features

Tavormina et al. (1999) reported 4 unrelated individuals with a skeletal dysplasia whose severity approached that of thanatophoric dysplasia type I. Three of the 4 developed extensive areas of acanthosis nigricans starting in early childhood and had severe neurologic impairment. These patients survived past infancy without prolonged life-support measures. ⚠︎

Bellus et al. (1999) gave a more detailed clinical description of the SADDAN phenotype. The disturbance of endochondral bone growth approached and overlapped that of thanatophoric dysplasia type I, but most patients survived into adulthood. Some patients had other unusual bone deformities, including femoral bowing with reverse (posterior apex) bowing of the tibia and fibula, and 'ram's horn' bowing of the clavicle. Surviving patients had progressive acanthosis nigricans, structural anomalies of the central nervous system, seizures, and severe developmental delay. Unlike thanatophoric dysplasia type II, which is caused by a mutation in the same FGFR3 codon, SADDAN was not associated with cloverleaf skull or craniosynostosis. ⚠︎

Zankl et al. (2008) described the prenatal and postnatal features of a patient with SADDAN. They noted that this was one of only 6 cases with molecular analysis reported in the literature. The case confirmed the severe morbidity of SADDAN and added to reports of early mortality associated with the disorder. Zankl et al. (2008) also reviewed the clinical and radiologic characteristics of all reported SADDAN cases.

### Molecular Genetics

In 4 unrelated individuals with SADDAN, Tavormina et al. (1999) identified a missense mutation in the FGFR3 gene, an A1949T change that results in a lys650-to-met (K650M) substitution. The mutation lies at the nucleotide next to the thanatophoric dysplasia type II mutation (A1948G; lys650-to-glu). It changes a highly conserved codon in the activation loop of the kinase domain. In transient transfection studies, the K650M mutation caused a dramatic increase in constitutive receptor kinase activity, about 3 times greater than that seen with the K650E mutation.

In a patient with SADDAN, Zankl et al. (2008) confirmed the clinical diagnosis by detecting the FGFR3 K650M mutation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bellus et al. (1999) noted that the K650M mutation lies in the same FGFR3 codon as the thanatophoric dysplasia type II mutation (K650E) and has a similar effect on constitutive activation of the FGFR3 tyrosine kinase. However, K650M is not associated with cloverleaf skull or craniosynostosis and is most often compatible with survival into adulthood. Iwata et al. (2001) pointed out that FGFR3 carrying the SADDAN mutation is more highly activated in vitro than FGFR3 carrying the TDII mutation. Even so, TDII is lethal at birth, whereas most SADDAN patients survive the perinatal period.

### Animal Model

Iwata et al. (2001) introduced the K644M point mutation into the mouse Fgfr3 gene; this corresponds to the human SADDAN mutation K650M. Heterozygous mutant mice had a phenotype similar to human SADDAN, and most survived the perinatal period. The authors suggested that the survival of SADDAN patients is indeed due to the K650M mutation. The long bone abnormalities in the SADDAN mice were milder than those in the TDII mouse model. The mice also showed overgrowth of cartilaginous tissue in the rib cartilage, trachea, and nasal septum. At low concentration, FGF ligand activated MAP kinase differently in primary chondrocyte cultures from wildtype and SADDAN mice.
