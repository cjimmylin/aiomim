### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis, also called intermediate cystinosis, is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Late-onset cystinosis is a rarer form of cystinosis than infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age (Attard et al., 1999). Thoene et al. (1999) referred to this form as intermediate cystinosis. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of the disorder.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis and reported an unusual clinical variant.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis.

### Inheritance

Langman et al. (1985) reported late-onset cystinosis in a sibship. Thoene et al. (1999) found that intermediate cystinosis resulted from either homozygous or compound heterozygous CTNS mutations, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) studied 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis and found mutations in the CTNS gene. Two of the mutations had not been described before. Some patients were compound heterozygous for a mild mutation and a mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis. Others were homozygous for a predicted mild mutation. Transfecting cystinotic fibroblasts with normal CTNS cDNA partially corrected their phenotype. Partial correction was also seen with a cDNA carrying a mutation that causes intermediate cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) screened the CTNS gene in patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, in patients with late-onset cystinosis, and in patients whose phenotype did not fit the classical definitions. In 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis, the mutations affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin. The authors concluded that this explained the milder phenotype.

Macías-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 patients with cystinosis from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) found 23 different CTNS mutations, 14 of which were novel. Of 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, 12 had 2 severely truncating mutations, consistent with a loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions, which were predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains and abolish protein function. In contrast, the mutations in 2 late-onset patients affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin. Three further patients had onset of cystinosis before 7 years of age but a milder course than the infantile nephropathic form. The authors suggested that the missense mutations in these patients allow some functional protein to be produced.
