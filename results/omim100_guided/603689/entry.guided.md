### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding titin (TTN) on chromosome 2q.

### Description

Myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is an autosomal dominant myopathy with onset in adulthood. Patients have proximal and/or distal limb weakness, and respiratory muscle weakness develops early, often while they can still walk. Neck flexor and ankle dorsiflexor weakness are common, and muscle biopsy shows myofibrillar lesions, including eosinophilic inclusions, cytoplasmic bodies, and rimmed vacuoles (Edström et al., 1990; Pfeffer et al., 2012; Pfeffer et al., 2014). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Pfeffer et al. (2014) suggested that hereditary myopathy with early respiratory failure (HMERF) caused by TTN mutations should be classified as myofibrillar myopathy (MFM)-titinopathy, because patients carrying the p.C30071R mutation met clinical and pathologic criteria for MFM.

### Clinical Features

Edström et al. (1990) described 16 patients from 7 pedigrees with an unusual limb-girdle myopathy of adult onset. All patients had respiratory muscle weakness early in the disease, and in many of them acute respiratory incapacity prompted the first neurologic examination. Other early symptoms were neck flexor weakness and sometimes foot extensor weakness. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed a myopathy, and serum muscle enzymes were normal or slightly elevated. On light microscopy, muscle biopsies showed myofibrillar changes, including plaques that stained strongly with rhodamine-conjugated phalloidin, a marker for F-actin. On electron microscopy, these plaques consisted of moderately dense thin filaments that were associated with Z-disc splitting or extended from Z-discs. The authors suggested defective myofibrillogenesis, possibly involving defective actin polymerization.

Nicolao et al. (1999) studied 2 Swedish families with autosomal dominant myopathy, proximal weakness, early respiratory failure, and characteristic cytoplasmic bodies on muscle biopsy. Xiang et al. (1999) studied a French family with a similar clinical and pathologic phenotype.

Chinnery et al. (2001) described a 3-generation family with an autosomal dominant myopathy that presented in mid-adult life with weakness of the tibialis anterior. They assessed 24 individuals in detail, 11 of whom were affected, and examined muscle pathology in 7, including 1 at autopsy. Patients in the second affected generation had earlier onset and faster progression than those in the first. From the time of presentation, lung function tests showed progressive global respiratory muscle weakness, with the diaphragm preferentially involved in some patients. Hip girdle and shoulder girdle weakness developed later. Pathologic changes matched clinical severity. Clinically unaffected muscles showed minimal change. Moderately affected muscles showed fiber splitting, eosinophilic inclusions, and vacuoles with basophilic rims. In severely affected muscles, fat and fibrous connective tissue had replaced the muscle fibers. The inclusions were Congophilic and reacted with antibodies to desmin, beta-amyloid, and phosphorylated tau.

Birchall et al. (2005) performed muscle MRI in 7 patients (age range 37 to 66 years; 4 male). Early, selective involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus muscles was seen on imaging but could not be detected clinically, and closely related muscles progressed at different rates. The authors considered these findings specific to the disorder and useful for early noninvasive diagnosis in at-risk individuals.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) studied 31 mutation carriers, some followed for up to 31 years. They came from the family originally reported by Chinnery et al. (2001), a second family, and a third familial case. Presentation was variable, with predominantly distal, proximal, or respiratory muscle weakness. Age at onset ranged from early adulthood to advanced age, and some older patients had a mild phenotype. In nearly all patients, weakness began earlier and was more severe in the lower limbs. Seven patients also had axial muscle weakness. Respiratory function declined gradually over time. No patient had cardiomyopathy. Muscle MRI in 21 affected patients showed semitendinosus involvement in 20, including 3 presymptomatic patients. Muscle histopathology most often showed eosinophilic inclusions and rimmed vacuoles, but in a minority of patients it was nonspecific.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) reported 5 British families with the p.C30071R mutation. Onset was usually in the fourth or fifth decade, with tripping and falling, myalgia, and walking difficulty. CK ranged from normal to 1,195 IU/L. The disease progressed slowly and involved proximal, distal, and axial muscles. All patients except one remained ambulant; that patient lost ambulation after about 25 years of disease. Pulmonary function tests were abnormal in 10 of 11 patients, and 4 patients needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation about 20 to 30 years after onset. Findings not previously described in this disorder included scapular winging, often asymmetric, contractures of the elbow and/or Achilles tendon, variable spinal rigidity, and muscle hypertrophy. One patient had mild left ventricular impairment and another had supraventricular tachycardia. MRI showed early involvement of the semitendinosus and peroneus longus muscles. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic or dystrophic changes. Some biopsies also showed rimmed vacuoles, eosinophilic inclusions, and dark blue inclusions on Gomori trichrome staining. Six of 7 biopsies showed cytoplasmic accumulation of desmin, myotilin, p62, and ubiquitin, and in 3 biopsies the aggregates had a 'cheetah skin' appearance, particularly with myotilin staining.

### Inheritance

Edström et al. (1990) found evidence of autosomal dominant inheritance in most of their cases. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also shown in the families reported by Nicolao et al. (1999) and Chinnery et al. (2001).

### Mapping

Nicolao et al. (1999) performed a genome-wide linkage screen in 2 Swedish families and found linkage to chromosome 2q24-q31. The maximum combined 2-point lod score was 4.87 at theta = 0 for marker D2S1245. Haplotype analysis placed the gene within a 17-cM region between markers D2S2384 and D2S364. Affected members of both families shared the same haplotype, suggesting a common origin.

In a French family with a similar phenotype, Xiang et al. (1999) excluded the 2q24-q31 region. A genome-wide scan gave a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.11, the highest possible in this family, for markers between D2S1272 and D2S1260, a region spanning 4 cM. This suggested a locus on chromosome 2q21.

Chinnery et al. (2001) excluded linkage to all known distal myopathy loci in their family.

### Heterogeneity

Xiang et al. (1999) excluded the 2q24-q31 locus, previously mapped in Swedish families by Nicolao et al. (1999), in a French family with a similar phenotype and pathology. Instead, they found suggestive linkage to chromosome 2q21.

### Molecular Genetics

Lange et al. (2005) reported that a human mutation in the titin kinase domain causes hereditary muscle disease by disrupting a signaling pathway. In this pathway, the titin kinase domain interacts with nbr1, which targets p62/SQSTM1 to sarcomeres, and p62 in turn interacts with MuRF2. MuRF2 is a muscle-specific E3 ligase that regulates the serum response transcription factor (SRF).

In the family originally reported by Chinnery et al. (2001), Pfeffer et al. (2012) used genome-wide linkage analysis and whole-exome sequencing to identify a likely causal variant in the TTN gene, g.274375T-C (p.Cys30071Arg; C30071R), within a shared 2.93-Mbp haplotype on chromosome 2. The variant segregated with the phenotype in 21 members of the original family, in 9 members of a second family with the same selective pattern of muscle involvement on MRI, and in a third familial case.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) used Sanger sequencing to screen 127 undiagnosed patients with features compatible with MFM. Five unrelated probands (3.9%) carried the heterozygous p.C30071R mutation in the 119th fibronectin-3 (FN3) domain of TTN, as did 4 of the 72 patients (5.5%) with a clinical diagnosis of MFM. In the 3 families in which segregation could be tested, the mutation segregated with the disease. Haplotype analysis of these 5 families and 3 previously reported families showed a shared core region of 171 kbp, suggesting a British founder effect. No patient had a mutation at the p.R32450 kinase domain position. Two further patients had novel heterozygous variants in the same FN3 domain, p.P30091L and p.N30145K. Both variants were at highly conserved residues and were absent from 400 control chromosomes, but their pathogenicity was uncertain. The p.P30091L variant was also found in the patient's healthy 61-year-old brother, and segregation of p.N30145K could not be studied.

### Diagnosis

Pfeffer et al. (2012) suggested that this disorder be considered in patients with adult-onset proximal or distal myopathy and early respiratory failure, even when muscle pathology is nonspecific. They recommended muscle MRI as an initial investigation and, if the MRI is suggestive, screening for TTN mutations. Pfeffer et al. (2014) recommended sequencing the 119th FN3 domain of TTN as part of the diagnostic workup for MFM, particularly when there is early respiratory involvement and supportive muscle MRI.

### Population Genetics

Pfeffer et al. (2014) found the p.C30071R mutation in 3.9% of 127 undiagnosed UK patients with features compatible with MFM, and in 5.5% of those with a clinical diagnosis of MFM. Because the affected families shared a 171-kbp haplotype, the authors attributed this frequency to a likely British founder effect.
