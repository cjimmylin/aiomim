### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the Na+/glucose cotransporter gene SLC5A1 (SGLT1) on chromosome 22q13.1.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disorder that begins in the first weeks of life. Affected infants cannot absorb dietary glucose and galactose from the intestine, which causes severe diarrhea and dehydration. The disorder is usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991; Martin et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

Anderson et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption. She also had a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption. Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported remission of glucose intolerance in familial glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 children with glucose-galactose malabsorption from 8 different families. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. The mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. The children presented with chronic watery diarrhea present since birth and with failure to thrive. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All presented with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. Initial presentation also included increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite their illness, and irritability and apathy. When the disease was not recognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) studied glucose transport in jejunal mucosa in vitro in a 3-year-old girl with GGM, her family, and normal volunteers. Mucosa from 6 normal controls and from the proband's asymptomatic 5-year-old brother accumulated glucose to 16 times the concentration in the incubation medium. The proband's mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. Both parents and a half sister had impaired glucose transport, with values between those of normal subjects and the proband. In these heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired, but glucose efflux from the mucosa was identical to that in 3 normal controls. Kinetic analysis showed a reduced capacity (Vmax) and a normal affinity (Km) for glucose transport. All subjects accumulated fructose similarly. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption in the proband. Her brother's titration curve was similar to those of 7 normal volunteers.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait and that heterozygotes can be detected by their reduced capacity for intestinal glucose transport.

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1991) noted that the SGLT1 gene lies on the distal long arm of chromosome 22. Using fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe, Turk et al. (1993) refined the localization of SGLT1 to chromosome 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family with GGM. They identified a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the phenotype. Xenopus oocytes injected with the mutant cRNA showed complete loss of Na(+)-dependent glucose transport. Turk et al. (1993) stated that the mutation in the 2 patients was an asp28-to-asn (D28N) substitution, which eliminated SGLT1 transport activity in vitro.

Martin et al. (1996) noted that they had previously found a missense mutation in SGLT1 in 2 sisters with GGM. They screened the gene in 30 new patients with GGM and used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype. They reported that defects in both SGLT1 trafficking and SGLT1 function cause GGM.

Xin and Wang (2011) sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons and exon-intron boundaries of SLC5A1 in 33 Old Order Amish patients with GGM. They found 4 homozygous missense mutations that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype in all affected individuals: N51S, A411T, R558H, and H615Q.

### Diagnosis

Martin et al. (1996) performed prenatal screening for GGM in 2 pregnancies in a large consanguineous family whose 2 affected sibs carried the D28N mutation in SGLT1. They PCR-amplified the first exon of SGLT1 from genomic DNA and tested for the D28N mutation by EcoRV restriction digestion. The proband's sib was heterozygous, and a cousin was not a carrier. At 2 years of age, both children were healthy and had had no diarrhea.

### Clinical Management

All 8 Arab children reported by Abdullah et al. (1996) responded clinically to a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

In the Amish patients reported by Xin and Wang (2011), correct diagnosis and adequate management produced a dramatic turnaround: the diarrhea stopped immediately, the patients rehydrated quickly, and growth and development were normal afterward.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested that the SLC5A1 mutations in 33 Old Order Amish patients with GGM reflect a founder effect. They recommended population-specific genetic testing to allow early diagnosis.
