### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A (JEB5A) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding integrin beta-4 (ITGB4). ⚠︎

### Description

Intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A (JEB5A) is a milder, nonlethal form of the blistering disorder caused by mutation in the ITGB4 gene. Mucocutaneous fragility is relatively mild, and patients have survived into childhood and adulthood. Some patients have had pyloric atresia (Mellerio et al., 1998) and others have not (Inoue et al., 2000; Jonkman et al., 2002). Other reported features have included nail dystrophy and enamel hypoplasia (Jonkman et al., 2002). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Has et al. (2020) published a consensus reclassification of inherited epidermolysis bullosa and other disorders with skin fragility. It reviewed clinical and genetic aspects, genotype-phenotype correlations, disease-modifying factors, and the natural history of EB.

### Clinical Features

Mellerio et al. (1998) reported 2 unrelated patients with junctional epidermolysis bullosa and pyloric atresia who survived into early childhood. Both had mild cutaneous involvement, whereas the disorder is usually fatal within the first weeks or months of life even after surgical correction of the intestinal obstruction.

Inoue et al. (2000) reported non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia in a patient with a homozygous ITGB4 mutation.

Jonkman et al. (2002) reported a 49-year-old woman whose disease mainly resembled epidermolysis bullosa simplex. She had mild blistering of the hands and feet from birth, nail dystrophy with onychogryposis, and enamel hypoplasia. She had no alopecia and no history of pyloric atresia. On electron microscopy and antigen mapping of a skin blister, the separation was intraepidermal, low in the basal keratinocytes, through the attachment plaque of the hemidesmosome. On immunofluorescence microscopy, monoclonal antibody 450-11A, which recognizes the third fibronectin type III repeat in the intracellular domain of integrin beta-4, did not bind. Antibodies against amino-terminal and carboxy-terminal epitopes bound at reduced levels.

### Inheritance

JEB5A is transmitted as an autosomal recessive trait. Patients have been reported with homozygous ITGB4 mutations (Inoue et al., 2000) and with compound heterozygous ITGB4 mutations (Mellerio et al., 1998).

### Molecular Genetics

Mellerio et al. (1998) found compound heterozygous ITGB4 mutations in 2 unrelated patients with mild junctional epidermolysis bullosa and pyloric atresia. The first patient carried a splice site mutation in exon 30 (3793+1G-A) and a nonsense mutation in exon 36 (W1478X). The second carried a missense mutation in exon 3 (C38R) and a 1-bp deletion in exon 36 (4776delG). The nonsense and deletion mutations were predicted to cause markedly reduced beta-4 integrin mRNA levels. The authors suggested that the missense or splice site mutation on the second allele might allow some functional, albeit perturbed, beta-4 polypeptide to be made.

In a patient with non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia, Inoue et al. (2000) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the cytoplasmic tail of beta-4 integrin (G931D). The mutation disrupted hemidesmosome assembly.

In a 49-year-old woman with mild epidermolysis bullosa that mainly resembled the simplex type, Jonkman et al. (2002) identified a heterozygous 2-bp deletion in the ITGB4 gene (4733delCT). The deletion caused in-frame skipping of exon 36 and was predicted to remove 50 amino acids (residues 1450-1499) within the third fibronectin type III repeat of the cytoplasmic domain. Immunoblot analysis showed a beta-4 polypeptide that was 5 kDa shorter. No full-size protein was detected in vitro, and the 450-11A epitope was absent in vivo. On this basis, the authors expected the patient to be heterozygous for a null allele on the other chromosome as well.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Pulkkinen et al. (1998) studied 3 patients with the lethal form of epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia and identified ITGB4 mutations on 5 alleles. Three of the 4 distinct mutations predicted truncated polypeptides. In contrast, each of the mildly affected surviving patients reported by Mellerio et al. (1998) carried a missense or splice site mutation on 1 allele, which the authors suggested might allow some functional beta-4 polypeptide to be made. Jonkman et al. (2002) concluded that an in-frame deletion of the third fibronectin type III repeat in the cytoplasmic domain of integrin beta-4, which is thought to interact with BP180/type XVII collagen, is pathogenic and causes a mild phenotype that mainly resembles epidermolysis bullosa simplex.
