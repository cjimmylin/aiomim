### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is caused by mutation in one of several mitochondrial genes encoding subunits of complex I: MTND6, MTND4, MTND1, and MTND3. ⚠︎

### Description

Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder. Affected members of a family show varying combinations of Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) and progressive generalized dystonia, which is associated with bilateral basal ganglia lesions (Novotny et al., 1986; Jun et al., 1994; Wang et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Miyoshi et al. (1969) reported a disorder they called familial holotopistic striatal necrosis.

Marsden et al. (1986) described 7 members of 2 families with dystonia, which in some patients was accompanied by subacute visual loss or asymptomatic optic atrophy. All had striking bilateral symmetric lucencies on CT scan, mainly in the putamen. The authors noted that the disorder might be a variant of Leigh disease but that it differed considerably from pathologically proven Leigh disease. ⚠︎

Novotny et al. (1986) studied a kindred in which 8 members had the neuroretinopathy of Leber disease, 14 had progressive generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration, and 1 had both disorders.

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family in which members had Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, early-onset dystonia with bilateral basal ganglia lesions, or both.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia. Some family members had both the neurologic and the ophthalmologic lesions, and others had only one of them. A muscle biopsy showed severe complex I deficiency.

Watanabe et al. (2006) described a Japanese family in which generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration began in childhood and optic neuropathy began later.

Spruijt et al. (2007) reported a family in which some members had LHON and others had spastic dystonia. The clinical picture resembled that of previously described families with LHON and dystonia caused by MTND6 mutations. ⚠︎

Wang et al. (2009) described a Chinese family with 18 LDYT patients.

### Inheritance

Novotny et al. (1986) found that the pattern of inheritance in their kindred was compatible with maternal transmission and suggested that the disorder might be mitochondrial. Jun et al. (1994) confirmed maternal transmission in a Hispanic family carrying a mitochondrial DNA mutation.

### Molecular Genetics

Jun et al. (1994) amplified by PCR and sequenced more than 90% of the buffy coat mtDNA from a severely affected child in a Hispanic family with LDYT. The mtDNA belonged to Native American haplogroup D and differed from the Cambridge sequence at 40 positions. One of these differences was a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 14459 in the MTND6 gene, which caused an ala72-to-val substitution at a moderately conserved residue. The variant was not found in 38 Native American haplogroup D mtDNAs, 108 Asian, 103 Caucasian, or 99 African mtDNAs. Six maternal relatives across 3 generations carried the mutation. One of them, a female with LHON, was heteroplasmic.

In a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia, De Vries et al. (1996) found none of the mtDNA mutations previously reported in LHON. Sequencing of the mitochondrial protein-coding genes identified 2 previously unreported mutations. The first was a heteroplasmic A-to-G transition at nucleotide 11696 in the MTND4 gene, causing a val312-to-ile substitution. The second was a homoplasmic T-to-A change at nucleotide 14596 in the MTND6 gene, causing an ile26-to-met substitution. A muscle biopsy showed severe complex I deficiency, which the authors proposed as the link between these mutations and the phenotype.

Watanabe et al. (2006) sequenced the entire mtDNA of the proband from a Japanese family with LDYT and found 42 nucleotide changes compared with the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence. Two of these, the G14459A mutation in MTND6 and an A3203G change, were absent from 200 healthy Japanese individuals. The significance of the A3203G change was unknown. ⚠︎

Spruijt et al. (2007) screened the complete mtDNA of a patient with LHON who lacked the 3 primary LHON mutations. They identified a 3697G-A mutation in the MTND1 gene, which appeared to be associated with isolated complex I deficiency. The authors concluded that this mutation caused both LHON and spastic dystonia in the same family. They noted that the mutation can also cause MELAS syndrome and suggested that other genetic factors may contribute to its clinical expression.

In a Chinese family with 18 LDYT patients, Wang et al. (2009) sequenced the entire mitochondrial genome using a resequencing microarray. They identified a 10197G-A mutation in the MTND3 gene, on a haplogroup D4b background, that substituted threonine for a highly conserved alanine at codon 47. Quantitative analysis showed the mutation to be homoplasmic in leukocytes of all affected individuals on the maternal side. The authors noted that this mutation had recently been reported in French and Korean patients with Leigh syndrome.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Spruijt et al. (2007) and Wang et al. (2009) noted that the mutations they identified in LDYT families, MTND1 3697G-A and MTND3 10197G-A respectively, had also been associated with other phenotypes: MELAS syndrome for 3697G-A and Leigh syndrome for 10197G-A.

### Diagnosis

Marsden et al. (1986) stated that this condition must be excluded in all patients thought to have idiopathic dystonia, subacute visual failure resembling Leber optic neuropathy, or a combination of the two.
