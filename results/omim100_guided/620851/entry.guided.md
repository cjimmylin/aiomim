### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene on chromosome 12. Most of these mutations occur de novo.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder marked by moderate to severe global developmental delay, intellectual disability, and absent or very limited speech. Other common features are hypotonia, microcephaly, short stature, seizures, and brain MRI abnormalities. Many patients also have distinctive facial features and problems in several organ systems, including the eyes, gastrointestinal tract, and skeleton. The disorder is caused by heterozygous, mostly de novo, mutations in a short critical region of RNU4-2, the gene encoding the U4 small nuclear RNA of the major spliceosome (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) reviewed Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms for 46 cases from the 100,000 Genomes Project (100KGP) who carried RNU4-2 variants and had a neurodevelopmental abnormality. Of these, 91% had intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay. Several terms were significantly more frequent than in other cases with a neurodevelopmental abnormality: microcephaly (57%), drooling (13%), proportionate short stature (28%), generalized hypotonia (39%), seizures (52%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion (13%).

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. They followed her for 5 years and documented facial features that they considered recognizable. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed clinical information on 49 individuals (21 females and 28 males; median age at last evaluation, 10 years; range, 2 to 38 years) with RNU4-2 variants. All had moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability. Of those older than 5 years, 83% could walk, although walking was delayed (average age 3.4 years; range, 17 months to 7.5 years). Some had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Most patients were nonverbal, some had a few words, and only 3 had fluent speech. Short stature was present in 76% and microcephaly in 77%; the microcephaly was mostly congenital. Other features included hypotonia (87%), seizures (77%), behavioral problems (67%), autism spectrum disorder (48%), sleep problems (47%), and intrauterine growth restriction (18%). Seizure onset varied, with a median of 3 years and a range from the first year of life to 10 years. Seizure types included infantile spasms, focal seizures, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. Brain MRI was abnormal in 91%. The most frequent findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, and delayed myelination.

In the same cohort, Chen et al. (2024) found dysmorphic facial features in 94% of patients. These included a myopathic face, deep-set eyes (widely or narrowly spaced), epicanthus, a wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, and large cupped ears, some posteriorly rotated. Other facial features were full cheeks, full lips with downturned corners, a high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. Visual problems were reported in 79%, including strabismus (51%), nystagmus (45%), optic nerve hypoplasia (22%), and cortical blindness. Hearing loss occurred in 22%. Gastrointestinal problems included feeding difficulties (76%), constipation (66%), and gastroesophageal reflux (49%); 32% needed a gastrostomy tube. Skeletal findings (64%) included osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, and hip dysplasia. Endocrine problems (44%) included hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, and pituitary abnormalities. Limb, genitourinary, dental, cardiac, and skin problems were also reported. Presentation did not differ significantly between males and females.

### Inheritance

Heterozygous RNU4-2 mutations in ReNU syndrome usually occur de novo (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Greene et al. (2024) described 1 case in which the patient inherited the n.76C-T variant from an affected mother. They also found that the mother of a patient with n.65A-G was mosaic for the variant. Chen et al. (2024) determined the parent of origin in 54 individuals and found the variant on the maternal allele in all of them.

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) performed a rare-variant association analysis of 41,132 noncoding genes using whole-genome sequencing data from the 100KGP. They compared 5,529 unrelated cases with intellectual disability against 46,401 unrelated controls. RNU4-2, which encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA (snRNA), showed the strongest association by far (posterior probability of association approximately 1). Variants were found in 47 cases and fell in 2 regions, nucleotides n.62-70 and n.73-79, where no unaffected participants carried rare variants. These regions map to the U4-U6 quasi-pseudoknot and to stem III. Of the 47 variants, 37 were de novo and 10 had unknown inheritance. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, arose de novo in 33 families. In replication cohorts (the NIHR BioResource, the 100KGP Pilot, and the Genomic Medicine Service), the authors found 26 more affected probands. In 19 of them the variant was de novo. In the 100KGP, RNU4-2 accounted for 0.50% of cases with a neurodevelopmental abnormality, more than any other gene. In the Genomic Medicine Service it accounted for 0.38%, second only to MECP2. The authors suggested that disruption of the U4-U6 interaction might delay U4-U6 unwinding.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 164 trios with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. They found the recurrent de novo n.64_65insT variant in RNU4-2 in a girl with ReNU syndrome. Extensive earlier genetic testing in this patient had not found another cause. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) found heterozygous variants in an 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 (nucleotides 62-79) in 115 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders, drawn from Genomics England and other cohorts. This region is strongly depleted of variation in population datasets. Of the 115 individuals, 89 (77.4%) had the recurrent n.64_65insT variant. Sequencing data from both parents in 86 individuals confirmed de novo occurrence. Single-base insertions in the region were strongly enriched in patients: 54 of 8,841 undiagnosed probands with neurodevelopmental disorders carried one, compared with 2 of 490,132 UK Biobank individuals. SNVs were only modestly enriched. Chen et al. (2024) performed RNA sequencing of blood from 5 patients. Compared with controls, patients used unannotated 5-prime splice sites more often, and these sites deviated from consensus at the +3, +4, and +5 positions, which pair with the U6 ACAGAGA sequence. RNU4-2 was expressed in the developing brain at much higher levels than RNU4-1. The authors estimated that variants in the critical region explain about 0.4% of individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) found the n.76C-T variant in gnomAD (1 allele in 152,108) and identified a family in which it was inherited from an affected mother. They also found n.76C-G in a person without a neurodevelopmental abnormality. They suggested that some changes at position 76 may cause a milder phenotype than n.64_65insT or may have little effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that children with SNVs were less often reported to have severe global developmental delay than children with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV. The authors noted that this suggestion of a milder phenotype with SNVs rested on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 is not captured by exome sequencing. In the DDD cohort, no more than 5 off-target reads covered the n.64_65insT position in any individual, so the sensitivity of exome data for detecting the variant was very low. Schot et al. (2024) found the variant by reanalyzing a diagnostic genome that had been reported as negative.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 variants in 0.50% of 100KGP cases with a neurodevelopmental abnormality and in 0.38% of such cases in the UK Genomic Medicine Service. Chen et al. (2024) estimated that variants in the 18-bp critical region explain about 0.4% of all individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. In cohorts recruited mainly for autism spectrum disorder, the rate was much lower (3 of 7,149; 0.042%).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 64T [n.64_65insT; NR_003137.2:n.64_65insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291839:T:TA]

Greene et al. (2024) found a de novo heterozygous 1-bp insertion (n.64_65insT) in the RNU4-2 gene in patients with ReNU syndrome (RENU) from 33 families in the 100,000 Genomes Project. The insertion lies within the quasi-pseudoknot that U4 forms with U6 snRNA. The authors proposed that it could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports this structure.

Schot et al. (2024) identified the de novo n.64_65insT variant (NR_003137.2) in a 5-year-old girl with RENU who had severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. They found it by reanalyzing trio whole-genome sequencing data. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) found the heterozygous n.64_65insT variant in 89 of 115 individuals (77.4%) with neurodevelopmental disorders and variants in the RNU4-2 critical region. In Genomics England it had arisen de novo in 38 probands with unaffected parents. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in 8 individuals. It was absent from 76,215 genomes in gnomAD v4.0 and from 245,400 individuals in the All of Us dataset, and it was seen once in the UK Biobank. In all 46 patients in whom parental origin could be determined, the variant was on the maternal allele. RNA sequencing of blood from patients with this and other RNU4-2 variants showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.

**.0002 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, n.65A-G [n.65A>G]

Greene et al. (2024) found a heterozygous n.65A-G variant in the RNU4-2 gene in a boy with RENU from the 100,000 Genomes Project. His mother had a homozygous reference genotype call, but inspection of the sequencing reads showed she was probably mosaic for the variant. Chen et al. (2024) also reported an individual whose n.65A-G variant appeared mosaic in the mother (54 reference and 8 alternate reads).

**.0003 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, n.76C-T [n.76C>T]

Greene et al. (2024) found a heterozygous n.76C-T variant in the RNU4-2 gene in patients with RENU. The variant was de novo in 4 families. In a fifth family the proband inherited it from an affected mother. The variant lies in stem III of the U4-U6 duplex and was present in gnomAD (1 allele in 152,108). The authors suggested that it may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases. Chen et al. (2024) also reported that this SNV was maternally inherited in 1 individual.

**.0004 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 77T [n.77_78insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291826:T:TA]

Greene et al. (2024) listed the n.77_78insT variant in the RNU4-2 gene among the variants found in patients with RENU. They predicted that it disrupts Watson-Crick base pairing within stem III of the U4-U6 duplex.

Chen et al. (2024) found the recurrent heterozygous n.77_78insT variant in 6 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders, 2 of whom were affected sibs. RNA sequencing of blood from 1 carrier, analyzed together with other patients, showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.
