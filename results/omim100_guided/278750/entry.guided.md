### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the variant type of xeroderma pigmentosum (XPV) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding DNA polymerase eta (POLH; also called hRAD30). ⚠︎

### Description

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant (XPV) is an inherited disorder with clinical photosensitivity and a higher rate of sunlight-induced skin cancer. Unlike cells from the excision-repair-deficient forms of xeroderma pigmentosum (groups XP-A to XP-G), XPV cells carry out normal nucleotide excision repair. However, they cannot replicate ultraviolet (UV)-damaged DNA normally (Masutani et al., 1999; Itoh et al., 2000).

### Nomenclature

The term 'pigmented xerodermoid' had been used for a condition whose mild clinical symptoms suggested it was similar to, but distinct from, xeroderma pigmentosum. Cleaver et al. (1980) found that a pigmented xerodermoid cell culture could not be distinguished from XP variant cells in UV-irradiated assays of unscheduled DNA synthesis, strand breakage during repair, chain growth during semiconservative replication with or without caffeine, and recovery of DNA replication. They concluded that the term 'pigmented xerodermoid' was redundant.

### Biochemical Features

Cleaver (1972) described variants of xeroderma pigmentosum that had normal DNA repair and normal sensitivity to ultraviolet light.

Lehmann et al. (1975) found that cells from the class of XP patients with normal excision repair handled DNA synthesis abnormally after UV irradiation. In these variant cells, low-molecular-weight DNA made after irradiation took much longer than in normal cells to convert to high-molecular-weight DNA. Caffeine strongly inhibited this slow conversion but had no effect in normal cells. Two excision-repair-defective XP cell lines showed intermediate effects in both respects.

Cleaver et al. (1980) proposed that XP variant cells lack a gene product that lets normal cells replicate DNA without interruption at UV-induced pyrimidine dimers. Without it, replication forks are blocked more often and at lower doses.

Wang et al. (1991) noted that XP variant cells are only slightly more sensitive than normal cells to the cytotoxic effect of UV, but are significantly more sensitive to its mutagenic effect. They transfected an XP variant cell line with a UV-irradiated shuttle vector carrying the supF gene. Mutant frequency rose linearly with dose, with a slope 5 times steeper than in normal cells. Of 53 independent mutants, 52 had base substitutions, and 62 of 64 substitutions involved a dipyrimidine. Twenty-eight percent of mutations involved A:T base pairs, compared with 11% in normal cells. The authors suggested that XP variant cells are less likely than normal cells to insert dAMP opposite bases involved in photoproducts.

Using DNA combing, Despras et al. (2010) found that in UV-irradiated XPV cells, the translesion synthesis defect sharply reduced the number of active replication forks and left a high proportion of forks stalled. Irradiated XPV cells formed extensive regions of single-strand DNA during replication, which over-activated the ATR/Chk1 pathway after low UVC doses. Adding a low concentration of caffeine after irradiation reduced Chk1 activation and abolished DNA synthesis in XPV cells. Inhibiting Chk1 with UCN-01 worsened fork stalling and sensitized XPV cells to UVC, as caffeine does. The authors concluded that polymerase eta acts at stalled forks to restart their progression. In its absence, Chk1 becomes essential for resuming replication by alternative pathways through fork stabilization.

### Inheritance

Itoh et al. (2000) identified compound heterozygous mutations in a patient with XPV, each inherited from a heterozygous parent. This is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Masutani et al. (1999) isolated from HeLa cells a DNA polymerase that bypasses UV-induced thymine dimers to continue replication on damaged DNA in XPV cell extracts. They identified it as the human homolog of yeast Rad30, DNA polymerase eta. All XPV cells they examined carried mutations in the DNA polymerase eta gene. Recombinant human DNA polymerase eta corrected the inability of XPV cell extracts to replicate past thymine dimers on damaged DNA.

Johnson et al. (1999) found nonsense or frameshift mutations in hRAD30, the human counterpart of yeast RAD30, in XPV cell lines. Seven of the 8 mutations they identified were predicted to produce a severely truncated hRad30 protein.

In a Japanese family with XPV, Itoh et al. (2000) found that the patient carried 2 heterozygous missense mutations in the POLH gene, each inherited from a parent. One was an A-to-G transition at nucleotide 1840 causing a lys535-to-glu (K535E) substitution. The other was an A-to-C transversion at nucleotide 2003 affecting lys589. Neither mutation was found in 52 unrelated Japanese individuals. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Itoh et al. (1996) described a simple autoradiographic method for diagnosing XPV. Patient fibroblasts and normal control cells were plated side by side and tested for 3 markers after UV irradiation: unscheduled DNA synthesis, recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. Among the hereditary photosensitive disorders tested, which included xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome, only XPV cells showed normal unscheduled DNA synthesis and normal RNA synthesis recovery together with reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis (approximately 50% of control). Caffeine increased this reduction.

Itoh et al. (2000) found that recovery of replicative DNA synthesis in XPV cells was 51 +/- 6% of normal controls and fell to 36 +/- 5% with a nontoxic level of caffeine. They studied 2 families, each with a photosensitive patient identified as having XPV. Cells from the heterozygous parents had normal unscheduled DNA synthesis, recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. With 1 mM caffeine, however, their recovery of replicative DNA synthesis was reduced (53 +/- 8% of normal control). In colony-forming assays, the heterozygous cells survived UV normally without caffeine but slightly less well with 1 mM caffeine. The authors suggested that XPV heterozygotes can be identified by their UV sensitivity in the presence of nontoxic caffeine.
