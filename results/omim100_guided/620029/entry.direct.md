### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is mainly a neurologic disorder. Affected individuals have developmental delay or intellectual disability, hypotonia, and expressive language delay, and may also have autism, ataxia, and epilepsy. Orthopedic abnormalities are common in individuals with nontruncating variants. These include clubfoot, congenital finger contractures, adducted thumbs, hip dysplasia, kyphosis or scoliosis, and joint hypermobility. Most patients do not have significant cardiac involvement or the other classic features of Timothy syndrome (Bozarth et al., 2018; Rodan et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She did not have prolongation of the QT interval. The authors described her condition as Timothy syndrome-like.

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (patient 1) born at 38 weeks' gestation to nonconsanguineous parents of English and Ukrainian ancestry. Her birth length was 40.6 cm (z-score -3.92). She was evaluated after birth for hypoglycemia and multiple congenital anomalies. These included profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubfeet, bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, and camptodactyly. Dysmorphic features included micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, very scant hair, and bilaterally inverted nipples. She also had mild optic nerve hypoplasia, severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, and anal stenosis. She needed a gastrostomy tube at 8 months because of severe dysphagia. At 18 months, her developmental milestones had not progressed, she was profoundly hypotonic, and she could not visually track or fixate. She also had severe disturbances of the sleep-wake cycle. Seizures began within a few hours of birth, with numerous tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures each day. Epileptic tonic spasms were diagnosed at 3 months. Her seizures did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs. Clobazam reduced the seizure burden substantially, but at 18 months she still had multiple daily tonic and focal seizures. EEG at 5 days showed diffuse excessive fast activity mixed with other frequencies, and the lack of normal organization persisted beyond the neonatal period. Brain MRI at 7 days and 12 months was normal. Polysomnography showed severe central and obstructive sleep apnea. An echocardiogram and a 24-hour Holter monitor at 13 months were normal. Bozarth et al. (2018) classified the disorder as neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. They noted that she had features of Timothy syndrome but no cardiac abnormalities.

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals from 22 families, 13 male and 12 female, who carried heterozygous CACNA1C variants and had mainly neurologic features. Ages at last follow-up ranged from 10 months to 40 years. Fourteen unrelated individuals had nontruncating variants. Prenatal findings in this group included reduced fetal movement (3 of 14), bilateral clubfoot on ultrasound (2 of 14), and oligohydramnios (2 of 14). All 14 had developmental delay or intellectual disability. Nine were nonverbal and 6 were nonambulatory at last follow-up. One had a formal diagnosis of autism, and 6 had autistic features. Twelve of the 14 were hypotonic, 4 had abnormal balance and coordination (ataxia), and 1 had bilateral vestibular areflexia. Ten of the 14 had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years. Both generalized and focal epilepsies occurred, including West syndrome and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. Seizures were fever-sensitive in 3 individuals and medically refractory in 4. Regression occurred in 1 individual in the setting of infantile spasms. Brain MRI was normal except for an incidental arachnoid cyst in 1 individual and prominent cerebellar folia in another. Twelve of the 14 had orthopedic abnormalities: bilateral clubfoot and congenital finger contractures (2), adducted thumbs (2 additional), kyphosis or scoliosis (2), congenital hip dysplasia (3), syndactyly (2), and joint hypermobility (3). Other features included hyperopia (4), strabismus (4), tremor (2), and leg spasticity (1). Two individuals had microcephaly and 3 had short stature. One individual had a mildly prolonged QTc at 7 years. Holter monitoring in this individual showed significant QT prolongation and T-wave alternans. Another individual had a possible ventricular conduction delay. Facial dysmorphisms were minor and inconsistent.

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating CACNA1C variants. Most of them had essentially normal early motor development and walked independently. Ten of the 11 had delayed expressive language. Five had autism, and 3 of these were nonverbal. Cognition ranged from normal to moderate intellectual disability. Only 1 of the 11 had a history of seizures, and 3 had hypotonia. Four had poor balance or coordination and 2 had tremor. One individual had paroxysmal dyskinesia, and another had a paroxysmal movement disorder in infancy that later resolved. Recurrent infections occurred in 4 individuals, 1 of whom had hypogammaglobulinemia. ECGs were normal in the 5 individuals tested.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous CACNA1C mutations in the patients reported by Kosaki et al. (2018) and Bozarth et al. (2018) arose de novo. In the cohort of Rodan et al. (2021), all nontruncating variants and all but 3 truncating variants arose de novo. The 3 inherited truncating variants came from the father and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families, which is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Rodan et al. (2021) noted variable penetrance and expressivity among relatives who carried truncating variants.

### Molecular Genetics

In the girl with syndactyly and developmental delay, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (R1024G).

In patient 1, who had neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the CACNA1C gene (V1363M). The variant was absent from the gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes databases. Exome sequencing also detected a de novo heterozygous deletion of exons 11 to 16 of the MSH2 gene.

Rodan et al. (2021) found heterozygous CACNA1C variants in 25 individuals from 22 families by exome sequencing or an intellectual disability gene panel. Patients were identified through GeneMatcher and ClinVar. Thirteen unrelated individuals had missense variants and 1 had an in-frame deletion. Eleven individuals from 8 families had predicted truncating variants, one of which affected a canonical splice site. None of the variants were present in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or the Exome Variant Server. Whole-cell patch clamp studies were done on 4 missense variants in HEK293 cells. L1408V reduced current density to about 30% of control without changing protein levels, which indicated loss of function. L657F increased both current density and protein expression about 2-fold, which indicated gain of function. L614P produced a modest increase in current that was not statistically significant. L614R had no significant effect on current or gating. The authors noted that the L614R experiments used the CACNB2 accessory subunit, whereas the other variants were tested with CACNB3.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Rodan et al. (2021) found phenotypic differences between individuals with nontruncating and truncating CACNA1C variants. Those with nontruncating variants had more severe global developmental delay or intellectual disability and greater motor impairment. About half of them were nonverbal and nonambulatory, most had hypotonia, and more than two-thirds had epilepsy. Those with truncating variants more often had selective expressive language deficits with essentially normal early motor development. Autism was more common in this group (5 of 11), and only 1 had seizures. The authors noted that their sample size was limited.

In a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension but no prolongation of the QT interval, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous arg1024-to-gly (R1024G) mutation in the CACNA1C gene.

**.0002 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, VAL1363MET [NM_000719.6:c.4087G>A; p.Val1363Met]

In a girl with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, profound hypotonia, bilateral clubfeet, syndactyly, and camptodactyly, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.4087G-A transition in exon 33 of the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a val1363-to-met (V1363M) substitution. The mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV and was absent from the gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes databases. Structural modeling suggested that the larger methionine residue could disrupt the adjacent sixth transmembrane helix and affect channel gating or calcium transmission.

**.0003 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, LEU614PRO [c.1841T>C; p.Leu614Pro]

In a 24-year-old man (P8) with intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, fever-sensitive generalized epilepsy beginning at 1 year, and kyphosis, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1841T-C transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu614-to-pro (L614P) substitution. He was nonverbal and nonambulatory. In patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells, the mutation caused a modest increase in calcium current that was not statistically significant. Protein levels were similar to wildtype. In the results, the authors said the variant appeared to confer gain-of-function properties, but in the discussion they described its effect as neutral.

**.0004 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, LEU657PHE [c.1969C>T; p.Leu657Phe]

In a 15-year-old girl (P9) with moderate to severe intellectual disability, language delay, and seizures beginning at 6 years (behavioral arrest spells with or without head or limb jerking), Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1969C-T transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu657-to-phe (L657F) substitution. She also had small-appearing feet and tapered fingers. In patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells, the mutation significantly increased calcium current and shifted the voltage dependence of activation in the hyperpolarizing direction. Protein expression was also elevated. Both current density and protein level rose about 2-fold, which is consistent with a gain-of-function effect.

**.0005 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, LANGUAGE DELAY, AND SKELETAL DEFECTS WITH OR WITHOUT SEIZURES**  
CACNA1C, LEU1408VAL [c.4222C>G; p.Leu1408Val]

In a 6.8-year-old girl (P12) with severe intellectual disability, hypotonia, and myoclonic seizures beginning at 6 months that progressed to a generalized epilepsy syndrome, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.4222C-G transversion in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu1408-to-val (L1408V) substitution. She was nonverbal and nonambulatory. She also had kyphoscoliosis, coxa valga, strabismus, and a possible ventricular conduction delay. In patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells, the mutation reduced current density to about 30% of control. Activation kinetics and protein expression were unchanged, which indicated a loss-of-function effect.
