### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is a neurologic disorder with variable features. These include developmental delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, delayed expressive language, autism or autistic features, ataxia, and epilepsy. Some patients also have orthopedic abnormalities such as clubfoot, congenital finger contractures, adducted thumbs, hip dysplasia, scoliosis, and syndactyly. Most patients do not have the long QT interval or the other cardiac features of Timothy syndrome. Patients with de novo nontruncating variants tend to be more severely affected than those with truncating variants, who mainly have expressive language deficits and autism (Rodan et al., 2021).

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She did not have prolongation of the QT interval.

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl of English and Ukrainian descent, born to nonconsanguineous parents, who had neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy (patient 1). At birth her length was 40.6 cm (z-score -3.92). She had profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubfeet, bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, and camptodactyly. Her dysmorphic features included micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, very scant hair, and bilateral inverted nipples. She also had mild optic nerve hypoplasia, severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, anal stenosis, and severe dysphagia that required a gastrostomy tube at 8 months. Seizures began within a few hours of birth and included tonic, tonic-myoclonic, and focal seizures as well as epileptic tonic spasms. They were refractory to multiple antiepileptic drugs. EEG showed diffuse excessive fast activity that persisted beyond the neonatal period. At 18 months she had made no developmental progress, could not visually track or fixate, and had severe sleep-wake cycle disturbances. Polysomnography showed severe central and obstructive sleep apnea. Brain MRI, echocardiography, and Holter monitoring were normal.

Rodan et al. (2021) reported 25 individuals from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants and a predominantly neurologic phenotype. The 13 males and 12 females ranged in age from 10 months to 40 years. All 14 individuals with nontruncating variants had developmental delay or intellectual disability. Nine were nonverbal and 6 were nonambulatory. One had a formal diagnosis of autism and 6 others had autistic features. Twelve were hypotonic, 4 had abnormal balance and coordination, and 10 had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years. Seizure types included infantile spasms (West syndrome), myoclonic, absence, generalized tonic-clonic, and focal seizures, and seizures were fever-sensitive in 3 individuals. Prenatal findings included reduced fetal movement (3 of 14), bilateral clubfoot (2 of 14), and oligohydramnios (2 of 14). Twelve of the 14 had orthopedic abnormalities: bilateral clubfoot with congenital finger contractures (2), persistently adducted thumbs (2), kyphosis or scoliosis (2), congenital hip dysplasia (3), syndactyly (2), and joint hypermobility (3). Other features included hyperopia (4), strabismus (4), tremor (2), microcephaly (2), and short stature (3). One individual had a prolonged QT interval, and brain MRI was normal apart from incidental findings. The 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating variants mostly had normal early motor development and walked independently. Ten had delayed expressive language, and 5 had autism. One had a history of seizures. Other features were hypotonia (3), poor balance or coordination (4), tremor (2), and recurrent infections (4). ECGs, when performed, were normal.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous CACNA1C mutations identified by Kosaki et al. (2018), Bozarth et al. (2018), and Rodan et al. (2021) in patients with NEDHLSS occurred de novo in most cases. Rodan et al. (2021) found that 3 truncating variants were paternally inherited and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families, which is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. These families showed variable penetrance and expressivity. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-year-old girl with syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension but no QT prolongation, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a de novo heterozygous arg1024-to-gly (R1024G) mutation in the CACNA1C gene.

In a girl with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, hypotonia, syndactyly, and camptodactyly, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.4087G-A transition (NM_000719.6) in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a val1363-to-met (V1363M) substitution. The residue is highly conserved and lies in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV, facing the sixth transmembrane helix. The variant was absent from the gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes databases. Structural modeling predicted that the change to the larger methionine residue would disrupt the sixth transmembrane helix and affect channel gating or calcium transmission. The patient also carried a de novo heterozygous deletion of MSH2 exons 11 to 16.

Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous CACNA1C variants in 25 individuals from 22 families with a predominantly neurologic phenotype. The variants were found by exome sequencing or an intellectual disability gene panel, and the patients were ascertained through GeneMatcher and ClinVar. Thirteen unrelated individuals had missense variants and 1 had an in-frame deletion, all of which occurred de novo. Eleven individuals from 8 families had predicted truncating variants, including a canonical splice site variant. Of the truncating variants, all but 3 occurred de novo, and the 3 inherited ones segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families. None of the variants was found in the gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or Exome Variant Server databases. All missense variants affected highly conserved residues, and they clustered in linker segments of domain II (exon 13) and domain IV (exon 35). Patch-clamp studies in HEK293 cells showed different effects on channel function. The L1408V variant reduced current density to about 30% of control with normal protein expression (loss of function). The L657F variant roughly doubled both current density and protein level (gain of function). The L614P variant produced modest current increases that were not statistically significant. The L614R variant had no significant effect on current or gating. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Rodan et al. (2021) noted that individuals with nontruncating CACNA1C variants had more severe global developmental delay or intellectual disability and motor impairment than those with truncating variants. About half of the nontruncating group were nonverbal and nonambulatory, and epilepsy was present in more than two-thirds of them. By contrast, individuals with truncating variants had more selective developmental abnormalities involving expressive language, mostly normal early motor development, a higher frequency of autism (5 of 11), and seizures in only 1 of 11.

In a 15-year-old girl with NEDHLSS (P9), Rodan et al. In 3 male members of a family with NEDHLSS (P22a, P22b, and P22c), Rodan et al. (2021) identified a heterozygous c.5860C-T transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in an arg1989-to-ter (R1989X) substitution.
