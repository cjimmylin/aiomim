### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene (PRPH2) on chromosome 6p. Digenic RP7 is caused by double heterozygosity for mutations in the PRPH2 gene and the ROM1 gene. Homozygous mutation in PRPH2 has been reported in patients with early-onset retinal dystrophy with Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) or juvenile RP phenotypes. ⚠︎

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is an autosomal dominant retinal degeneration caused by mutation in the gene encoding the photoreceptor glycoprotein peripherin/RDS (PRPH2). PRPH2 mutations cause highly variable phenotypes, and retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus have been observed within a single family. A digenic form of RP occurs in individuals who are double heterozygotes for mutations in PRPH2 and ROM1.

### Nomenclature

Farrar et al. (1991) used the designation RP6 in the title of their report localizing an autosomal dominant RP locus to chromosome 6p, but referred to the locus as RP5 in the body of the abstract. Jordan et al. (1992) referred to the locus as RP6.

### Clinical Features

Farrar et al. (1991) studied a large Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant RP. ⚠︎

Weleber et al. (1993) reported a family in which 3 separate phenotypes occurred. The 75-year-old mother presented at age 63 years with a profoundly abnormal electroretinogram (ERG) and adult-onset RP that progressed dramatically over 12 years, with marked loss of peripheral visual field. One daughter developed pattern macular dystrophy at age 31 years; at age 44 years her ERG was moderately abnormal, but her clinical disease was limited to the macula. Another daughter presented at age 42 years with macular degeneration and over 10 years developed the clinical picture of fundus flavimaculatus, with preserved peripheral visual field and a moderately abnormal ERG. The son had onset of macular degeneration at age 44 years, with pericentral scotomas, a markedly abnormal ERG, and punctate pigment epithelial transmission defects on fluorescein angiography.

Kikawa et al. (1994) reported autosomal dominant RP associated with bull's-eye maculopathy.

Wang et al. (2013) reported 3 patients with homozygous PRPH2 mutations who exhibited LCA or juvenile RP phenotypes, including visual impairment within the first year of life, nystagmus in the 2 patients with LCA, nondetectable or reduced ERGs, and a very similar form of maculopathy. Fundus findings included a multilobulated central atrophic maculopathy, concentric rings of yellow deposits, vessel narrowing, peripheral and macular pigment deposits, choroidal sclerosis, optic disc pallor, and retinal pigment epithelium mottling and loss. Heterozygous carrier relatives were asymptomatic but showed detectable maculopathy: the 56-year-old father of 1 patient had macular pattern dystrophy and clear-cut foveal changes with essentially normal visual acuity, and the 57-year-old mother of another patient had 20/20 visual acuity but a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy and a number of other retinal flecks.

Manes et al. (2015) found that the clinical severity of disease in French patients with autosomal dominant RP due to PRPH2 mutations was moderate, with 78% of eyes having 1-0.5 visual acuity and 52% of eyes retaining more than 50% of the visual field. Some patients characteristically showed vitelliform deposits or macular involvement. In some families, pericentral RP or macular dystrophy was found in some family members while widespread RP was present in other members of the same family.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of RP7 was established by the cosegregation of PRPH2 mutations with the disease in families with autosomal dominant RP (Farrar et al., 1991; Kajiwara et al., 1991). Kajiwara et al. (1994) described digenic inheritance in 3 families in which only double heterozygotes for mutations in PRPH2 and ROM1 developed RP. Wang et al. (2013) reported homozygous PRPH2 mutations in patients with LCA or juvenile RP whose heterozygous relatives were asymptomatic but had maculopathy.

### Mapping

By linkage analysis in an Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant RP, Farrar et al. (1991) obtained positive 2-point lod scores with 5 markers on chromosome 6p, including D6S105 (Z = 6.081 at theta = 0.00), HLA-DRA (Z = 4.364 at theta = 0.00), and RDS (Z = 5.376 at theta = 0.00). Multipoint analysis yielded a lod score of 6.6, maximizing at HLA-DRA.

In the same Irish family, Jordan et al. (1992) found that an intragenic marker from the peripherin-RDS gene cosegregated with the disease with zero recombination (lod score 5.46). After mapping peripherin-RDS relative to other 6p markers using the CEPH reference panel, multipoint analysis produced a lod score of 8.21, maximizing at the peripherin-RDS locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Kajiwara et al. (1991) identified 3 mutations in the RDS gene, the human homolog of the mouse retinal degeneration slow gene, that cosegregated with autosomal dominant RP in separate families.

In the Irish pedigree with RP linked to 6p, Farrar et al. (1991) identified a 3-bp deletion in the peripherin-RDS gene that resulted in loss of one of a pair of highly conserved cysteine residues in the predicted third transmembrane domain of peripherin. The deletion segregated with the disease and was not present in unaffected controls.

In a family with RP, pattern dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus, Weleber et al. (1993) identified a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the PRPH2 gene.

Wells et al. (1993) found a cysteine deletion at codon 118/119 of the RDS gene associated with RP in 1 family, whereas mutations at codon 172 and a stop at codon 258 were found in families with macular dystrophies.

Kikawa et al. (1994) identified an asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the PRPH2 gene in autosomal dominant RP associated with bull's-eye maculopathy, detected by nonradioisotopic SSCP.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with mutations in the unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes ROM1 and PRPH2 in which only double heterozygotes developed RP.

By targeted next-generation sequencing of 163 retinal disease genes in 179 prescreened patients with LCA or juvenile RP, followed by Sanger confirmation and sequencing of PRPH2 in another 135 unsolved patients, Wang et al. (2013) identified homozygous missense mutations in the PRPH2 gene in 3 patients: C213R in 1 patient with LCA and L185P in 1 patient with LCA and 1 with juvenile RP. Heterozygous carrier relatives had maculopathy without visual symptoms. The authors noted that L185P, together with a null mutation in ROM1, had been reported to cause digenic RP, and suggested that the severe early-onset disease in the homozygous patients was likely due to a dose-dependent effect.

By Sanger sequencing of PRPH2 in 310 French families with autosomal dominant RP, Manes et al. (2015) identified 15 mutations, including 6 novel and 9 previously reported changes, in 32 families. A recurrent novel mutation, leu254-to-gln (L254Q), led to protein aggregation when peripherin-2 was produced in yeast and analyzed by Western blot, suggesting abnormal folding.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wells et al. (1993) concluded that both RP and macular dystrophies are caused by mutations in RDS and that the functional significance of certain amino acids in peripherin-RDS may differ in cones and rods. Weleber et al. (1993) concluded that a single 3-bp deletion in PRPH2 can produce clinically disparate phenotypes even within the same family.

### Population Genetics

Manes et al. (2015) found that PRPH2 mutations accounted for 10.3% of autosomal dominant RP in a French cohort of 310 families, higher than previously reported (0% to 8%), making PRPH2 the second most frequent autosomal dominant RP gene after RHO in their series.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**  
PRPH2, 3-BP DEL, CODON 153/154

In a family in which a 75-year-old woman had adult-onset RP, 1 daughter had pattern macular dystrophy, another daughter had fundus flavimaculatus, and a son had macular degeneration, Weleber et al. (1993) identified a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the PRPH2 gene.

**.0002 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**  
PRPH2, CYS118/119DEL

Wells et al. (1993) found a cysteine deletion at codon 118/119 of the RDS gene associated with RP in a family with autosomal dominant retinal dystrophy.

**.0003 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**  
PRPH2, ASN244LYS [p.Asn244Lys]

Kikawa et al. (1994) identified an asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the PRPH2 gene causing autosomal dominant RP associated with bull's-eye maculopathy; the mutation was detected by nonradioisotopic SSCP.

**.0004 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7, DIGENIC**  
PRPH2, LEU185PRO [c.554T>C; p.L185P]

Wang et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.554T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a leu185-to-pro (L185P) substitution at a residue conserved across species, in a patient with LCA (patient 3256) and in a patient with juvenile RP (patient 741). The mutation was found by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing; both parents of patient 741 were heterozygous, and the father had macular pattern dystrophy with essentially normal visual acuity. The authors noted that L185P, together with a null mutation in ROM1, had been reported to cause digenic RP. ⚠︎

**.0005 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**  
PRPH2, CYS213ARG [c.637T>C; p.C213R]

In a patient with LCA (patient 1318), Wang et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.637T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a cys213-to-arg (C213R) substitution at a residue conserved across species. The mutation, previously associated with autosomal dominant pattern dystrophy, was found by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both parents and the patient's son were heterozygous, and a brother with normal vision was homozygous wildtype. The heterozygous mother, at 57 years of age, had 20/20 visual acuity but a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy, and the 7-year-old son had a miniature form of foveal butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy.

**.0006 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**  
PRPH2, LEU254GLN [p.Leu254Gln]

In French families with autosomal dominant RP, Manes et al. (2015) identified a recurrent novel leu254-to-gln (L254Q) mutation in the PRPH2 gene. Peripherin-2 carrying the mutation, produced in yeast and analyzed by Western blot, showed protein aggregation, suggesting abnormal folding.
