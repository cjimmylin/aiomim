### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes the glucose transporter GLUT1.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects is a rare form of hereditary stomatocytosis. Red cells leak monovalent cations, and the leak increases on cooling. Patients have hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder (Fricke et al., 2004; Flatt et al., 2011). The disorder is a form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome in which SLC2A1 mutations cause both loss of glucose transport and a red cell cation leak (Flatt et al., 2011). Temperature-dependent loss of potassium from red cells can cause pseudohyperkalemia (Bawazir et al., 2012).

### Nomenclature

Flatt et al. (2011) described stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis (sdCHC) as a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome. Bawazir et al. (2012) used the term 'cation-leaky GLUT1-deficiency syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Fricke et al. (2004) studied red cells from 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree of stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis, and expanded on 1 previously reported case. The cases differed in how the passive potassium leak depended on temperature. In 2 cases the leak was lowest at around 20 to 25 degrees C, so the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, giving the cryohydrocytosis phenotype. Fricke et al. (2004) said these were the only 2 known cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Both patients had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts, and massive hepatosplenomegaly, which the authors suggested probably defined a new hemato-neurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that their 2 previously reported patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis had a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder.

Bawazir et al. (2012) reported an infant who presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, and microcephaly. Seizures and delayed developmental milestones followed. Brain MRI and CT scans showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. The infant had visual impairment caused by both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. The red cells were extremely leaky, which caused the hemolysis. Potassium loss from the red cells depended on temperature, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia. Bawazir et al. (2012) stated that this was the fourth pedigree described with this syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied the erythrocyte membrane proteins of 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased red cell permeability to sodium and potassium; 1 patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis. SDS gel electrophoresis separated band 7 into components of about 30,000, 28,000, and 26,000 daltons. The 28,000-dalton component was decreased in both patients. Two-dimensional electrophoresis split this component into 2 proteins, designated 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. Protein 22 x 8 was completely absent in both patients, whereas 60 x 8 was present. All band 7 proteins were present in normal subjects and in reticulocytosis controls.

In all of their cases, Fricke et al. (2004) found by Western blotting that red cell membranes were deficient in the 32-kD membrane protein stomatin. All showed marked cation leaks at 37 degrees C and markedly abnormal intracellular sodium and potassium concentrations. Immunocytochemistry showed that the stomatin deficiency was not complete. On typical blood films, some red cells were stomatin-positive while most were negative, although in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive.

Flatt et al. (2011) found that red cells lose stomatin during reticulocyte maturation and that this loss involves endocytosis.

### Inheritance

Flatt et al. (2011) noted that the hereditary stomatocytoses, the group to which stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis belongs, are dominantly inherited hemolytic anemias.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 2 patients with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis previously reported by Fricke et al. (2004), Flatt et al. (2011) identified mutations in the SLC2A1 gene. Expression studies in Xenopus oocytes showed that the mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Based on a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, the authors proposed possible mechanisms for the phenotypes of the 2 mutations.

In an infant with SDCHCN, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deleted isoleucine 435 or 436 of GLUT1.

### Pathogenesis

Bawazir et al. (2012) suggested that the disease affects many organ systems for 2 likely reasons: glucose transport is deficient at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency, and the cation-leaky membrane protein has harmful osmotic effects in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.

In an infant with stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN), Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a trinucleotide deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deleted isoleucine 435 or 436 of GLUT1.
