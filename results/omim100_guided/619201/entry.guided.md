### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is caused by homozygous mutation in the KIRREL1 gene on chromosome 1.

### Description

Nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is an autosomal recessive kidney disorder. It presents as steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) in childhood or adolescence, with proteinuria and no extrarenal features. Renal biopsy has shown mesangial hypercellularity, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), or findings consistent with minimal change disease, along with podocyte foot process effacement. The response to treatment varies, and chronic kidney disease may develop (Solanki et al., 2019).

### Clinical Features

Solanki et al. (2019) reported 2 boys from unrelated consanguineous families with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome. Neither child had extrarenal manifestations or syndromic features. The first patient (A666_21) was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 5 years and was steroid-resistant at initial presentation. He was also resistant to various immunosuppressive drugs. He never achieved complete remission and continued to have undulating proteinuria with a partial response to treatment. His renal function stayed normal throughout 12.5 years of follow-up. On light microscopy, his renal biopsy showed only minor mesangial hypercellularity, consistent with minimal change disease. His younger sister had reported periorbital edema and proteinuria. Among extended relatives, 3 children had end-stage renal disease and 1 child had nephrotic syndrome, but none of these relatives were genotyped. The second patient (B742_21), a child of Italian descent, was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at age 14 years and was steroid-resistant at initial presentation. He also had high blood pressure. Treatment with tacrolimus, a calcium channel blocker, and an ACE inhibitor led to complete remission. However, over 4 years of follow-up he developed progressive chronic kidney disease, reaching CKD stage 2. His renal biopsy showed FSGS. Electron microscopy showed extensive foot process effacement, increased mesangial matrix, and mesangial proliferation.

### Inheritance

Solanki et al. (2019) found homozygous KIRREL1 mutations in affected individuals from 2 consanguineous families, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance of NPHS23.

### Molecular Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) performed whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping in a cohort of 596 individuals from 406 families with SRNS. In 2 patients from unrelated consanguineous families with NPHS23, they identified homozygous missense mutations in the KIRREL1 gene: R440C and S573L. Both mutations lay within the maximum homozygosity peak on chromosome 1, and both affected residues are conserved to D. rerio. In gnomAD, R440C had a minor allele frequency of 3.97 x 10(-6) and S573L had a minor allele frequency of 7.87 x 10(-4). S573L had an allele frequency of 0.0011 in European populations and was found in the homozygous state in 1 control subject. The authors raised the possibility of incomplete penetrance or variable expressivity for this variant. Flag-tagged mutant proteins were stably expressed in cultured human podocytes. Both mutants were mislocalized and were present at significantly lower levels in the membrane fraction than wildtype. They showed increased localization to the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes, suggesting defective trafficking. Podocyte monolayers expressing the mutants had significantly increased albumin flux compared with wildtype. Membrane localization of NPHS1 was not affected.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2003) noted that Neph1-deficient mice develop nephrotic syndrome at birth. In rodents, they localized Neph1 to the glomerular slit diaphragm and showed that it interacts directly with nephrin and ZO-1. In vivo, combined injection of subnephritogenic doses of anti-Neph1 and anti-nephrin antibodies disrupted the Neph1-nephrin interaction. This caused proteinuria that did not depend on complement or leukocytes, and foot processes were preserved. Nephrin and Neph1 protein expression in podocytes was modestly reduced, and ZO-1 protein expression was dramatically reduced.

In cultured human podocytes, the mutant protein was mislocalized away from the cell membrane and showed increased localization to the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes.
