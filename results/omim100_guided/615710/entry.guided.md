### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene on chromosome 6q21-q22.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are neonatal diabetes, a hypoplastic or annular pancreas, duodenal and/or jejunal atresia, and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder (Mitchell et al., 2004). Other features are intrahepatic cholestasis and intractable diarrhea that does not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement. Many affected infants have died in the first year of life (Smith et al., 2010; Sansbury et al., 2015). In some patients, diabetes does not appear until childhood (Sansbury et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Martínez-Frías et al. (1992) reported 2 sibs born to consanguineous parents. Both had low birth weight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, a hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. The authors suggested that this was a previously unreported autosomal recessive syndrome. Annerén et al. (1998) reported a lethal autosomal recessive syndrome with intrauterine growth retardation, intra- and extrahepatic biliary atresia, and esophageal and duodenal atresia. Gentile and Fiorente (1999) reported esophageal, duodenal, rectoanal, and biliary atresia, intestinal malrotation, a malformed or hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. They considered this further evidence of a new, distinct syndrome. Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) described a female infant born to consanguineous parents. She had low birth weight, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, a hypoplastic pancreas, and intestinal malrotation, and she died 60 days after birth. Her karyotype was normal (46,XX).

Chappell et al. (2008) suggested that patients with neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis, and those with overlapping patterns such as the patients of Martínez-Frías et al. (1992), may have variations of the same syndrome. Martinovici et al. (2010) diagnosed Martínez-Frías syndrome in a newborn with intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus. Smith et al. (2010) disagreed. They noted that the original Martínez-Frías syndrome patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias but no diabetes, and that no RFX6 mutation was found in the parents of 1 case of Martínez-Frías syndrome. On this basis they considered their patients with RFX6 mutations distinct from Martínez-Frías syndrome. They named the condition Mitchell-Riley syndrome after the 2 clinicians who first described it.

### Clinical Features

Mitchell et al. (2004) described 5 infants: 2 sib pairs from 2 families and 1 isolated case. One sib pair was born to consanguineous parents. The infants had neonatal diabetes, a hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder. One patient had a milder form and survived without insulin. The other 4 children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. Pancreatic tissue from 3 infants was studied by immunohistochemistry. It showed a few scattered chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but no insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. Exocrine histology varied.

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient born to consanguineous parents who had neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis. This was the first such patient with neonatal diabetes but no demonstrable structural abnormality of the pancreas.

Martinovici et al. (2010) reported a newborn born to consanguineous parents. The infant had intrauterine growth retardation, intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus, as well as neonatal hemochromatosis. The authors suggested that neonatal hemochromatosis may be part of the syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) noted that their patients did not lack exocrine pancreatic enzymes, even though pancreatic size was somewhat reduced. Autopsies of 2 patients showed exocrine pancreas of normal appearance with clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. The syndrome also included a hypoplastic gallbladder and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome whose diabetes was not diagnosed until the ages of 3 and 6 years. In both, a period of asymptomatic hyperglycemia of at least 1 year came before the diagnosis. The girl was born at 32 weeks' gestation with duodenal atresia, a jejunal web, and Meckel diverticulum. She was also found to have an absent gallbladder. She presented with diabetic ketoacidosis at age 3 years, with insulin below 2 microIU/ml and C-peptide of 0.023 ng/ml. The boy was born at 34 weeks' gestation with duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation. He presented with hyperglycemia (16.0 mmol/l) at age 6 years. Neither child had liver involvement, and islet autoantibodies were negative in both. Both were alive at age 9 years. The authors also reviewed 8 previously published patients with molecularly confirmed disease. All had duodenal atresia, 7 of 8 had gallbladder agenesis, and 7 of 8 had intrahepatic cholestasis. Five of the 8 had died, all within the first year of life.

### Inheritance

Mitchell et al. (2004) proposed autosomal recessive inheritance because of affected sib pairs and parental consanguinity. Smith et al. (2010) confirmed it: patients were homozygous or compound heterozygous for RFX6 mutations inherited from carrier parents. Sansbury et al. (2015) found no evidence that heterozygous carriers of either of their family's RFX6 mutations had abnormal HbA1c or C-peptide levels or a higher risk of diabetes. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) performed high-resolution homozygosity mapping in 2 probands, the offspring of first cousins and of second cousins. Only 3 homozygous-by-descent regions, totaling 24 Mb, were shared by both probands: on 2p15-p16, 6q21-q22, and 6q23. Of the 194 RefSeq genes in these regions, only RFX6, in the largest segment at 6q21-q22, showed pancreas-enriched expression in the TiGER database (Liu et al., 2008).

### Molecular Genetics

Mitchell et al. (2004) studied DNA from 1 patient in the consanguineous family. They found no duplication or uniparental isodisomy of PLAGL1 at 6q24, no contiguous gene deletion involving the glucokinase gene, and no mutation in the PDX1 gene. Chappell et al. (2008) found no mutations in KCNJ11, ABCC8, GCK, IPF1, HNF1B, NEUROD1, TCF7L2, or HNF6 in their patient. ⚠︎

Smith et al. (2010) used deep sequencing to analyze the captured exons in the shared homozygous regions in proband 2. The only nonsynonymous variant found was a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) mutation in RFX6. Direct sequencing of RFX6 found mutations in 5 of 6 probands, all inherited from carrier parents. Probands 1, 4, and 5 died in the first few months of life. Each was homozygous for a different mutation: a splice donor site mutation in intron 2 (IVS2+2T-C), an out-of-frame deletion in exon 7, or the missense mutation R181Q. Proband 3 was alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin. This patient was compound heterozygous for splice site mutations in intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G) and intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G). In autopsy pancreas from proband 1, the properly spliced transcript was not detected. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, R181Q completely abolished DNA binding, whereas S217P only modestly reduced it. No RFX6 mutation was found in proband 6. DNA from this proband was not available, and the authors considered the case most likely a phenocopy.

Sansbury et al. (2015) studied 2 double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes. Targeted next-generation sequencing, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, showed that both children were compound heterozygous for 2 nonsense mutations in RFX6: R726X in exon 17 and R866X in exon 18. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X, and the mothers were heterozygous for R866X. Both mutations had been inherited from the grandmothers.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) noted a genotype-phenotype correlation. Patients homozygous for a splice site mutation, a frameshift deletion, or the DNA-binding domain missense mutation R181Q died in the first few months of life. A patient homozygous for S217P, which only modestly reduced DNA binding, was alive at age 4.5 years.

Sansbury et al. (2015) noted that the R866X mutation is predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay. It could therefore produce a partially truncated protein with a fully preserved DNA-binding domain. They suggested that this might explain the later onset of diabetes and the milder phenotype in their patients.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) generated Rfx6-null mice. The homozygous mice were born at the expected mendelian ratio but did not feed normally. They had gross bowel distension from small bowel obstruction and died within 2 days of birth. Some had a smaller pancreas. The mutant pancreas still contained many endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin, and pancreatic polypeptide-expressing cells were increased. Neurogenin-3 expression was not affected, whereas Arx, Irx2, Pax6, Mafa, Neurod1, and Pdx1 expression was reduced. Rfx6 formed a heterodimeric DNA-binding complex with Rfx3.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2 [c.380+2T>C; IVS2+2T>C]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous splice donor site mutation in intron 2 of RFX6 (IVS2+2T-C) in a patient with Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) born to first-cousin parents (proband 1). The patient died in the first few months of life. RT-PCR of autopsy pancreas RNA did not detect the properly spliced transcript or any RNA from exons 1 and 2, which the authors attributed to nonsense-mediated decay. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this mutation (c.380+2T-C) as homozygous in 2 Pakistani sibs who had been reported by Mitchell et al. (2004) and who died at 158 and 194 days.

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [c.649T>C; p.Ser217Pro]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) mutation in RFX6 in a patient with MTCHRS born to second-cousin parents (proband 2). The mutation was found by deep sequencing of captured exons and confirmed by direct sequencing. Both parents were carriers. The residue lies between the DNA-binding and dimerization domains. The mutation only modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation. The patient was alive at age 4.5 years. Sansbury et al. (2015) gave the mutation as c.649T-C and identified this patient as the one reported by Chappell et al. (2008).

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181GLN [c.542G>A; p.Arg181Gln]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous arg181-to-gln (R181Q) mutation in RFX6 in a patient with MTCHRS (proband 5) who died in the first few months of life. The mutation affects a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain and completely abolished DNA binding in electrophoretic mobility shift assays. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this patient's mutation as homozygous c.542G-A (written as p.Arg181Gly in their table) and identified the patient as the one reported by Martinovici et al. (2010).

**.0004 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, 13-BP DEL, NT776 [c.776_780+8del13]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7 of RFX6 in a patient with MTCHRS (proband 4) who died in the first few months of life. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this mutation as c.776_780+8del13 in a French boy with non-consanguineous parents who died at 2.5 months. ⚠︎

**.0005 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2 [c.672+2T>G; IVS6+2T>G]

Smith et al. (2010) studied a patient with MTCHRS (proband 3) who was alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin. The patient was compound heterozygous for 2 RFX6 mutations: loss of the splice donor site in intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G) and disruption of the acceptor site in intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G). Both were inherited from carrier parents. Sansbury et al. (2015) gave these mutations as c.672+2T-G and c.224-12A-G.

**.0006 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS1AS, A-G, -12 [c.224-12A>G; IVS1-12A>G]

For discussion of the IVS1-12A-G mutation in the RFX6 gene, found in compound heterozygous state in a patient with MTCHRS by Smith et al. (2010), see RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2.

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.Arg726Ter]

Sansbury et al. (2015) studied 2 Turkish double first cousins with MTCHRS whose diabetes began at ages 3 and 6 years. Targeted next-generation sequencing, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, showed that both were compound heterozygous for 2 nonsense mutations in RFX6. One was a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution. The other was a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution. The fathers carried R726X, the mothers carried R866X, and both mutations had been inherited from the grandmothers. R726X is predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay. R866X lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary and is predicted to escape it.

**.0008 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG866TER [c.2596C>T; p.Arg866Ter]

For discussion of the arg866-to-ter (R866X) mutation in the RFX6 gene, found in compound heterozygous state in 2 cousins with MTCHRS by Sansbury et al. (2015), see RFX6, ARG726TER.
