### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that spondylometaphyseal dysplasia of the Sedaghatian type (SMDS) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the GPX4 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia is a neonatal lethal form of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. Its features are severe metaphyseal chondrodysplasia with mild limb shortening, platyspondyly, cardiac conduction defects, and central nervous system abnormalities (summary by Smith et al., 2014).

### Clinical Features

Sedaghatian (1980) reported 2 brothers, born within a year of each other, who had severe congenital metaphyseal involvement, mild rhizomelic shortness of the upper limbs, and mild platyspondyly. Both died at 3 days of age of cardiorespiratory insufficiency. Only one had antemortem laboratory tests. These showed low calcium, high phosphorus, and high alkaline phosphatase, which were attributed to a renal defect. At autopsy, the same infant had pulmonary, renal, and adrenal hemorrhage, as well as subendocardial myocarditis and myocardial necrosis. The parents were normal and had recently had an affected daughter.

Opitz et al. (1987) described a second Iranian family. The affected infant died on day 4, and histology showed severe epimetaphyseal dysplasia. The authors noted that one of Sedaghatian's patients also had microphthalmia, asymmetric ears, a depressed nasal bridge, a broad nose, a short neck, a prominent sternum, and short lower extremities. They suggested that the syndrome may be more complexly pleiotropic than the radiographs indicate.

Peeden et al. (1992) reported another infant with a lethal course. They summarized the earlier cases as showing subacute myocarditis, renal cortical necrosis, adrenal and pulmonary hemorrhage, mild rhizomelic limb shortness, platyspondyly, and 'laciness' of the iliac wings.

Elcioglu and Hall (1998) described the radiologic findings in 2 unrelated cases. From these cases and a review of the literature, they identified additional anomalies: disproportionately long fibulae, dysharmonious maturation, and turricephaly.

Koutouby et al. (2000) reported an infant with SMDS born to nonconsanguineous Yemeni parents. All previously reported patients had died within 4 days of life, but this patient survived 161 days.

English et al. (2006) reported a female infant with SMDS born to nonconsanguineous Caucasian parents. She was hypotonic from birth and had recurrent cyanotic episodes within a few hours of delivery. Cerebral imaging showed absence of the corpus callosum and marked frontotemporal pachygyria. She developed seizures on day 14 of life and died at 17 days of age. The authors suggested that neuronal migration abnormalities can occur in SMDS and proposed a novel association with agenesis of the corpus callosum.

Aygun et al. (2012) reported a patient with SMDS who had metaphyseal dysplasia, congenital atrioventricular block, a simplified gyral pattern, hypogenesis of the corpus callosum, and severe cerebellar hypoplasia. They suggested that central nervous system malformations might be a major component of the disorder.

### Inheritance

Sedaghatian (1980) presumed autosomal recessive inheritance because the parents were normal and had affected children of both sexes. Opitz et al. (1987) reported an affected infant born to first-cousin parents. They also noted that all known cases at that time were Iranian, and suggested that SMDS is an autosomal recessive trait with a high gene frequency in Iranians. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Koutouby et al. (2000) sent genomic DNA from their patient and the patient's parents for analysis, but no abnormal gene was detected.

Smith et al. (2014) performed whole-exome sequencing in a girl with SMDS and her unaffected parents. The girl was compound heterozygous for 2 rare variants in the GPX4 gene (NM_001039848.1), both predicted to affect splicing. The first, c.587+5G-A, was inherited from the mother. The second, c.588-8_588-4del, had arisen de novo. In vitro studies showed that the first mutation caused part of exon 4 to be spliced out and the second caused skipping of exon 5. Both led to a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4. In a second, unrelated family, DNA from the affected child was not available. Sanger sequencing showed that both unaffected parents carried the same heterozygous stop mutation in exon 3 of GPX4, c.381C-A (Y127X).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPONDYLOMETAPHYSEAL DYSPLASIA, SEDAGHATIAN TYPE**  
GPX4, 587+5G-A [NM_001039848.1:c.587+5G>A]

Smith et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to study a girl with SMDS. They found a c.587+5G-A mutation in the GPX4 gene (NM_001039848.1), inherited from her mother. The girl was compound heterozygous for this mutation and a de novo 5-bp deletion (c.588-8_588-4del). In vitro studies showed that the c.587+5G-A mutation caused part of exon 4 to be spliced out, leading to a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4.

**.0002 SPONDYLOMETAPHYSEAL DYSPLASIA, SEDAGHATIAN TYPE**  
GPX4, 5-BP DEL, 588-8 [NM_001039848.1:c.588-8_588-4del]

Smith et al. (2014) found a de novo c.588-8_588-4del mutation in the GPX4 gene (NM_001039848.1) in a girl with SMDS. She was compound heterozygous for this mutation and a maternally inherited splice site mutation (c.587+5G-A). In vitro studies showed that the deletion caused skipping of exon 5, leading to a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4.

**.0003 SPONDYLOMETAPHYSEAL DYSPLASIA, SEDAGHATIAN TYPE**  
GPX4, TYR127TER [NM_001039848.1:c.381C>A; p.Tyr127Ter]

In a family with a child affected by SMDS, Smith et al. (2014) used Sanger sequencing to show that both unaffected parents carried the same heterozygous c.381C-A mutation in exon 3 of the GPX4 gene (NM_001039848.1). This mutation results in a tyr127-to-ter (Y127X) substitution. DNA from the affected child was not available.
