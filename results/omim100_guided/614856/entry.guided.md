### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene on chromosome 8p21.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is an autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder with recurrent fractures. Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) and Cho et al. (2015) described a severe form with bone deformities, umbilical hernia, short stature and delayed motor development. Bone mineral density has been reported as decreased (Martínez-Glez et al., 2012), normal (Valencia et al., 2014) or increased (Asharani et al., 2012; Fahiminiya et al., 2015). Fahiminiya et al. (2015) described a form with mainly lower extremity fractures and a mineralization defect in bone tissue.

### Clinical Features

Munns et al. (2004) described 3 unrelated children with an apparently new bone fragility disorder associated with an idiopathic mineralization defect. Recurrent lower limb fractures began with weight bearing. The patients had none of the phenotypic, radiologic or histomorphometric features typical of osteogenesis imperfecta, idiopathic juvenile osteoporosis or mild autosomal dominant osteopetrosis. Radiographs showed increased metaphyseal trabeculation and normal to increased cortical thickness, with no evidence of rickets or osteomalacia. Areal and volumetric bone mineral density of the lumbar spine showed no major change. Iliac bone biopsies showed a paucity of the birefringent pattern of normal lamellar bone. Histomorphometry showed osteomalacia, with a prolonged mineralization lag time and a decreased mineral apposition rate. Calcium and phosphate metabolism was normal, and type I collagen mutation analysis was negative.

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) studied 2 sibs born to Egyptian parents who were first cousins. The 15-year-old female proband had a tibial fracture at birth and then had 10 to 15 spontaneous fractures per year. She had a triangular face, broad forehead, wide palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, faint blue sclerae, long philtrum, thin lips and prominent ears. She had a large umbilical hernia, generalized hypotonia, muscle wasting and hirsutism. Gross motor development was delayed, and she could not stand unsupported. Mental development was normal, and dentinogenesis imperfecta was absent. She had severe generalized bone deformities, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible elbow, wrist and interphalangeal joints, kyphoscoliosis and pectus carinatum. Radiographs showed multiple fractures, lack of bone modeling, serpentine thin tibiae and fibulae, platyspondyly, wormian bones and generally decreased bone density. At 13 years, weight was -3.8 SD, length -11.5 SD and head circumference -3.0 SD. Her 5-year-old brother had femoral bowing detected on prenatal ultrasound and fractures of both femora at birth, followed by about 15 fractures per year. His findings were similar to his sister's, including a large umbilical hernia. He also had a wide symphysis pubis, with length -7.2 SD and a normal head circumference. In both sibs, serum calcium and phosphate were normal and alkaline phosphatase was slightly high. Both were diagnosed with OI Sillence type III, one of the groups defined by Sillence et al. (1979).

Asharani et al. (2012) described a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures.

Valencia et al. (2014) reported an additional patient with osteogenesis imperfecta and normal bone density.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) studied 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder mainly involving lower extremity fractures, with a mineralization defect in bone tissue but not at the growth plate. Peripheral quantitative computed tomography showed elevated volumetric cortical bone mineral density in all 4. Iliac bone histomorphometry and quantitative backscattered electron imaging showed delayed onset of mineralization at bone formation sites, but the mineralized matrix was hypermineralized.

Cho et al. (2015) reported a Korean patient with OI who had an umbilical hernia, frequent fractures, markedly short stature, delayed motor development, scoliosis, and dislocation of the radial head with a bowed radius and ulna.

### Inheritance

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) established autosomal recessive inheritance of OI13. In their consanguineous family, the affected sibs were homozygous for a BMP1 mutation, and the unaffected parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous.

### Mapping

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) performed homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Egyptian family with OI. The affected sibs were not homozygous at the CRTAP, PPIB, SERPINH1, SP7 and SERPINF1 loci, and only 1 of them was homozygous at LEPRE1 and FKBP10. Both affected sibs, but not 2 unaffected sibs, shared homozygous regions on chromosome 3 (35.4 Mb) and chromosome 8p (10.27 Mb). The chromosome 8p region contained the BMP1 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 Egyptian sibs with OI13, born to consanguineous parents, Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) found a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in exon 6 of the BMP1 gene, causing a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution. The mutation was found by sequencing BMP1 within a region of shared homozygosity and segregated with the disease. It was absent from 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin and 884 European control chromosomes. Phe249 lies in the protease domain and is conserved in all members of the astacin family of metalloproteases. Patient fibroblasts showed impaired C-terminal processing of procollagen type I, with less pN-alpha-1(I) and free C-propeptide, although some residual activity remained. Overexpressing wildtype mTLD (the longer BMP1 isoform) in NIH3T3 cells and human fibroblasts increased C-propeptide cleavage, but overexpressing the mutant did not.

In a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures, Asharani et al. (2012) used whole-exome sequencing with filtering for homozygous stretches and found a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene. The mutation lies in the BMP1 signal peptide. It caused impaired secretion and altered posttranslational modification.

In a patient with OI and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) found the homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene, which had been reported before. Patient dermal fibroblasts had decreased levels of both alternatively spliced BMP1 products and abnormal cleavage of the procollagen type I C-terminal propeptide. Fluorescence and electron microscopy showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in cultured fibroblasts from this patient and from a previously reported patient homozygous for the c.747C-G mutation. The authors concluded that BMP1 is essential for human type I collagen fibrillogenesis.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder involving mainly lower extremity fractures and a bone mineralization defect. Three were homozygous for a c.*241T-C change in BMP1, affecting the polyadenylation signal of the transcript for the short isoform, BMP1-1. Their skin fibroblasts had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein. The fourth child was compound heterozygous for c.*241T-C and a c.2107G-C change in exon 15, which affected splicing of both BMP1-1 and the long isoform, BMP1-3. In both genotypes, skin fibroblast supernatants contained less free procollagen type I C-propeptide than controls. The authors concluded that isolated lack of BMP1-1 causes bone fragility in children.

In a Korean patient with OI13, Cho et al. (2015) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous variants in the BMP1 gene. One was a c.808A-G change causing a met270-to-val (M270V) substitution. The other was a c.1297G-T change causing skipping of exon 10. A zebrafish assay showed that both variants were hypofunctional.

### Animal Model

Asharani et al. (2012) characterized a zebrafish bone mutant with lesions in bmp1a, showing that BMP1 function in osteogenesis is conserved across species. Genetic, biochemical and histologic analyses showed that Bmp1a is critically required to generate mature collagen in bone, downstream of osteoblast maturation.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**  
BMP1, PHE249LEU [NM_006129.4:c.747C>G; p.Phe249Leu]

In 2 sibs with severe OI13 born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) found a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in exon 6 of the BMP1 gene, causing a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution. The residue lies in the protease domain and is conserved across the astacin family of metalloproteases. The parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous. The mutation was absent from 100 ethnically matched and 884 European control chromosomes. Patient fibroblasts showed deficient processing of the procollagen type I C-propeptide. Overexpressed mutant mTLD did not increase C-propeptide cleavage, whereas wildtype mTLD did.

Valencia et al. (2014) found impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in cultured fibroblasts from a previously reported patient homozygous for the c.747C-G mutation.

**.0002 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**  
BMP1, 34G-C [c.34G>C]

In a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures, Asharani et al. (2012) found a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the signal peptide of BMP1, identified by whole-exome sequencing. The mutation led to impaired secretion and altered posttranslational modification.

Valencia et al. (2014) found the homozygous c.34G-C mutation in a patient with OI and normal bone density. Patient fibroblasts had decreased protein levels of both BMP1 splice products, abnormal cleavage of the procollagen type I C-terminal propeptide, and impaired assembly of collagen fibrils.

**.0003 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**  
BMP1, *241T-C [c.*241T>C]

In 3 unrelated children with bone fragility, mainly lower extremity fractures and a bone mineralization defect, Fahiminiya et al. (2015) used whole-exome sequencing to find a homozygous c.*241T-C change in the polyadenylation signal of the BMP1 transcript coding for the short isoform, BMP1-1. Skin fibroblasts had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein and less free procollagen type I C-propeptide than controls. A fourth child was compound heterozygous for this variant and c.2107G-C.

**.0004 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**

One was a c.2107G-C change in exon 15 that affected splicing of both the BMP1-1 and BMP1-3 isoforms. The other was the c.*241T-C polyadenylation signal variant.
