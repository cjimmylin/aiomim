### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder caused by deficiency of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4). It begins with infantile hypotonia that progresses to spasticity, either spastic paraplegia or spastic tetraplegia. Other features are microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, inability to walk, stereotypic laughter, drooling, and growth retardation. Some patients have seizures. Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) first described the disorder as an autosomal recessive form of spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy. Because disruption of different AP-4 subunits produces similar phenotypes, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), Abou Jamra et al. (2011), and Kong et al. (2013) regarded it as part of a clinically recognizable 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome'. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with a neurologic condition that had been described as cerebral palsy. The female proband was born at term after an uneventful labor and delivery. She had microcephaly and hypotonia at birth, which progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia by the first year of life. She never developed speech or independent walking. From 5 years of age she was wheelchair-bound and had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Examination at age 23 showed microcephaly (-3 SD), profoundly impaired cognition, adaptive skills at age equivalents below 1 year, drooling, and outbursts of stereotypic laughter. Dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face with a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum with an everted full upper vermilion, prominent antehelix of the ears, and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss, most pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum. EEG showed generalized theta slowing. Her brother had microcephaly and hypotonia after birth, and developmental delay was evident by the first year of life. He acquired a few dysarthric words and could walk with support, but increasing spasticity made him wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years. His seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. At age 22 he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia and hyperreflexia, stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, and severe cognitive deficits. Head CT showed moderate ventriculomegaly. Both sibs had a Babinski sign, no sphincter control, and normal eye and hearing evaluations. The authors noted that the clinical features closely resembled those of a family with an AP4M1 mutation.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families with mutations in 3 different AP-4 subunit genes, one of which was AP4E1. Features included severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, and muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia. Patients also had microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. The pregnancy was uncomplicated, but neonatal asphyxiation was noted at delivery. At age 3 years both twins had marked developmental retardation with microcephaly (head circumference -2 SD and -2.5 SD), inability to walk unaided, abnormal speech, and abnormal circadian development. Other features were smiling or laughing for no obvious reason, drooling, muscular hypotonia, short stature, and low body weight. Neurologic examination showed spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities without hyperreflexia or Babinski sign. Neither twin had seizures, and sphincter control was absent. Both had a prominent, bulbous nose, a wide mouth, and coarse features. EEG showed diffuse slow waves, and CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy. One twin had conductive hearing loss on the left side. The twins were diagnosed with type I complex hereditary spastic paraplegia. Both had also been vaccinated with BCG a few days after birth. At 9 months of age each developed unilaterally enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph nodes containing acid-fast bacilli. No abnormality of the IL-12/IFN-gamma axis or of the oxidative burst was found.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 is autosomal recessive. Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) found affected sibs in a consanguineous family who were homozygous for an AP4E1-containing deletion, and their unaffected mother was a heterozygous carrier. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) and Kong et al. (2013) also reported consanguineous families with homozygous AP4E1 mutations. In the family reported by Kong et al. (2013), both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. ⚠︎

### Mapping

By chromosomal microarray analysis, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) found a homozygous deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 in 2 affected sibs. The deletion included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes.

### Heterogeneity

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that a homozygous mutation in AP4M1, another AP-4 subunit gene, had been reported in a family with a very similar phenotype. Abou Jamra et al. (2011) found mutations in AP4S1 and AP4B1, as well as in AP4E1, in families with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia. These findings indicated that disruption of any of the AP-4 subunits can cause a similar disorder.

### Molecular Genetics

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) performed chromosomal microarray analysis in 2 sibs with spastic tetraplegia, intellectual disability, microcephaly, and epilepsy from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. Both sibs were homozygous for a 192-kb deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 that included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The deletion was confirmed by FISH. The mother was a heterozygous carrier, and methylation analysis indicated that the father was also a carrier. The authors could not completely exclude a contribution from SPPL2A deletion, but they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main cause of the phenotype. They also proposed that disruption of any of the 4 AP-4 subunits abolishes the function of the complex.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families using autozygosity mapping combined with either candidate gene sequencing or exome sequencing. In one family they found a homozygous splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The mutation caused deletion of r.421_542 and was predicted to result in a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). In the other 2 families they found mutations in the AP4S1 and AP4B1 genes. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2013) performed whole-exome sequencing in Moroccan identical twins with complex spastic paraplegia and BCG disease. They found a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation and a healthy sib was wildtype. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient cells, AP4E1 mRNA levels and splicing were normal. However, the AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of the AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta subunits were also reduced. Native immunoprecipitation showed that levels of assembled AP-4 complex were more than 95% lower in patient fibroblasts and EBV-transformed B cells than in controls. The AP-4 binding partner tepsin was not detectable in patient fibroblasts. Kong et al. (2013) found no defects in known genes for mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) and proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the twins' mycobacterial disease.

Kong et al. (2018) later restudied the same Moroccan twins, who had both hereditary spastic paraplegia due to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation and MSMD. Using whole-exome sequencing and genome-wide linkage analysis, they found that the twins were also homozygous for a splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene (c.733+1G-A). This mutation caused skipping of exon 6. An unrelated Turkish boy with BCG disease was homozygous for a different SPPL2A splice site mutation. Kong et al. (2018) attributed the mycobacterial disease to autosomal recessive SPPL2A deficiency, which led to accumulation of the CD74 N-terminal fragment, depletion of CD1c+ conventional dendritic cells, and impaired IFN-gamma production by mycobacterium-specific memory TH1* cells.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL [chr15:48835480-49028171 del (hg18)]

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) found a homozygous 192-kb deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18) in 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. The sibs had spastic tetraplegia, profound intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, and white matter loss. The deletion removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. It was identified by chromosomal microarray analysis and confirmed by FISH. The mother was a heterozygous carrier, and the father was inferred to be a carrier.

**.0002 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 4-BP DEL, NT542+1 [NM_007347.3:c.542+1_542+4delGTAA; r.421_542del; p.Glu181GlyfsTer20]

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) studied a consanguineous family with severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, progressive spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, and growth retardation. Affected members were homozygous for a 4-bp deletion at the splice donor site of the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The deletion resulted in loss of r.421_542 and a predicted frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). The mutation was identified by autozygosity mapping combined with sequencing. ⚠︎

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [NP_001239056.1:p.Arg1105Ter; p.R1105X]

Kong et al. (2013) found a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene in identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. The twins had intellectual disability, spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, and short stature. The mutation was identified by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It segregated with the disorder in the family and was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient cells, mRNA levels were normal, but AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable and assembled AP-4 complex was reduced by more than 95%. The twins also had BCG disease. Kong et al. (2018) later found that they were also homozygous for a splice site mutation in SPPL2A and attributed the mycobacterial disease to SPPL2A deficiency.
