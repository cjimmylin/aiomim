### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder. Early infantile hypotonia progresses to spastic paraplegia or tetraplegia with hyperreflexia, and is accompanied by severe intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, inability to walk, microcephaly, stereotypic laughter, and drooling. Some patients have seizures, short stature, and dysmorphic facial features. Brain imaging can show ventriculomegaly, white matter loss, and cerebellar and cortical atrophy (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) described the disorder as an autosomal recessive form of spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy. Because disruption of any of the 4 subunits of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4) appeared to produce the same phenotype, they proposed the term 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.' Kong et al. (2013) classified the disorder as type I complex hereditary spastic paraplegia.

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs, a woman aged 23 years and a man aged 22 years, from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. Both had microcephaly and hypotonia from birth, which progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia, hyperreflexia, and Babinski signs. Both had severe to profound intellectual disability, absent normal speech, no sphincter control, drooling, outbursts of stereotypic laughter, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. The woman never walked independently and had used a wheelchair since age 5 years. Her brother walked with support but needed a wheelchair by age 9 years, said only some dysarthric words, and had jerky nystagmus. Head circumference was -3 SD in both. Dysmorphic features included bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face with a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum with an everted full upper vermilion, a prominent antehelix, and facial hypotonia. Brain MRI in the woman showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss that was most marked in the frontal region and corpus callosum. Head CT in her brother showed moderate ventriculomegaly. EEG showed generalized theta slowing of the background.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families. Their features were severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation.

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents. Neonatal asphyxia was noted at delivery. At age 3 years, both had marked developmental delay, microcephaly (head circumference -2 SD and -2.5 SD), inability to walk unaided, absent normal speech, muscular hypotonia, and spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities. Both also had stereotypic laughter, drooling, short stature, and low body weight. Neither had seizures, hyperreflexia, or a Babinski sign. Facial features included a prominent bulbous nose, a wide mouth, and coarse features. EEG showed diffuse slow waves, and CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy. At 9 months of age, both twins developed axillary lymphadenitis due to BCG vaccination.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance was established by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), who found a homozygous deletion in affected sibs from a consanguineous family. Their mother was a heterozygous carrier, and their father was inferred to be one. Kong et al. (2013) supported this pattern in a consanguineous family in which the affected twins were homozygous for an AP4E1 mutation and both parents were heterozygous.

### Mapping

Using chromosomal microarray analysis, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) found a homozygous deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 in 2 affected sibs.

### Molecular Genetics

Using chromosomal microarray analysis, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 in 2 sibs with SPG51 from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. The deletion included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes, and FISH confirmed it. The mother was a heterozygous carrier. A paternal sample was not available, but SNRPN methylation analysis showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, so the father was inferred to be a carrier. The authors could not rule out a contribution from SPPL2A deletion. However, they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main cause, based on the similar phenotype reported in patients with mutation in another AP-4 subunit gene.

In 3 consanguineous families with an AP-4 deficiency phenotype, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) used autozygosity mapping combined with Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. In each family they found a mutation in a different AP-4 subunit gene. In AP4E1 the mutation was a splice-site deletion (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA) that caused deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20).

Using whole-exome sequencing and Sanger confirmation, Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X) in identical Moroccan twins with SPG51. The mutation segregated with the disorder in the family. It was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells the mutation did not affect AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing. However, the AP4E1 protein was barely detectable, and levels of the AP4M1 and AP4B1 subunits were also reduced. Native immunoprecipitation showed that levels of assembled AP-4 complex in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells were more than 95% lower than in controls. The twins also had BCG lymphadenitis. Kong et al. (2013) found no defect of the IL-12/IFN-gamma axis or oxidative burst, and proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease.

Kong et al. (2018) later studied the same twins with combined whole-exome sequencing and genome-wide linkage analysis. They found that the twins were also homozygous for a splice-site mutation (c.733+1G-A) in the SPPL2A gene, which caused skipping of exon 6. They identified autosomal recessive SPPL2A deficiency as a cause of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease, and attributed the twins' spastic paraplegia to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.

In affected members of a consanguineous family with severe intellectual disability, progressive spastic paraplegia, shy character, and short stature, Abou Jamra et al. It was found by autozygosity mapping combined with sequencing.

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105X]

In identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X), found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was homozygous wildtype. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient cells, AP4E1 mRNA levels were normal, but AP4E1 protein was barely detectable. Levels of the AP4M1 and AP4B1 subunits were reduced, and levels of assembled AP-4 complex were more than 95% lower than in controls. Kong et al. (2018) found that the twins were also homozygous for an SPPL2A splice mutation, which they linked to the twins' mycobacterial disease.
