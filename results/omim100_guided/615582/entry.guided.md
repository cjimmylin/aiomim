### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also called Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene on chromosome 14q24. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5) is a connective tissue disorder caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene. Its systemic features overlap with those of Loeys-Dietz, Shprintzen-Goldberg, and Marfan syndromes. They include cleft palate or bifid uvula, hypertelorism, skeletal overgrowth or growth retardation, arachnodactyly, joint hyperextensibility, cervical spine instability, and clubfoot (Rienhoff et al., 2013; Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015; Kuechler et al., 2015). Cardiovascular involvement is highly variable. It includes thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysm and dissection and mitral valve disease (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Kuechler et al. (2015) tentatively called the disorder associated with TGFB3 mutations 'Rienhoff syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) described a 9-year-old European American girl born to nonconsanguineous parents. At birth she had contractures of the right hand, most severe in the third and fourth fingers, as well as contractures of all toes, together with a midline facial nevus flammeus and mild hypotonia. Her birth weight was 2.9 kg (5th centile). At 3 months she was evaluated for failure to thrive and delayed gross motor function. At 17 months she weighed 7.5 kg (less than the 1st centile). Findings at that age included pes planus, mild pectus excavatum, hyperextensibility of multiple large joints, mild retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, bifid uvula with an intact hard palate, a small metopic ridge, blue sclerae, decreased bulk of all appendicular and axial muscles, low tone, diminished reflexes, and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. She stood independently with a positive Gowers sign at 20 months and walked with a 'hip waddle' at 24 months. Her neurocognitive development was appropriate for age. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at age 7 years showed type 1 fiber predominance with mild, focal type 1 fiber disproportion, consistent with disuse, and no dystrophic or inflammatory changes. Yearly echocardiograms from 18 months showed no cardiac defect, and her aortic root dimension at age 6.5 years was normal (z score 0.39). The authors noted that she did not meet diagnostic criteria for Marfan, Beals-Hecht, or Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) and Kuechler et al. (2015) later noted that this child had tall stature and was 10.5 years old, and Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that the child had cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with autosomal dominant features overlapping Marfan syndrome and LDS. Seven family members aged 40 to 68 years had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three of them died of aortic dissection and rupture. Four family members had mitral valve abnormalities ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation. Craniofacial features were subtle and included a long face, high-arched palate, and retrognathia. Pectus deformity and scoliosis were frequent. Other recurrent findings were velvety skin, varices, and hiatal hernia. Autoimmune features included HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease, and celiac disease. Pathology reports from 2 deceased patients described extensive elastic fiber fragmentation and aortic medial degeneration.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized 43 patients from 11 families with TGFB3 mutations. Vascular involvement ranged from no cardiovascular abnormalities at age 64 to type A aortic dissection (median age 51 years, range 40 to 80) or type B aortic dissection (median age 44.5 years, range 30 to 57), abdominal aortic dissection, and death from cerebral aneurysm dissection at age 55. No early arterial dissection, dissection at small aortic dimension, or striking arterial tortuosity was observed. Other cardiovascular findings included mitral valve disease, persistent foramen ovale, and atrial or ventricular septal defects. Common LDS features were hypertelorism (14 patients), bifid uvula (11), cleft palate (5), cervical spine instability, and clubfoot. Other recurrent features were dolichocephaly, high-arched palate, retrognathia, tall stature (12), joint hypermobility (9), arachnodactyly (16), pectus deformity (8), kyphoscoliosis (11), and inguinal hernia. Early-onset osteoarthritis was reported in 2 individuals. One patient with the D263H mutation had short stature. The authors noted striking intrafamilial and interfamilial clinical variability.

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a family with features overlapping Marfan syndrome and LDS. Two affected children and their affected father had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula. None had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation, or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

The TGFB3 mutations in the patients reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013) and Matyas et al. (2014) occurred de novo. Autosomal dominant inheritance was shown by segregation of heterozygous TGFB3 mutations with the disorder in multigenerational families (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015) and in a father and his 2 children (Kuechler et al., 2015).

### Mapping

In a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) carried out genomewide linkage analysis using SNP genotypes from 6 family members. They identified 2 large genomic regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15, shared by all affected members. The chromosome 14 region included the TGFB3 gene on 14q24.

### Molecular Genetics

In the girl with LDS5 reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013), exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (C409Y), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The proband also carried a de novo nonsense mutation in CDH2 (Q70X). N-cadherin levels in her fibroblasts did not differ significantly from controls, and the authors concluded that the CDH2 mutation was unlikely to drive the phenotype. In transfected cells the mutant TGFB3 ligand did not activate TGF-beta signaling. In Xenopus embryos it had a dominant-negative effect on SMAD2 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation but did not affect BMP signaling. Earlier analysis of TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1, and FBN2 had identified no mutation.

Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide (LAP) domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Kuechler et al. (2015) gave this mutation as R300Q.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms in which sequencing of known thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (TAAD) genes was negative. Linkage analysis pointed to TGFB3, and sequencing identified a heterozygous splice site mutation (c.754+2T-C) that segregated with the phenotype. Screening of TGFB3 in 470 additional TAAD probands, by Sanger sequencing (350) or whole-exome sequencing (120), identified heterozygous mutations in 10 more probands. These comprised 4 missense mutations (D263H; R300W in 4 families; I322T; L401P), 1 nonsense mutation (Y365X), which occurred de novo, and 2 single-base deletions causing frameshifts (L386Rfs*21 and N235Mfs*11). All mutations were absent from EVS, 1000 Genomes, and the Genome of the Netherlands.

In a father and his 2 children with LDS5, Kuechler et al. (2015) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous missense mutation in TGFB3 (R300G). Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and its segregation with the phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that TGFB3 mutations appear to have opposing effects on height. The patients carrying C409Y (Rienhoff et al., 2013) and D263H had short stature, whereas the patient reported by Matyas et al. (2014) and several of their own patients had tall stature. They also noted that mutations affecting arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula, both in their patients and in the patient reported by Matyas et al. (2014). Kuechler et al. (2015) suggested that mutations at arg300 presumably increase TGF-beta signaling, whereas C409Y is a loss-of-function mutation. They proposed that the short or tall stature seen in patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of the mutations on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) proposed that the C409Y mutation is hypomorphic. In their view, decreased TGF-beta signaling during early myogenesis and palatogenesis explained the low muscle mass and bifid uvula in their patient, a pathophysiology distinct from the excess TGF-beta signaling in Marfan syndrome. Consistent with this, a 3-year trial of losartan produced no change in the patient's muscle strength or mass.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) proposed that the TGFB3 mutations they identified cause loss of function, citing the truncating and in-frame splice site mutations and missense changes that alter residues needed for integrin activation and processing. However, immunohistochemical analysis of dissected aortic wall from a patient with the D263H mutation showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition. It also showed paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling, with increased pSMAD2 and pERK and elevated TGFB1 mRNA. They noted that these results contrast with the decreased signaling reported for C409Y by Rienhoff et al. (2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers, together with at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.Cys409Tyr]

In a 9-year-old girl with LDS5, who had distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, low muscle mass, and growth retardation, Rienhoff et al. (2013) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation results in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. It was found by exome sequencing, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and was not present in dbSNP130, EVS, or 1000 Genomes. The penultimate cysteine is conserved in all TGF-beta family ligands and forms part of the 'cysteine knot.' Mutant ligand purified from transfected 293T cells did not activate a TGF-beta reporter. In Xenopus embryos, coexpression of mutant and wildtype TGFB3 RNA at a 1:1 ratio reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals to approximately 40% and 60%, respectively, of the signals produced by wildtype alone, but did not affect BMP-dependent SMAD1 phosphorylation.

**.0002 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLN [c.899G>A; p.Arg300Gln]

Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Kuechler et al. (2015) gave this mutation as c.899G-A, resulting in an arg300-to-gln (R300Q) substitution. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that the patient was 10.5 years old and had cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, IVS4DS, T-C, +2 [c.754+2T>C; p.Glu216_Lys251del]

In affected members of a large Dutch family with LDS5 (family 1), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.754+2T-C transition at the canonical donor splice site of exon 4 of the TGFB3 gene. Affected family members had thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysms and dissections and mitral valve disease. The mutation was identified after linkage analysis and segregated with the phenotype. It was absent from EVS, the Genome of the Netherlands, and 1000 Genomes. cDNA sequencing in 2 patients confirmed skipping of exon 4, causing an in-frame deletion of 108 nucleotides that is expected to delete 36 amino acids (Glu216_Lys251del). The mutation was also present in a 17-year-old family member without documented cardiovascular features.

**.0004 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ASP263HIS [p.Asp263His]

In a family with LDS5 (family 3), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous asp263-to-his (D263H) mutation in exon 5 of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the latency-associated peptide domain and disrupts the RGD integrin-binding motif. The proband had short stature and aortic dissection. His dissected aortic wall showed elastic fiber fragmentation and evidence of increased TGF-beta signaling, with increased pSMAD2, pERK, and TGFB1 mRNA. ⚠︎

**.0005 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300TRP [p.Arg300Trp]

In 4 families with LDS5 (families 2, 4, 5, and 11), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous arg300-to-trp (R300W) mutation in exon 5 of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation affects the last residue of the latency-associated peptide domain, disrupting the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif. It was absent from control databases. Mutations at arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

**.0006 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, TYR365TER [p.Tyr365*]

In the proband of a family with LDS5 (family 6), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a de novo heterozygous tyr365-to-ter (Y365X) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T), inherited from his mother, who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement.

**.0007 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLY [c.898C>G; p.Arg300Gly]

In a father and his 2 children with LDS5, Kuechler et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.898C-G transversion in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. All 3 had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula, but no cardiac, aortic, or eye involvement. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing, and Sanger sequencing confirmed it and its segregation with the phenotype.
