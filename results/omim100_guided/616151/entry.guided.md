### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that vitelliform macular dystrophy-4 (VMD4) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the IMPG1 gene.

### Description

Vitelliform macular dystrophies are inherited retinal dystrophies characterized by yellow, round deposits seen on fundus examination (Manes et al., 2013). Most patients with VMD4 have a normal or moderately decreased electrooculogram (EOG) Arden ratio (Manes et al., 2013). In patients with IMPG1 mutations, the vitelliform material lies above a well-preserved retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and drusen-like lesions next to the vitelliform deposits are common (Meunier et al., 2014). The disorder can be inherited in an autosomal dominant or an autosomal recessive pattern (Manes et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Manes et al. (2013) studied a large family with autosomal dominant vitelliform macular dystrophy and 2 more families with autosomal dominant VMD and the same phenotype. They also studied 2 families with autosomal recessive VMD. Most patients had a normal or moderately decreased EOG Arden ratio.

Meunier et al. (2014) described 11 patients from 3 families with IMPG1-related VMD and 2 patients from 1 family with IMPG2-related VMD. In this combined group of 13 patients, onset was late (mean age of onset, 42 years) and visual impairment was moderate (mean visual acuity, 20/40). In family 3, a sporadic case with presumed recessive transmission, onset was at age 38 years and mean visual acuity was 20/50. Nine of the 13 patients had drusen-like lesions adjacent to the vitelliform deposits. At every stage of the dystrophy, spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) showed the vitelliform material above the RPE. The RPE was well preserved and kept its normal reflectivity. EOG results were normal or borderline in 9 cases.

Brandl et al. (2017) reported 2 simplex patients with IMPG1-related VMD whose disease began in early childhood. The first patient was a boy from a German family. At age 13 years, his visual acuity was reduced to 0.2 logMAR in the right eye and 0.1 logMAR in the left eye. The fundus of both eyes showed central RPE abnormalities with foveal detachment. The second patient was a boy from a consanguineous Turkish family who was diagnosed with VMD at age 2 years. Over 8 years of follow-up, his visual acuity fluctuated around 0.2 logMAR in both eyes. At age 10 years, his multifocal ERG was normal and his EOG Arden ratio was borderline to slightly abnormally low. Color fundus images showed a single vitelliform lesion in the central macula of each eye, without satellite drusen. SD-OCT showed a bilateral, central, dome-shaped foveal detachment of the neurosensory retina. The hyperreflective material beneath the detachment was reabsorbed over time, leaving an optically empty cavity. Photoreceptor outer segments were elongated, and Bruch membrane and the RPE appeared well preserved. Fundus autofluorescence showed circular hyperfluorescence that decreased over the course of the disease.

Gupta et al. (2021) reported a 25-year-old woman with a symptomatic scotoma, decreased vision, and small yellow spots in both foveae. Optical coherence tomography showed disorganization and decreased reflectance of the foveal ellipsoid and interdigitation zones, more severe in the left eye than in the right. Fundus autofluorescence imaging showed minimal findings, and dye angiography was unrevealing. Multifocal electroretinogram showed slightly decreased retinal sensitivity in the central retina of the left eye.

### Inheritance

Manes et al. (2013) showed that VMD4 can be inherited in an autosomal dominant or an autosomal recessive pattern. Brandl et al. (2017) reported homozygous IMPG1 mutation in a patient from a consanguineous family; his heterozygous parents were clinically asymptomatic.

### Mapping

Manes et al. (2013) used gene mapping in a large family with autosomal dominant VMD, followed by whole-exome sequencing, to identify the causative mutation in the IMPG1 gene.

### Heterogeneity

Meunier et al. (2014) studied families with VMD in whom no mutation, small deletion, or large rearrangement had been found in BEST1 or PRPH2. IMPG1 and IMPG2 together were the causal genes in 8% of these families. Brandl et al. (2017) found heterozygous IMPG2 mutations in 8 patients with VMD from a cohort that was negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations. They observed that clinical symptoms tended to be more severe in patients with IMPG1 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large family with autosomal dominant VMD4, Manes et al. (2013) used gene mapping and whole-exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.713T-G mutation in the IMPG1 gene, resulting in a leu238-to-arg (L238R) substitution. They subsequently found the same mutation in 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD and the same phenotype. Structural modeling indicated that the L238R substitution destabilizes the conserved SEA1 domain of the SPACR protein, which IMPG1 encodes. Manes et al. (2013) then screened 144 probands with various forms of macular dystrophy and found 3 more IMPG1 mutations. In 1 family with autosomal recessive VMD, 2 affected individuals were homozygous for a splice site mutation (c.807+1G-T). In another family, 2 affected individuals were compound heterozygous for a c.461T-C mutation, resulting in a leu154-to-pro (L154P) substitution, and a c.1519C-T mutation, resulting in an arg507-to-ter (R507X) substitution.

Meunier et al. (2014) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 49 families with VMD who did not have a mutation in BEST1 or PRPH2. They found that IMPG1 was the causal gene in 3 families with a total of 11 patients. Families 1 and 2 showed autosomal dominant transmission and carried the c.713T-G (L238R) mutation in IMPG1. Family 3 was a sporadic case with presumed recessive transmission.

Brandl et al. (2017) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 106 unrelated patients with VMD who were negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations. In 2 simplex patients with early childhood onset, they identified 2 novel IMPG1 mutations. A German boy carried a heterozygous c.713T-C mutation in exon 7, resulting in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution in the SEA I domain. Data on his parents were not available. A boy from a consanguineous Turkish family was homozygous for a splice site mutation in intron 7 (c.807+5G-A), and segregation analysis showed that both parents were heterozygous. In an in vitro splicing assay in HEK293 cells, the c.807+5G-A mutation caused skipping of exon 7 in about half of the transcripts.

In a 25-year-old woman with early-onset vitelliform dystrophy, Gupta et al. (2021) found a heterozygous L154P mutation in the IMPG1 gene. They proposed that this mutation may cause symptomatic outer retinal disturbance in the heterozygous state.

Olivier et al. (2021) identified a splice variant in IMPG1 by exome sequencing in a family with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. They then found the same variant in individuals from 2 families with either retinitis pigmentosa or VMD. A retrospective study of patients with retinitis pigmentosa or VMD identified 8 more families with different missense or nonsense variants in IMPG1.

### Diagnosis

Meunier et al. (2014) recommended screening IMPG1 and IMPG2 in adult-onset vitelliform dystrophy when the following features are present: moderate visual impairment; drusen-like lesions; normal reflectivity of the RPE line on SD-OCT; and vitelliform deposits located between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines on SD-OCT. They noted that these features are not seen in the classic forms of BEST1- or PRPH2-related vitelliform dystrophy.

**.0003 MACULAR DYSTROPHY, VITELLIFORM, 4**

In 2 affected members of a family with autosomal recessive VMD4, Manes et al. (2013) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the IMPG1 gene. One allele carried a c.461T-C transition, resulting in a leu154-to-pro (L154P) substitution. The other allele carried a c.1519C-T transition, resulting in an arg507-to-ter (R507X) substitution.

In a 25-year-old woman with early-onset vitelliform dystrophy, symptomatic scotoma, and decreased vision, Gupta et al. (2021) identified a heterozygous L154P mutation in the IMPG1 gene. They suggested that the mutation may cause symptomatic outer retinal disturbance in the heterozygous state.

**.0004 MACULAR DYSTROPHY, VITELLIFORM, 4**  
IMPG1, ARG507TER [c.1519C>T; p.Arg507Ter]

In 2 affected members of a family with autosomal recessive VMD4, Manes et al. (2013) identified compound heterozygosity for a c.1519C-T transition in the IMPG1 gene, resulting in an arg507-to-ter (R507X) substitution, and the L154P mutation.

**.0005 MACULAR DYSTROPHY, VITELLIFORM, 4**  
IMPG1, LEU238PRO [c.713T>C; p.Leu238Pro]

In a German boy with simplex VMD4 that began in early childhood, Brandl et al. (2017) identified a heterozygous c.713T-C transition in exon 7 of the IMPG1 gene, resulting in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution in the SEA I domain. All 3 in silico algorithms used predicted the variant to be pathogenic. Data on his parents were not available. Brandl et al. (2017) noted that the L238R mutation affects the same residue.

**.0006 MACULAR DYSTROPHY, VITELLIFORM, 4**  
IMPG1, IVS7DS, G-A, +5 [c.807+5G>A]

In a boy from a consanguineous Turkish family who was diagnosed with VMD4 at age 2 years, Brandl et al. (2017) identified a homozygous c.807+5G-A mutation in intron 7 of the IMPG1 gene. The mutation was predicted to weaken the donor splice site of exon 7. Segregation analysis confirmed that both parents were heterozygous, and they were clinically asymptomatic. In a minigene assay in HEK293 cells, exon 7 was skipped in about half of the transcripts carrying the mutation.
