### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775). ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is a RASopathy with facial features resembling Noonan syndrome together with distinctive ectodermal abnormalities. Features include short stature, often with growth hormone (GH) deficiency, macrocephaly, cognitive deficits, hyperactive behavior, and cardiac anomalies, notably mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects. The hair is easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow growing (loose anagen hair). The skin is often darkly pigmented, with eczema or ichthyosis, and the voice is hypernasal (Mazzanti et al., 2003; Cordeddu et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed the name 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair' for the disorder they described. Motta et al. (2019) used the term 'Mazzanti syndrome' for the RASopathy caused by the recurrent SHOC2 S2G mutation.

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. She had short, blond, easily pluckable hair that had never been cut. On trichogram, nearly all bulbs were abnormally shaped anagen bulbs without inner or outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

In a series of 19 patients with loose anagen hair, Tosti et al. (1997) included a 5-year-old boy who also had Noonan syndrome.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) reported 3 children with the same facial phenotype, short stature, macrocephaly, and enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces. They had a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities. Two of the children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. The skin was darkly pigmented and hairless, and the hair was described as loose anagen hair. Because the appearance was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, the authors proposed that the children had a distinct syndrome.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) reviewed 25 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation and found a consistent phenotype. Facial features appeared typical for Noonan syndrome and included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, and low-set, posteriorly rotated ears. Patients also had a short, webbed neck and pectus anomalies. Growth was reduced, often with proven GH deficiency. Patients had cognitive deficits and distinctive hyperactive behavior, which improved with age in most. The hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow growing, and loose anagen hair was confirmed by microscopy of pulled hairs in 12 subjects. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis, and the voice was characteristically hypernasal. Most had cardiac anomalies, and mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects were overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population. Referring pediatricians had suspected Costello syndrome or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome in several of the patients as newborns or young infants.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the S2G mutation. All had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a boy with the S2G mutation who was delivered prematurely at 32 weeks because of fetal distress. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, which progressed rapidly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors noted that his cardiac findings and other features were not typical of patients previously reported with this mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 boys with the S2G mutation, 4 of whom had initially been suspected of having Costello syndrome. All 5 had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay. All 5 also had unusually long eyelashes. Each of the 4 patients evaluated had a structural cardiac anomaly, including pulmonic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, atrial septal defect, and mitral valve abnormality. Four had hypotonia and macrocephaly. Three had polyhydramnios and increased birth weight, and 3 required a feeding tube. Brain imaging showed relative megalencephaly and enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, as well as a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium. Cerebellar tonsillar ectopia was present in 3 of 4 patients, and 1 patient developed a Chiari I malformation postnatally. One patient had periventricular nodular heterotopia and a thick, dysplastic corpus callosum. Another patient developed myelofibrosis at age 2 years without a JAK2 V617F mutation and died at age 2.5 years. None of the patients had been formally diagnosed with loose anagen hair.

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that the SHOC2 S2G mutation had arisen de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available. Hannig et al. (2014) observed transmission of a heterozygous SHOC2 variant from an affected father to his affected daughter, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent p.pro49-to-arg mutation in the PPP1CB gene. He had sparse, thin, slow-growing hair similar to that seen in Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, as well as craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related disease closely resembles the SHOC2-related disorder. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) used in silico protein network analysis to select SHOC2 as a candidate gene. They sequenced it in 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who were negative for mutations in known disease genes and identified a heterozygous c.4A-G transition, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 4 unrelated sporadic patients. The mutation was absent from the parents of the 3 patients for whom parental DNA was available, and it was present in several tissues. In a further cohort of 410 mutation-negative patients with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, they found the same mutation in 21 patients, and it had arisen de novo in all 12 families with available parental DNA. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. The S2G substitution introduced an N-myristoylation site. In transfected Cos-1 cells, the mutant protein incorporated myristic acid and was targeted to the plasma membrane, whereas wildtype SHOC2 was distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus and moved to the nucleus after EGF stimulation. The mutant did not translocate to the nucleus after growth factor stimulation. Expression of the mutant enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells. The mutant did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome or related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They identified the S2G mutation in 8 patients. No SHOC2 mutations were found in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a premature infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in the SHOC2 gene by clinical testing of genes associated with Noonan syndrome and related disorders.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the S2G mutation in 5 patients. In the 2 patients whose parents were tested, the mutation had arisen de novo. ⚠︎

Hannig et al. (2014) studied a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry, born to nonconsanguineous parents, and her father. Her features overlapped Noonan syndrome, cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome, and NSLH. Using a RASopathy gene panel, they identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The variant was inherited from her father, who also had features of the disorder, and it was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. Met173 lies in the fourth leucine-rich repeat and is conserved in all vertebrate SHOC2 orthologs. In SHOC2-depleted Cos-1 cells, the mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 activity to only about 10% above basal level, whereas wildtype SHOC2 restored activity fully. The mutant bound PP1c less effectively than wildtype and produced only a mild increase in RAF1 S338 phosphorylation. Protein stability, subcellular localization, and binding to MRAS and RAF1 were unchanged. The authors concluded that M173I is a loss-of-function variant.

Motta et al. (2019) identified a SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT, predicting p.gln269_his270delinsHisTyr, in association with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. In functional studies, the mutant protein bound MRAS and PPP1CB more strongly than wildtype and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade. Unlike the S2G mutant, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the 2 patients with the SHOC2 M173I mutation had milder features than patients with the S2G mutation. The proband did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair, and her father was tall and had very subtle features. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS, SHOC2, and PP1 form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site of RAF kinases. Noonan syndrome-associated mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB consistently promoted formation of this complex. The authors suggested that disease caused by these mutations is driven by enhanced ternary complex formation.

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype human SHOC2 could replace the C. elegans ortholog SUR-8, whereas SHOC2 S2G could not. In wildtype worms, expression of SHOC2 S2G from the early L3 stage caused protruding vulva, reduced egg laying, and a bag-of-worms phenotype. These neomorphic phenotypes also occurred with wildtype SHOC2 carrying an added N-myristoylation sequence, but not with wildtype SHOC2 alone.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.Ser2Gly]

In 25 patients with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair (NSLH1), Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation had arisen de novo in all families with available parental DNA. It introduced an N-myristoylation site, causing aberrant targeting of SHOC2 to the plasma membrane, impaired nuclear translocation after growth factor stimulation, and enhanced MAPK activation in a cell type-specific manner. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) identified the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome or related disorders who did not have mutations in other known genes.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in a premature infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the S2G mutation in 5 patients, in 2 of whom it had arisen de novo. One of these patients developed myelofibrosis.
