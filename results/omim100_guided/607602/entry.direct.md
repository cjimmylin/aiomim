### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that annular epidermolytic ichthyosis-1 (AEI1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding keratin 10 (KRT10).

### Description

Annular epidermolytic ichthyosis is a rare phenotypic variant of bullous congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma (epidermolytic ichthyosis). Affected individuals have bullous ichthyosis in early childhood, followed later by hyperkeratotic lichenified plaques on the flexural areas and extensor surfaces. They also have intermittent bouts of annular and polycyclic, erythematous, scaly plaques on the trunk and proximal extremities. Histologic examination shows epidermolytic hyperkeratosis (Joh et al., 1997; Suga et al., 1998; Mikilita et al., 2020).

### Nomenclature

Sahn et al. (1992) proposed that the kindred they described might represent a new phenotype of epidermolytic (acanthokeratolytic) ichthyosis, which they called annular epidermolytic ichthyosis. After identifying a mutation in keratin 10, Joh et al. (1997) concluded that annular epidermolytic ichthyosis should be considered a variant of bullous congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma. Suga et al. (1998) also described it as a distinct phenotypic variant of bullous congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma.

### Clinical Features

Sahn et al. (1992) reported a 30-year-old white woman who developed bullous and ichthyosiform skin lesions at 8 months of age. The blistering stopped at puberty, but the hyperkeratotic plaques persisted. Two of her 5 children were affected with a bullous type of ichthyosis. At 27 years of age she developed many annular and polycyclic, erythematous, hyperkeratotic plaques on the trunk and extremities. These plaques were pruritic, enlarged slowly, and then resolved. Histopathologic examination of the annular plaques showed epidermolytic hyperkeratosis. On electron microscopy, the annular plaques showed interrupted perinuclear tonofilament rings, consistent with either bullous ichthyotic erythroderma of Brocq or ichthyosis bullosa of Siemens.

Joh et al. (1997) reported a second family with annular epidermolytic ichthyosis, with affected members in 2 generations. The proband had bullous ichthyosis and bouts of disease activity in which many annular and polycyclic, erythematous, hyperkeratotic plaques developed on the trunk and proximal extremities. Histologic examination showed the typical pathology of epidermolytic hyperkeratosis. Ultrastructural analysis showed abnormal keratin filament networks and clumping of tonofilaments in a perinuclear distribution.

Suga et al. (1998) described a third kindred with annular epidermolytic ichthyosis. They noted that individuals with this variant have bullous ichthyosis in early childhood and hyperkeratotic lichenified plaques on the flexural areas and extensor surfaces at later ages. They also have intermittent bouts of annular and polycyclic, erythematous, scaly plaques on the trunk and proximal extremities.

Mikilita et al. (2020) reported an 8-year-old girl with diffuse dermatosis that began in the first months of life. There was no family history of similar cases and no consanguinity. She had erythematous, scaly, hyperkeratotic plaques with prominent borders. These involved the mesogastrium, cubital and popliteal fossae, and the inguinal and cervical regions. She also had palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and yellowish hyperkeratotic plaques on the scalp and nasal introitus. Hair, nails, and mucosa were unaffected, and she reported no symptoms. One month later the plaques had changed in appearance: the affected areas were more extensive, but the polycyclic aspect had disappeared. The initial diagnosis was erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva. A skin biopsy from the extensor forearm showed acanthosis, papillomatosis, and hyperkeratosis with marked epidermolysis in the granular layer, which led to the diagnosis of annular epidermolytic ichthyosis. In a review of the literature in English, Portuguese, and Spanish, Mikilita et al. (2020) found 19 cases in 10 publications. Some patients had no lesions at birth, and palmoplantar keratoderma was reported in some cases but not in others.

### Inheritance

Joh et al. (1997) noted that the pattern of inheritance of annular epidermolytic ichthyosis is consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. In the family reported by Sahn et al. (1992), an affected mother had 2 affected children, and in the family reported by Joh et al. (1997), affected individuals were found in 2 generations.

### Heterogeneity

Mikilita et al. (2020) stated that annular epidermolytic ichthyosis results from dominant mutations in the keratin 1 and keratin 10 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a family with annular epidermolytic ichthyosis, Joh et al. (1997) found a tandem 2-bp CG-to-GA mutation on a single allele of the keratin 10 gene (KRT10). The mutation caused an arg-to-glu substitution at residue 83 (R83E) of the 2B helical segment.

In a third kindred with annular epidermolytic ichthyosis, Suga et al. (1998) found a mutation in the KRT10 gene that caused an ile-to-thr substitution at residue 107 (codon 446). This residue lies within the highly conserved helix termination motif at the end of the keratin 10 rod domain.

### Diagnosis

Mikilita et al. (2020) noted that the main differential diagnosis of annular epidermolytic ichthyosis is erythrokeratodermia variabilis et progressiva (EKVP). Features that distinguish EKVP include onset during childhood, no epidermal fragility, and no epidermolysis on histology. The main ultrastructural feature of EKVP is a reduced number of keratinosomes in the granular layer.

### Clinical Management

In their literature review, Mikilita et al. (2020) found that the small number of reported patients had been treated with topical agents, including retinoids, corticosteroids, propylene glycol, calcipotriene, and keratolytics, with little response. Three reports described a good response to systemic retinoids: 2 to acitretin and 1 to low-dose isotretinoin.
