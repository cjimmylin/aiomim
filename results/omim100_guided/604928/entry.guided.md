### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome-2 is an autosomal recessive disorder. Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy are its main diagnostic features (El-Shanti et al., 2000). Unlike Wolfram syndrome-1, it does not include diabetes insipidus or psychiatric disorders. Instead, patients have bleeding upper intestinal ulcers and defective platelet aggregation (El-Shanti et al., 2000; Mozzillo et al., 2014).

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients from 4 families with the Wolfram syndrome phenotype. They also had features not reported before. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus, and several had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) reported a Caucasian Italian girl with WFS2. Her parents said they were not consanguineous, but their grandparents had been born on the same small Italian island. From age 5 years, she had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. At age 14, malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception were diagnosed. At age 16, she developed reduced visual acuity and dyschromatopsia. She had bilateral optic disc pallor, normal pattern visual evoked potentials, and normal optic nerve caliber on MRI. Non-autoimmune diabetes mellitus began at age 17 years. She also had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss in the high frequencies, slow intestinal transit, and enterogastric bile reflux. Visual field testing, electroretinography, and OCT showed moderate optic nerve involvement in both eyes. The authors considered this compatible with optic neuropathy, not optic atrophy, and her findings were unchanged at age 22 years. Platelet aggregation was reduced and reversible in response to ADP up to 10 microM, while responses to collagen, epinephrine, and ristocetin were normal. Platelet secretion in response to ADP was absent. Her parents and brother were clinically unaffected but showed reversible defective platelet aggregation in response to ADP. Mozzillo et al. (2014) suggested that the 'asymptomatic' optic atrophy described in the Jordanian patients may in fact have been optic neuropathy. They questioned whether WFS2 should be considered a subtype of Wolfram syndrome.

### Inheritance

El-Shanti et al. (2000) mapped the locus by homozygosity mapping. Amr et al. (2007) then found a homozygous mutation in consanguineous families. Both findings are consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance of WFS2. ⚠︎

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 4 families with Wolfram syndrome. Using 3 microsatellite markers linked to the 4p16.1 locus (D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366), they significantly excluded linkage to 4p16.1 in 3 of the families. In the fourth family, the 2 affected members were homozygous for all 3 markers in the 4p16.1 region. Homozygosity mapping in the other 3 families showed linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24.

### Heterogeneity

El-Shanti et al. (2000) found linkage to 4q22-q24, not to the 4p16.1 Wolfram syndrome locus, in 3 of 4 families. This provided evidence of genetic heterogeneity in Wolfram syndrome. Amr et al. (2007) noted that these families did not carry mutations in the WFS1 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with WFS2 that mapped to the 4q22-q25 locus. In all 3 families, they identified the same homozygous 109G-C transversion in the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene, which predicts a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution at a conserved residue. The authors attributed the disease to aberrant splicing rather than to the amino acid change. The mutation eliminated exon 2 from the mRNA and introduced a premature stop codon. The CISD2-encoded protein, ERIS, localized to the endoplasmic reticulum but did not interact directly with wolframin. When stimulated with thapsigargin, lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals showed a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium than control cells. Resting intracellular calcium concentrations did not differ. Amr et al. (2007) found no WFS2 mutations in 377 probands with hearing loss.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) studied a Caucasian Italian girl with WFS2. Exon 2 of CISD2 could not be amplified in the patient, and cDNA sequencing showed a homozygous deletion of the whole of exon 2. SNP array analysis showed that her parents and brother were heterozygous for the deletion. The maximum size of the deletion was 2,050 bp, which the authors described as spanning intron 2 to intron 3. They stated that the deleted region included the codons for amino acids 102 to 106, within the 2Fe-2S domain. Analysis of long runs of homozygosity did not show a parental relationship, but microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) reported a novel CISD2 mutation in Italian sibs with WFS2.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction together with autophagic cell death. These changes came before the earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration, and the mice developed features suggestive of premature aging. Cisd2 was found mainly in mitochondria. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased as the premature aging phenotype developed. Chen et al. (2009) proposed that CISD2 mutation causes WFS2 as a mitochondria-mediated disorder.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, GLU37GLN [109G-C; E37Q]

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2). In affected members, they identified a homozygous G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 of the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene, which predicts a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution at a conserved residue. The mutation disrupted mRNA splicing by eliminating exon 2 and introduced a premature stop codon. The authors concluded that this aberrant splicing was the basis of the disease. Lymphoblastoid cells from patients showed a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium after thapsigargin stimulation than control cells.

**.0002 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, EX2 DEL

Mozzillo et al. (2014) studied a Caucasian Italian girl with Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2). In this patient, they identified a homozygous intragenic deletion of CISD2 that removed the whole of exon 2. It was detected by failure to amplify exon 2 and confirmed by cDNA sequencing and SNP array analysis. The maximum size of the deletion was 2,050 bp, and the authors stated that the deleted region included the codons for amino acids 102 to 106 in the 2Fe-2S domain. Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers and showed subclinical defective platelet aggregation in response to ADP.
