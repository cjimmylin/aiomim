### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363), which lies in the chromosome 2q24-q31.1 region.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Seizures begin in the neonatal period or early infancy and are often epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures, with a suppression-burst pattern or hypsarrhythmia on EEG. Affected individuals have severe to profound developmental delay and intellectual disability, with axial hypotonia, spasticity, or both. Some patients also have cleft palate, joint contractures, pes equinovarus, scoliosis, omphalocele, and dysmorphic facial features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients with DEE from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish (2 families), Iranian, and Brazilian origin. Four patients had died, at ages 9 days to 4 years. In all 10 patients whose early history was available, seizures began in the first month of life, mainly as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. Seven of 8 patients with a neonatal EEG had a suppression-burst or burst attenuation pattern, and 2 patients first recorded later had hypsarrhythmia. All living patients had severe psychomotor delay and profound intellectual disability. Neurologic findings included axial hypotonia, spasticity, tetraparesis, and dystonia or hyperkinetic movements. Seven patients had cleft palate (cleft soft palate in some), 6 had joint contractures, 5 had pes equinovarus, and 4 had scoliosis. Both sibs in an Egyptian family had omphalocele and multiple dysmorphic features. Three patients had facial dysmorphism without a clear common pattern; features included hypertelorism, broad or depressed nasal bridge, anteverted nares, low-set ears, and microretrognathia. Head circumference was normal in all patients but one. Brain MRI was normal in 5 patients. Other patients had a hypoplastic corpus callosum with progressive cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, or mild cerebral atrophy with ventricular enlargement. Some patients had additional findings, including polyhydramnios, intestinal atresia, Hirschsprung disease, cervical and axillary webbing, hirsutism, conductive or later-onset hearing loss, nystagmus, nephrocalcinosis, and failure to thrive. Although 2 Iranian sibs and a Turkish family carried the same mutation, the Iranian sibs had no pes equinovarus, joint contractures, or cleft palate.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients with DEE from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian, and Turkish ancestry. The text stated that 4 were born to consanguineous parents. All were born at term after a normal pregnancy. Seizures began at 2 to 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures with or without impaired awareness. Some patients also had epileptic spasms, focal nonmotor seizures, or bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. EEG at onset showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Seizures were controlled in 3 patients and drug-resistant in 3. All patients had severe developmental delay, and most did not achieve speech; 1 patient had moderate intellectual disability and developed simple speech. Five patients had reduced muscle strength or tone, ranging from mild to severe hypotonia with limited head control and inability to sit. Two patients depended on nasogastric tube feeding because of dysphagia. Four patients had mild dysmorphic facial features, and only 1 had cleft palate. Other variable features included high myopia, hearing impairment, scoliosis, clinodactyly, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, short arms, congenital hip dislocation, diastasis recti, hirsutism, nephrocalcinosis and kidney stones, and reduced head circumference. Brain MRI was normal in all but 2 patients: 1 had slight ventricular enlargement and 1 had moderate global atrophy.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 in the families reported by Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) used whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing in 11 patients with DEE89 from 6 consanguineous families. In every family they identified a homozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene, and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disorder. Four families carried mutations predicted to truncate the protein. These were a splice site mutation (c.1414-1G-C), a 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG), and a nonsense mutation (R531X) that was shared by an Iranian family and a Turkish family. cDNA analysis of the splice site mutation showed no normal mRNA product. Another family carried an in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA; K232del), and a further family carried a missense mutation (E509K). The authors proposed a loss-of-function mechanism. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing in 6 patients with DEE89 from 6 unrelated families. Five patients carried homozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: 3 missense mutations (N564S, F324C, and T347M), a nonsense mutation (Y29X), and a 1-bp deletion (c.568delC). The sixth patient was compound heterozygous for R531X and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations segregated with the disorder. The variants were absent from or very rare in gnomAD and were predicted to be damaging. The authors proposed a loss-of-function mechanism. No functional studies were performed. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) found that seizures responded well to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 patients treated with the drug. They noted that the response appeared to depend on seizure type, because patients with epileptic spasms seemed to benefit most.

Von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported treatment with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet in a patient with GAD1 deficiency.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) found that Gad67 -/- mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate on the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67 -/- mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%. The brain showed no discernible defects.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [NM_000817.2:c.1414-1G>C]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 2 sibs with DEE89 (DEE89) born to Algerian first-cousin parents. Whole-exome sequencing showed that both were homozygous for a c.1414-1G-C transversion in the GAD1 gene, and Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disorder. The mutation was predicted to abolish the acceptor splice site of exon 15. cDNA analysis of lymphoblastoid cells from the parents showed no normal mRNA product. Instead there were 3 aberrant transcripts: one skipping exon 15, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [NM_000817.2:c.695_697delAGA; p.Lys232del]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 2 sibs with DEE89 born to Egyptian first-cousin parents. The sibs had omphalocele, pes equinovarus, and joint contractures, and the female sib also had cleft palate. The proband was homozygous for a 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA), which deleted a highly conserved lysine (lys232del; listed as lys231del in the authors' table). The mutation was not found in the 1000 Genomes Project, Greater Middle East, ExAC, or gnomAD databases. In silico analysis predicted that it would cause splice site loss. However, in a cell-free assay the mutant protein was expressed at a level comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing and therefore does not exclude a splicing effect.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, 812TTAAG [NM_000817.2:c.812_816delTTAAG; p.Val271Aspfs*9]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 2 sibs with DEE89 born to Turkish first-cousin parents. The sibs had refractory epilepsy beginning in the first weeks of life, together with cleft palate. Whole-genome sequencing showed that both were homozygous for a 5-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene (c.812_816delTTAAG), which causes a frameshift (val271aspfs*9). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with the disorder.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [NM_000817.2:c.1591C>T; p.Arg531*]

Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution, in patients with DEE89 from 2 consanguineous families. One family was Iranian (2 sibs) and the other was Turkish (2 sibs). The authors suggested that the families might share a haplotype because of their relative geographic proximity. The Iranian sibs had no pes equinovarus, joint contractures, or cleft palate, whereas these features were present in the Turkish family.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported an African American boy with DEE89 who was compound heterozygous for the R531X mutation and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC) in the GAD1 gene. R531X was reported only in the heterozygous state in gnomAD.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [NM_000817.2:c.1525G>A; p.Glu509Lys]

Chatron et al. (2020) reported a 7-year-old girl with DEE89 born to Brazilian first-cousin parents. She was homozygous for a c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. The mutation was absent from gnomAD. It affects an evolutionarily highly conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that it would disrupt salt bridges with arg316, lys318, and lys389.
