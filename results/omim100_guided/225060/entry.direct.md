### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome (CLPED1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the PVRL1 gene on chromosome 11q23.

### Description

Cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its features include an unusual facial appearance, cleft lip/palate, ectodermal dysplasia with hypotrichosis and dental anomalies, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and onychodysplasia, and cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers and/or toes. Some patients have mental retardation (Suzuki et al., 1998; Yoshida et al., 2015). Sözen et al. (2001) found that heterozygosity for a PVRL1 nonsense mutation was associated with sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate (OFC7) in northern Venezuela.

### Nomenclature

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) called the disorder Zlotogora-Ogur syndrome. Suzuki et al. (1998) called it Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia (ED4). Suzuki et al. (2000) named it CLPED1 and noted that it had previously been designated ED4.

### Clinical Features

Zlotogora et al. (1987) reported 2 sibs with cleft lip and palate, sparse scalp hair, malformed protruding ears, and partial syndactyly of the fingers and toes. The older child also had mental retardation and pili torti.

Oğur and Yüksel (1988) reported 2 Turkish brothers born to parents who were second cousins. Both had tetramelic syndactyly, ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip and palate, renal anomalies, and mental retardation.

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) reported 3 Brazilian brothers born to normal consanguineous parents (F = 1/16). The brothers had ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, syndactyly of fingers 2-3, accessory nipples, and ear anomalies.

Bustos et al. (1991) described 27 individuals from 7 families on Margarita Island in northeastern Venezuela (Nueva Esparta State). The families were very probably related to one another and came from a relatively isolated, inbred population. Patients had ectodermal dysplasia and other anomalies, and expressivity was variable. Females were affected as severely as males, and all parents were normal. The authors could not classify the condition among the known ectodermal dysplasias and considered it a newly recognized autosomal recessive dysplasia/malformation syndrome.

Suzuki et al. (1998) described Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia as characterized by unusual facies, dental anomalies, hypotrichosis, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and onychodysplasia, syndactyly, and cleft lip/cleft palate.

Yoshida et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old Japanese boy who was the first child of a consanguineous marriage. He had hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia with sparse, brittle, fine, dry hair and hypodontia, along with the characteristic facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers, and mild mental retardation. Scanning electron microscopy of the hair showed pili torti and pili trianguli et canaliculi.

### Inheritance

Zlotogora et al. (1987) considered autosomal recessive inheritance most likely, based on 2 affected sibs. Affected sibs born to consanguineous parents were reported by Oğur and Yüksel (1988) and by Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990). In the Margarita Island families, Bustos et al. (1991) found that females were affected as severely as males and that all parents were normal, which supports autosomal recessive inheritance. Yoshida et al. (2015) found that the parents of a patient with a homozygous PVRL1 mutation were both heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Suzuki et al. (1998) used an affected-only DNA-pooling strategy to map the gene for Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia to 11q23 by linkage disequilibrium. Haplotype analysis of 4 complex Margarita Island families placed the gene in an interval of approximately 1 to 2 Mb, spanned by just 2 YACs.

### Molecular Genetics

Suzuki et al. (2000) used positional cloning to identify PVRL1 as the gene responsible for CLPED1. PVRL1 encodes nectin-1, an immunoglobulin-related transmembrane cell-cell adhesion molecule that is part of the NAP cell adhesion system.

Sözen et al. (2001) stated that homozygosity for a nonsense mutation in PVRL1, W185X, causes CLPED1 on Margarita Island. They found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for this mutation and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate in northern Venezuela.

Yoshida et al. (2015) analyzed exon 2 of PVRL1 in a Japanese boy with CLPED1 who was born to consanguineous parents. They found a homozygous nonsense mutation, R134X. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Yoshida et al. (2015) noted that all 4 PVRL1 mutations identified in CLPED1 up to that time, including this one, produced truncated proteins lacking the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1, which are needed to initiate cell-cell adhesion.

### Population Genetics

Nectin-1 is the principal cell surface receptor for alpha-herpesviruses (HveC). Suzuki et al. (2000) suggested that the high frequency of CLPED1 on Margarita Island might result from resistance of heterozygotes to infection by these viruses.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CLEFT LIP/PALATE-ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA SYNDROME**  
PVRL1, TRP185TER [W185X]

Sözen et al. (2001) stated that homozygosity for a trp185-to-ter (W185X) nonsense mutation in the PVRL1 gene causes autosomal recessive CLPED1 on Margarita Island. They also found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for this mutation and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate in northern Venezuela.

**.0002 CLEFT LIP/PALATE-ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA SYNDROME**  
PVRL1, ARG134TER [c.400C>T; p.Arg134*]

Yoshida et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old Japanese boy with CLPED1 who was born to consanguineous parents. He was homozygous for a c.400C-T transition in exon 2 of the PVRL1 gene, resulting in an arg134-to-ter (R134X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The predicted truncated protein lacks the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1.
