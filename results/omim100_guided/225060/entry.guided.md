### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome (CLPED1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the PVRL1 gene on chromosome 11q23. Heterozygosity for a PVRL1 nonsense mutation has been associated with sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip/palate (OFC7) in northern Venezuela.

### Description

Cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its features include an unusual facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, ectodermal dysplasia (sparse hair, dental anomalies, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and onychodysplasia), and cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers and/or toes. Some patients also have mental retardation (Suzuki et al., 1998; Yoshida et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) called the association of ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, and syndactyly the Zlotogora-Ogur syndrome. The disorder seen on Margarita Island has been called Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia (ED4) (Suzuki et al., 1998). Suzuki et al. (2000) designated the disorder CLPED1, previously ED4.

### Clinical Features

Zlotogora et al. (1987) reported 2 sibs with cleft lip and palate, sparse scalp hair, malformed protruding ears, and partial syndactyly of the fingers and toes. The older child also had mental retardation and pili torti.

Oğur and Yüksel (1988) reported 2 Turkish brothers, born of a second-cousin marriage, with tetramelic syndactyly, ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip and palate, renal anomalies, and mental retardation.

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) reported 3 Brazilian brothers born to normal consanguineous parents (F = 1/16). The boys had ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, syndactyly of fingers 2-3, accessory nipples, and ear anomalies.

Bustos et al. (1991) described 27 individuals from 7 families on Margarita Island, northeastern Venezuela (Nueva Esparta State), a relatively isolated and inbred population. The families were very probably related to one another. Affected individuals had ectodermal dysplasia and other anomalies, with variable expressivity, and females were affected as severely as males. All parents were normal. The authors could not classify the clinical picture among the known ectodermal dysplasias and considered it a newly recognized syndrome.

Suzuki et al. (1998) characterized Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia by unusual facies, dental anomalies, hypotrichosis, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and onychodysplasia, syndactyly, and cleft lip/cleft palate.

Yoshida et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old Japanese boy, the first child of a consanguineous marriage, who had hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia with sparse, brittle, fine, dry hair and hypodontia. He also had the characteristic facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers, and mild mental retardation. Scanning electron microscopy of the hair showed pili torti and pili trianguli et canaliculi.

### Inheritance

Zlotogora et al. (1987) considered autosomal recessive inheritance most probable, based on their report of affected sibs. Parental consanguinity in the families of Oğur and Yüksel (1988) and Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) was consistent with this mode of inheritance. Bustos et al. (1991) found that females were affected as severely as males and that all parents were normal, and they concluded that the disorder in the Margarita Island families is autosomal recessive. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Using an affected-only DNA-pooling strategy, Suzuki et al. (1998) mapped the ED4 gene to 11q23 by linkage disequilibrium. Haplotype analysis of 4 complex Margarita Island ED4 families narrowed the location to an interval of approximately 1 to 2 Mb, spanned by just 2 YACs.

### Molecular Genetics

By positional cloning, Suzuki et al. (2000) identified PVRL1 as the gene mutated in CLPED1. PVRL1 encodes nectin-1, an immunoglobulin-related transmembrane cell-cell adhesion molecule. In patients from Margarita Island, the disorder results from homozygosity for a nonsense mutation, trp185-to-ter (W185X) (Sözen et al., 2001).

In a Japanese boy with CLPED1 born of consanguineous parents, Yoshida et al. (2015) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2 of the PVRL1 gene (R134X). Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The authors noted that all 4 PVRL1 mutations reported in CLPED1 to that date predicted truncated proteins that lack the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1.

Sözen et al. (2001) found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for the W185X mutation in PVRL1 and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip/palate (OFC7) in northern Venezuela.

### Population Genetics

Bustos et al. (1991) found the disorder in 7 probably related families from a relatively isolated, inbred population on Margarita Island. Nectin-1 is the principal cell surface receptor for alpha-herpesviruses. Suzuki et al. (2000) suggested that the high frequency of CLPED1 on Margarita Island might result from resistance of heterozygotes to infection by these viruses. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CLEFT LIP/PALATE-ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA SYNDROME**  
PVRL1, TRP185TER [W185X]

In patients with cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome (CLPED1) from Margarita Island, Suzuki et al. (2000) identified a mutation in the PVRL1 gene. Sözen et al. (2001) described this mutation as a homozygous nonsense mutation, trp185-to-ter (W185X). ⚠︎

Sözen et al. (2001) found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for the W185X mutation and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip/palate (OFC7) in northern Venezuela.

**.0002 CLEFT LIP/PALATE-ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA SYNDROME**  
PVRL1, ARG134TER [c.400C>T; p.Arg134*]

In a 7-year-old Japanese boy with cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome (CLPED1), the first child of a consanguineous marriage, Yoshida et al. (2015) identified a homozygous c.400C-T transition in exon 2 of the PVRL1 gene, resulting in an arg134-to-ter (R134X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The predicted truncated protein lacks the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1.
