### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene (605870) on chromosome 13q12.

### Description

Cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is a rare early-onset neurodevelopmental disorder. Its features are nonprogressive cerebellar ataxia, intellectual disability of varying severity, hypotonia, dysarthria, and cerebellar atrophy, and some patients walk on all fours (quadrupedal locomotion). Severity ranges from congenital ataxia with near-normal cognition and independent walking to severe disease with involuntary movements, optic atrophy, feeding difficulty, and no head control (Onat et al., 2013; Mohamadian et al., 2020; Damásio et al., 2021; Narishige et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family in which 4 members had cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome, with cerebellar atrophy and quadrupedal locomotion. Mohamadian et al. (2020) later summarized the features of this family as intellectual disability, mild cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, dysarthria, and truncal ataxia.

Mohamadian et al. (2020) reported 3 sibs with CAMRQ4 from a consanguineous Iranian family of Arab descent; the parents were first cousins. The proband was a 9-year-old girl who had severe hypotonia as a newborn. All her psychomotor milestones were delayed, and she used a wheelchair because she could not walk, sit, or hold up her head. She had had feeding difficulties since infancy. Her affected brother and sister, aged 17 and 15 years, had similar features. All 3 patients had IQ levels of 20 to 40, delayed speech and language, dysarthria, and absent deep tendon reflexes, but no significant hearing impairment. Brain MRI showed cerebellar atrophy in all 3, and metabolic studies were normal.

Damásio et al. (2021) reported 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who learned to walk independently by age 4 years. After 16 years of follow-up, both had near-normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4 who developed athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. Both also had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, and severely delayed development, and one had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. Over time they developed areflexia and flattened auditory brainstem responses. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or control the head, although some nonverbal communication was preserved. The disease did not progress in early development, which the authors noted could make it hard to tell apart from dyskinetic cerebral palsy.

### Inheritance

CAMRQ4 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Onat et al. (2013) and Mohamadian et al. (2020) found homozygous ATP8A2 mutations in affected members of consanguineous families. Narishige et al. (2022) found compound heterozygous mutations. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Using homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4, Onat et al. (2013) found several homozygous regions shared by affected members, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Ozcelik et al. (2008) studied 4 consanguineous Turkish families with quadrupedal gait, cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia, and intellectual disability. In 2 families the condition was caused by mutations in the VLDLR gene. In the other 2 families it was not linked to chromosome 9p, so the condition is genetically heterogeneous. Gulsuner et al. (2011) identified a mutation in the WDR81 gene in another consanguineous Turkish family with quadrupedal locomotion, intellectual disability, and cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia.

### Molecular Genetics

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4. Homozygosity mapping, followed by targeted next-generation sequencing of 1 affected member, identified a homozygous missense mutation in the ATP8A2 gene, ile376-to-met (I376M). The authors supported the variant with segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction programs. The mutation lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain. Onat et al. (2013) also pointed to an unrelated person with intellectual disability and severe hypotonia who carried a de novo balanced t(10; 13) translocation that disrupts ATP8A2. ⚠︎

Mohamadian et al. (2020) studied 3 sibs with CAMRQ4 from a consanguineous Iranian family. Whole-exome sequencing of the proband identified a homozygous c.1339G-A transition (NM_016529.6) in exon 14 of the ATP8A2 gene, causing a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation. All 3 affected sibs were homozygous, while both parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous and 1 unaffected sib did not carry it. The mutation was absent from public variant databases and from 100 healthy controls of the same ethnicity. It changes a conserved residue in the ATP-binding domain. In silico structural modeling predicted that it lowers protein stability and alters the ligand-binding pocket.

Damásio et al. (2021) studied 2 sibs with a relatively mild form of congenital ataxia. Exome sequencing identified a homozygous c.1580-18C-G change in intron 17 of the ATP8A2 gene, which was predicted to create a new splice acceptor site. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the mutant construct produced an abnormal transcript that kept 17 bp of intron 17. This in-frame insertion of 4 amino acids leads to a premature stop codon. ⚠︎

Narishige et al. (2022) studied 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4. Whole-exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous mutations in the ATP8A2 gene (NM_016529.6): a c.1741C-T transition causing an arg581-to-ter (R581X) substitution, which had been reported before, and a new c.2158C-T transition causing an arg720-to-ter (R720X) substitution.
