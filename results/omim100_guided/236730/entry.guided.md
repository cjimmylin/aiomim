### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene on chromosome 10q24.

### Description

Urofacial syndrome (UFS) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are an 'inverted' facial expression, in which patients look as if they are crying when they smile, and a neurogenic bladder that does not empty completely even though there is no anatomic outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage. Patients have enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and vesicoureteral reflux. This can lead to kidney damage, and some severely affected patients develop hypertension and end-stage renal disease (Wang et al., 1997; Derbent et al., 2009; Daly et al., 2010). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Elejalde (1979) used the designation 'the Ochoa syndrome.' Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) proposed the term urofacial syndrome for the disorder.

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) reported 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from unrelated families. One family was consanguineous (first cousins). The patients had peculiar facies and gestures when smiling and crying. Their urinary findings included hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal caliber of the prostatic and membranous urethra, and urethral valves. The bladder was abnormal, with trabeculation and diverticula, and the mucosa showed severe hypertrophy with sclerotic changes. Elejalde (1979) noted that the peculiar facies allows the condition to be recognized early.

Between 1965 and 1986, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) saw 36 children with enuresis and urinary tract infection together with an 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic workup showed mild neuropathic bladder in all cases, and most patients had severe urinary tract damage. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation.

Chauve et al. (2000) reported a French family with UFS, the first European cases.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European cases of urofacial syndrome. They suggested that the disorder may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing is not recognized.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported the first published case of urofacial syndrome from Turkey. The patient had been followed for recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux and was diagnosed with UFS at 16 years of age.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 Turkish families. Median follow-up was 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months).

### Inheritance

From a genetic analysis of 3 families, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant, with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987), however, concluded that the disorder is autosomal recessive. They based this on its occurrence in multiple sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio. Homozygous HPSE2 mutations in affected individuals (Pang et al., 2010; Daly et al., 2010) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) performed a genome screen in 20 selected patients, combining homozygosity mapping with DNA pooling. The marker D10S677 showed an excess of homozygosity in patients compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)). All 31 patients were homozygous for 2 closely linked markers, D10S1726 and D10S198, which lie about 5 cM telomeric to D10S677. Only 12% of unaffected relatives were homozygous for both markers (P less than 10(-19)). Recombination events placed the UFS gene within a 1-cM interval between D10S1433 and D10S603 on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) confirmed linkage to the region identified by Wang et al. (1997) by homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage. Using new polymorphic markers, they narrowed the UFS gene to within a single YAC clone of 1,410 kb. Mutation analysis excluded the GOT1 gene, which lies in the critical region.

Chauve et al. (2000) carried out haplotype analysis in a French family. The results were compatible with the same critical region on 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (2003) built a genomic sequence map of the UFS interval and narrowed the disease region to 220 kb. This region contains the ACDP1 gene and part of GOT1. Searches of the ACDP1 coding region, 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, promoter, and exon/intron junctions found no pathogenic mutation in UFS patients. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Several studies concluded that UFS is genetically homogeneous. Wang et al. (1999) and Chauve et al. (2000) found linkage to the same region in American patients of Irish heritage and in a French family. Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same gene on chromosome 10q is responsible for all UFS patients from multiple ethnic groups. In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 UFS patients, which they interpreted as evidence of genetic heterogeneity. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all UFS patients from Colombia, the United States, and France. These patients had previously been used to map the disorder to chromosome 10q24 by homozygosity mapping. ⚠︎

Daly et al. (2010) performed whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 individual with UFS and found a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24. Within this region they identified a 10-kb deletion that removes exons 8 and 9 of HPSE2. In 5 further unrelated families, they found homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations in HPSE2. HPSE2 was expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system and in bladder smooth muscle.
