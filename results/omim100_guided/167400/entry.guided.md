### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN9A gene, which encodes the sodium channel Nav1.7.

### Description

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD), previously known as familial rectal pain, is an inherited condition marked by attacks of rectal, ocular, or submandibular pain with flushing (Fertleman et al., 2006). Onset is in the neonatal period or infancy and the disorder lasts throughout life. Autonomic signs are most prominent early on. These include skin flushing, harlequin color change, tonic attacks, and syncope with bradycardia. Later, attacks of excruciating deep burning pain predominate. Defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion can trigger these attacks (Fertleman et al., 2007).

### Nomenclature

Fertleman and Ferrie (2006) described the renaming of familial rectal pain syndrome as paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.

### Clinical Features

Hayden and Grossman (1959) reported a family with rectal, ocular, and submaxillary pain. They described it as a familial autonomic disorder related to proctalgia fugax.

Dugan (1972) and Mann and Cree (1972) published reports of familial rectal pain.

Through an international consortium, Fertleman et al. (2007) collected clinical data on 77 affected individuals from 15 families. Fourteen affected individuals completed a validated pain questionnaire. Onset was in the neonatal period or infancy, and the disorder persisted throughout life. Autonomic manifestations predominated at first. All patients had skin flushing, and most had harlequin color change and tonic attacks. Dramatic syncopes with bradycardia, and sometimes asystole, were common. Later, patients had attacks of excruciating deep burning pain, often in the rectal, ocular, or jaw areas but sometimes diffuse. Triggers included defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion.

Meglič et al. (2014) described a 3-year-old girl with an unusual presentation of PEXPD. She had had extremely painful voiding since birth. Her father carried the same mutation. Since childhood he had occasionally had some pain in his jaw when yawning.

### Inheritance

Fertleman et al. (2007) described PEXPD as an autosomal dominant condition. In the family reported by Meglič et al. (2014), the heterozygous SCN9A mutation was inherited from the father.

### Molecular Genetics

Fertleman et al. (2006) performed a genome-wide linkage search followed by mutation analysis of the candidate gene SCN9A. They identified 8 missense mutations in 11 families with PEXPD and in 2 sporadic cases. In vitro functional analysis of 3 of the mutant Nav1.7 channels showed reduced fast inactivation, which led to persistent sodium current.

In a 3-year-old girl with PEXPD, Meglič et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.554G-A mutation in the SCN9A gene, resulting in an arg185-to-his (R185H) substitution. The mutation was inherited from her mildly affected father. The authors noted that the same mutation had been found in patients with small fiber sensory neuropathy. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fertleman et al. (2006) noted that other SCN9A mutations, which shift the activation threshold to more negative values, cause primary erythermalgia. Carbamazepine is effective in PEXPD but not in primary erythermalgia. In their study, carbamazepine selectively blocked the persistent current of PEXPD mutant channels but did not affect the negative activation threshold of a primary erythermalgia mutant. The authors concluded that PEXPD and primary erythermalgia are allelic disorders with distinct biophysical mechanisms.

Meglič et al. (2014) noted that the R185H mutation had previously been found in patients with small fiber sensory neuropathy. This did not fit the clinical picture in their patient and her father. They suggested that phenotypic variability within and between families with gain-of-function Nav1.7 variants might explain the difference.

### Diagnosis

Fertleman et al. (2007) noted that PEXPD is most likely to be misdiagnosed as epilepsy. In infancy in particular, it may also be mistaken for hyperekplexia or reflex anoxic seizures.

### Clinical Management

Fertleman et al. (2007) found that carbamazepine was effective in almost all patients who tried it, although the response was often incomplete. Meglič et al. (2014) reported that carbamazepine significantly reduced pain in their patient.
