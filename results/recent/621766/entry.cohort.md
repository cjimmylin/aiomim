### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that spermatogenic failure-104 (SPGF104) is caused by biallelic mutation in the CCDC9 gene on chromosome 19q13.32. ⚠︎

### Description

Spermatogenic failure-104 (SPGF104) is a form of male infertility. Sha et al. (2019) reported it as severe asthenozoospermia, with abnormal morphology and ultrastructure of the spermatozoa. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Sha et al. (2019) reported a man from a consanguineous family who had severe asthenozoospermia. His spermatozoa had severely damaged morphology and ultrastructure, and their CCDC9 protein levels were significantly low.

Khan et al. (2025) studied 7 large Pakistani families with male infertility, 5 of which were consanguineous. One of these families had male infertility and carried biallelic CCDC9 variants. The abstract did not give further clinical details on the affected men in this family.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPGF104 is autosomal recessive. Sha et al. (2019) found a homozygous CCDC9 variant in a man from a consanguineous family, and Khan et al. (2025) found biallelic CCDC9 variants in a Pakistani family. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a man from a consanguineous family who had severe asthenozoospermia, Sha et al. (2019) identified a homozygous missense variant in the CCDC9 gene (NC_000019.9: g.47763960C-T; NM_015603.3), which resulted in a ser109-to-leu (S109L) substitution. In silico analysis predicted the variant to be highly pathogenic. The patient's spermatozoa had significantly reduced CCDC9 protein, along with severely damaged morphology and ultrastructure. Sha et al. (2019) concluded that CCDC9 may be a novel pathogenic gene for severe asthenozoospermia (see allelic variant SER109LEU).

Khan et al. (2025) performed whole-genome sequencing on 26 individuals from 7 large Pakistani families with male infertility. They identified plausible genetic causes in 5 of the 7 families (71%), compared with the 20% yield they had previously obtained from whole-exome sequencing of unrelated singleton cases. In 1 family, they found biallelic missense substitutions in CCDC9, which they described as a gene with emerging relevance to male infertility. The abstract did not specify the variants.
