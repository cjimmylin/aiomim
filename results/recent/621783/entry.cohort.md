### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that spermatogenic failure-105 (SPGF105) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ADGB gene (614630) on chromosome 6q24.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Spermatogenic failure-105 (SPGF105) is an autosomal recessive form of male infertility. Affected men have reduced sperm motility and abnormal sperm morphology (asthenozoospermia or asthenoteratozoospermia), with malformations of the sperm acrosome and flagellum. Severe oligozoospermia has also been reported. Fertilization and pregnancy have been achieved by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Qu et al. (2023) reported an infertile man with asthenozoospermia.

Gao et al. (2025) reported 3 infertile Han Chinese men from unrelated families who had severe asthenoteratozoospermia. On light and electron microscopy, the patients' spermatozoa showed multiple malformations of the acrosome and the flagellum.

Wang et al. (2026) reported a Chinese man with primary infertility and severe idiopathic oligoasthenoteratozoospermia. His sperm concentration was 2.0 x 10(6)/mL, fewer than 1% of sperm showed progressive motility, no sperm had normal morphology, and sperm vitality was 16%. Sperm head abnormalities included microcephaly and conical heads. Tail defects included short, angled, coiled, and absent tails. On transmission electron microscopy, spermatozoa showed loss of the acrosome, a disorganized helical structure of the mitochondrial sheath, and disrupted or partially absent '9+2' microtubule structures in the axoneme. His reproductive hormone levels were normal, his karyotype was 46,XY, and he had no Y-chromosome microdeletion. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPGF105 is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Gao et al. (2025) found homozygous and compound heterozygous ADGB mutations in affected men, and Wang et al. (2026) found compound heterozygous ADGB mutations in an affected man. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Qu et al. (2023) identified variants in the ADGB gene in an infertile man with asthenozoospermia (SPGF105). The variants disrupted the binding of ADGB to calmodulin.

Gao et al. (2025) performed whole-exome sequencing in 105 Han Chinese men with idiopathic asthenoteratozoospermia and found biallelic ADGB mutations in 3 infertile men from unrelated families. One man was homozygous for a frameshift mutation (c.2801_2802del; K934Rfs*33). A second man was homozygous for a thr1056-to-ile (T1056I) missense mutation. A third man was compound heterozygous for the T1056I mutation and an ala1066-to-val (A1066V) missense mutation. All of the variants were rare in the general population, and multiple bioinformatics tools predicted them to be damaging.

Wang et al. (2026) performed whole-exome sequencing in 781 Chinese men with primary infertility and found 148 ADGB variants. They classified 6 of these as likely pathogenic, and 2 of the 6 occurred together in a single man with oligoasthenoteratozoospermia. Sanger sequencing showed that this man was compound heterozygous for 2 missense mutations in the ADGB gene: arg1620-to-gln (R1620Q) and lys1661-to-glu (K1661E). On Western blot, ADGB protein was absent from the patient's sperm. On immunofluorescence, both ADGB and the acrosome marker PNA were reduced. In normal sperm, ADGB was enriched in the acrosome and in the mitochondrial sheath of the flagellum. In the human testis, ADGB expression was low before puberty and increased after puberty. ADGB was enriched in spermatocytes and spermatids and localized to the acrosome and flagella.

### Pathogenesis

Using mass spectrometry, Qu et al. (2023) identified 42 candidate proteins that interact with ADGB and are involved in sperm assembly, flagella formation, and sperm motility. They confirmed that CFAP69 and SPEF2 bind to ADGB.

Gao et al. (2025) found that the structural defects in sperm from patients with SPGF105 were associated with dysregulation of ADGB and multiple other spermatogenesis proteins. Patient spermatozoa had normal PLC-zeta expression but were deficient in calmodulin.

Using coimmunoprecipitation, Wang et al. (2026) showed that ADGB interacts with TTC29 and CFAP47 in sperm. Both proteins were reduced in the patient's sperm. The authors suggested that ADGB may help maintain acrosome function and normal axoneme assembly through these interactions.

### Clinical Management

Gao et al. (2025) reported successful fertilization by ICSI for all 3 of their ADGB-deficient patients. They suggested that ICSI, rather than in vitro fertilization (IVF), is the optimal treatment for these patients, and noted an underlying risk of IVF failure.

Wang et al. (2026) reported that their patient's partner achieved a successful pregnancy and full-term delivery after ICSI. They noted that all 5 reported infertile couples with ADGB mutations had achieved clinical pregnancy with ICSI.

### Animal Model

Qu et al. (2023) found that male Adgb -/- mice were infertile because of reduced sperm concentration (less than 1 x 10(6)/mL) and reduced sperm motility. Spermatogenesis was abnormal, with malformed elongating and elongated spermatids. The cauda epididymis had about twice as many apoptotic cells. ICSI with testicular spermatids allowed fertilization and development to the blastocyst stage.

Wang et al. (2026) found that in mice, Adgb mRNA was highly expressed specifically in the testis and peaked at sexual maturity (postnatal day 40).
