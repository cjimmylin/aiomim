### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that spermatogenic failure-105 (SPGF105) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ADGB gene.

### Description

Spermatogenic failure-105 (SPGF105) is a form of male infertility characterized by asthenozoospermia, with reduced sperm motility. Affected men have shown asthenoteratozoospermia or oligoasthenoteratozoospermia, with sperm showing acrosome and flagellum malformations. Fertilization has been achieved by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) (Qu et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Qu et al. (2023) reported an infertile man with asthenozoospermia.

Gao et al. (2025) studied 3 infertile men from unrelated Han Chinese families who had severe asthenoteratozoospermia. Light and electron microscopy showed multiple acrosome and flagellum malformations in their spermatozoa.

Wang et al. (2026) reported a Chinese man with primary infertility and severe idiopathic oligoasthenoteratozoospermia. Semen analysis showed a volume of 4.3 mL, sperm concentration of 2.0 x 10(6)/mL, vitality of 16%, progressive motility of less than 1%, and 0% normal morphology. Abnormal heads were seen in 76.5% of sperm, abnormal neck and midpiece in 14%, and abnormal flagella in 62.5%. Reproductive hormones were normal, the karyotype was 46,XY, and there was no Y-chromosome microdeletion. Head abnormalities included microcephalic and conical heads, and tail defects included short tails, acutely angled curved tails, coiled tails, and tailless sperm. Transmission electron microscopy showed loss of the acrosome, a disorganized helical structure of the mitochondrial sheath, and disrupted or partly absent '9 + 2' microtubule structures in the axoneme.

### Inheritance

Gao et al. (2025) and Wang et al. (2026) found biallelic ADGB mutations (homozygous or compound heterozygous) in affected men, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Qu et al. (2023) identified variants in the ADGB gene in an infertile man with asthenozoospermia. The variants disrupted the binding of ADGB to calmodulin. Mass spectrometry identified 42 candidate proteins that interact with ADGB and are involved in sperm assembly, flagella formation, and sperm motility. Of these, CFAP69 and SPEF2 were confirmed to bind ADGB.

Gao et al. (2025) performed whole-exome sequencing in a cohort of 105 Han Chinese men with idiopathic asthenoteratozoospermia and identified biallelic ADGB mutations in 3 infertile men from unrelated families. Sanger sequencing was also performed. One man was homozygous for a frameshift mutation (c.2801_2802del, K934Rfs*33), another was homozygous for a thr1056-to-ile (T1056I) missense mutation (c.3167C-T), and the third was compound heterozygous for T1056I and an ala1066-to-val (A1066V) missense mutation (c.3197C-T). The variants were rare in the general population and multiple bioinformatics tools predicted them to be damaging. In functional assays, the structural defects were associated with dysregulation of ADGB and of multiple spermatogenesis proteins.

Wang et al. (2026) performed whole-exome sequencing in 781 Chinese men with primary infertility and identified 148 ADGB variants. Using ACMG criteria, 6 of these were classified as likely pathogenic. Two of the likely pathogenic missense mutations were found in compound heterozygous state, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, in a man with oligoasthenoteratozoospermia: c.4859G-A, resulting in an arg1620-to-gln (R1620Q) substitution, and c.4981A-G, resulting in a lys1661-to-glu (K1661E) substitution. Arg1620 was conserved across vertebrates. Lys1661 was conserved in mammals but was replaced by glutamine, serine, and glycine in birds, amphibians, and fish, respectively. Western blot analysis showed no ADGB protein in the patient's sperm, and immunofluorescence showed reduced ADGB and PNA staining. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Gao et al. (2025) found normal expression of PLC-zeta but calmodulin deficiency in spermatozoa from patients with ADGB mutations.

Wang et al. (2026) found that ADGB was highly expressed in human and mouse testes after puberty and localized to the acrosome and flagella of spermatogenic cells. Coimmunoprecipitation showed that TTC29 and CFAP47 interact with ADGB, and immunofluorescence showed reduced TTC29 and CFAP47 in sperm from the patient with ADGB mutations.

### Clinical Management

Gao et al. (2025) reported successful fertilization with ICSI in all 3 patients with ADGB mutations. They suggested that ICSI, rather than in vitro fertilization, is the optimal treatment for men with ADGB deficiency.

The patient reported by Wang et al. (2026) achieved a successful pregnancy through ICSI. In the first ICSI cycle, 36 oocytes were retrieved from his partner and the normal 2-pronuclear fertilization rate was 70%. After transfer of a single frozen-thawed blastocyst, a term delivery followed.

### Animal Model

Qu et al. (2023) found that Adgb -/- male mice were infertile because of reduced sperm concentration (less than 1 x 10(6)/mL) and reduced sperm motility. Spermatogenesis was abnormal, with malformed elongating and elongated spermatids, and the cauda epididymis contained about twice as many apoptotic cells. ICSI with testicular spermatids from the mice allowed fertilization and development to the blastocyst stage.
