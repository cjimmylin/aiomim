### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that oocyte/zygote/embryo maturation arrest-26 (OZEMA26) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEI1 gene (608797) on chromosome 22q13.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Oocyte/zygote/embryo maturation arrest-26 (OZEMA26) is an autosomal recessive form of female primary infertility. The main features are early embryonic arrest and recurrent implantation failure. Some affected women have recurrent hydatidiform moles, and others show abnormal fertilization with multiple pronuclei. Embryos from affected women have frequently shown chromosomal aneuploidy or triploidy. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Dong et al. (2021) first studied a pedigree in which 2 sisters with primary infertility were diagnosed with recurrent hydatidiform moles in December 2018. They then extended the study to patients with embryonic arrest, recurrent implantation failure, and recurrent pregnancy loss who were recruited between December 2018 and May 2020. This identified 7 more affected women from 6 families, all with primary infertility, early embryonic arrest, and/or recurrent implantation failure. ⚠︎

Wu et al. (2024) reported an infertile woman with embryonic arrest and recurrent implantation failure. Two of her day-3 embryos and 2 day-6 blastocysts were all aneuploid, with numerous monosomy and trisomy events.

Liu et al. (2025) studied a consanguineous family that included an infertile woman with embryonic arrest. Preimplantation genetic testing showed aneuploidy in her arrested embryos. A male relative from the same family had infertility with azoospermia.

Jiao et al. (2025) reported 2 Chinese women with primary infertility. The proband of family 1 was a 32-year-old woman with a 4-year history of primary infertility and no prior pregnancies. She had regular menstrual cycles and a normal ovarian reserve, and laparoscopy showed a pelvic adhesion. ICSI produced tripronuclear and polypronuclear zygotes. None of the 2PN-derived embryos from conventional IVF formed blastocysts, and no viable embryos were frozen or implanted. In contrast, 2 of 3 embryos derived from 3PN/PPN zygotes after ICSI developed into late-stage blastocysts. Her embryos showed triploidy of maternal origin and aneuploidy, and she had a sister with normal fertility. The proband of family 2 was a 35-year-old woman with an 8-year history of primary infertility, regular menstrual cycles, and normal ovarian function. Her zygotes included mono-pronuclear and 2.1PN forms. None of her embryos formed blastocysts, and transfer of viable frozen embryos did not result in pregnancy. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2026) described women from 2 unrelated families with primary infertility characterized by early embryonic arrest and implantation failure.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of OZEMA26 is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Wu et al. (2024) found that each of the 2 MEI1 variants in their proband was inherited from a heterozygous parent, consistent with autosomal recessive transmission. Liu et al. (2025) found a homozygous MEI1 exon deletion in affected members of a consanguineous family, and their mother was a heterozygous carrier. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Dong et al. (2021) studied 9 women with primary infertility from 7 families. Using whole-exome sequencing with Sanger confirmation, they identified 5 novel biallelic mutations in the MEI1 gene. Two of these mutations were found in a pedigree with 2 sisters who had recurrent hydatidiform moles. The other 3 were found in 7 patients from 6 families with embryonic arrest or recurrent implantation failure. On in vitro Western blotting, protein-truncating and missense mutations reduced MEI1 protein levels. In minigene assays, the splicing mutations caused abnormal alternative splicing of MEI1. The authors noted that the exact mechanism remained unknown because no in vivo oocyte data were available.

In a woman with embryonic arrest and recurrent implantation failure, Wu et al. (2024) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the MEI1 gene. One was a 1-bp deletion (c.3002delC). The other was a 16-bp deletion (c.2264_2268+11delGTGAGGTATGGACCAC). Each mutation was inherited from a heterozygous parent.

In a woman with embryonic arrest from a consanguineous family, Liu et al. (2025) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous deletion of exon 19 of the MEI1 gene. The same deletion was found in her male relative with azoospermia, and their mother was a heterozygous carrier. cDNA analysis showed almost no detectable expression of MEI1 exon 19 in the patients. Western blotting in transfected HEK293T cells showed a truncated MEI1 protein, consistent with premature termination in exon 20. The authors described this as the first exon rearrangement reported in MEI1. ⚠︎

Jiao et al. (2025) performed whole-exome sequencing in 20 women with a high rate of abnormal fertilization during ICSI and identified biallelic MEI1 variants in 2 of them. The proband from family 1 was compound heterozygous for a ser208-to-pro (S208P) missense variant (c.622T-C) and an arg883-to-ter (R883X) nonsense variant (c.2647C-T). The proband from family 2 was compound heterozygous for a trp1013-to-gly (W1013G) missense variant (c.3037T-G) and a deletion of exons 21 to 31 (c.2546_3525del). The variants were validated in the probands and family members by Sanger sequencing, with qPCR also used for the exon deletion. In HEK293T cells, R883X increased MEI1 mRNA but reduced MEI1 protein, and W1013G increased protein levels. Neither S208P nor the exon 21-31 deletion changed protein expression.

In women from 2 unrelated families with primary infertility characterized by early embryonic arrest and implantation failure, Chen et al. (2026) used whole-exome sequencing with Sanger confirmation to identify a novel homozygous variant in one family and compound heterozygous variants in the other, all in the MEI1 gene. Bioinformatic analysis suggested that the variants were rare and possibly deleterious. In transfected HEK293T cells, the variants did not alter the subcellular localization of MEI1. However, they significantly affected MEI1 mRNA and protein expression levels.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jiao et al. (2025) observed that MEI1 variants associated with nonobstructive azoospermia tended to lie toward the N terminus of the protein. Variants associated with embryonic developmental arrest tended to cluster toward the C terminus. The authors noted that this pattern requires further validation.

### Pathogenesis

Wu et al. (2024) found that 2 day-3 embryos and 2 day-6 blastocysts from a woman with MEI1 mutations were all aneuploid. Global DNA methylation in the 2 blastocysts was 0.344 and 0.168, well outside the optimal window of 0.25 to 0.27. The authors suggested that the genetic makeup of arrested embryos from MEI1 patients may explain why some affected women have embryonic arrest while others have androgenetic hydatidiform moles.

Liu et al. (2025) found high rates of chromosomal anomalies on preimplantation genetic testing of arrested embryos from a woman with a homozygous MEI1 mutation. They suggested that embryonic aneuploidy may explain the reduced developmental potential and the embryonic arrest.

Jiao et al. (2025) analyzed embryos from 1 patient and found that all were triploid, with an excess maternal genome of meiotic origin. One arose from a meiosis II error and 2 from meiosis I errors, and 2 embryos also had chromosomal aneuploidies. RNA-seq of day-3 embryos from 1 patient showed that downregulated genes were enriched for meiotic cell cycle, histone demethylase/deacetylase, and transcription coactivator functions. Maternal-effect genes, including FBXO43, OOEP, and WEE2, were dysregulated. The authors suggested that impaired zygotic genome activation may contribute to the phenotype.

### Clinical Management

Jiao et al. (2025) recommended screening for MEI1 variants in patients with abnormal fertilization patterns, especially multiple pronuclei after ICSI. They noted that even embryos with apparently normal 2PN morphology may be triploid.
