### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that complex cortical dysplasia with other brain malformations-17 (CDCBM17) is caused by homozygous mutation in the DMRTA2 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Complex cortical dysplasia with other brain malformations-17 (CDCBM17) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Its main features are microcephaly, pachygyria or a simplified gyral pattern, and agenesis of the corpus callosum (Urquhart et al., 2016; Shen et al., 2025). Affected individuals have also had global developmental delay and could not stand or walk (Shen et al., 2025). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Urquhart et al. (2016) studied a consanguineous family with 3 sibs who had a severe prenatal neurodevelopmental disorder. Its features were frontoparietal pachygyria, agenesis of the corpus callosum, and progressive severe microcephaly.

Shen et al. (2025) studied a consanguineous Pakistani family with 3 affected individuals (V-3, V-5, and V-7) in 3 branches of the family. Two of them (V-3 and V-5) underwent detailed clinical evaluation, and the third (V-7) was said to have similar features. The patients had global developmental delay, dysarthria, muscle atrophy, aggressive behavior, and peripheral neuropathy, and they could not stand or walk. Brain MRI of V-3 at 6 years of age showed microcephaly, a simplified gyral pattern with pachygyria, an absent cingulate gyrus, and agenesis of the corpus callosum. It also showed colpocephaly, with dilated posterior horns and relatively parallel lateral ventricles suggesting complete agenesis of the corpus callosum. The occipital and temporal horns were enlarged, without periventricular white matter changes. The brainstem and cerebellum were normal, and there were no calcifications. An EEG of V-5 at 5 years of age showed abnormalities consistent with a mild encephalopathy.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CDCBM17 in the families reported by Urquhart et al. (2016) and Shen et al. (2025) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Both families were consanguineous, and affected members carried homozygous DMRTA2 mutations. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Urquhart et al. (2016) used autozygosity mapping, together with exome sequencing, in a consanguineous family with 3 affected sibs to identify the causative gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Urquhart et al. (2016) used autozygosity mapping and exome sequencing in 3 sibs with CDCBM17 from a consanguineous family. They identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion (c.1197delT) in the DMRTA2 gene, predicted to cause a frameshift (Pro400LeufsTer33). The authors noted that data from murine and zebrafish knockout models were consistent with this variant causing the cortical phenotype. They suggested that loss of DMRTA2 function leads to a disorder of cortical development.

Shen et al. (2025) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 affected members (V-3 and V-5) of a consanguineous Pakistani family with CDCBM17, a family identified through GeneMatcher. They identified a homozygous c.347G-C transversion in the DMRTA2 gene, resulting in an arg116-to-pro (R116P) substitution in the DM domain. Sanger sequencing showed that this was the only shared homozygous variant that segregated with the phenotype in the family. Arg116 is conserved down to C. elegans. In EMSA studies, the mutant protein, produced either in vitro or in transfected HEK293T cells, was unable to bind a DMRTA2 consensus binding site, whereas the transfected mutant protein remained mainly nuclear. The authors proposed that the R116P mutation causes cortical malformations by disrupting DNA binding.

### Animal Model

Shen et al. (2025) found that the cerebral hemispheres of Dmrta2 -/- mouse embryos at E18.5 were reduced in size by 60.1% compared with wildtype. Loss of 1 Pax6 allele lessened this reduction (to 31.6%) and partially restored Wnt3a expression in the cortical hem. In Dmrta2 -/-;Pax6 sey/sey double-mutant embryos, the cerebral hemispheres were virtually absent, and the subpallial markers Gsx2 and Gsx1 were expressed ectopically in the abortive cortical primordium. In mice conditionally overexpressing Dmrta2, the V1 area was expanded; additional overexpression of Pax6 reduced this expansion. In P19 cells, Dmrta2 repressed Pax6-driven activity of the Pax6 E60 enhancer in a manner that depended on DNA binding. Dmrta2 associated with components of the NuRD complex and with Zfp423 through its DM domain. The authors concluded that Dmrta2 cooperates with Pax6 to maintain cortical identity and controls cortical patterning by repressing Pax6.
