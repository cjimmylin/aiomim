### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hyposulfatemia with skeletal dysplasia (HSSD) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the SLC13A1 gene (606193) on chromosome 7q31.

### Description

Hyposulfatemia with skeletal dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in SLC13A1. Patients come to medical attention because of short stature, scoliosis, and/or skeletal dysplasia. The dysplasia ranges from multiple epiphyseal to spondyloepiphyseal and spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia. Loss of renal sulfate reabsorption causes reduced plasma sulfate and increased urinary sulfate excretion, which can serve as a measurable biomarker (van de Kamp et al., 2023; Tise et al., 2025).

### Clinical Features

van de Kamp et al. (2023) reported a Dutch boy who was the only child of nonconsanguineous parents. He presented at 4 months of age with enlarged knees that could not fully extend; his length and head circumference were normal. A skeletal survey at 6 months showed ovoid vertebral bodies, spiky widening of the metaphyses, irregular tarsal bones, slight rib flaring, and somewhat small epiphyses, which were more noticeable at 4 years. The skull and pelvis were normal, as were serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, PTH, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D3. The radiologic abnormalities improved with age. By 11 years, only flattening of the cervical vertebral bodies and irregular lumbar vertebral endplates remained.

At 12 years, the patient of van de Kamp et al. (2023) had enlarged knees and elbows, reduced movement in the lumbar spine and knees, winged scapulae, finger hypermobility, and flat feet. His height was normal (+1 SDS), but his trunk was relatively short (sitting height/height ratio -2.0 SDS). His gait was slightly abnormal and he could not run easily. He sustained a forearm fracture at 8 years. DEXA at 13 years showed BMD Z-scores of 0.2 in the lumbar spine and -2.1 in the proximal femur. Puberty began at 11.5 years, when his bone age was more than 1 year advanced. His cognitive development was above average and he had no epilepsy. However, he had autistic features, including difficulty with social communication, restricted behaviors and interests, sensitivity to environmental changes, and rigid thinking, which frequently led to panic attacks and school absenteeism. He had no joint pain, visual or hearing complaints, or skin abnormalities.

Tise et al. (2025) reported 5 children from 4 families. All came to medical attention because of concern about short stature, and all had scoliosis and/or skeletal dysplasia. Proband 1 was a 3-year-old boy of European ancestry born to nonconsanguineous parents. He had bilateral developmental hip dysplasia, which was braced until age 9 months, and came to attention at age 2 years with increasing hip pain. He had proportionate short stature (z-score below -2 SD) and mild dysmorphic features: epicanthal folds, a flattened nasal bridge, and a short, anteverted nose. He also had tapered fingers and deep-set nails of the halluces. Pelvic x-rays showed delayed, stippled femoral ossification consistent with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. A spine x-ray showed mild anterior beaking of the lumbar vertebrae.

Proband 2 of Tise et al. (2025) was a girl of Asian ancestry born to nonconsanguineous parents. Her legs were noted to be bowed when she began walking at about 14 months. At age 3 years she had tibial bowing and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, with small, fragmented proximal capital femoral epiphyses and wedge-shaped vertebral bodies. By age 11 the bowing had improved spontaneously, but she had developed mild scoliosis. At age 12 years her height was 137.7 cm (3rd percentile, z = -1.85), and her stature was proportionate.

Tise et al. (2025) also described fraternal twins (proband 3, male; proband 4, female) born at 34 weeks to nonconsanguineous parents. Their skeletal surveys showed spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia, which had been interpreted as consistent with Kniest dysplasia. Proband 3 had exaggerated thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis, anterior beaking of the vertebrae, short and broad femoral necks, and metaphyseal flaring. His scoliosis progressed from 12 degrees at age 4 years to an S-shaped 28-degree thoracic curve at 13 years. He also had pectus carinatum, a type 1 Chiari malformation, an atrial septal defect, and broad toes. Proband 4 had ovoid vertebrae with anterior wedging and widened metaphyses, along with broad thumbs, 2-3 toe syndactyly, and short, broad halluces. At age 10 years, their heights were 129.6 cm (z = -1.66) and 134.4 cm (z = -0.85), respectively.

Proband 5 of Tise et al. (2025) was a boy born to nonconsanguineous parents who was evaluated for scoliosis at age 14 years. He had proportionate short stature (157.3 cm, z = -2.13). Imaging showed thoracolumbar scoliosis, reduced height of several mid and lower thoracic vertebral bodies, and concave endplates; these findings were reported as suggestive of osteogenesis imperfecta or metabolic bone disease. He also had mild to moderate neurocognitive impairment, an atypical developmental disorder, Asperger's disorder, and anxiety disorders.

### Other Features

The patient reported by van de Kamp et al. (2023) also had cystic fibrosis, found incidentally by exome sequencing. It was due to a homozygous founder CFTR variant (c.1364C-A, ala455-to-glu) located 5.6 Mb from SLC13A1, within the same region of homozygosity. His CF was mild, with a sweat chloride of 64 mmol/L and stable lung function.

### Biochemical Features

van de Kamp et al. (2023) found severely reduced plasma sulfate (over 80% reduction) in their patient. His fractional sulfate excretion was close to 100% (reference 13-34%), consistent with complete loss of renal sulfate reabsorption. Both heterozygous parents had plasma sulfate below the control range, and the mother had a fractional sulfate excretion of 45%. Serum cholesterol sulfate was reduced in the patient (443 ng/ml; reference 500-1236 ng/ml), whereas other serum steroid sulfates were normal and the urinary steroid metabolome was age-appropriate. The percentage of chondroitin sulfate disaccharides in urinary glycosaminoglycans did not differ from that in parents and controls.

Tise et al. (2025) found that proband 1 excreted more sulfate in urine than normal, with a urinary sulfate/creatinine ratio of 10.96 (reference 0.5-6.0). His heterozygous parents had normal values (father 1.71, mother 3.81). His plasma sulfate could not be measured because of inadequate specimen transport. Proband 2, who was homozygous for Y582H, had a plasma sulfate of 0.107 mM (reference 0.3-0.5 mM). Her fractional excretion index of sulfate was increased (reported as 0.38 in the text; reference 0.17-0.34). Her plasma level was lower than the mean of 0.25 mM reported in heterozygous carriers of SLC13A1 nonsense variants, and similar to the 0.065 mM reported in a child homozygous for R12X. The authors considered this consistent with the missense variant being a loss-of-function allele.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HSSD is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. van de Kamp et al. (2023) found homozygosity in a patient whose nonconsanguineous parents were heterozygous. Tise et al. (2025) noted that the SLC13A1 variants were biallelic in all 4 families they reported, and that the heterozygous parents were healthy.

### Molecular Genetics

In a Dutch boy with HSSD, van de Kamp et al. (2023) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.34C-T nonsense mutation in exon 1 of the SLC13A1 gene (NM_022444.3), resulting in an arg12-to-ter (R12X) substitution. This variant had previously been shown to cause total loss of function in Xenopus oocytes. The nonconsanguineous parents were heterozygous. The patient had a 20-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 7q31.32, suggesting a common ancestor.

In proband 1 of Tise et al. (2025), trio exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous mutations in SLC13A1: a paternally inherited R12X nonsense mutation and a maternally inherited c.1343G-A missense mutation resulting in a gly448-to-asp (G448D) substitution. In stably transfected MDCK cells, G448D completely abolished [35S]-sulfate transport, although the variant protein reached the plasma membrane at levels similar to wildtype.

In proband 2 of Tise et al. (2025), trio genome sequencing found no cause in any known disease gene. A targeted search of SLC13A1 then identified a homozygous c.1744T-C missense mutation resulting in a tyr582-to-his (Y582H) substitution, inherited from both parents. The variant completely abolished sulfate transport in MDCK cells.

In the fraternal twins (probands 3 and 4) of Tise et al. (2025), quad exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous mutations in SLC13A1: a paternally inherited c.1547T-C missense mutation resulting in a leu516-to-pro (L516P) substitution and a maternally inherited c.144G-A nonsense mutation resulting in a trp48-to-ter (W48X) substitution. Earlier sequencing of COL2A1 had found no variant. L516P completely abolished sulfate transport in MDCK cells. ⚠︎

In proband 5 of Tise et al. (2025), trio genome sequencing identified a homozygous W48X mutation in SLC13A1, inherited from both parents.

Tise et al. (2025) built a homology model of SLC13A1 based on the SLC13A5 structure. The model predicted the following effects: R237C loses most contacts and hydrogen bonds at the intracellular end of transmembrane domain 5; G448D introduces clashes within transmembrane domain 9; L516P removes contacts and introduces clashes in transmembrane domain 10, part of the ligand recognition pocket; and Y582H removes nearly all contacts in the extracellular C terminus. In stably transfected MDCK cells, each of the missense variants R237C, G448D, L516P, and Y582H completely abolished [35S]-sulfate uptake. Each variant protein still reached the plasma membrane at levels similar to wildtype, suggesting that the substitutions impair transport without abolishing trafficking. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Tise et al. (2025) noted that proband 5, who was homozygous for W48X, had a neurodevelopmental phenotype, and that a previously reported child homozygous for R12X had autistic features. They stated that more patients will be needed to determine whether SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants, or possibly only nonsense variants, are associated with a neurodevelopmental phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Citing previous work showing that SLC13A1 is not expressed in cartilage or bone, Tise et al. (2025) proposed that the osteochondrodysplasia is secondary to a systemic metabolic disorder of sulfate homeostasis caused by loss of renal sulfate reabsorption. They suggested that this disorder may also affect other organ systems. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

van de Kamp et al. (2023) gave their patient intravenous N-acetylcysteine as a 133 mg/kg bolus over 60 minutes, followed by 133 mg/kg over 8 hours. Plasma sulfate normalized during the bolus and maintenance infusion but returned to baseline 15 hours after the infusion was stopped. Erythema during the bolus resolved with clemastine. Because of a potential risk of hepatotoxicity, the authors advised avoiding acetaminophen.

Tise et al. (2025) suggested that oral sulfate supplementation, given directly or through sulfate sources such as N-acetyl-L-cysteine, warrants evaluation in humans. Because Slc13a1-deficient mice show enhanced acetaminophen hepatotoxicity, they advised caution in giving acetaminophen and other drugs metabolized by sulfation to individuals with biallelic SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants.

### Population Genetics

van de Kamp et al. (2023) noted that the SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants R12X and W48X have relatively high global allele frequencies (0.20% and 0.05%, respectively), and that 1 R12X homozygote is reported in gnomAD. They hypothesized that biallelic loss-of-function variants are more common than recognized and may present later in life with degenerative bone and joint disease, with mild presentations escaping diagnosis.

Tise et al. (2025) reported gnomAD v4.1.0 counts for the SLC13A1 variants. G448D, L516P, and Y582H were not seen in the homozygous state, with 221, 7, and 3 heterozygous alleles, respectively. R12X was seen in 3,884 heterozygotes and 7 homozygotes, and R237C in 3,129 heterozygotes and 5 homozygotes. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

van de Kamp et al. (2023) noted that homozygous SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants occur naturally in Texel sheep and miniature Poodles. Both have skeletal dysplasia, including stunted growth, enlarged joints, joint stiffness, and early-onset osteoarthritis. The Slc13a1-null mouse shows hyposulfatemia, hypersulfaturia, growth retardation, behavioral abnormalities, and seizures.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HYPOSULFATEMIA WITH SKELETAL DYSPLASIA**  
SLC13A1, ARG12TER [c.34C>T; g.122839967G>A; p.R12; p.R12*]

In a Dutch boy with HSSD, van de Kamp et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.34C-T transition (NM_022444.3) in exon 1 of the SLC13A1 gene, resulting in an arg12-to-ter (R12X) substitution. This variant had previously been shown to cause total loss of function in Xenopus oocytes. The nonconsanguineous parents were heterozygous and had plasma sulfate below the control range. The patient had severely reduced plasma sulfate and a fractional sulfate excretion close to 100%. The variant has a global allele frequency of 0.20%, and 1 homozygote is reported in gnomAD.

In proband 1 of Tise et al. (2025), a boy of European ancestry with HSSD, the R12X mutation was inherited from the father and was compound heterozygous with a maternally inherited G448D mutation. R12X was seen 3,884 times in the heterozygous state and 7 times in the homozygous state in gnomAD v4.1.0.

**.0002 HYPOSULFATEMIA WITH SKELETAL DYSPLASIA**  
SLC13A1, GLY448ASP [c.1343G>A; p.G448D]

In a 3-year-old boy of European ancestry with HSSD (proband 1), Tise et al. (2025) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the SLC13A1 gene by trio exome sequencing: a maternally inherited c.1343G-A transition, resulting in a gly448-to-asp (G448D) substitution, and a paternally inherited R12X mutation. G448D was absent in the homozygous state from gnomAD v4.1.0, with 221 heterozygous alleles. Homology modeling predicted that the substitution introduces clashes within transmembrane domain 9. **.0003 HYPOSULFATEMIA WITH SKELETAL DYSPLASIA**  
SLC13A1, TYR582HIS [c.1744T>C; p.Y582H]

In a girl of Asian ancestry with HSSD (proband 2) born to nonconsanguineous parents, Tise et al. (2025) identified a homozygous c.1744T-C transition in the SLC13A1 gene, resulting in a tyr582-to-his (Y582H) substitution, inherited from both parents. The mutation was found by targeted analysis of genome sequencing data. It was absent in the homozygous state from gnomAD v4.1.0, with 3 heterozygous alleles.
