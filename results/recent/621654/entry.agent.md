### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hyposulfatemia with skeletal dysplasia (HSSD) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the SLC13A1 gene on chromosome 7q31.

### Description

Hyposulfatemia with skeletal dysplasia (HSSD) is an autosomal recessive disorder in which loss of the renal and intestinal sodium-sulfate cotransporter SLC13A1 causes increased urinary sulfate excretion and low plasma sulfate. Affected children have skeletal abnormalities that can include spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia in early childhood, proportionate short stature and scoliosis. Some radiologic abnormalities have improved with age. Neurodevelopmental features, including autistic features, have been reported in some patients (van de Kamp et al., 2023; Tise et al., 2025).

### Clinical Features

van de Kamp et al. (2023) reported a Dutch boy, the only child of nonconsanguineous parents, who presented at 4 months of age with enlarged knees that could not fully extend. His length (-0.5 SDS) and head circumference (+0.5 SDS) were normal. A skeletal survey at 6 months showed generalized abnormalities: ovoid vertebral bodies, spiky widening of the metaphyses, irregular tarsal bones, slight flaring of the ribs and somewhat small epiphyses, which were more noticeable at 4 years. The skull and pelvis were normal. Serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, parathyroid hormone and 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 were also normal. His growth was normal, and his only complaints were a slightly abnormal gait and an inability to run easily. He had a forearm fracture at 8 years, which healed well. The radiologic abnormalities improved, and by 11 years only flattening of the cervical vertebral bodies and irregular lumbar vertebral endplates remained. At 12 years he had enlarged knees and elbows, reduced movement in the lumbar spine and knees, winged scapulae, hypermobile fingers and flat feet. His height was normal (+1 SDS), but his trunk was relatively short, with a sitting height/height ratio of -2.0 SDS. On DEXA at 13 years, bone mineral density Z-scores were 0.2 in the lumbar spine and -2.1 in the proximal femur. Puberty began at 11.5 years. His cognitive development was above average, but he had autistic features that often led to panic attacks and school absenteeism. He also had cystic fibrosis, an incidental finding on exome sequencing.

Tise et al. (2025) reported 5 children from 4 families who were identified through exome or genome sequencing and GeneMatcher. All of them presented with concern for short stature and had scoliosis and/or skeletal dysplasia. Proband 1 was a 3-year-old boy of European ancestry born to nonconsanguineous parents. He presented at 2 years with increasing hip pain and had a history of bilateral developmental hip dysplasia that required bracing until 9 months of age. He had proportionate short stature (z-score below -2 SD) and mild dysmorphic facial features: bilateral epicanthal folds, a flattened nasal bridge, and a short, anteverted nose. He also had tapered fingers and deep-set nails of the halluces. Serial pelvic x-rays from 9 months showed delayed, stippled femoral ossification consistent with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. A spine x-ray at 2 years showed mild anterior beaking of the lumbar vertebrae.

Proband 2 of Tise et al. (2025) was a girl of Asian ancestry born to nonconsanguineous parents. Bowed legs were noted when she began walking at about 14 months. At 3 years she had tibial bowing and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, with small, fragmented proximal capital femoral epiphyses and wedge-shaped vertebral bodies. By 11 years the leg bowing had improved spontaneously, but she had developed mild scoliosis. At 12 years her height was 137.7 cm (3rd percentile, z = -1.85).

Probands 3 (male) and 4 (female) of Tise et al. (2025) were fraternal twins conceived by in vitro fertilization and born at 34 weeks' gestation to nonconsanguineous parents. In proband 3, x-rays taken at 10 months for pectus carinatum showed abnormal vertebrae. His skeletal surveys showed exaggerated thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis, anterior beaking of thoracic and lumbar vertebral bodies, short and broad femoral necks, and flaring of the femoral and tibial metaphyses. These findings were consistent with spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia (SEMD) and were interpreted as most consistent with Kniest dysplasia. He had a 12-degree thoracic curve at 4 years, which progressed to S-shaped scoliosis with a 28-degree thoracic curve by 13 years. His other history included a type 1 Chiari malformation, an atrial septal defect, idiopathic thrombocytopenia and strabismus. Proband 4 had skeletal surveys at 14 months and 2 years showing ovoid vertebrae with anterior wedging, broad and short femoral necks, and widened metaphyses of the tubular bones, also consistent with SEMD. She had broad thumbs, 2-3 toe syndactyly, and short, broad halluces. At 10 years their heights were 129.6 cm (5th percentile) and 134.4 cm (20th percentile), respectively. Urine glycosaminoglycan analysis was normal in proband 3, and COL2A1 sequencing found no variants in either twin.

Proband 5 of Tise et al. (2025) was a boy, the only child of nonconsanguineous parents, who presented at 14 years with concern for scoliosis. He had proportionate short stature (157.3 cm, z = -2.13). Radiographs showed a 42-degree right thoracic curve and a 26-degree left thoracic curve, reduced vertebral body height of multiple mid and lower thoracic vertebrae, and concave endplates. He also had mild to moderate neurocognitive impairment, Asperger disorder and anxiety disorders. Tise et al. (2025) noted that a previously reported child homozygous for R12X had autistic features, and that more patients would be needed to determine whether SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants are associated with a neurodevelopmental phenotype.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by van de Kamp et al. (2023), plasma sulfate was severely reduced, which the authors described as a reduction of more than 80%. Plasma sulfate in both heterozygous parents was also below the control range. The patient's fractional sulfate excretion was close to 100% (reference range, 13% to 34%), consistent with complete loss of renal sulfate reabsorption. His mother's fractional sulfate excretion was 45%. Serum cholesterol sulfate was reduced (443 ng/ml; reference range 500-1236 ng/ml), whereas other serum steroid sulfates were within the reference range. The percentage of chondroitin sulfate disaccharides in urinary glycosaminoglycans did not differ significantly between the patient, his parents and controls.

Tise et al. (2025) were able to measure sulfate in 2 of their 5 probands. In proband 1, urinary sulfate excretion was elevated (sulfate/creatinine ratio 10.96; reference 0.5-6.0), while both parents had normal urinary sulfate excretion (1.71 and 3.81). Proband 2 had a reduced plasma sulfate of 0.107 mM (reference 0.3-0.5 mM). The text gives her fractional excretion index of sulfate as 0.38 (reference 0.17-0.34), but Table 1 of the same paper lists 0.038. The authors noted that her plasma sulfate was similar to the 0.065 mM reported in a child homozygous for R12X.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HSSD in the reported patients was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. van de Kamp et al. (2023) found a homozygous SLC13A1 mutation in a patient whose parents were both heterozygous. Tise et al. (2025) found biallelic SLC13A1 variants in 5 children from 4 families, and they stated that this was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

In a Dutch boy with HSSD, van de Kamp et al. (2023) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.34C-T transition in the first exon of the SLC13A1 gene, predicted to cause an arg12-to-ter (R12X) substitution. This variant had previously been shown to cause total loss of function in Xenopus laevis oocytes. The patient also carried a homozygous CFTR mutation. Both variants lay within a 20-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 7q31.32, and both of his nonconsanguineous parents were heterozygous for both variants, suggesting a common ancestor.

In 5 children from 4 families with HSSD, Tise et al. (2025) identified homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the SLC13A1 gene by exome or genome sequencing. Proband 1 was compound heterozygous for R12X, inherited from his father, and a c.1343G-A transition resulting in a gly448-to-asp (G448D) substitution, inherited from his mother. Proband 2 was homozygous for a c.1744T-C transition resulting in a tyr582-to-his (Y582H) substitution, which was found by a targeted search of SLC13A1 after trio genome sequencing had not identified a cause in a known disease gene. The twin probands 3 and 4 were compound heterozygous for a c.1547T-C transition resulting in a leu516-to-pro (L516P) substitution and a c.144G-A transition resulting in a trp48-to-ter (W48X) substitution. The text states that L516P was paternal and W48X maternal, whereas Table 1 assigns them the other way. Proband 5 was homozygous for W48X. The 4 missense variants tested in MDCK cells, R237C, G448D, L516P and Y582H, all caused complete loss of [35S]-sulfate transport activity. Plasma membrane expression of these variants was similar to that of wildtype.

### Clinical Management

van de Kamp et al. (2023) gave their patient intravenous N-acetylcysteine (NAC) while he was on a protein-restricted diet. The dose was a bolus of 133 mg/kg over 60 minutes, followed by 133 mg/kg over 8 hours. Plasma sulfate normalized after the bolus and during the maintenance infusion but returned to baseline 15 hours after the infusion stopped. The patient developed erythema during the bolus, which resolved after intravenous clemastine. Because of a possible increased risk of hepatotoxicity, he was strongly advised to avoid acetaminophen. Tise et al. (2025) also advised caution in giving acetaminophen and other drugs metabolized by sulfation to individuals with biallelic SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants.

### Population Genetics

van de Kamp et al. (2023) noted that the SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants R12X and W48X have relatively high global allele frequencies of 0.20% and 0.05%, respectively. Homozygosity for R12X was reported once in the gnomAD database. They hypothesized that biallelic SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants may be more common than recognized and that mild presentations may go undiagnosed. Tise et al. (2025) reported that, in gnomAD v4.1.0, R12X was observed in 3,884 heterozygotes and 7 homozygotes. ⚠︎

SLC13A1, ARG12TER [NM_022444.3:c.34C>T; p.(Arg12Ter); p.(Arg12*)]

In a Dutch boy with HSSD born to nonconsanguineous parents, van de Kamp et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.34C-T transition in exon 1 of the SLC13A1 gene, resulting in an arg12-to-ter (R12X) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing. Both parents were heterozygous, and the variant lay within a 20-Mb region of homozygosity. The variant had previously been shown to cause total loss of function in Xenopus laevis oocytes. The patient had severely reduced plasma sulfate and a fractional sulfate excretion close to 100%.

In a 3-year-old boy of European ancestry with HSSD (proband 1), Tise et al. (2025) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the SLC13A1 gene by trio exome sequencing: R12X, inherited from his father, and G448D, inherited from his mother. His urinary sulfate/creatinine ratio was elevated at 10.96.
