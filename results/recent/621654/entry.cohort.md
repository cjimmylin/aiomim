### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hyposulfatemia with skeletal dysplasia (HSSD) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the SLC13A1 gene (606193) on chromosome 7q31.

### Description

Hyposulfatemia with skeletal dysplasia is an autosomal recessive disorder. It is characterized by reduced plasma sulfate and increased urinary sulfate excretion, together with skeletal involvement. The skeletal findings range from short stature and scoliosis to a mild spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia (van de Kamp et al., 2023; Tise et al., 2025). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Van de Kamp et al. (2023) reported a Dutch boy whose parents were nonconsanguineous. He presented at 4 months of age with enlarged knees that could not fully extend. His length (-0.5 SDS) and head circumference (+0.5 SDS) were normal. Radiographs showed ovoid vertebral bodies, spiky widening of the metaphyses, irregular tarsal bones, slight flaring of the ribs, and small irregular epiphyses with delayed epiphyseal ossification. The skull and pelvis were normal. Later findings included abnormal gait, a forearm fracture, enlarged elbows, reduced movement of the lumbar spine and knees, winged scapulae, hypermobile fingers, flat feet, and a relatively short trunk. He did not have short stature. Bone mineral density of the proximal femur was reduced (Z-score -2.1), and bone age was advanced. He had autistic features and panic attacks but no intellectual disability, seizures, joint pain, or skin abnormalities. Serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, PTH, and vitamin D were normal. The early spondyloepimetaphyseal abnormalities improved with age. By 11 years, only flattening of the cervical vertebral bodies and irregular lumbar endplates remained. He also had persistent fatigue, chronic cough, recurrent respiratory infections, and elevated sweat chloride. These findings were attributed to another cause.

Tise et al. (2025) described 5 children from 4 families. They had come to attention because of concern about short stature and were found to have scoliosis and/or skeletal dysplasia. None of the families were consanguineous.

Proband 1 of Tise et al. (2025) was a 3-year-old boy of European ancestry. He presented at 2 years of age with increasing hip pain. He had a history of bilateral developmental hip dysplasia that required bracing until 9 months of age. He had proportionate short stature, bilateral epicanthal folds, a flattened nasal bridge, a short anteverted nose, tapered fingers, and deep-set nails of the halluces. Radiographs showed delayed and stippled ossification of the capital femoral epiphyses, consistent with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia, and mild anterior beaking of the lumbar vertebrae.

Proband 2 of Tise et al. (2025) was an Asian girl with onset at 14 months. At 3 years of age, orthopedic evaluation showed tibial bowing and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, with small, fragmented proximal capital femoral epiphyses and wedge-shaped vertebral bodies. When she was reevaluated at 11 years, the leg bowing had improved spontaneously, but she had developed mild scoliosis. She had proportionate short stature. ⚠︎

Probands 3 and 4 of Tise et al. (2025) were male and female twins born at 34 weeks' gestation. Proband 3 had onset at 10 months. Skeletal surveys showed exaggerated thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis, and anterior beaking of several thoracic and lumbar vertebral bodies with platyspondyly. They also showed short, broad femoral necks and proximal and distal flaring of the femoral and tibial metaphyses, consistent with spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia (SEMD), as well as nonossification of the proximal humeral epiphyses. He had S-shaped thoracolumbar scoliosis, pectus carinatum, broad toes, and proportionate stature that was shorter than expected. His past medical history included a type 1 Chiari malformation with resolved ventriculomegaly, a surgically repaired atrial septal defect, idiopathic thrombocytopenia, and strabismus. Urine glycosaminoglycans were normal. His twin sister (proband 4) had skeletal surveys at 14 months and 2 years of age. These showed ovoid vertebral bodies with anterior wedging, broad and short femoral necks, widened metaphyses of the tubular bones, and fragmented proximal epiphyses, consistent with SEMD. She had a normal gait, broad thumbs, 2-3 toe syndactyly, and short, broad halluces. Her height was 134.4 cm (20th percentile, z = -0.85), which was shorter than expected.

Proband 5 of Tise et al. (2025) was a boy, the only child of healthy nonconsanguineous parents. He presented at 14 years of age with concern about scoliosis. He had proportionate short stature (157.3 cm, 1.7th percentile, z = -2.13) with normal weight. Imaging showed thoracolumbar scoliosis, diminished thoracic vertebral body height, and concave vertebral endplates, with normal bone mineralization. He also had mild to moderate neurocognitive impairment, Asperger disorder, an attentional disorder, generalized and social anxiety disorder, masked depression, and obsessive-compulsive personality traits.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by van de Kamp et al. (2023), fractional sulfate excretion was close to 100% (reference, 13-34%), consistent with complete loss of renal sulfate reabsorption. Plasma sulfate was reduced by more than 80%. Both heterozygous parents had plasma sulfate below the control range, and the mother had a fractional sulfate excretion of 45%. In the patient, the percentage of chondroitin sulfate disaccharides in urinary glycosaminoglycans did not differ significantly from that in his parents and controls. Serum cholesterol sulfate was reduced, whereas other steroid sulfates were normal.

Tise et al. (2025) found reduced plasma sulfate and/or increased urinary sulfate excretion in both probands they tested. In proband 1, urinary sulfate excretion was elevated (sulfate/creatinine ratio, 10.96; reference, 0.5-6.0), whereas both parents had normal ratios (father, 1.71; mother, 3.81). In proband 2, the fractional excretion index of sulfate was increased at 0.38 (reference, 0.17-0.34) and plasma sulfate was reduced at 0.107 mM (reference, 0.3-0.5 mM).

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HSSD is autosomal recessive. In the patient reported by van de Kamp et al. (2023), who was homozygous for an SLC13A1 mutation, both parents were heterozygous carriers. Tise et al. (2025) found biallelic inheritance of SLC13A1 variants in all of their patients, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

In a Dutch boy with HSSD, van de Kamp et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.34C-T transition in exon 1 of the SLC13A1 gene (NM_022444.3), resulting in an arg12-to-ter (R12X) substitution. The boy had a 20-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 7q31.32. This region contained both SLC13A1 and the CFTR gene, and the boy was also homozygous for an ala455-to-glu (A455E) variant in CFTR. The authors did not consider the CFTR variant causal for the skeletal disorder. The parents were heterozygous for both variants, suggesting a common ancestor. Van de Kamp et al. (2023) noted that the R12X variant had previously been shown to cause total loss of function in Xenopus laevis oocytes. ⚠︎

Tise et al. (2025) identified biallelic SLC13A1 mutations in 5 children from 4 families with HSSD. In proband 1, trio exome sequencing found compound heterozygous mutations: a paternally inherited R12X and a maternally inherited gly448-to-asp (G448D) substitution. In proband 2, a targeted search of SLC13A1 found a homozygous tyr582-to-his (Y582H) mutation with confirmed biparental inheritance. In the affected twins (probands 3 and 4), quad exome sequencing found compound heterozygous mutations: a paternally inherited leu516-to-pro (L516P) substitution and a maternally inherited trp48-to-ter (W48X) mutation. In proband 5, trio genome sequencing found a homozygous W48X mutation with confirmed biparental inheritance. Tise et al. (2025) expressed the missense variants R237C, G448D, L516P, and Y582H in stably transfected MDCK cells. None showed detectable [35S]-sulfate transport, but their plasma membrane localization was similar to wildtype, suggesting that the substitutions affect transport rather than trafficking. They also built a homology model of SLC13A1 based on the SLC13A5 structure. The model predicted that the missense substitutions disrupt contacts and hydrogen bonds or introduce clashes in transmembrane helices TM5, TM9, and TM10 or in the extracellular C terminus. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Tise et al. (2025) noted that their patient who was homozygous for the W48X nonsense mutation had a neurodevelopmental phenotype. They stated that further study is needed to determine whether SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants, or possibly only nonsense variants, are associated with neurodevelopmental features.

### Diagnosis

Tise et al. (2025) recommended measuring sulfate in plasma and urine to help interpret SLC13A1 variants. They also recommended adding SLC13A1 to gene panels for short stature, scoliosis, and skeletal dysplasia.

### Clinical Management

In their patient, van de Kamp et al. (2023) found that intravenous N-acetylcysteine normalized plasma sulfate. However, levels returned to baseline 15 hours after the infusion was stopped. Because of a possible increased risk of acetaminophen hepatotoxicity, the patient was advised to avoid acetaminophen.

Tise et al. (2025) suggested that oral sulfate supplementation, given directly or through sources such as N-acetyl-L-cysteine, warrants evaluation. They advised caution in giving acetaminophen and other drugs metabolized by sulfation to individuals with biallelic SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants.

### Population Genetics

Van de Kamp et al. (2023) noted relatively high global allele frequencies for the SLC13A1 loss-of-function variants R12X (0.20%) and W48X (0.05%), with 1 R12X homozygote in gnomAD. They hypothesized that biallelic loss-of-function variants are more common than recognized and may present later in life with degenerative bone and joint disease.

Tise et al. (2025) noted that the G448D, L516P, and Y582H variants were not found in the homozygous state in gnomAD v4.1.0. Their heterozygous allele counts were 221, 7, and 3, respectively.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HYPOSULFATEMIA WITH SKELETAL DYSPLASIA**  
SLC13A1, ARG12TER [c.34C>T; g.122839967G>A; p.R12; p.R12*]

In a Dutch boy with HSSD, van de Kamp et al. (2023) identified a homozygous c.34C-T transition in exon 1 of the SLC13A1 gene (NM_022444.3), resulting in an arg12-to-ter (R12X) substitution. The boy had a 20-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 7q31.32, and his parents were heterozygous for the variant. The authors noted that this variant had previously been shown to cause total loss of function in Xenopus laevis oocytes. Its global allele frequency was 0.20%, with 1 homozygote in gnomAD.

In a 3-year-old European boy with HSSD (proband 1), Tise et al. (2025) identified compound heterozygosity for the R12X mutation, inherited from his father, and a G448D mutation inherited from his mother. The mutations were found by trio exome sequencing. The proband had elevated urinary sulfate excretion, whereas both parents had normal urinary sulfate.

**.0002 HYPOSULFATEMIA WITH SKELETAL DYSPLASIA**  
SLC13A1, GLY448ASP [c.1343G>A; p.G448D]

In a boy with HSSD (proband 1), Tise et al. (2025) identified compound heterozygosity for a c.1343G-A transition in the SLC13A1 gene (NM_022444.4), resulting in a gly448-to-asp (G448D) substitution, inherited from his mother, and the R12X mutation inherited from his father. In stably transfected MDCK cells, the G448D variant showed no detectable [35S]-sulfate transport, although its plasma membrane localization was similar to wildtype.
