### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immune dysregulation with autoimmunity, autoinflammation, and immunodeficiency (IDAAI) is caused by homozygous mutation in the IRAK2 gene.

### Description

Immune dysregulation with autoimmunity, autoinflammation, and immunodeficiency (IDAAI) is an autosomal recessive disorder with a broad and variable phenotype. Most patients have recurrent infections, autoantibody production, and gastrointestinal ulceration. Patients can be grouped into immunodeficiency-predominant, autoimmunity-predominant, and autoinflammation-predominant subgroups. Reported manifestations include recurrent infections, systemic lupus erythematosus with lupus nephritis, Hashimoto thyroiditis, autoimmune encephalitis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, and inflammatory ulceration of the digestive tract. Some patients have an elevated type I interferon signature (Fei et al., 2026).

### Nomenclature

Fei et al. (2026) called the disorder DIRAK2 (deficiency of IRAK2).

### Clinical Features

Fei et al. (2026) reported 12 patients from 11 unrelated, nonconsanguineous Chinese families with immune dysregulation. Both males and females were affected. Eleven patients were clinically affected, and 1 (P4) was preclinical. Age at onset ranged from 1 month to adulthood. Six patients (50%) had recurrent infections that required hospitalization, with gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Two patients (P1 and P2) had recurrent pulmonary infections and were diagnosed with primary immunodeficiency. Chest CT showed dense consolidation with air bronchogram and ground-glass opacities in P1, and patchy ground-glass opacities and centrilobular nodules in P2. P2 died at age 65 years from severe pneumonia and macrophage activation syndrome. Six patients (50%) had autoantibodies. Four of them (P3, P5, P6, and P7) were diagnosed with systemic lupus erythematosus with lupus nephritis. In P3, renal biopsy showed mesangial, subendothelial, and epithelial deposits, electron-dense mesangial deposits, and diffuse IgG deposition in the mesangial areas and capillary loops. One patient (P11) had Hashimoto thyroiditis. Another patient had a progressively elevated type I interferon signature together with rising antinuclear antibody titers. Two patients mainly had gastrointestinal involvement, with abdominal pain, vomiting, and bloody stools. Endoscopy showed multiple ulcers throughout the digestive tract. Histopathology showed active or chronic inflammation, cryptitis, and crypt abscesses in the ileocecal region, and an ileal biopsy from P8 showed crypt distortion and immune cell infiltration in the lamina propria. P10 was diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic arthritis, with pain and restricted mobility of the left knee and hips. MRI in P10 showed an abnormal signal in the left ilium next to the sacroiliac joint and mild bilateral hip effusions with synovial thickening. P12 was diagnosed with autoimmune encephalitis. Based on their main manifestations, Fei et al. (2026) grouped the patients as immunodeficiency-predominant (P1 and P2), autoimmunity-predominant (P3-P7, P11, and P12), or autoinflammation-predominant (P8-P10). They noted that infections in this cohort were generally less severe than those reported in MyD88 or IRAK4 deficiency.

### Biochemical Features

Fei et al. (2026) found significantly higher serum IFN-beta levels in 6 patients (P1, P3, P4, P8, P9, and P11) than in unaffected controls. Baseline phosphorylated IRF3 was markedly elevated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from P1, P8, and P11. Patients also showed expansion of CD8+ T cells. After LPS stimulation, gene set enrichment analysis of patient PBMCs showed upregulated interferon-alpha and interferon-gamma responses, and type I interferon pathway genes were upregulated in inflammatory monocytes. In a principal component analysis based on interferon-related genes, samples from patients with autoimmunity or autoinflammation clustered close to those from patients with interferonopathies.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IDAAI in the families reported by Fei et al. (2026) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In all 11 pedigrees, disease status segregated with biallelic inheritance of the IRAK2 mutation, and all heterozygous carriers were unaffected. One homozygous patient was preclinical, which suggested possible reduced penetrance.

### Molecular Genetics

Fei et al. (2026) studied 12 patients with IDAAI from 11 unrelated Chinese families. Using whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing, they identified the same homozygous copy number variant in the IRAK2 gene in all patients: a deletion-insertion causing an in-frame deletion of exon 2 (c.95_277del; p.Ala32_Trp93delinsGly), designated IRAK2-delta-ex2. Sanger sequencing confirmed that all patients shared the same genomic breakpoints. No other rare variants or copy number variants were found in genes previously associated with immune disorders. The mutant transcript was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype. The deleted region lies at the interaction surface of the IRAK2 death domain with IRAK4. The mutant protein failed to interact with IRAK4 but still interacted with TRAF6. In HEK293T-TLR4 cells, it failed to activate NF-kappa-B and MAPK signaling. Patient PBMCs showed reduced induction of IL1B, IL6, and IL10 after LPS or R848 stimulation, and the defects were most pronounced in monocytes and dendritic cells. IRAK2-knockout THP-1 cells reconstituted with the mutant had a higher interferon response than cells reconstituted with wildtype IRAK2. This response was suppressed by inhibitors of TIRAP, TRIF, or TBK1/IKK-epsilon and by TRIF knockdown, and was partly reduced by TAK1 inhibition. Fei et al. (2026) concluded that the type I interferon signaling was amplified through a TRIF-dependent pathway, with a secondary contribution from the NF-kappa-B and MAPK pathways.

Amali et al. (2024) reported novel IRAK2 mutations in 2 patients with repeated or sequential severe invasive infections. One patient had herpes simplex virus-triggered hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis together with tuberculosis. The other had Streptococcus pneumoniae bacteremia and candidemia. The mutations impaired the ability of IRAK2 to ubiquitinate, or functionally modify, TRAF6 and led to impaired TNF-alpha production. Fei et al. (2026) found that 2 previously reported IRAK2 single-nucleotide variants, L78P and R506W, preserved the ability to activate NF-kappa-B, and they classified these variants as benign.

### Clinical Management

Fei et al. (2026) reported that P1 and P2 were treated with antibiotics and intravenous immunoglobulin for recurrent infections. Most of the other patients received immunosuppressive therapy, including glucocorticoids, hydroxychloroquine, cyclophosphamide, tacrolimus or sirolimus, mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine, belimumab or telitacicept, infliximab, and methotrexate. In PBMCs from 2 untreated patients (P4 and P11), the progressively elevated type I interferon signature was reduced by baricitinib ex vivo. Baricitinib also reduced type I interferon signaling in IRAK2-knockout THP-1 cells. The authors suggested that targeting the interferon pathway may be a possible treatment for patients with autoimmune manifestations, but stated that further study is needed.

### Population Genetics

Fei et al. (2026) noted that the IRAK2-delta-ex2 mutation is listed in gnomAD SVs v4.1.0 at an overall population frequency of 0.0005631. It is more frequent in East Asians (0.01431), and 2 homozygous individuals are recorded. The authors suggested that the deletion region is a hotspot for Alu-mediated homologous recombination. They also suggested that IRAK2 deficiency may be an underrecognized cause of immune dysregulation in East Asians.

### Animal Model

Fei et al. (2026) generated homozygous Irak2 knock-in mice lacking exon 2. After high-dose intraperitoneal LPS, the mutant mice had higher survival, lower serum cytokine and chemokine levels, lower Il1b and Il6 expression in kidney, liver, and spleen, and less severe acute liver injury than wildtype mice. In an intraperitoneal E. coli infection model, the mutant mice cleared bacteria less effectively and showed reduced inflammatory cytokine induction. Bone marrow-derived macrophages from the mutant mice had defective signaling through Tlr4, Tlr7/8, Tlr2/Tlr6, Tlr1/Tlr2, and Tlr9, whereas Tlr3 signaling was intact. These macrophages also showed increased Ifnb1 expression. At 50 weeks of age, the mutant mice did not differ from wildtype littermates in body weight, spleen index, autoantibodies, serum cytokines, tissue inflammation, or kidney histology. The authors noted that mice express 4 Irak2 isoforms, 2 of which naturally lack exon 2, and that this may reduce the effect of the mutation in mice. ⚠︎
