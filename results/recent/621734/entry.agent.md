### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myopathy with rigid spine, scoliosis, and male-limited hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (MRSG) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the TBCE gene, which encodes tubulin-specific chaperone E. ⚠︎

### Description

MRSG is a TBCE-related disorder that is milder than hypoparathyroidism-retardation-dysmorphism syndrome and Kenny-Caffey syndrome type 1. Symptoms begin in childhood. Features include muscle atrophy and weakness, a rigid spine, scoliosis, and testicular failure with cryptorchidism in males. Intellectual disability is absent or mild, and short stature may be present. Some patients also have recurrent infections, neutropenia, eosinophilia, and nocturnal hypoxemia, while calcium-phosphate metabolism and brain MRI are normal (Badura-Stronka et al., 2025; Sparber et al., 2025). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) reported 3 unrelated patients, 2 of Polish and 1 of Ukrainian descent, none with a significant family history. Patient 1, a 13-year-old Polish girl, walked independently at 14 months and had slightly delayed speech. At age 6 she developed muscle weakness and limited mobility of the cervical spine, with increased tone in the paraspinal muscles. Over the following years the rigid spine progressed to involve the thoracic and lumbar segments, and thoracic scoliosis worsened from age 11. At age 13 she had reduced strength in the proximal muscles, limb muscle atrophy, diminished tendon reflexes, and a waddling gait. Her weight and height were below the 3rd percentile. She had nocturnal hypoxemia with central, obstructive, and mixed apneas and was started on nocturnal noninvasive ventilation. Densitometry showed osteoporosis (Z-score -1.5, T-score -3.9). Blood tests showed eosinophilia, neutropenia, and platelet anisocytosis without thrombocytopenia. ⚠︎

Patient 2 of Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) was a 20-year-old Polish man with bilateral cryptorchidism. He had mildly delayed motor development, walking at 15 months, and delayed speech. Starting at age 4 he had febrile seizures during pneumonia, and pneumonia continued to recur. Generalized muscle pain began at age 5. By age 11 he had difficulty rising from a squat, distal limb paresis, and contractures of the shoulders, elbows, and ankles. At 13 he had limited spinal mobility, muscle atrophy mainly of the shoulder girdle, and nocturnal hypoxemia, for which he was started on nocturnal noninvasive ventilation. Muscle MRI showed marked atrophy of the paraspinal muscles and fatty degeneration of the iliopsoas and psoas muscles. Cervical spine MRI showed kyphotic alignment and a partial cleft in the C2-C3 vertebral arches. At 14 he had gynecomastia and osteopenia. At 18 he had convergent strabismus, a high palate, scoliosis, and multiple contractures. Rigidity of the paraspinal muscles prevented him from flexing the thoracic and lumbar spine. Eosinophilia and neutropenia had been present consistently for 10 years. EMG showed moderate myogenic features with normal nerve conduction, and a muscle biopsy at age 11 showed mild, nonspecific myopathic changes. He was not intellectually disabled but was diagnosed with ADHD at age 10.

Patient 3 of Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) was an 18-year-old Ukrainian man with a birth weight of 2,800 g. He had bilateral inguinal cryptorchidism, treated by orchidopexy at ages 3 and 4. Motor development was normal. Recurrent respiratory infections, otitis, and bronchitis began at age 3. The testes were removed at ages 10 and 15, and histology showed atrophic and sclerotic changes with no spermatogenesis. He had intellectual disability and dysmorphic features. He also had neutropenia but no eosinophilia, and a rigid spine was not reported.

Across the 3 patients, Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) found that symptoms began at a mean age of 5.0 years. All 3 patients learned to walk and to speak. Thyroid and parathyroid function was normal in all of them, and brain MRI showed no clinically significant changes. Compared with patients carrying other TBCE variants identified in a systematic review of 98 patients, these patients were distinguished by later onset, normal motor development, eventual normal speech, cryptorchidism in males, and predominantly proximal muscle atrophy. ⚠︎

Sparber et al. (2025) reported 7 patients with a novel, milder TBCE-related phenotype. The patients had amyotrophy, testicular failure, and mild intellectual disability, with or without short stature.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MRSG is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) found homozygous mutations in affected individuals and heterozygous carrier status in their parents. Sparber et al. (2025) found homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in affected individuals.

### Molecular Genetics

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) performed whole-exome sequencing in 3 patients with MRSG, 2 of Polish and 1 of Ukrainian descent. All 3 were homozygous for a splice site mutation in the TBCE gene (c.100+1G-A). The parents were confirmed to be heterozygous carriers. The mutation affects the donor splice site in intron 2 and was predicted to disrupt splicing. The authors noted that this variant had so far been identified only in patients of Central-Eastern European descent, suggesting a possible founder mutation.

Sparber et al. (2025) found that all 7 of their patients were either homozygous or compound heterozygous for the TBCE c.100+1G-A mutation. Splicing analysis showed that the variant altered splicing and caused an early frameshift. However, the authors found that translation from an alternative open reading frame produced a partially functional protein, which they proposed accounts for the milder phenotype. In patient dermal fibroblasts, Golgi compactness was reduced but microtubule dynamics were normal. Electron microscopy showed varying degrees of acto-myosin degradation.

### Population Genetics

Sparber et al. (2025) found that the TBCE c.100+1G-A variant was 10 times more frequent in Slavic samples than in gnomAD. They suggested that this phenotype is underdiagnosed.

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) identified a homozygous G-to-A transition in intron 2 of the TBCE gene (c.100+1G-A) in 3 patients with MRSG: 2 of Polish and 1 of Ukrainian descent. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing, and the parents were confirmed to be heterozygous carriers. The variant affects the donor splice site of intron 2. ClinVar listed conflicting interpretations: pathogenic (2), likely pathogenic (3), and uncertain significance (1).

Sparber et al. (2025) found the c.100+1G-A mutation in the homozygous or compound heterozygous state in 7 patients with MRSG. The variant caused a splicing alteration and an early frameshift, but translation from an alternative open reading frame produced a partially functional protein. The variant was 10 times more frequent in Slavic samples than in gnomAD.
