### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myopathy with rigid spine, scoliosis, and male-limited hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (MRSG) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the TBCE gene (604934) on chromosome 1q42.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Myopathy with rigid spine, scoliosis, and male-limited hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (MRSG) is an autosomal recessive disorder. Its main features are childhood-onset muscle weakness and atrophy, mainly of proximal muscles, together with a rigid spine and scoliosis. Affected males have cryptorchidism and testicular failure. Patients may also have speech delay or mild intellectual disability, short stature, nocturnal hypoxemia, neutropenia, and eosinophilia. The phenotype is milder than that of other TBCE-related disorders (Badura-Stronka et al., 2025; Sparber et al., 2025). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) reported 3 unrelated patients, 2 Polish and 1 Ukrainian, none with a significant family history. Patient 1 was a 13-year-old Polish girl. Her first symptoms appeared at age 6 years: muscle weakness and limited mobility of the cervical spine. She had slight speech delay, proximal muscle weakness, limb muscle atrophy, diminished tendon reflexes, a waddling gait, a rigid spine, and thoracic scoliosis. Her weight and height were both below the 3rd percentile. She had nocturnal hypoxemia with central and obstructive sleep apneas. Densitometry showed osteoporosis (Z-score -1.5, T-score -3.9). Blood tests showed eosinophilia, neutropenia, and platelet anisocytosis without thrombocytopenia. Brain imaging showed a thinned trunk of the corpus callosum. Thyroid and parathyroid function were normal, and she did not have intellectual disability.

Patient 2 of Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) was a 20-year-old Polish man born at term with a normal birth weight. Bilateral cryptorchidism was noted at birth. He had mild motor delay, speech delay, febrile seizures, and recurrent pneumonia, and his symptoms began at age 4 years. Later findings were: - Musculoskeletal: generalized muscle pain, distal limb paresis, and contractures of the shoulders, elbows, wrists, and ankles. He had a rigid spine, thoracic scoliosis, cervical kyphosis at C3-C5, and a partial cleft of the C2-C3 vertebral arches. - Muscle imaging and testing: atrophy of the shoulder girdle and paraspinal muscles, and fatty degeneration of the iliopsoas and gluteal muscles on MRI. EMG was myogenic, and muscle biopsy showed mild nonspecific myopathic changes. - Respiratory: nocturnal hypoxemia. - Endocrine and bone: at age 14 he had gynecomastia and abnormal densitometry (Z-score 0.3, T-score -1.5), consistent with osteopenia. A small hypophysis was noted at age 10. - Other: convergent strabismus, a high palate, diffuse red skin patches, and attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder. - Blood: eosinophilia and neutropenia were seen consistently over 10 years, from age 10. He did not have intellectual disability.

Patient 3 of Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) was an 18-year-old Ukrainian man. He had previously been briefly mentioned in a report on possible disorders of sex development in Ukrainian patients. His symptoms began at age 3 years. He had bilateral inguinal cryptorchidism and underwent gonadectomy of 1 testis at age 10 and the other at age 15. Histology of both showed atrophic and sclerotic testes with no signs of spermatogenesis. He also had recurrent respiratory infections, otitis, and bronchitis. Other findings were speech delay, intellectual disability, dysmorphic features, neutropenia without eosinophilia, a suspected pituitary microadenoma, and a pineal cyst.

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) noted that muscle atrophy in their patients mainly affected proximal muscles. Increased tone was most marked in the paraspinal muscles and produced a rigid spine, a feature not previously documented in TBCE-related conditions.

Sparber et al. (2025) reported 7 patients with a novel, milder TBCE phenotype. The patients had amyotrophy, testicular failure, and mild intellectual disability, and some also had short stature.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MRSG was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the 3 patients reported by Badura-Stronka et al. (2025), the parents of each patient were heterozygous carriers of a TBCE mutation that was homozygous in the affected child. In the patients reported by Sparber et al. (2025), the mutation was homozygous or compound heterozygous.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 unrelated patients (2 Polish and 1 Ukrainian) with MRSG, Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous splice-donor mutation in intron 2 of the TBCE gene (c.100+1G-A). In each family, the parents were heterozygous carriers. ClinVar listed conflicting classifications for the variant.

Sparber et al. (2025) reported 7 patients with MRSG who were homozygous or compound heterozygous for the TBCE c.100+1G-A mutation (NM_003193.5). The mutation caused a splicing alteration and an early frameshift. However, translation from an alternative open reading frame produced a partially functional protein, which the authors proposed as the explanation for the milder phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) compared patients homozygous for the TBCE c.100+1G-A mutation with previously reported TBCE patients who carried the c.155_166del or c.464T-A variants. The c.100+1G-A homozygotes had a milder course. They had later onset (mean age 5 years), normal motor development, eventual normal speech, normal calcium-phosphate metabolism, and no significant brain MRI changes. Males also had cryptorchidism.

### Pathogenesis

Sparber et al. (2025) found that dermal fibroblasts from patients had a less compact Golgi complex but normal microtubule dynamics after nocodazole treatment. Electron microscopy of muscle showed varying degrees of acto-myosin degradation. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) noted that the TBCE c.100+1G-A mutation had so far been found only in patients of Central-Eastern European descent, which suggested a possible founder mutation in this population.

Sparber et al. (2025) found that the TBCE c.100+1G-A variant was 10 times more frequent in Slavic samples than in gnomAD, which suggested that the phenotype is underdiagnosed.
