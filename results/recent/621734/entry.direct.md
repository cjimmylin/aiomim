### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myopathy with rigid spine, scoliosis, and male-limited hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (MRSG) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the TBCE gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Myopathy with rigid spine, scoliosis, and male-limited hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (MRSG) is an autosomal recessive disorder. It is milder than other TBCE-related disorders. Onset is in childhood, with muscle weakness and atrophy that mainly affect proximal muscles, limited spinal mobility from paraspinal rigidity, and scoliosis. Males have cryptorchidism and testicular failure. Some patients have recurrent infections, neutropenia, eosinophilia, nocturnal hypoxemia, short stature, and mild intellectual disability. Calcium-phosphate metabolism is normal, and brain MRI shows no significant abnormalities (Badura-Stronka et al., 2025; Sparber et al., 2025).

### Clinical Features

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) reported 3 unrelated patients, 2 of Polish descent and 1 of Ukrainian descent. None had a significant family history. ⚠︎

Patient 1 was a 13-year-old Polish girl. She had been born at term with normal birth weight and walked independently at 14 months, but her speech was slightly delayed. Symptoms began at age 6 years with muscle weakness, limited mobility of the cervical spine, and increased tone in the paraspinal muscles. Over the next years, the rigid spine progressed to involve the thoracic and lumbar segments, and from age 11 years thoracic scoliosis worsened. Rigid spine muscular dystrophy or spondyloarthropathy was suspected. At age 13 years, examination showed reduced strength in proximal muscles, atrophy of limb muscles, diminished tendon reflexes, and a waddling gait. Weight and height were below the 3rd percentile. She had nocturnal hypoxemia, with central, obstructive, and mixed apneas during sleep, and was started on nocturnal noninvasive ventilation. Densitometry indicated osteoporosis (Z-score -1.5, T-score -3.9). Blood tests showed eosinophilia, neutropenia, and platelet anisocytosis without thrombocytopenia. EMG and nerve conduction were normal, and brain MRI showed only thinning of the trunk of the corpus callosum.

Patient 2 was a 20-year-old Polish man with bilateral cryptorchidism. His motor development was mildly delayed: he sat at 9 months and walked at 15 months. His speech was also delayed. From age 4 years he had pneumonia episodes and 5 febrile seizures. Generalized muscle pain began at age 5. At age 11 years he had difficulty rising from a squat, distal limb paresis, and contractures of the shoulders, elbows, and ankles. At age 13 he had limited spinal mobility and muscle atrophy mainly of the shoulder girdle, and nocturnal hypoxemia began, for which nocturnal noninvasive ventilation was started. Muscle MRI showed marked atrophy of the paraspinal muscles and fatty degeneration of the iliopsoas and psoas muscles. Later MRI showed atrophy of the lumbosacral paraspinal, iliopsoas, and tibialis posterior muscles, with moderate involvement of the gluteal, vastus, and semitendinosus muscles. Cervical spine MRI showed kyphotic alignment at C3-C5 and a partial cleft in the C2-C3 vertebral arches. At age 14 years he had gynecomastia and abnormal densitometry (T-score -1.5). At age 18 years he had convergent strabismus of the right eye, a high palate, thoracic scoliosis, multiple contractures, and diffuse red skin patches. Because of paraspinal rigidity he could not flex his thoracic and lumbar spine. Eosinophilia and neutropenia were present continuously for 10 years from age 10. EMG showed moderate myogenic features, and a muscle biopsy at age 11 showed mild, nonspecific myopathic changes. ADHD had been diagnosed at age 10 years. ⚠︎

Patient 3 was an 18-year-old Ukrainian man. He was born at term weighing 2,800 g (-1 SD) and had bilateral inguinal cryptorchidism, treated by orchidopexy at ages 3 and 4 years. His motor development was normal. From age 3 years he had recurrent respiratory viral infections, otitis, and bronchitis. His testes were removed at ages 10 and 15 years, and histology showed atrophic and sclerotic changes with no spermatogenesis. He had intellectual disability, dysmorphic features, and neutropenia. Brain MRI at age 16 raised suspicion of a pituitary microadenoma.

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) also compared their patients with previously reported patients with other TBCE mutations. In their 3 patients, symptoms began later (mean 5.0 years), all achieved independent walking and speech, and thyroid and parathyroid function was normal. None had microcephaly or hypocalcemic seizures, and brain MRI showed no clinically significant changes. Both male patients had cryptorchidism.

Sparber et al. (2025) reported 7 patients with a milder TBCE-related phenotype. They had amyotrophy, testicular failure, and mild intellectual disability, with or without short stature.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MRSG is autosomal recessive. Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) found homozygous TBCE mutations in affected individuals, and their parents were confirmed to be heterozygous carriers. Sparber et al. (2025) found affected individuals to be homozygous or compound heterozygous for TBCE mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) used whole-exome sequencing in 3 unrelated patients with MRSG, 2 Polish and 1 Ukrainian. All 3 were homozygous for a splice site mutation in the TBCE gene (c.100+1G-A) affecting the donor splice site of intron 2. The parents were confirmed to be heterozygous carriers. ⚠︎

Sparber et al. (2025) found 7 patients with MRSG who were homozygous or compound heterozygous for the c.100+1G-A mutation in TBCE. RT-PCR products were analyzed by deep next-generation sequencing, which showed that the mutation altered splicing and caused an early frameshift. However, translation from an alternative open reading frame produced a partially functional protein, which the authors proposed explained the milder phenotype. Patient dermal fibroblasts showed reduced Golgi compactness but normal microtubule dynamics. Electron microscopy showed varying degrees of acto-myosin degradation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) compared their c.100+1G-A homozygotes with patients carrying other TBCE mutations reported in the literature. Patients homozygous for an in-frame deletion (c.155_166del) had the most severe phenotype. Its features were intrauterine growth restriction, neonatal hypocalcemic seizures, dysmorphic features, microcephaly, and intellectual disability. Patients with the c.464T-A mutation had an intermediate phenotype, with onset in infancy, intellectual disability, optic atrophy, distal amyotrophy, ataxia, and spasticity, and the 5 males had no genital abnormalities. The c.100+1G-A homozygotes had the mildest course. Their symptoms began later, motor development was normal, speech eventually developed normally, and brain MRI showed no significant pathology. Males had cryptorchidism. Muscle atrophy affected mainly proximal muscles, and increased tone was most marked in the paraspinal muscles.

### Population Genetics

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) noted that the c.100+1G-A mutation in TBCE had so far been found only in patients of Central-Eastern European descent, suggesting a possible founder mutation. Sparber et al. (2025) found the mutation to be 10 times more frequent in Slavic samples than in gnomAD, suggesting that this phenotype is underdiagnosed.
