### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myopathy with rigid spine, scoliosis, and male-limited hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (MRSG) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the TBCE gene (604934) on chromosome 1q42.3. ⚠︎

### Description

MRSG is a TBCE-related disorder that is milder than other phenotypes associated with TBCE variants. Features include amyotrophy, testicular failure, and mild intellectual disability, with or without short stature (Sparber et al., 2025). Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) described later onset than in other TBCE-related disease, rigid spine, proximal muscle atrophy, scoliosis, cryptorchidism in males, neutropenia, eosinophilia, and nocturnal hypoxemia.

### Clinical Features

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) reported a 13-year-old Polish girl (P1) with no significant family history. She was born at term with normal birth weight, walked independently at 14 months, and had slight speech delay. At age 6 years she developed muscle weakness and limited mobility of the cervical spine, with increased paraspinal muscle tone. The rigid spine progressed to involve the thoracic and lumbar segments, and thoracic scoliosis worsened from age 11. At 13 years she had proximal muscle weakness, limb muscle atrophy, diminished tendon reflexes, waddling gait, and weight and height below the 3rd percentile. She had nocturnal hypoxemia with central and obstructive apneas that required nocturnal noninvasive ventilation. She also had osteoporosis (T-score -3.9), eosinophilia, neutropenia, and platelet anisocytosis.

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) also reported a 20-year-old Polish man (P2) with bilateral cryptorchidism and mildly delayed motor and speech development. He had febrile seizures from age 4 years during episodes of recurrent pneumonia and generalized muscle pain from age 5. At age 11 he had contractures and distal limb paresis, and muscle biopsy showed mild, nonspecific myopathic changes. From age 13 he had limited spine mobility, shoulder girdle atrophy, and nocturnal hypoxemia that required noninvasive ventilation. MRI showed atrophy and fatty infiltration of the paraspinal, iliopsoas, gluteal, and thigh muscles. Other features included gynecomastia at age 14, thoracic scoliosis, strabismus, high palate, inability to flex the thoracic and lumbar spine, and persistent eosinophilia and neutropenia from age 10. EMG showed moderate myogenic features.

The third patient reported by Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) was an 18-year-old Ukrainian man (P3) with bilateral inguinal cryptorchidism, which was treated by orchidopexy at ages 3 and 4 years. From age 3 he had recurrent respiratory infections, otitis, and bronchitis. He underwent gonadectomy at ages 10 and 15 years. Histology showed atrophic and sclerotic testes with no spermatogenesis. He had intellectual disability, dysmorphic features, and neutropenia, but his motor development was normal.

Sparber et al. (2025) reported 7 patients with a novel, milder TBCE-associated phenotype. The patients had amyotrophy, testicular failure, and mild intellectual disability, with or without short stature. Electron microscopy showed varying levels of acto-myosin degradation.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MRSG is autosomal recessive. In the 3 families reported by Badura-Stronka et al. (2025), affected individuals were homozygous for a TBCE mutation and all parents were confirmed to be heterozygous carriers. Sparber et al. (2025) found affected individuals to be homozygous or compound heterozygous for the same variant.

### Molecular Genetics

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) used whole-exome sequencing in 3 patients with MRSG (2 Polish and 1 Ukrainian) and identified a homozygous splice site mutation in the TBCE gene (c.100+1G-A) that affects the donor splice site of intron 2. All parents were confirmed to be heterozygous carriers. The variant had conflicting pathogenicity classifications in ClinVar and had not previously been reported in patients.

In 7 patients with a milder TBCE-related phenotype, Sparber et al. (2025) identified homozygosity or compound heterozygosity for the c.100+1G-A splice site mutation in the TBCE gene (NM_003193.5). Deep sequencing of RT-PCR products showed that the mutation altered splicing and caused an early frameshift.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) systematically reviewed 98 previously reported patients with TBCE variants together with their own 3 patients. They concluded that homozygosity for c.100+1G-A causes a milder course than other TBCE variants. Patients with this genotype had later onset (mean age 5 years), normal motor development, eventually normal speech, normal calcium-phosphate metabolism, and no significant changes on CNS MRI. Their distinctive features included cryptorchidism in males, rigid spine, proximal muscle atrophy, scoliosis, neutropenia, eosinophilia, and nocturnal hypoxemia. By contrast, patients homozygous for c.155_166del had the HRDS/KCS1 phenotype, and patients with c.464T-A had severe neurodevelopmental disease without genital abnormalities.

### Pathogenesis

Sparber et al. (2025) found that the mild phenotype associated with the TBCE c.100+1G-A mutation arises from translation of an alternative open reading frame, which produces a partially functional protein. Dermal fibroblasts from patients showed reduced Golgi compactness but normal microtubule dynamics.

### Population Genetics

Badura-Stronka et al. (2025) noted that the TBCE c.100+1G-A mutation had so far been found only in patients of Central-Eastern European descent. They suggested that it may be a founder mutation in this population.

Sparber et al. (2025) found the c.100+1G-A mutation to be 10 times more frequent in Slavic samples than in gnomAD, which suggested that this phenotype is underdiagnosed.
