### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MAGIS syndrome (MAGIS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAI2 gene (139360) on chromosome 3p21.31.

### Description

MAGIS syndrome is characterized by midline malformations of the brain, anterior pituitary gland dysfunction, growth retardation, immunodysregulation/immunodeficiency, and skeletal defects. It is caused by heterozygous germline activating (gain-of-function) mutations in GNAI2. Nearly all patients have both immune and nonimmune features, but severity varies (Lamborn et al., 2025).

### Nomenclature

Lamborn et al. (2025) used the acronym MAGIS for midline malformations of the brain, anterior pituitary gland dysfunction, growth retardation, immunodysregulation/immunodeficiency, and skeletal defects.

### Clinical Features

Hamada et al. (2017) reported an individual with periventricular nodular heterotopia and intellectual disability.

Lamborn et al. (2025) studied a cohort of 20 patients from 18 families. In this cohort, 90% had growth retardation, including intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) in 53% and postnatal short stature in 90%. Eight of 11 tested patients had low IGF1, and all 7 who were formally tested had growth hormone deficiency. Fifty-nine percent had structural or functional defects of the anterior pituitary, 30% had midline brain malformations, 85% had skeletal dysostoses, and all had dysmorphia. Immune-mediated disease was present in 95% of the cohort, with immunodeficiency in 90% and immune dysregulation in 50%. Infections were mainly sinopulmonary, and 25% had cutaneous viral infections, mainly warts. Autoimmunity (35%) included autoimmune hemolytic anemia (20%), autoimmune thrombocytopenia (10%), and psoriasis (10%). Premature mortality was 10%. Lymphocyte immunophenotyping usually showed low to normal T cells, a low ratio of naive to memory T cells, and low B cells with low serum IgM. Mitogen- or antigen-induced proliferation was normal or increased.

In family XIII, Lamborn et al. (2025) described a boy (P13) and his affected father (P14). At age 2 years, P13 had IUGR, congenital heart valve abnormalities with hydrops fetalis, dysmorphism (retrognathia, high forehead/anterior hairline, low-set ears), short femurs, hypospadias, severe congenital sensorineural hearing loss, neurodevelopmental delay with behavioral abnormalities and stereotypy, a small pons, a thin corpus callosum, constipation, gastroesophageal reflux, postnatal growth delay, urosepsis, and pan-lymphocytopenia. His newborn screen for severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) was abnormal, with an absolute T cell count of 0.577 x 10(3) cells/microliter at 7 weeks of age. His father had dysmorphism (midface retrusion, high forehead/anterior hairline, posteriorly rotated ears with other pinna abnormalities, upslanting palpebral fissures, finger clinodactyly), osteoporosis, cryptorchidism, hearing loss with vestibular dysfunction, borderline intellectual disability, epilepsy, migraine, anxiety, and a midline arachnoid cyst. He also had growth delay with short stature, an annular pancreas with malrotation of the bowel, esophageal hiatal hernia, gastroesophageal reflux with aspiration pneumonias, recurrent infections (sinusitis, otitis media, tonsillitis, paronychia), and celiac disease. His lymphocyte numbers were not measured.

In family XVII, Lamborn et al. (2025) described 2 affected brothers (P18 and P19). P18, an adult, had dysmorphism (micrognathia, high anterior hairline, low-set ears), cryptorchidism, inguinal hernia, pelvic kidney, childhood failure to thrive with adult short stature, arthritis, gout, suspected osteoporosis, chronic diarrhea, sensorineural hearing loss, compensated hypothyroidism, anxiety, depression, and asthma. He had recurrent infections (otitis media, croup, pharyngitis, bronchitis, pneumonia) and dysgammaglobulinemia (low serum IgM, poor vaccine titers). His deceased brother, P19, had dysmorphism (micrognathia, high anterior hairline, low-set ears), cryptorchidism, inguinal hernia, deviated nasal septum, IUGR with childhood failure to thrive and adult short stature, neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, ataxia, nystagmus, migraine headaches, autism spectrum disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, anxiety, depression, and psychosis. He also had irritable bowel syndrome, mild splenomegaly, recurrent infections (otitis media, pneumonia, bronchitis, sinusitis, shingles), dysgammaglobulinemia (low serum IgM, low isohemagglutinins, and variably low vaccine titers), and T cell lymphopenia.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous GNAI2 mutation found by Hamada et al. (2017) in a patient with periventricular nodular heterotopia and intellectual disability occurred de novo.

Lamborn et al. (2025) found that most GNAI2 variants in their cohort arose de novo. In 2 families the variant was transmitted in a way consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, and penetrance was full in the families studied. In family XIII an affected father passed the mutation to his affected son, and in family XVII 2 affected brothers carried the same mutation.

### Molecular Genetics

In an individual with periventricular nodular heterotopia and intellectual disability, Hamada et al. (2017) identified a de novo heterozygous missense mutation (c.680C-T, A227V) in the GNAI2 gene.

Lecoquierre et al. (2019) searched a database of more than 1,200 solo exomes for missense variants that were also listed in denovo-db, a public collection of de novo variants from patients with developmental disorders. They found 30 variants with strong evidence of pathogenicity and named GNAI2 as a good candidate gene for syndromic intellectual disability. ⚠︎

Lamborn et al. (2025) studied 20 patients from 18 families with MAGIS syndrome. Every patient carried an extremely rare heterozygous missense variant in GNAI2. Variants recurred at thr182 (thr182 to ala, ile, or pro in 6 families) and at arg179 (R179H or R179C in 7 patients from 5 families). Most variants arose de novo, but 2 families showed transmission consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. In family XIII, the proband (P13) and his affected father (P14) carried the R179C mutation. In family XVII, 2 affected brothers (P18 and P19) carried the R179H mutation. Biochemically, mutant Galpha-i2 proteins bound GTP up to 20-fold faster and hydrolyzed GTP up to 100-fold more slowly than wildtype, and most were insensitive to RGS GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Lamborn et al. (2025) found that the R179H and R179C mutants were biochemically milder than the other mutants: they bound GTP more slowly than wildtype and remained sensitive to GAPs. The authors suggested that this explains why these variants were transmitted across generations.

### Pathogenesis

Lamborn et al. (2025) found impaired migration and impaired chemokine-induced calcium flux in T cells and neutrophils from patients. The mutant proteins remained largely decoupled from G protein-coupled receptors, which made the cells constitutively insensitive to CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling. The authors noted that this mechanism is the opposite of that in WHIM syndrome. T cells were hyperresponsive because active Galpha-i2 sequestered RASA2, which enhanced RAS-ERK and PI3K-AKT-S6 signaling. To mimic low cAMP, the authors used CRISPR to knock out AC3 and AC7 in T cells from healthy donors. This impaired neither chemotaxis nor activation, suggesting that the chemotaxis defect and the T cell hyperresponsiveness do not depend on cAMP. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Lamborn et al. (2025) noted that rare features such as coloboma or subcortical band heterotopia should raise suspicion of MAGIS syndrome.

### Clinical Management

Lamborn et al. (2025) reported a patient who achieved full donor chimerism after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation from a matched unrelated donor. The transplant eradicated granulomatous skin disease due to vaccine-strain rubella and the recurrent infections, and the patient's autoimmune hemolytic anemia remained in remission for more than 3 years. Nonimmune features were unaffected. In vitro, combined inhibition of RAS/ERK/MAPK and PI3K/AKT corrected T cell hyperresponsiveness. The authors suggested that agents such as sirolimus or PDE inhibitors are possible therapies.

### Population Genetics

Lamborn et al. (2025) found that all of the GNAI2 variants in their patients were absent from population databases except R179H (rs137853227). This variant was present in 2 individuals of unknown affection status in gnomAD v4.1.0 (minor allele frequency 0.000001281).

### Animal Model

Hamada et al. (2017) knocked down Gnai2 acutely in mouse by in utero electroporation. Knockdown delayed the radial migration of excitatory cortical neurons, possibly because of impaired neuronal morphology, and an RNAi-resistant Gnai2 rescued this defect. Knockdown did not affect axon elongation, formation of the dendritic arbor, or neurogenesis at the ventricular zone. Mice with acute Gnai2 knockdown had defects in social interaction, novelty recognition, and active avoidance learning, and showed increased anxiety.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, ALA227VAL [c.680C>T; p.A227V]

In an individual with periventricular nodular heterotopia and intellectual disability, Hamada et al. (2017) identified a de novo heterozygous c.680C-T transition in the GNAI2 gene, resulting in an ala227-to-val (A227V) substitution. In mouse, acute knockdown of Gnai2 delayed the radial migration of cortical neurons.

**.0002 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, ARG179CYS [p.R179C]

In family XIII with MAGIS syndrome, Lamborn et al. (2025) found a heterozygous arg179-to-cys (R179C) mutation in the GNAI2 gene in a boy (P13) and his affected father (P14). The boy had IUGR, congenital heart valve abnormalities with hydrops fetalis, dysmorphism, severe congenital sensorineural hearing loss, neurodevelopmental delay, and pan-lymphocytopenia, and his newborn screen for SCID was abnormal. The father had dysmorphism, short stature, hearing loss, borderline intellectual disability, epilepsy, and recurrent infections. The R179C mutant bound GTP more slowly than wildtype and remained sensitive to GAPs, and the authors suggested that this milder effect explains its transmission across generations.

**.0003 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, ARG179HIS [p.R179H]

In 2 brothers with MAGIS syndrome (family XVII; P18 and P19), Lamborn et al. (2025) found a heterozygous arg179-to-his (R179H) mutation in the GNAI2 gene. Both brothers had dysmorphism, cryptorchidism, inguinal hernia, short stature, recurrent infections, and dysgammaglobulinemia with low serum IgM. P19, who was deceased, also had neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, and T cell lymphopenia. The variant (rs137853227) was present in 2 individuals of unknown affection status in gnomAD v4.1.0 (minor allele frequency 0.000001281). The R179H mutant bound GTP more slowly than wildtype and remained sensitive to GAPs, and the authors suggested that this milder effect explains its transmission across generations.
