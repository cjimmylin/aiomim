### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MAGIS syndrome (MAGIS) is caused by heterozygous activating mutation in the GNAI2 gene on chromosome 3.

### Description

MAGIS syndrome is a multisystem developmental disorder with immune dysregulation. It is caused by heterozygous germline activating mutations in GNAI2, which encodes the heterotrimeric G-protein subunit G-alpha-i2. Its 5 cardinal features are midline malformations of the brain, anterior pituitary gland dysfunction, growth retardation, immunodysregulation/immunodeficiency, and skeletal defects. Expression is highly variable, even among relatives carrying the same mutation. Immune involvement includes susceptibility to a broad range of infections, autoimmunity, and autoinflammation (Ham et al., 2024; Lamborn et al., 2025).

### Nomenclature

Lamborn et al. (2025) named the disorder MAGIS, an acronym for its 5 cardinal features: Midline malformations of the brain, Anterior pituitary gland dysfunction, Growth retardation, Immunodysregulation/Immunodeficiency, and Skeletal defects. They noted that the Latin word 'magis' means 'more,' a reminder that the GNAI2 mutations in this syndrome are gain-of-function.

### Clinical Features

Hamada et al. (2017) reported an individual with periventricular nodular heterotopia and intellectual disability.

Ham et al. (2024) studied 20 patients from 18 unrelated families of diverse ancestries (Latino/Admixed American, non-Finnish European, Finnish European, West Sub-Saharan African, and Middle Eastern Arab). Developmental abnormalities included intrauterine growth retardation, dysmorphism, bone dysostosis, neuroanatomic abnormalities, and birth defects in other organs. Dysmorphic features included frontal prominence, flat face, high anterior hairline, and sandal gap. Midline structural defects were observed, most commonly congenital nasal septum deviation, dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, pituitary hypoplasia with growth hormone deficiency, Chiari I malformation, micropenis, sagittal cleft ('butterfly') vertebrae, and scoliosis. Some patients had rare birth defects, including subcortical band heterotopia, agenesis of the olfactory bulbs, and coloboma. Other findings included brachydactyly type E, swan-neck deformity, misshapen sella turcica, diffuse leukodystrophy progressing to end-stage neurodegeneration, hippocampal malrotation, cerebellar dysplasia, polymicrogyria, subependymal heterotopia, and intestinal malrotation. Most patients also had postnatal abnormalities, including short stature with neurodevelopmental delay, neurobehavioral deficits, and gastrointestinal dysfunction.

Nearly all (90%) of the patients reported by Ham et al. (2024) had immune involvement, with recurrent, unusual, and/or severe infections. Skin infections were prominent and included bacterial, superficial fungal, or unusually severe viral infections such as recurrent shingles, extensive warts, and rubella vaccine-associated skin granulomas. Respiratory, middle ear, and sinus infections were common, some patients developed bronchiectasis, and some had invasive bacterial infections. Inflammatory or autoimmune complications included lymphocytic infiltration of organs, psoriasis, discoid lupus, autoimmune cytopenias with splenomegaly, Hashimoto thyroiditis, type I diabetes mellitus, colitis, and macrophage activation syndrome. Several patients had asthma or atopic dermatitis. Laboratory studies showed monocytosis and neutrophilia that were precipitated by acute infection and persisted in some patients. T-cell counts were initially low, with decreased recent thymic emigrants and a paucity of naive versus effector/memory T cells, but rose with age. Lymphocyte proliferation and activation were normal or even increased. Dysgammaglobulinemia, with low serum IgM and poorer vaccine titers, was accompanied by decreased B-cell counts and atretic lymphoid follicles in secondary lymphoid organs. Patients with mutations at thr182 had greater immune involvement because of additional inflammatory or autoimmune complications.

Lamborn et al. (2025) reviewed the features of the 20 patients reported by Ham et al. (2024). Growth retardation was the most penetrant feature (90%) and included prenatal intrauterine growth restriction (53%) and postnatal short stature (90%). Serum insulin-like growth factor-1 was low in 8 of 11 patients tested, and growth hormone deficiency was found in all 7 patients formally tested. Structural (41%) or functional (53%) defects of the anterior pituitary gland were present in 59% of patients. Excluding pituitary and sella turcica defects, 30% had midline brain malformations, including agenesis or hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, cerebellar hypoplasia, pons hypoplasia, and Chiari I malformation. Skeletal defects, comprising craniofacial, appendicular, and axial dysostoses, affected 85% of patients, and dysmorphism was present in all (100%). Patients could also have cardiovascular, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and dermatologic disease. Lamborn et al. (2025) stated that extremely rare features, such as coloboma or subcortical band heterotopia, should raise suspicion of MAGIS.

Lamborn et al. (2025) also summarized the immune features. Immune-mediated disease was present in 95% of patients, including immunodeficiency (90%) and immune dysregulation (50%). Immune dysregulation comprised systemic autoinflammation (15%), autoimmunity (35%), and splenomegaly (35%). Infections involved the respiratory tract (75%), middle ear (65%), sinuses (35%), and skin (25%), and invasive infections included meningitis (10%), bacteremia (20%), and cutaneous abscess (15%). Twenty-five percent of patients were extensively affected by cutaneous viral infections, mainly human papillomavirus-related warts. Some individual patients had life-threatening lymphocytic infiltration of the brain, lung, or liver, or macrophage activation syndrome. Autoimmune manifestations included autoimmune hemolytic anemia (20%), autoimmune thrombocytopenia (10%), and psoriasis (10%), as well as Hashimoto thyroiditis, type I diabetes mellitus, alopecia, celiac disease, and autoimmune enteritis in individual patients. Hypogammaglobulinemia affected 20% of patients. Myelokathexis was not observed, although only 3 patients had bone marrow examination, and neutropenia was seen only intermittently in 1 critically ill patient. Newborn screening for severe combined immunodeficiency was abnormal in 1 patient (P13), whose absolute T-cell count was 0.577 x 10(3) cells/microliter at 7 weeks of age and 1.486 x 10(3) cells/microliter 1 month later. Premature mortality in the original cohort of 20 patients was 10%.

Lamborn et al. (2025) illustrated intrafamilial variability in 2 families. In family XIII, the proband (P13) and his father (P14) both carried an arg179-to-cys (R179C) mutation. At 2 years of age, P13 had intrauterine growth restriction, congenital heart valve abnormalities with hydrops fetalis, dysmorphism (retrognathia, high forehead/anterior hairline, low-set ears), short femurs, hypospadias, severe congenital sensorineural hearing loss, neurodevelopmental delay, behavioral abnormalities, stereotypy, small pons, thin corpus callosum, feeding difficulties, postnatal growth delay, and multiple neonatal infections, including urosepsis with pan-lymphocytopenia. His father had dysmorphism, osteoporosis, cryptorchidism, hearing loss with vestibular dysfunction, neurodevelopmental delay with borderline intellectual disability, epilepsy, migraine, anxiety, and a midline arachnoidal cyst. He also had growth delay with short stature, an annular pancreas with bowel malrotation, esophageal hiatal hernia, gastroesophageal reflux with aspiration pneumonias, recurrent infections, and celiac disease.

In family XVII, 2 brothers (P18 and P19) carried an arg179-to-his (R179H) mutation (Lamborn et al., 2025). P18 had dysmorphism (micrognathia, high anterior hairline, low-set ears), cryptorchidism, inguinal hernia, and a pelvic kidney. He also had childhood failure to thrive with adult short stature, arthritis, gout, suspected osteoporosis, chronic diarrhea, sensorineural hearing loss, compensated hypothyroidism, anxiety, depression, asthma, recurrent infections, and dysgammaglobulinemia with low serum IgM and poor vaccine titers. His deceased brother, P19, had similar dysmorphism, cryptorchidism, inguinal hernia, deviated nasal septum, and intrauterine growth restriction with childhood failure to thrive and adult short stature. P19 also had neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, ataxia, nystagmus, migraine, autism spectrum disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, anxiety, depression, psychosis, irritable bowel syndrome, and mild splenomegaly. In addition, he had recurrent infections (including shingles), dysgammaglobulinemia, and T-cell lymphopenia.

### Inheritance

Ham et al. (2024) found that the GNAI2 variants segregated with an autosomal dominant disorder. De novo mutations predominated, occurring in 12 patients. The mutations were detected in several tissues, suggesting that they arose in germ cells or early in embryonic development. Lamborn et al. (2025) noted autosomal dominant transmission from father to son (R179C) in 1 family and an R179H mutation in 2 brothers in another family. They reported full penetrance in the families studied.

### Molecular Genetics

By whole-exome sequencing in an individual with periventricular nodular heterotopia and intellectual disability, Hamada et al. (2017) identified a de novo heterozygous c.680C-T transition in the GNAI2 gene, resulting in an ala227-to-val (A227V) substitution. In a later study, Ham et al. (2024) included A227V in a cAMP assay and classified it as a variant of unknown significance.

Lecoquierre et al. (2019) looked for missense variants present both in their database of more than 1,200 solo exomes and in denovo-db, a public collection of de novo variants in patients with developmental disorders. They identified GNAI2 as a good candidate gene for syndromic intellectual disability. ⚠︎

In 20 patients from 18 unrelated families with MAGIS, Ham et al. (2024) identified heterozygous missense mutations in the GNAI2 gene by whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing. The index patient was studied by trio exome sequencing with an unaffected sib. Other patients were found through GeneMatcher or through inquiries driven by immune phenotype, and the familial mutation in 1 patient was identified by Sanger sequencing. The variants were predicted to be deleterious (CADD score greater than 25). They were absent from the Greater Middle Eastern Variome and gnomAD v3.1.2, except for R179H, which was found in 1 individual of unknown affection status. Twelve mutations had occurred de novo. Patient cells expressed equal amounts of mutant and wildtype transcripts and normal levels of total G-alpha-i2 protein. The mutated residues were evolutionarily conserved and clustered in the Ras-like GTPase domain, particularly the P-loop and switch regions. Most mutant proteins bound GTP-gamma-S more readily than wildtype, R179H being the exception. All showed decreased GTP hydrolysis, and all except R179H were resistant to the GTPase-activating effect of RGS16. Overexpressed mutant proteins suppressed forskolin-induced cAMP production, and patient fibroblasts produced less cAMP. Ham et al. (2024) concluded that these were activating mutations. No other candidate gene was shared among the patients.

Lamborn et al. (2025) noted recurrent mutations among unrelated patients, particularly at thr182 (T182A, T182I, or T182P in 6 families) and arg179 (R179H or R179C in 7 patients from 5 families).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Ham et al. (2024) found greater immune system involvement in patients with mutations at thr182, because of additional inflammatory or autoimmune complications. Lamborn et al. (2025) noted that the R179H and R179C mutant proteins impair intrinsic GTP hydrolysis but are less severely activating than the other mutants: they bind GTP more slowly than wildtype and remain sensitive to GTPase-activating proteins. They suggested that these properties explain why the R179H and R179C variants can be transmitted through successive generations and be found, though extremely rarely, in the general population.

### Pathogenesis

Ham et al. (2024) found that CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from several patients showed decreased chemotaxis toward multiple chemokines, and T cells from 1 patient tested had reduced chemokine-induced calcium fluxes. Expressing the mutant proteins in healthy donor T cells reproduced these defects, except for I55M, and L38R and R179C had intermediate, more variable effects. Patient neutrophils also showed reduced directional migration. In 1 patient, leukocytes accumulated poorly in the oral mucosa and in induced skin blisters. In mice, T cells expressing mutant G-alpha-i2 migrated less well into lymph nodes and splenic white pulp after adoptive transfer. Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer studies showed that activated mutant G-alpha-i2 interacted minimally with chemokine receptors at steady state. Knockout of adenylyl cyclases AC3 or AC7 in T cells did not affect chemotaxis, indicating that the migration defect was independent of cAMP.

Ham et al. (2024) found that patient T cells showed increased activation and proliferation after T-cell receptor (TCR) stimulation. Proximal TCR signaling was normal, but RAS activation and downstream ERK/MAPK and PI3K-AKT-S6 signaling were enhanced. CRISPR-Cas9 deletion of the mutant GNAI2 allele in T cells from 1 patient restored normal responses, and expressing T182A or Q205L G-alpha-i2 in healthy donor T cells reproduced the hyperresponsiveness. By proteomic analysis, Ham et al. (2024) identified RASA2, a RAS GTPase-activating protein, as an interactor that bound activated G-alpha-i2 more strongly than wildtype. Activated G-alpha-i2 did not directly inhibit RASA2 GAP activity. Instead, it redistributed RASA2 toward the plasma membrane, and RAS activation at the Golgi was enhanced. The T-cell hyperactivation was independent of cAMP. Ham et al. (2024) proposed that the resulting increase in TCR signaling breaks peripheral tolerance and predisposes patients to autoimmunity. Because of the activated RAS-MAPK signaling, they suggested that the disorder falls within the RASopathy spectrum.

Lamborn et al. (2025) noted that MAGIS mechanistically behaves as a deficiency of CXCR4 signaling, because the mutant G-alpha-i2 becomes decoupled from its receptors. They contrasted this with WHIM syndrome, in which CXCR4 mutations cause constitutive sensitivity to signaling through this pathway.

### Clinical Management

Lamborn et al. (2025) recommended a full immunologic workup for patients with MAGIS, including quantitative immunoglobulins and vaccine titers. They advised considering immunoglobulin replacement in patients with low total IgG, nonprotective vaccine responses, or persistent infections. One patient received hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) from a fully matched unrelated donor and achieved full donor chimerism. HSCT eradicated his granulomatous skin disease caused by vaccine-strain rubella virus, as well as his recurrent upper respiratory tract infections, otitis media, and bronchitis. His autoimmune hemolytic anemia remained in remission for more than 3 years, although he received immunosuppressants for chronic graft-versus-host disease. HSCT did not affect his nonimmune features, including autism spectrum disorder and growth hormone deficiency. In vitro, inhibitors of the RAS/ERK/MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways each partially corrected T-cell hyperresponsiveness, and the 2 together corrected it completely. Lamborn et al. (2025) suggested gene therapy, inhibitors of these downstream pathways such as sirolimus, or phosphodiesterase inhibitors as possible treatments. They considered WHIM-specific drugs such as plerixafor or mavorixafor unlikely to be useful.

### Animal Model

Hamada et al. (2017) knocked down G-alpha-i2 in the developing mouse cerebral cortex by in utero electroporation. This delayed the radial migration of excitatory neurons, perhaps because of impaired morphology, and an RNAi-resistant G-alpha-i2 rescued the defect. Knockdown did not affect axon elongation, dendritic arbor formation, or neurogenesis in the ventricular zone. The knockdown mice had defects in social interaction, novelty recognition, and active avoidance learning, as well as increased anxiety.

Ham et al. (2024) tried to knock in the T182I mutation in mice. No heterozygous embryos developed beyond the 8-cell stage, indicating in utero lethality.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, THR182ALA [p.Thr182Ala]

In patients with MAGIS, Ham et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous thr182-to-ala (T182A) mutation in the GNAI2 gene. The mutant protein did not bind RGS16, and fibroblasts from a patient with the mutation had decreased cAMP production. In bioluminescence resonance energy transfer studies, T182A G-alpha-i2 interacted minimally with chemokine receptors at steady state. Expressing it in healthy donor CD4+ T cells increased TCR-induced responsiveness, and the GTP-gamma-S-bound mutant protein bound RASA2. Patients with mutations at thr182 had greater immune involvement. Lamborn et al. (2025) noted that mutations at thr182 (T182A, T182I, or T182P) occurred in 6 families.

**.0002 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, THR182ILE [p.Thr182Ile]

In patients with MAGIS, Ham et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous thr182-to-ile (T182I) mutation in the GNAI2 gene. The mutant protein did not bind RGS16. They described it as highly constitutively activating. Attempts to knock it into mice produced no heterozygous embryos beyond the 8-cell stage.

**.0003 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, LYS46THR [p.Lys46Thr]

In a patient with MAGIS, Ham et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous lys46-to-thr (K46T) mutation in the GNAI2 gene. Primary dermal fibroblasts from the patient showed decreased cAMP production.

**.0004 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, ARG209TRP [p.Arg209Trp]

In a patient with MAGIS, Ham et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous arg209-to-trp (R209W) mutation in the GNAI2 gene. The mutation lies in the conserved 'Gly-Arg-Glu' triad. Patient fibroblasts showed decreased cAMP production, although the overexpressed mutant protein did not decrease cAMP production. The mutation was associated with impaired leukocyte migration.

**.0005 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, ARG179CYS [p.Arg179Cys]

In a father and son (P14 and P13, family XIII) with MAGIS, Ham et al. (2024) and Lamborn et al. (2025) identified a heterozygous arg179-to-cys (R179C) mutation in the GNAI2 gene. Expressing the mutant protein in healthy donor T cells had intermediate, variable effects on chemotaxis (Ham et al., 2024). Lamborn et al. (2025) noted that R179C impairs intrinsic GTP hydrolysis but is less severely activating than other mutants: it binds GTP more slowly than wildtype and remains sensitive to GTPase-activating proteins.

**.0006 MAGIS SYNDROME**  
GNAI2, ARG179HIS [p.Arg179His; NC_000003.12:g.50256263G>A]

In 2 brothers (P18 and P19, family XVII) with MAGIS, Ham et al. (2024) and Lamborn et al. (2025) identified a heterozygous arg179-to-his (R179H) mutation in the GNAI2 gene. The variant was present in 1 individual of unknown affection status in gnomAD v3.1.2. Unlike the other mutants, R179H did not bind GTP-gamma-S more readily than wildtype and remained responsive to RGS16, but it showed decreased GTP hydrolysis (Ham et al., 2024). Lamborn et al. (2025) noted that the variant (rs137853227) was found in 2 individuals of unknown affection status in gnomAD v4.1.0.
