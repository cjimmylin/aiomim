### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that isolated glycolic aciduria (GYCA) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HAO1 gene (605023), which encodes glycolate oxidase, on chromosome 20p12.3.

### Description

Isolated glycolic aciduria is a biochemical abnormality caused by loss of glycolate oxidase. It is marked by increased urinary glycolic acid excretion, and in one case also by raised plasma glycolate. Oxalate excretion is normal and there is no evident clinical phenotype. The kidneys appeared normal on ultrasound in the reported individuals (Frishberg et al., 2014; McGregor et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Frishberg et al. (2014) reported 2 brothers of Arab descent, born to healthy consanguineous parents, who were evaluated for psychomotor delay associated with a triple-A-like syndrome (anisocoria, alacrima, and achalasia). The proband had markedly increased urinary glycolic acid excretion. His excretion of oxalate, citrate, and glycerate was normal. Abdominal ultrasound showed normal-sized kidneys with normal echotexture. The authors found that the genetic cause of the triple-A-like syndrome in this family was unrelated to the metabolic abnormality. They described the glycolic aciduria as isolated and asymptomatic.

McGregor et al. (2020) described a healthy British-Pakistani woman in her fifth decade who had a lifelong complete HAO1 knockout. She was identified by exome sequencing of autozygous population cohorts (Born in Bradford and Genes and Health). She was overweight (BMI 30-35 kg/m2) and was the mother of 3 healthy children. A renal ultrasound was normal, and apart from common short-term illnesses she was otherwise healthy. The authors concluded that lifelong HAO1 knockout was safe and caused no clinical phenotype in this woman.

### Biochemical Features

In the HAO1-null woman, McGregor et al. (2020) found a plasma glycolate of 171 nmol/mL (reference 14 or less), about 12 times the upper limit of normal. Her urinary glycolate/creatinine ratio was 199 to 309 mg/g (reference 50 or less), about 6 times the upper limit of normal. Plasma and urinary oxalate, plasma glycerate, the serum anion gap, and standard serum biochemistries were all normal.

McGregor et al. (2020) profiled 1,871 plasma metabolites and lipids in the woman and compared them with 25 controls. Eighteen biochemicals, including glycolate, were markedly elevated (more than 5 SD above controls). Six of these had structures compatible with being potential HAO1 substrates. Unconjugated bile acids were elevated, and glycine- and taurine-conjugated bile acids were reduced. The authors suggested that this might reflect reduced local availability of glycine. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of isolated glycolic aciduria in the families reported is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Frishberg et al. (2014) found a homozygous HAO1 mutation in a patient born to consanguineous parents. McGregor et al. (2020) found a homozygous HAO1 mutation lying within an autozygous region in an affected woman. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Frishberg et al. (2014) studied a patient of Arab descent with isolated glycolic aciduria who was born to consanguineous parents. Direct sequencing of the HAO1 gene identified a homozygous splice-site mutation (c.814-1G-C) at the invariant -1 position of the intron 5 splice acceptor site. Because HAO1 is expressed specifically in the liver, the authors tested the mutation with an in vitro hybrid-minigene assay. Cells transfected with the mutant minigene produced an abnormal splice variant. The authors concluded that expression of a defective glycolate oxidase splice variant causes isolated asymptomatic glycolic aciduria.

In a healthy British-Pakistani woman with raised plasma and urinary glycolate, McGregor et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion in the HAO1 gene (c.997delC; rs1186715161). The deletion was found by exome sequencing of blood DNA and confirmed by Sanger sequencing of saliva DNA. It was predicted to cause a frameshift (Leu333SerfsTer4) that truncates the final 37 amino acids. The variant lay within a 5.2-Mb autozygous region. The woman was not homozygous for any other rare predicted loss-of-function variant, and gnomAD v2.1 contained no homozygous predicted loss-of-function HAO1 variants. In CHO-K1 cells, the variant protein was markedly reduced and diffusely mislocalized, whereas mRNA levels were unaffected. The authors linked this to loss of the C-terminal SKI peroxisomal targeting tripeptide. Using lumasiran phase 1 data from healthy volunteers for comparison, they estimated that the woman retained less than 2% of normal glycolate oxidase activity. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Frishberg et al. (2014) suggested that loss of glycolate oxidase causes only asymptomatic glycolic aciduria. They proposed that this supports developing substrate reduction as a new treatment approach for primary hyperoxaluria type I.

McGregor et al. (2020) concluded that the clinically silent lifelong HAO1 knockout supports the potential safety of chronic HAO1 inhibition for treating primary hyperoxaluria type 1, for example with the RNAi therapeutic lumasiran.

### Population Genetics

McGregor et al. (2020) reported that about 1 in 2,700 individuals in gnomAD v2.1 is heterozygous for an HAO1 predicted loss-of-function variant. They estimated that homozygotes would occur at a frequency of about 1 in 30 million in an outbred population.
