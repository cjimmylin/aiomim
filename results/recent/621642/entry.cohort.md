### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that isolated glycolic aciduria (GYCA) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HAO1 gene (605023), which encodes glycolate oxidase, on chromosome 20p12.3.

### Description

Isolated glycolic aciduria is an asymptomatic metabolic condition caused by loss of glycolate oxidase function. Affected individuals have markedly increased urinary glycolate, and plasma glycolate is raised as well. Oxalate excretion is normal and the kidneys appear normal on ultrasound (Frishberg et al., 2014; McGregor et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Frishberg et al. (2014) studied 2 brothers born to healthy consanguineous parents of Arab descent. The brothers were evaluated for psychomotor delay associated with a triple-A-like syndrome (anisocoria, alacrima, and achalasia). The proband had markedly increased urinary glycolic acid excretion, with normal excretion of oxalate, citrate, and glycerate. Abdominal ultrasound showed normal-sized kidneys with normal echotexture. Frishberg et al. (2014) concluded that the proband had isolated asymptomatic glycolic aciduria and that the triple-A-like syndrome in the kindred was unrelated to the metabolic abnormality.

McGregor et al. (2020) identified a healthy woman of British-Pakistani ethnicity in her fifth decade through genotype-based recall from exome-sequenced population cohorts. She was overweight (BMI 30-35 kg/m2). Apart from common non-serious short-term illnesses and pregnancy symptoms, she was otherwise healthy. A renal ultrasound, carried out as part of a gynecology assessment, was normal. Her serum anion gap and her plasma and urinary oxalate were normal, but her plasma and urinary glycolate levels were markedly elevated.

### Biochemical Features

In the proband reported by Frishberg et al. (2014), urinary glycolic acid excretion was markedly increased, while excretion of oxalate, citrate, and glycerate was normal.

In the HAO1-null woman reported by McGregor et al. (2020), plasma glycolate was 12 times the upper limit of normal and urinary glycolate was 6 times the upper limit of normal. Plasma and urinary oxalate were normal. McGregor et al. (2020) also ran plasma metabolomics and lipidomics covering 1,871 biochemicals. Most of her metabolites were similar to those of 25 controls, but 18 biochemicals, including glycolate, were extreme outliers at more than 5 SD above controls. Her unconjugated bile acids were raised and her glycine- and taurine-conjugated bile acids were reduced. The authors suggested that this might reflect reduced local availability of glycine.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of isolated glycolic aciduria is autosomal recessive. In the family reported by Frishberg et al. (2014), the proband, born to consanguineous parents, was homozygous for an HAO1 mutation. McGregor et al. (2020) reported a woman who was homozygous for an HAO1 frameshift variant that lay within an autozygous region. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Frishberg et al. (2014) directly sequenced the HAO1 gene in the proband from a consanguineous Arab kindred with isolated glycolic aciduria. They found a homozygous c.814-1G-C mutation at the invariant -1 position of the intron 5 splice acceptor site. Because HAO1 is expressed specifically in the liver, they used an in vitro hybrid-minigene approach, which showed that the mutation resulted in an abnormal splice variant in transfected cells. They concluded that defective splicing of glycolate oxidase caused the glycolic aciduria.

McGregor et al. (2020) exome sequenced 2 large population cohorts with substantial autozygosity (Born in Bradford and Genes and Health). They identified a healthy British-Pakistani woman who was homozygous for a 1-bp deletion in the HAO1 gene (c.997delC; rs1186715161), which lay within a 5.2-Mb autozygous region. The deletion causes a frameshift (Leu333SerfsTer4) and is predicted to truncate the final 37 amino acids of the protein. When the variant was expressed in CHO-K1 cells, HAO1 protein was markedly reduced while mRNA levels were unaffected, and the protein was spread diffusely through the cells. These results were consistent with loss of the C-terminal SKI peroxisomal targeting tripeptide. The woman's markedly elevated plasma and urinary glycolate confirmed the loss of function predicted from her genotype. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Frishberg et al. (2014) suggested that their observation supports substrate reduction, by inhibition of glycolate oxidase, as a novel approach to treating primary hyperoxaluria type I. ⚠︎

McGregor et al. (2020) compared their HAO1-null woman with healthy volunteers given lumasiran, an RNAi therapeutic that targets HAO1 and is used for primary hyperoxaluria type 1. Her plasma and urinary glycolate were 3 to 5 times higher than the volunteers' peak levels at the highest dose. The authors estimated that she retained less than 2% residual glycolate oxidase activity. They concluded that lifelong HAO1 knockout had been safe in her, which supported the safety of chronic HAO1 inhibition. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

McGregor et al. (2020) found no individuals homozygous for predicted loss-of-function HAO1 variants among 141,456 individuals in gnomAD v2.1, in which about 1 in 2,700 individuals is heterozygous. They estimated that homozygotes would occur in an outbred population at a frequency of about 1 in 30 million.
