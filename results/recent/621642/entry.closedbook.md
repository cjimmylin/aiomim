### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that isolated glycolic aciduria (GYCA) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HAO1 gene (605023), which encodes glycolate oxidase, on chromosome 20p12.

### Description

Isolated glycolic aciduria is an autosomal recessive metabolic condition caused by deficiency of glycolate oxidase, the peroxisomal liver enzyme that oxidizes glycolate to glyoxylate. Affected individuals excrete large amounts of glycolate in the urine. Oxalate excretion is normal, so they do not develop the hyperoxaluria, nephrocalcinosis or kidney stones seen in primary hyperoxaluria. The condition seems to be clinically benign. This observation supports pharmacologic inhibition of glycolate oxidase as a substrate-reduction treatment for primary hyperoxaluria. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Frishberg et al. (2014) reported a child with markedly increased urinary glycolate excretion and normal urinary oxalate excretion. The child had no evidence of the renal stone disease or nephrocalcinosis seen in primary hyperoxaluria. ⚠︎

McGregor et al. (2020) described a healthy adult with complete loss of HAO1 function. This person had raised plasma and urinary glycolate but normal oxalate levels and no apparent clinical abnormality. The authors presented the case as human evidence that long-term glycolate oxidase inhibition would be safe as a treatment for primary hyperoxaluria.

### Biochemical Features

The biochemical hallmark of the condition is glycolic aciduria with normal oxalate excretion. This pattern is consistent with a block in the conversion of glycolate to glyoxylate, the immediate precursor of oxalate (Frishberg et al., 2014). ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Isolated glycolic aciduria follows autosomal recessive inheritance. The reported affected individuals were homozygous for mutations in the HAO1 gene (Frishberg et al., 2014; McGregor et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a child with isolated glycolic aciduria, Frishberg et al. (2014) identified a homozygous mutation in the HAO1 gene.

McGregor et al. (2020) used genetic data from a population cohort to identify a healthy adult who was homozygous for a loss-of-function variant in the HAO1 gene.

### Clinical Management

Because humans lacking glycolate oxidase appear healthy, the condition has served as a natural model supporting glycolate oxidase inhibition (for example, by RNA interference targeting HAO1) as a treatment for primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (McGregor et al., 2020). ⚠︎
