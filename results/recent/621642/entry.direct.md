### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that isolated glycolic aciduria (GYCA) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HAO1 gene, which encodes glycolate oxidase.

### Description

Isolated glycolic aciduria is a biochemical condition caused by loss of glycolate oxidase (HAO1) activity. Affected individuals have markedly increased urinary glycolate, and plasma glycolate is also raised. Urinary and plasma oxalate are normal. The condition appears to cause no symptoms (Frishberg et al., 2014; McGregor et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Frishberg et al. (2014) studied 2 brothers born to healthy consanguineous parents of Arab descent. The brothers were referred for psychomotor delay associated with a triple-A-like syndrome (anisocoria, alacrima, and achalasia). The proband had markedly increased urinary excretion of glycolic acid, while his excretion of oxalate, citrate, and glycerate was normal. Abdominal ultrasound showed normal-sized kidneys with normal echotexture. The genetic cause of the triple-A-like syndrome in this family was unrelated to the glycolic aciduria. The authors concluded that the glycolic aciduria was isolated and asymptomatic.

McGregor et al. (2020) exome-sequenced 2 large population-based cohorts with substantial autozygosity (Born In Bradford, Genes and Health). They identified a healthy woman of British-Pakistani ethnicity, in her fifth decade, with a lifelong complete knockout of HAO1. She was a mother of 3 healthy children and was overweight (BMI 30-35 kg/m2). Apart from common non-serious short-term illnesses and pregnancy symptoms, she was otherwise healthy. A renal ultrasound was normal. Her standard venous blood biochemistries, including serum sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, chloride, creatinine, transaminases, and bilirubin, had been repeatedly normal over the previous decade. Her serum anion gap was normal. The authors concluded that, at least in her genetic background, lifelong HAO1 knockout was safe and without clinical phenotype.

### Biochemical Features

In the HAO1-knockout woman, McGregor et al. (2020) found plasma glycolate of 171 nmol/mL (reference, 14 or less). Her urinary glycolate/creatinine ratio was 199 mg/g in a single void and 309 mg/g in a 24-hour collection (reference, 50 or less). These values were 12 times (plasma) and 6 times (urine) the upper limit of normal in 67 healthy reference individuals. Plasma oxalate, plasma glycerate, and urinary oxalate were normal. Plasma metabolomic and lipidomic profiling covered 1,871 biochemicals. Most of her metabolites were similar to those of 25 controls, but 18 biochemicals, including glycolate, were markedly elevated (more than 5 SD above controls). These included cholic acid-related compounds: unconjugated bile acids were elevated, and glycine- and taurine-conjugated bile acids were reduced. The authors suggested this might reflect reduced local availability of glycine. Six of the significantly elevated metabolites had structures compatible with being potential HAO1 substrates. The authors compared her values with data from healthy volunteers given lumasiran, an RNA interference therapeutic targeting HAO1, and estimated that she retained less than 2% residual glycolate oxidase activity. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Isolated glycolic aciduria follows autosomal recessive inheritance. Homozygous HAO1 mutations were found in a consanguineous family (Frishberg et al., 2014) and in a woman whose mutation lay within a 5.2-Mb autozygous region (McGregor et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a consanguineous Arab family with isolated glycolic aciduria, Frishberg et al. (2014) identified a homozygous splice site mutation in the HAO1 gene (c.814-1G-C) by direct sequencing. The mutation affected the invariant -1 position of the intron 5 splice acceptor site. HAO1 is a liver-specific enzyme, so the authors tested the mutation with an in vitro hybrid minigene assay. Cells transfected with the mutant minigene produced an abnormal splice variant.

McGregor et al. (2020) used exome sequencing of autozygous populations to identify a healthy adult woman with isolated glycolic aciduria. She was homozygous for a 1-bp deletion in the HAO1 gene (c.997delC), predicted to cause a frameshift (Leu333SerfsTer4) and to remove the final 37 amino acids of the protein. The genotype was confirmed by Sanger sequencing of saliva DNA. The woman was 7.4% autozygous, and the variant lay within a 5.2-Mb autozygous region. She was not homozygous for any other rare predicted loss-of-function variant. In transfected CHO-K1 cells, the mutant produced markedly reduced HAO1 protein, although mRNA levels were unaffected. The mutant protein was also diffusely distributed rather than correctly localized. The authors noted that HAO1 normally depends on its C-terminal SKI tripeptide for peroxisomal targeting. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

McGregor et al. (2020) found no homozygous predicted loss-of-function HAO1 variants among 141,456 individuals in the gnomAD v2.1 database. About 1 in 2,700 individuals in gnomAD was heterozygous for a known predicted loss-of-function HAO1 variant. The expected frequency of homozygotes in an outbred population was about 1 in 30 million.
