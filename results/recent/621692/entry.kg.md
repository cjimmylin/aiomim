### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-42B (DEE42B) is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the CACNA1A gene (601011) on chromosome 19p13. ⚠︎

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-42B is a severe neurologic disorder caused by biallelic CACNA1A variants. Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) reviewed their own case together with earlier reports. They found the consistent features to be infantile hypotonia, treatment-refractory seizures beginning in the first 6 months of life, cerebral visual impairment, profound developmental delay with prominent and persistent motor impairment, and progressive cerebellar changes on MRI.

### Clinical Features

Reinson et al. (2016) described 2 sibs, a sister and a brother, who were normal at birth. In early infancy both developed daily recurrent seizures along with extreme muscular hypotonia, hypokinesia, and global developmental delay. Brain MRI showed progressive cerebral, cerebellar, and optic nerve atrophy. By 5 years of age, both children were blind and bedridden and had profound developmental delay. The elder sister died at that age.

Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) reported a girl with a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, born to nonconsanguineous White British parents. She had hypotonia and a weak suck from birth. At 5 months she had an alternating convergent squint and delayed visual maturation. At 6 months, after a low-grade fever, she developed tonic-clonic status epilepticus. Her seizures did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs. She needed repeated hospital admissions for intractable seizures, chest infections, and respiratory arrests, and she needed a gastrostomy because of poor bulbar function. At 3 years she had secondary microcephaly, occasional myoclonus, and profound hypotonia, with no spasticity and no purposeful movement. By age 9 she had fixed flexion contractures and no distinctive dysmorphic features. At 9 to 10 years she still had generalized tonic-clonic seizures several times a week. At age 10, her Vineland composite score of 26 indicated severe intellectual disability, and her function was impaired in all social domains. Brain MRI at 6 months showed mild cerebellar hypoplasia and a thin corpus callosum. By 3 years, MRI showed mild cerebral volume loss and cerebellar volume loss involving both hemispheres, the vermis, and the middle cerebellar peduncles. By age 8, the EEG was dominated by continuous focal sharp and slow wave complexes over the posterior regions, with brief tonic seizures. Nerve conduction studies, EMG, electroretinography, and audiology were normal.

Mammadova et al. (2024) reported 3 brothers, born to nonconsanguineous parents, with a rapidly progressive, fatal, early-onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. The leading features were severe muscular hypotonia from the newborn period (floppy infant), refractory early-onset seizures with deep apneic spells, and optic atrophy. The first child had neonatal-onset hypotonia and died at 5 months with suspected sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). He was not investigated. The second child (II-2) had hypotonia from the first week of life. At 5 months he was admitted with seizures, respiratory problems, dysphagia, and severe hypotonia. EEG showed hypsarrhythmia, and PEHO syndrome was suspected. The table also lists blindness, edema, optic atrophy, and cerebellar hypoplasia, whereas the text describes his brain MRI as unrevealing. Multiple antiseizure drugs had no lasting effect. He needed tube feeding and transient ventilation, and he died at 14 months. The index patient (II-4) was hypotonic at birth and had his first seizures at 4 weeks. He went on to develop severe hypotonia with absent deep tendon reflexes, respiratory and swallowing insufficiency, and many seizures a day. These were bilateral clonic and myoclonic seizures with gaze rigidity and apneic spells. EEG showed burst suppression at 4 weeks and, by 5 months, generalized background slowing with multifocal sharp waves. MRI was normal, and treatment helped only transiently. He died of respiratory insufficiency at age 2 years (2.5 years in the table).

Mammadova et al. (2024) also described a fourth brother (II-3) in the same family. He carried a single heterozygous CACNA1A variant and had a milder phenotype. He was evaluated at age 5 years for episodic unsteady gait, dizziness, and decreased vigilance. He had mild intellectual impairment, learning disability, ADHD, and primary generalized epilepsy that responded to levetiracetam. His MRI was normal. The report is inconsistent about which variant he carried: the text gives the maternal c.2602delG variant, whereas Table 1 lists the paternal c.5476delC variant.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE42B is autosomal recessive. Reinson et al. (2016) found compound heterozygous CACNA1A mutations in 2 affected sibs, and each of their parents was heterozygous for one of the mutations. Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) noted that, unlike in previously reported biallelic families, the heterozygous carrier parents in their family showed no clear signs of a CACNA1A-associated syndrome. They suggested that the effect of calcium channel dysfunction is dose-dependent: monoallelic variants cause a highly variable spectrum of disease, whereas biallelic variants cause severe, progressive neurologic disease. They also noted that their case showed that a recessive diagnosis is possible in an apparently sporadic disorder. Mammadova et al. (2024) considered that CACNA1A-related disease shows both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Reinson et al. (2016) used exome sequencing to study 2 sibs with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy and progressive cerebral, cerebellar, and optic nerve atrophy. They identified compound heterozygous mutations in the CACNA1A gene. One was a c.4315T-A missense mutation in exon 27, resulting in a trp1439-to-arg (W1439R) substitution. The other was a 7-bp deletion in exon 3 (c.472_478delGCCTTCC), predicted to cause a frameshift (Ala158ThrfsTer6). The parents and 2 other sibs were each heterozygous for one of the mutations.

Arteche-López et al. (2021) reported a patient with a homozygous loss-of-function variant in the CACNA1A gene. They presented this patient among the first patients with homozygous disease-related variants in 3 genes previously associated with dominant inheritance. The patient's clinical phenotype was more severe than the typical dominant form, and the authors suggested that codominant or recessive inheritance could explain the difference.

Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) used a next-generation sequencing panel for epileptic encephalopathy to study a White British girl with DEE42B. They found that she was compound heterozygous for 2 CACNA1A variants and confirmed the result by Sanger sequencing. The first variant was a maternally inherited 2-bp deletion (c.2042_2043delAG; NM_001127221.2), predicted to cause a frameshift and premature termination (Gln681ArgfsTer100). This truncating variant had previously been reported as pathogenic in episodic ataxia type 2, with variable expressivity. The authors classified it as pathogenic by ACMG/AMP criteria. The mother, who carried this variant, had no neurological symptoms. The second variant was a paternally inherited c.1693G-A transition, resulting in a glu565-to-lys (E565K) substitution. It was absent from gnomAD and lay within an ion transport domain in a missense-constrained region. It affected a highly conserved residue and was predicted to be deleterious by SIFT and MutationTaster. However, it remained a variant of uncertain significance by ACMG/AMP criteria. The father, who carried this variant, had infrequent mild migraines without hemiplegia and was suspected of having mild learning difficulties.

Mammadova et al. (2024) used trio exome sequencing to study the index patient (II-4) in a family with 3 affected brothers. They found compound heterozygous frameshift variants in the CACNA1A gene (NM_001127222.1): c.2602delG (Ala868ProfsTer24), inherited from the mother, and c.5476delC (His1826ThrfsTer30), inherited from the father. Exome sequencing of material from his deceased brother (II-2) found the same 2 variants. No material was available from the first affected brother (II-1). Neither variant was in gnomAD or ClinVar. Both were predicted to cause loss of function and were classified as pathogenic under ACMG criteria. The mother and a maternal uncle were heterozygous for c.2602delG and had episodic ataxia type 2. The mother had chronic headache, ataxic gait, dizziness, hand tremor, and mild intellectual deficits, and she had had seizures at 2 to 3 years of age. The father was heterozygous for c.5476delC and had no neurological symptoms.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Reinson et al. (2016) noted that heterozygous carriers in their family expressed a milder phenotype. The carriers, comprising the parents and 2 sibs of the affected children, had intellectual disability, and the mother also had adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with slowly progressive cerebellar atrophy. ⚠︎

Mammadova et al. (2024) compared their patients with 9 previously reported individuals from 4 families with biallelic CACNA1A variants. They concluded that biallelic truncating loss-of-function variants were associated with a severe, early-onset, fatal epileptic encephalopathy. In their view, this supported a correlation between how deleterious the biallelic variants are and how severe the phenotype is. They noted that heterozygous relatives were either asymptomatic or had milder phenotypes.

### Pathogenesis

Mammadova et al. (2024) proposed that ictal apneic spells were the most likely cause of death in the 2 brothers with proven CACNA1A variants. They also suggested that the brother who had been diagnosed with SIDS probably died of ictal apnea.
