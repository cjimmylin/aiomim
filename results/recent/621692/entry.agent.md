### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-42B (DEE42B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1A gene (601011). ⚠︎

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-42B (DEE42B) is a severe autosomal recessive neurologic disorder caused by biallelic CACNA1A mutations. Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) listed its consistent features as infantile hypotonia, treatment-refractory seizures starting in the first 6 months of life, cerebral visual impairment, profound developmental delay with prominent, persistent motor impairment, and progressive cerebellar changes on MRI. Reinson et al. (2016) also reported progressive cerebral and optic nerve atrophy and reduced lifespan. Mammadova et al. (2024) described apneic spells, optic atrophy and early death. Heterozygous carriers in these families were either asymptomatic or had milder, variable neurologic features (Reinson et al., 2016; Wong-Spracklen et al., 2022; Mammadova et al., 2024). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Reinson et al. (2016) reported a sister and brother with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy. Both were normal at birth. In early infancy they developed daily recurrent seizures, along with extreme muscular hypotonia, hypokinesia and global developmental delay. Brain MRI showed progressive cerebral, cerebellar and optic nerve atrophy. At age 5 years both children were blind and bedridden with profound developmental delay, and the elder sister died at that age. Their parents and 2 sibs, each heterozygous for 1 of the mutations, had a milder phenotype. The abstract states that both of them had intellectual disability, and that the mother also had adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with slowly progressive cerebellar atrophy. ⚠︎

Arteche-López et al. (2021) reported patients homozygous for a loss-of-function CACNA1A variant who had a more severe clinical phenotype than the typical dominant form. Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) summarized this report as a consanguineous family in which 4 children with the homozygous variant presented with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy and died by 6 months of age. Both carrier parents had symptoms consistent with episodic ataxia type 2.

Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) reported a girl born at term to nonconsanguineous White British parents. She had hypotonia and a weak suck from birth. At 5 months she was diagnosed with alternating convergent squint and delayed visual maturation. At 6 months, after routine immunizations and low-grade fever, she developed tonic-clonic status epilepticus. Her seizures were refractory to multiple antiepileptic medications, and over the next 3 years she needed more than 20 hospital admissions for chest infections, intractable seizures and respiratory arrests. She required gastrostomy feeding. At age 3 years she had secondary microcephaly (head circumference below the 0.4th centile) and occasional myoclonus. She could not sit unsupported and had no purposeful movement. By age 9 she had fixed flexion contractures of the elbows, hips and knees, and she had no distinctive dysmorphic features. At age 10 she had profound motor and communication impairments: her Vineland composite score of 26 corresponded to severe intellectual disability. She continued to have generalized tonic-clonic seizures several times a week despite ketogenic diet and other treatments.

In the girl reported by Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022), MRI at 6 months showed mild cerebellar hypoplasia and a thin corpus callosum. By 3 years there was cerebellar volume loss involving both hemispheres, the vermis and the middle cerebellar peduncles, along with mild cerebral volume loss. By age 8 years, EEG was dominated by continuous focal sharp and slow-wave complexes over the posterior regions, together with brief tonic seizures. Nerve conduction studies, electromyography, electroretinography and audiology were normal. Her father had infrequent, nonsevere migraines without motor symptoms and was suspected to have mild learning difficulties. Her mother had no neurologic symptoms.

Mammadova et al. (2024) reported a family with nonconsanguineous parents and 4 affected male sibs. The first child had neonatal-onset hypotonia and died at 5 months of suspected sudden infant death syndrome; he was not studied before or after death. The second child had hypotonia from the first week of life. At 5 months he had seizures, respiratory problems and dysphagia, and EEG showed hypsarrhythmia. PEHO syndrome was suspected. His prolonged, deep apneic spells and subtle seizures did not respond lastingly to multiple antiseizure medications, and he died at 14 months. The index patient (II-4) was hypotonic at birth and had his first seizures at 4 weeks. These included bilateral clonic seizures with gaze rigidity and apneic spells, as well as myoclonic seizures, and EEG showed burst suppression. He had absent deep tendon reflexes, respiratory and swallowing insufficiency, blindness, optic atrophy and severe global developmental delay. The text gives his age at death from respiratory insufficiency as 2 years, and the table gives 2.5 years. Mammadova et al. (2024) considered ictal apnea the probable cause of death in both brothers with proven genotypes. ⚠︎

In the family reported by Mammadova et al. (2024), the third brother developed generalized epilepsy at 5 years and had mild intellectual impairment, ataxia, ADHD and dizziness. Levetiracetam made him seizure-free, and his MRI was normal. The mother and a maternal uncle had been diagnosed with episodic ataxia type 2. The mother had seizures at 2 to 3 years of age, mild hand tremor, poor gait balance and apparent mild intellectual deficits. The father had no specific neurologic symptoms. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE42B in the families reported by Reinson et al. (2016), Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) and Mammadova et al. (2024) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Affected individuals were compound heterozygous for CACNA1A mutations, and each parent carried one mutation. Arteche-López et al. (2021) reported homozygous patients and suggested co-dominant or recessive inheritance. Heterozygous relatives were either asymptomatic or had milder, variable neurologic phenotypes. Mammadova et al. (2024) therefore concluded that CACNA1A belongs to the genes with co-dominant, or both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive, inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a sister and brother with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, Reinson et al. (2016) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the CACNA1A gene. One was a missense mutation, c.4315T-A (W1439R), in exon 27. The other was a 7-bp deletion, c.472_478delGCCTTCC, in exon 3, predicted to cause a frameshift (Ala158ThrfsTer6). The parents and 2 sibs were each heterozygous for one of the mutations.

Arteche-López et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify patients homozygous for a loss-of-function variant in the CACNA1A gene. Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) and Mammadova et al. (2024) cited this as a homozygous truncating variant, p.Arg932* (c.2767C-T), found in a consanguineous family. ⚠︎

In a girl with DEE42B, Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) used a 105-gene epileptic encephalopathy panel to identify compound heterozygous variants in CACNA1A, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The first was a maternally inherited 2-bp deletion, c.2042_2043delAG, predicted to cause a frameshift (Gln681ArgfsTer100). It had previously been reported in episodic ataxia type 2 and was classified as pathogenic. The second was a paternally inherited c.1693G-A transition causing a glu565-to-lys (E565K) substitution in an ion transport domain. This variant was absent from gnomAD, altered a highly conserved residue and was predicted to be deleterious, but by ACMG criteria it remained a variant of uncertain significance. Neither parent showed clear signs of a CACNA1A-associated syndrome.

In 2 brothers with fatal early-onset DEE, Mammadova et al. (2024) used trio exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the CACNA1A gene. One was a maternally inherited 1-bp deletion, c.2602delG (Ala868ProfsTer24). The other was a paternally inherited 1-bp deletion, c.5476delC (His1826ThrfsTer30). The variants were found in the index patient and confirmed in material from his deceased brother (II-2). Material from the first affected brother was not available. Neither variant was in gnomAD or ClinVar, and both were predicted to cause loss of function. The mother and a maternal uncle with episodic ataxia type 2 carried c.2602delG, and the asymptomatic father carried c.5476delC. In the text, the third brother, who had generalized epilepsy and mild intellectual impairment, was heterozygous for the maternal c.2602delG variant, whereas Table 1 lists him as heterozygous for c.5476delC.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) suggested that calcium channel dysfunction has a dose-dependent effect. Monoallelic variants were associated with a highly variable spectrum, from asymptomatic to motor-only syndromes to epilepsy and cognitive impairment, whereas biallelic variants led to severe, progressive neurologic symptoms and cognitive disability. Mammadova et al. (2024) noted that biallelic truncating variants were associated with severe, early-onset encephalopathy with fatal outcome. In contrast, compound heterozygosity for a missense and a truncating variant was associated with long-term survival in 2 previously reported patients. They proposed that the deleteriousness of biallelic loss-of-function variants correlates with phenotypic severity.
