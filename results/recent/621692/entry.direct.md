### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-42B (DEE42B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1A gene (601011).

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-42B (DEE42B) is an autosomal recessive neurologic disorder. It begins in infancy with severe hypotonia and treatment-refractory seizures, followed by profound global developmental delay. Some patients have progressive cerebral, cerebellar, and optic nerve atrophy, visual impairment, apneic spells, and feeding difficulties. Several affected children have died in infancy or early childhood (Reinson et al., 2016; Wong-Spracklen et al., 2022; Mammadova et al., 2024). Heterozygous relatives carrying one of the variants have been asymptomatic or have had milder and variable neurologic features, including episodic ataxia, intellectual disability, or epilepsy (Reinson et al., 2016; Wong-Spracklen et al., 2022; Mammadova et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Reinson et al. (2016) reported a sister and brother with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy. Both were normal at birth. In early infancy they developed daily recurrent seizures, together with extreme muscular hypotonia, hypokinesia, and global developmental delay. Brain MRI showed progressive cerebral, cerebellar, and optic nerve atrophy. By 5 years of age, both patients were blind and bedridden and had profound developmental delay. The older sister died at that age. Their parents and 2 sibs were heterozygous for one of the variants and had a milder phenotype that included intellectual disability. The mother also had adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with slowly progressive cerebellar atrophy.

Arteche-López et al. (2021) reported patients who carried a homozygous loss-of-function variant in the CACNA1A gene and had a more severe clinical phenotype than the typical dominant form. In their literature review, Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) described this family as consanguineous, with 4 children who had DEE and died by 6 months of age, and with both carrier parents having symptoms consistent with episodic ataxia type 2. Mammadova et al. (2024) summarized the phenotype of these 4 sibs as early-onset progressive epileptic encephalopathy with muscular hypotonia and mild facial dysmorphic features, beginning in the neonatal period.

Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) reported a girl born to nonconsanguineous White British parents. She had hypotonia and a weak suck at birth. At 5 months, she was diagnosed with an alternating convergent squint and delayed visual maturation. At 6 months, she developed low-grade fever after immunization, followed by tonic-clonic status epilepticus that required intensive care. Her seizures were refractory to multiple antiepileptic medications, and over the next 3 years she required more than 20 hospital admissions for chest infections, intractable seizures, and respiratory arrests. Because of poor bulbar function she needed a gastrostomy. At age 3 years, she had secondary microcephaly and occasional myoclonus, could not sit unsupported, and made no purposeful movements. At age 9 she had fixed flexion contractures of the elbows, hips, and knees. She had no distinctive dysmorphic features. At 10 years of age, her Vineland Adaptive Behavior composite score of 26 corresponded to severe intellectual disability, and her social function was impaired across all domains. She continued to have afebrile generalized tonic-clonic seizures several times a week. Brain MRI at 6 months showed mild cerebellar hypoplasia and a thin corpus callosum. By 3 years, MRI showed cerebellar volume loss involving both hemispheres, the vermis, and the middle cerebellar peduncles, as well as mild cerebral volume loss. By age 8 years, EEG showed continuous focal sharp and slow wave complexes over the posterior regions and brief tonic seizures. Nerve conduction studies, electromyography, electroretinography, and audiology were normal. Her father had infrequent migraines without motor symptoms, and mild learning difficulties were suspected. Her mother had no neurologic symptoms. Two brothers, who were not tested, had mild learning difficulties. From their review of reported cases, Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) identified the following consistent features: infantile hypotonia, treatment-refractory seizures beginning in the first 6 months of life, cerebral visual impairment, profound developmental delay with persistent motor impairment, and progressive cerebellar changes on MRI.

Mammadova et al. (2024) reported a family in which the nonconsanguineous parents had 4 sons. Three of the boys had a severe early-onset phenotype. The first child had hypotonia from the neonatal period and died at 5 months of suspected sudden infant death syndrome; he was not tested genetically. The second child had muscular hypotonia from the first week of life. At 5 months, he presented with seizures, deep apneic spells, dysphagia, and severe hypotonia, and EEG showed hypsarrhythmia. He also had blindness, edema, and optic atrophy, so PEHO syndrome was suspected. Brain MRI showed cerebellar hypoplasia. His seizures were refractory to multiple antiseizure medications, he required tube feeding, and he died at 14 months. The index patient, the fourth child, had hypotonia at birth. Seizures began at 4 weeks of age and included bilateral clonic seizures, myoclonic seizures, gaze rigidity, and apneic spells. EEG initially showed burst suppression and later showed generalized background slowing with multifocal sharp waves. He had absent deep tendon reflexes, respiratory and swallowing insufficiency, blindness, optic atrophy, and severe global developmental delay. Brain MRI was normal. He died of respiratory insufficiency, at 2 years of age according to the text and at 2.5 years according to the table. A third brother had a milder phenotype: he presented at 5 years with episodic unsteady gait, dizziness, mild intellectual impairment, and primary generalized epilepsy that responded to levetiracetam. The mother and a maternal uncle had episodic ataxia type 2. The mother also had seizures in childhood, chronic headache, and mild intellectual deficits. The father had no specific neurologic symptoms. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE42B in the families reported by Reinson et al. (2016), Arteche-López et al. (2021), Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022), and Mammadova et al. (2024) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Heterozygous carriers in these families were either asymptomatic or had milder and variable neurologic phenotypes. Mammadova et al. (2024) concluded that CACNA1A belongs to a group of genes associated with codominant, or both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive, inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Exome sequencing by Reinson et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the CACNA1A gene in a brother and sister with DEE42B. One was a c.4315T-A transversion in exon 27, resulting in a trp1439-to-arg (W1439R) substitution. The other was a 7-bp deletion (c.472_478delGCCTTCC) in exon 3, resulting in a frameshift and premature termination (Ala158ThrfsTer6). The parents and 2 sibs were each heterozygous for one of the mutations.

Arteche-López et al. (2021) identified a homozygous loss-of-function variant in the CACNA1A gene in patients with a severe phenotype. According to the reviews by Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) and Mammadova et al. (2024), this variant was a nonsense mutation, c.2767C-T (R932X), and it was found in 4 sibs from a consanguineous family.

Using a targeted 105-gene epileptic encephalopathy panel, Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) identified compound heterozygous variants in the CACNA1A gene in a girl with DEE42B, and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. The maternally inherited variant was a 2-bp deletion (c.2042_2043delAG), resulting in a frameshift (Gln681ArgfsTer100); it had previously been reported as a pathogenic change associated with episodic ataxia type 2. The paternally inherited variant was a c.1693G-A transition, resulting in a glu565-to-lys (E565K) substitution. This missense variant was absent from gnomAD, affected a highly conserved residue within an ion transport domain, and was predicted to be deleterious. However, it remained classified as a variant of uncertain significance under ACMG/AMP criteria.

Trio exome sequencing by Mammadova et al. (2024) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the CACNA1A gene in a boy with DEE42B. One was a maternally inherited 1-bp deletion (c.2602delG), resulting in Ala868ProfsTer24. The other was a paternally inherited 1-bp deletion (c.5476delC), resulting in His1826ThrfsTer30. Exome sequencing of stored material confirmed both mutations in an affected deceased brother. Material from the first affected brother was not available. Neither variant was present in gnomAD or ClinVar, and both were predicted to result in loss of function. The report was inconsistent about the mildly affected brother: the text stated that he was heterozygous for the maternal c.2602delG variant, whereas the table listed him as heterozygous for the paternal c.5476delC variant.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Mammadova et al. (2024) compared reported biallelic CACNA1A genotypes. Biallelic truncating variants, including their family's compound heterozygous frameshift mutations and the homozygous R932X nonsense mutation, were associated with a severe early-onset epileptic encephalopathy and early death. Compound heterozygosity for a missense and a truncating variant was associated with hypotonia, early-onset epilepsy, and profound developmental delay, but with longer survival in some individuals. A homozygous in-frame insertion in 2 adult sibs was associated with progressive myoclonic epilepsy beginning after age 50 years. Mammadova et al. (2024) suggested that these findings supported a correlation between the deleteriousness of biallelic loss-of-function variants and the severity of the phenotype. Wong-Spracklen et al. (2022) suggested a dose-dependent effect of calcium channel dysfunction: monoallelic variants are associated with a highly variable spectrum, whereas biallelic variants lead to severe and progressive neurologic symptoms and cognitive disability.
