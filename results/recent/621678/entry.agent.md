### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the MAX gene on chromosome 14q23.3.

### Description

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is a syndrome of endocrine and nonendocrine tumors caused by germline pathogenic variants or genomic rearrangements of the MAX gene (Haider et al., 2025). Its main feature is highly penetrant pheochromocytoma, often bilateral and sometimes metastatic, with or without paraganglioma (Maggacis et al., 2026). Other reported tumors include pituitary neuroendocrine tumors that secrete growth hormone and/or prolactin, other neural crest-derived tumors such as ganglioneuroma and neuroblastoma, and possibly parathyroid adenomas and nonendocrine tumors (Seabrook et al., 2021; Lian et al., 2024; Maggacis et al., 2026).

### Nomenclature

Seabrook et al. (2021) reported 2 families with germline MAX variants, pheochromocytomas, and multiple other tumors. They suggested that MAX is a novel multiple endocrine neoplasia gene and raised the term 'multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5.' Maggacis et al. (2026) attributed the designation MEN5 to Seabrook et al. (2021).

In their review of the 2022 WHO classification of familial endocrine tumor syndromes, Nosé et al. (2022) described MEN5 as one of the newly defined familial cancer syndromes. Lian et al. (2024) concluded that germline MAX mutations may cause a new type of MEN originating from the neural crest, which may be called MEN5. Sahakian et al. (2024) noted that families with a MAX mutation and a phenotypic spectrum suggestive of MEN pointed to the existence of another syndrome, MEN5.

### Clinical Features

Comino-Mendez et al. (2011) studied 3 unrelated individuals with hereditary pheochromocytoma and then a selected series of 59 patients with pheochromocytoma. Their findings suggested that MAX mutations are associated with a malignant outcome.

Taieb et al. (2018) described imaging findings in 6 consecutive patients with MAX-related pheochromocytoma. All but 1 patient developed bilateral pheochromocytomas during their lifetime, and the tumors were often multiple within the same gland. The 2 lesions that 18F-FDOPA PET/CT missed were smaller than 1 cm and corresponded to nodular adrenomedullary hyperplasia.

Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) updated their report of a family with pheochromocytoma over 3 generations. The proband had neuroblastoma of the broad ligament at 17 months of age. At age 22 years she developed a right pheochromocytoma, a ganglioneuroma at the site of the original neuroblastoma, and an ileal schwannoma. Six years later she had a left pheochromocytoma with a mature ganglioneuroma, and a leiomyosarcoma of the mesosigmoid was removed at the same time. She later developed cutaneous and other metastases of the leiomyosarcoma, a hepatic carcinoma at 42 years, and a uterine leiomyoma. Her paternal grandfather had bilateral pheochromocytoma at 70 years. Her youngest brother presented at 31 years with cardiomyopathy complicated by Takotsubo syndrome, which led to the diagnosis of bilateral pheochromocytoma. Her paternal uncle was found at 56 years to have a clinically silent 11-mm right adrenal nodule with strong [18F]-DOPA uptake. Her father had lung adenocarcinoma at 47 years and prostate cancer at 54 years but no evidence of pheochromocytoma.

Seabrook et al. (2021) reported 2 families with germline MAX variants who were studied at university hospitals in Australia. Family A included several individuals with pheochromocytoma, some bilateral and some metastatic. Two children in the family, without pheochromocytoma to date, had neuroendocrine tumors: a paravertebral ganglioneuroma in one and an abdominal neuroblastoma in the other. One individual had acromegaly, and immunohistochemistry of that patient's pheochromocytoma tissue was positive for growth hormone-releasing hormone. Another individual with previously resected pheochromocytomas had pituitary enlargement and elevated IGF1. In family B, the proband had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, multigland parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas.

Lian et al. (2024) investigated 19 individuals from 3 families with pheochromocytoma. Six carried germline MAX variants, and 3 of these had pheochromocytoma. The proband of family A was a man diagnosed with right pheochromocytoma at 24 years after more than 2 years of high blood pressure (200/110 mm Hg at presentation). At 36 years a left adrenal mass was found, with plasma norepinephrine raised to 2,766.00 ng/L, and he was diagnosed with metachronous bilateral pheochromocytoma. The proband of family B, a 34-year-old man, had asymptomatic bilateral adrenal masses with normal plasma catecholamines and metanephrines; pathology confirmed bilateral pheochromocytoma. The proband of family C, a 64-year-old woman with occasional paroxysmal headache, had a right pheochromocytoma with raised plasma norepinephrine. The 3 carriers without disease, aged 13, 59, and 43 years, had no evidence of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, or other neuroendocrine tumors.

Lian et al. (2024) also reviewed 109 patients with germline MAX mutations from 87 families, comprising their 6 carriers and 103 carriers from 84 previously reported families. Of the 109 patients, 101 (92.7%) developed pheochromocytoma. The mean age at diagnosis was 32.8 +/- 12.6 years (range, 13-80), and the male-to-female ratio was 1.3:1. Fifty-nine patients (58.4%) had bilateral pheochromocytoma (47 simultaneous, 12 metachronous), and 19 (18.8%) had metastatic pheochromocytoma. Eleven (10.9%) also had chest or abdominal paraganglioma, and 1 had only head and neck paraganglioma. Nine patients (8.3%) had pituitary neuroendocrine tumors: 3 with raised growth hormone only, 4 with raised prolactin only, and 1 with both. Eleven had other neuroendocrine tumors diagnosed between ages 0.7 and 60 years, including 4 with primary hyperparathyroidism. Seven (6.4%) had non-neuroendocrine tumors, and 3 carriers were asymptomatic. Of the 87 families, 22 (25.3%) had a family history of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma.

Haider et al. (2025) described an extensive kindred with a germline MAX variant. The propositus developed pituitary gigantism due to a mixed growth hormone- and prolactin-secreting pituitary macroadenoma. This was controlled after 2 surgeries, medical therapy, and radiotherapy. He later developed bilateral and recurrent pheochromocytomas. After his death, an extra-adrenal myelolipoma was identified that was negative on MAX immunohistochemistry. Many members of the kindred had a history of pheochromocytoma or uncontrolled hypertension.

In a systematic review, Charoenngam et al. (2026) identified 45 studies covering 127 patients with germline MAX mutations. The median age at first tumor diagnosis was 29 years (interquartile range 23.0-39.0), and 37.0% of patients were women. The most frequent tumors were pheochromocytoma (112 patients), pituitary adenoma (11), and paraganglioma (10), with median ages at diagnosis of 31.0, 33.0, and 43.0 years, respectively. Of the pheochromocytomas, 38.3% were unilateral and 27.6% were metastatic, and most secreted norepinephrine. The authors considered the causal association between primary hyperparathyroidism and MAX loss to be uncertain. ⚠︎

In a review, Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that MAX-associated pituitary adenomas tend to be functioning tumors, typically of PIT1 lineage, secreting growth hormone and/or prolactin. They also noted that the broader MAX phenotype has been hypothesized to include ganglioneuromas, neuroblastomas, parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, myelolipoma, and lung adenocarcinoma.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) who had bilateral pheochromocytoma at 31 years, urinary normetanephrines were raised to 1979 nmol/mmol creatinine (8 times the upper limit) while total metanephrines were normal. The authors considered this pattern typical of patients with MAX pathogenic variants.

In the literature review by Lian et al. (2024), catecholamine or metabolite levels were reported for 63 of 101 patients with pheochromocytoma, and 61 (96.8%) were positive. Of these 61, 18 (29.5%) had a rise in normetanephrine/norepinephrine alone and 43 (70.5%) had a mixed rise that included metanephrine/adrenaline and/or dopamine. Maggacis et al. (2026) described the catecholamine phenotype as generally mixed noradrenaline and adrenaline secretion. In the systematic review by Charoenngam et al. (2026), most pheochromocytomas secreted norepinephrine.

### Inheritance

Charoenngam et al. (2026) described MEN5 as caused by heterozygous inactivating germline MAX mutations. Comino-Mendez et al. (2011) suggested that MAX mutations are preferentially transmitted from the father. In the family reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020), the MAX variant was present in 3 generations and showed incomplete penetrance, because a carrier father developed no symptomatic adrenal lesion. Haider et al. (2025) characterized both affected and unaffected carriers in their kindred. In their literature review, Lian et al. (2024) found that only 22 of 87 families (25.3%) had a clear family history of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, and they suggested that MAX mutations may have a very high de novo rate (65.8%).

### Molecular Genetics

Comino-Mendez et al. (2011) sequenced the exomes of 3 unrelated individuals with hereditary pheochromocytoma and identified mutations in the MAX gene. The tumors lacked MAX protein and showed loss of heterozygosity caused by uniparental disomy, supporting a role for MAX in the disease. Follow-up of a selected series of 59 patients with pheochromocytoma identified 5 further MAX mutations.

Taieb et al. (2018) found 5 different MAX mutations among 6 patients with MAX-related pheochromocytoma. In silico analysis predicted that the mutations disrupted MAX/MYC interaction and/or abolished DNA binding.

In the family reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020), the brother of the proband developed bilateral pheochromocytoma but did not carry the familial KIF1B variant. A gene panel identified a heterozygous c.145T-C transition in the MAX gene, resulting in a ser49-to-pro (S49P) substitution (see Ser49Pro under Allelic Variants). By ACMG criteria the variant was classified as of uncertain significance. However, both of the brother's tumors had lost the wildtype MAX allele, and structural modeling predicted that the variant was deleterious. The proband, her father, her grandfather, and her paternal uncle were also heterozygous for the variant. No loss of the wildtype MAX allele or second somatic MAX mutation was found in the proband's pheochromocytomas. The authors noted that a high proportion of normal tissue in the samples could not be ruled out as a cause. Exome sequencing of the proband excluded pathogenic variants in other pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma genes and in 30 cancer-predisposition genes. ⚠︎

In family A of Seabrook et al. (2021), a germline c.200C-A mutation in MAX, resulting in an ala67-to-asp (A67D) substitution, was present in all individuals with pheochromocytoma and in both children with neuroendocrine tumors. Pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity, and in vitro studies confirmed that the variant causes loss of function. In family B, the proband carried a truncating germline MAX mutation, c.22G-T, resulting in a glu8-to-ter (E8X) substitution.

Lian et al. (2024) used targeted next-generation sequencing of 24 hereditary pheochromocytoma genes, with Sanger confirmation, in 19 members of 3 families with pheochromocytoma. They identified heterozygous germline MAX mutations in 6 individuals: R75X in family A, a frameshift (c.242_243dupAC) in family B, and E158A in family C. In their literature review of 109 patients, they counted 53 different germline MAX alterations spread across all 5 exons. Nonsense mutations were the most common type (47.7%). The most frequent mutations were R33X (c.97C-T; 21.1%), R75X (c.223C-T; 13.8%), and A67D (c.200C-A; 7.3%).

Haider et al. (2025) identified a novel germline 1-bp deletion in exon 4 of MAX (c.228delG) in an extensive kindred with MEN5. The deletion was predicted to cause a frameshift and a truncated protein (Asn78ThrfsTer92).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Lian et al. (2024) compared patients with missense MAX mutations against patients with all other mutation types. The groups did not differ significantly in age at diagnosis or in rates of pheochromocytoma, metastatic pheochromocytoma, paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, other neuroendocrine tumors, or non-neuroendocrine tumors (all p greater than 0.05). Charoenngam et al. (2026) stated that further studies are needed to define genotype-phenotype associations.

### Pathogenesis

Seabrook et al. (2021) found loss of MAX staining on immunohistochemistry in pheochromocytomas and other neural crest tumors from carriers of the A67D variant. In vitro studies showed that the variant causes loss of function.

In their systematic review, Charoenngam et al. (2026) found evidence that MAX loss contributes to tumorigenesis in paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, renal oncocytoma, myelolipoma, and liver sarcoma. Maggacis et al. (2026) cited a patient with the MAX R33X variant and bilateral asynchronous pheochromocytoma whose prolactin/growth hormone cosecreting pituitary adenoma showed loss of MAX expression, consistent with tumoral loss of heterozygosity.

### Diagnosis

Taieb et al. (2018) found that 18F-FDOPA PET/CT visualized MAX-related pheochromocytomas in all cases and detected more adrenal and extraadrenal lesions than CT did (per-lesion sensitivity 90.9% vs 52.4% for CT/MRI). They recommended 18F-FDOPA PET/CT as the first-line functional imaging method for these patients.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that both loss of MAX immunohistochemical staining and loss of heterozygosity have been found in MAX-related pheochromocytoma. They suggested these tests when a causative MAX variant is suspected, for example when a germline MAX variant is of uncertain significance.

### Clinical Management

Lian et al. (2024) recommended a comprehensive baseline assessment of neural crest cell-derived diseases for all individuals with germline MAX mutations, taking into account the risk of bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma. ⚠︎

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that there are no formal guidelines for comprehensive MEN5 surveillance. They cited an international consensus statement on childhood pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, which recommended for carriers of a known pathogenic germline MAX variant annual clinical assessment from 10 years of age, together with annual plasma metanephrines and MRI from neck to pelvis every 2 to 3 years, starting at presentation or at 15 years of age. Maggacis et al. (2026) also noted that MAX-associated lactotroph adenomas tend to respond well to dopamine agonists, whereas MAX-associated somatotrophinomas tend to require several lines of therapy.

### Population Genetics

Lian et al. (2024) reported that MAX mutations are found in 0.8% to 1.9% of patients with pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma. Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that the exact prevalence of MEN5 is unknown but that it is thought to account for 0.8 to 1.9% of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma.

### History

Schlisio et al. (2008) identified inherited loss-of-function KIF1B-beta missense mutations in neuroblastomas and pheochromocytomas. Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) had previously described a family in which predisposition to pheochromocytoma segregated over 3 generations with a heterozygous KIF1B variant (c.4442G-A, S1481N). When a brother of the proband who lacked the KIF1B variant developed bilateral pheochromocytoma, they re-analyzed the family and found a MAX variant. They concluded that inheritance of pheochromocytoma in this family was linked to the MAX variant rather than the KIF1B variant, although the KIF1B variant may have contributed to the proband's neuroblastoma.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, SER49PRO [c.145T>C; p.Ser49Pro]

In a family with pheochromocytoma over 3 generations, Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) identified a heterozygous c.145T-C transition (NM_002382.5) in the MAX gene, resulting in a ser49-to-pro (S49P) substitution. The variant was present in the proband, who had neuroblastoma in infancy and later bilateral pheochromocytoma, and in her brother, who developed bilateral pheochromocytoma at 31 years. It was also present in her grandfather, who had bilateral pheochromocytoma at 70 years, in her paternal uncle, who had an [18F]-DOPA-avid adrenal nodule, and in her father, who did not have pheochromocytoma. By ACMG criteria the variant was classified as of uncertain significance. However, both of the brother's tumors had lost the wildtype MAX allele, and structural modeling predicted that the proline substitution was incompatible with the native conformation of MAX.

**.0002 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, ALA67ASP [c.200C>A; p.Ala67Asp]

In family A, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a germline c.200C-A mutation in the MAX gene, resulting in an ala67-to-asp (A67D) substitution. Family members had pheochromocytoma (including bilateral and metastatic disease), paravertebral ganglioneuroma, abdominal neuroblastoma, and acromegaly. The mutation was present in all individuals with pheochromocytoma and in both children with neuroendocrine tumors. Pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity, and pheochromocytomas and other neural crest tumors showed loss of MAX staining. In vitro studies confirmed that the variant causes loss of function.

In their literature review of 109 patients with germline MAX mutations, Lian et al. (2024) found that A67D (c.200C-A) accounted for 7.3% of mutations and was among the 3 most common.

**.0003 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, GLU8TER [c.22G>T; p.Glu8*]

In the proband of family B, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a truncating germline c.22G-T mutation in the MAX gene, resulting in a glu8-to-ter (E8X) substitution. The proband had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, multigland parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas.

**.0004 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, ARG75TER [c.223C>T; p.Arg75*]

In the proband of family A, Lian et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous c.223C-T nonsense mutation in exon 4 of the MAX gene (NM_002382.5), resulting in an arg75-to-ter (R75X) substitution. He had right pheochromocytoma at 24 years and left pheochromocytoma at 36 years. Of the family members tested, only the proband and his 13-year-old son carried the mutation, and the son had no abnormalities. The authors counted the proband among the de novo cases. The variant had a CADD score of 36, and modeling predicted a truncated MAX protein. In their literature review, R75X accounted for 13.8% of germline MAX mutations.

**.0005 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, 2-BP DUP, 242AC [c.242_243dupAC; p.Gln82Thrfs*89]

In a 34-year-old man (family B) with simultaneous bilateral pheochromocytoma, Lian et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous 2-bp duplication (c.242_243dupAC) in exon 4 of the MAX gene. The duplication was predicted to cause a frameshift (Gln82ThrfsTer89). Pathogenicity could not be predicted with CADD or REVEL, but the authors considered the variant pathogenic in light of the clinical features. Modeling predicted a shortened C terminus.

**.0006 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, GLU158ALA [c.473A>C; p.Glu158Ala]

In a 64-year-old woman (family C) with unilateral right pheochromocytoma, Lian et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous c.473A-C transversion in exon 5 of the MAX gene, resulting in a glu158-to-ala (E158A) substitution. The variant was also found in 1 of her daughters and 1 of her sisters, neither of whom had adrenal or biochemical abnormalities. The variant had a CADD score of 25 and a REVEL score of 0.528, and modeling predicted a change in the loop domain of MAX.

**.0007 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, 1-BP DEL, 228G [c.228delG; p.Asn78Thrfs*92]

In an extensive kindred with MEN5, Haider et al. (2025) identified a novel germline 1-bp deletion (c.228delG) in exon 4 of the MAX gene. The deletion was predicted to cause a frameshift and a truncated protein (Asn78ThrfsTer92). The propositus had pituitary gigantism from a mixed growth hormone- and prolactin-secreting macroadenoma, bilateral and recurrent pheochromocytomas, and an extraadrenal myelolipoma that was negative on MAX immunohistochemistry. Many members of the kindred had pheochromocytoma or uncontrolled hypertension, and both affected and unaffected carriers were identified.
