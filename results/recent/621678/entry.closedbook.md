### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is caused by heterozygous germline mutation in the MAX gene (154950) on chromosome 14q23.

### Description

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is an autosomal dominant tumor predisposition syndrome caused by germline mutation in the MAX gene. Pheochromocytoma, often bilateral and multiple, is the main tumor type, and paraganglioma also occurs. Some carriers also develop tumors in other endocrine tissues, mainly pituitary adenomas. The term MEN5 was proposed for this association by Seabrook et al. (2021). ✗

### Nomenclature

Seabrook et al. (2021) reviewed patients with germline MAX variants who had pheochromocytoma or paraganglioma together with other endocrine tumors. They proposed that this association be called multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5 (MEN5). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Comino-Mendez et al. (2011) studied families with hereditary pheochromocytoma that had no mutation in the known susceptibility genes. In carriers of MAX mutations, pheochromocytomas were often bilateral, and some carriers had metastatic disease. ✗

Burnichon et al. (2012) screened a large series of patients with pheochromocytoma or paraganglioma and found germline MAX mutations in a small proportion. The tumors were mainly pheochromocytomas and were often bilateral. Their catecholamine profile was noradrenergic or intermediate. ✗

Seabrook et al. (2021) reviewed the reported carriers of germline MAX variants. Besides pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma, some carriers had pituitary adenomas, and other endocrine tumors were reported in a smaller number. ✗

### Inheritance

MEN5 is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. Comino-Mendez et al. (2011) found that in the families they studied, the disease was transmitted through the father, which suggested a parent-of-origin effect. ✗

### Molecular Genetics

Comino-Mendez et al. (2011) performed exome sequencing in unrelated patients with hereditary pheochromocytoma and identified germline mutations in the MAX gene. The tumors had lost the wildtype MAX allele, often through uniparental disomy of chromosome 14. MAX protein was absent from the tumors, which was consistent with MAX acting as a tumor suppressor. The tumors had a transcriptional profile that pointed to activation of MYC-related pathways. ✗

Burnichon et al. (2012) identified germline MAX mutations in patients with pheochromocytoma or paraganglioma from a large series. They confirmed that the tumors lacked MAX protein on immunohistochemistry. ✗

### Pathogenesis

MAX is the obligate partner of MYC. Comino-Mendez et al. (2011) found that MAX-mutant tumors lost the wildtype allele, and proposed that losing MAX function deregulates the MYC-MAX-MXD1 network and drives tumor formation. ✗
