### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the MAX gene (154950) on chromosome 14q23.3.

### Description

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is an endocrine and non-endocrine tumor syndrome caused by heterozygous inactivating germline mutations or genomic rearrangements of the MAX gene (Haider et al., 2025; Charoenngam et al., 2026). Pheochromocytoma-paraganglioma (PPGL) and pituitary adenoma are the cardinal features. Parathyroid adenomas and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors are less common (Maggacis et al., 2026). Seabrook et al. (2021) linked germline MAX mutations to pheochromocytomas, ganglioneuromas, neuroblastomas and pituitary neuroendocrine tumors, and possibly to parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma and lung adenocarcinoma. On this basis they suggested that MAX is a novel multiple endocrine neoplasia gene. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Seabrook et al. (2021) suggested that MAX is a novel multiple endocrine neoplasia gene. Lian et al. (2024) concluded that MAX germline mutations may cause a new type of multiple endocrine neoplasia arising from the neural crest and proposed the name MEN5. Sahakian et al. (2024) noted that recently described families carrying a MAX mutation show a range of phenotypes that evokes MEN, and that these families point to another syndrome, MEN5. In their overview of the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors, Nosé et al. (2022) listed MEN5 among the newly defined familial cancer syndromes, together with MAFA-related syndrome and MEN4. Maggacis et al. (2026) stated that the syndrome was designated in 2021, after MAX variants had been associated with PPGL since 2011.

### Clinical Features

Taïeb et al. (2018) studied 6 consecutive patients with MAX-related pheochromocytoma using contrast-enhanced CT and 18F-FDOPA PET/CT. Four were imaged at initial diagnosis and 2 at follow-up. All but 1 patient developed bilateral pheochromocytomas during their lifetime, and the tumors were often multiple within the same gland. The 2 lesions missed on 18F-FDOPA PET/CT were smaller than 1 cm and corresponded to nodular adrenomedullary hyperplasia.

Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) updated a previously reported family with predisposition to pheochromocytoma (PCC) across 3 generations. The female proband (III-1) developed a neuroblastoma of the broad ligament at 17 months of age. At age 22 years she developed a right PCC, a ganglioneuroma at the site of the original neuroblastoma and an ileal schwannoma. Six years later she developed a left PCC with a mature ganglioneuroma. She went on to develop a metastatic leiomyosarcoma of the mesosigmoid, a hepatic carcinoma at age 42 and a uterine leiomyoma. Her paternal grandfather had bilateral PCC at age 70. Her youngest brother (III-3) presented at age 31 with a cardiomyopathy complicated by Takotsubo syndrome, which led to the diagnosis of bilateral PCC. His urinary normetanephrines were 8 times the upper limit while total metanephrines were normal, a pattern the authors noted is reminiscent of MAX-related tumors. At age 56 the paternal uncle (II-3) developed an 11-mm hypervascular right adrenal nodule with strong [18F]-DOPA uptake, which the authors considered suggestive of PCC or an adrenomedullary nodule.

Seabrook et al. (2021) reported an Australian family (family A) in which multiple individuals had pheochromocytoma, including bilateral and metastatic disease. Two children who had not yet developed pheochromocytoma had neuroendocrine tumors: a paravertebral ganglioneuroma in one and an abdominal neuroblastoma in the other. One individual had acromegaly, and that person's pheochromocytoma tissue was positive for growth hormone-releasing hormone on immunohistochemistry. Another individual, whose pheochromocytomas had previously been resected, had pituitary enlargement and elevated IGF-1.

Seabrook et al. (2021) also described the proband of a second Australian family (family B). This patient had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, adenomas in multiple parathyroid glands, chondrosarcoma and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas.

Lian et al. (2024) described 3 Chinese probands. The first (family A) was a man diagnosed with right pheochromocytoma at age 24 years after more than 2 years of hypertension (200/110 mm Hg). At age 36 he developed a 3.8-cm left adrenal pheochromocytoma with elevated plasma norepinephrine, making his disease metachronous and bilateral. No pituitary abnormality or other neuroendocrine tumor was found. The second (family B) was a 34-year-old man with asymptomatic simultaneous bilateral adrenal masses (2.8 and 2.2 cm), normal blood pressure, and normal plasma catecholamines and metanephrines. Bilateral pheochromocytoma was confirmed after adrenal-sparing surgery. His pituitary and neck studies were normal, as were calcium, calcitonin, PTH, GH and prolactin. The third (family C) was a 64-year-old woman with occasional paroxysmal headache, elevated plasma norepinephrine and a 1.9-cm right pheochromocytoma. ⚠︎

In the same report, Lian et al. (2024) reviewed the literature on 109 MAX germline mutation carriers from 87 families. Of these, 101 (92.7%) developed pheochromocytoma at a mean age of 32.8 years (range, 13-80), with a male-to-female ratio of 1.3:1. The pheochromocytomas were bilateral in 59 (58.4%; 47 simultaneous, 12 metachronous) and metastatic in 19 (18.8%). Eleven (10.9%) also had thoracic or abdominal paraganglioma, and 1 had an isolated head and neck paraganglioma. Catecholamines were elevated in 61 of 63 carriers tested, mostly in a mixed noradrenergic/adrenergic pattern. Nine carriers (8.3%) had pituitary adenomas, presenting with acromegaly and/or prolactinoma symptoms: 3 growth hormone-secreting tumors, 4 prolactinomas and 1 mixed adenoma. Eleven (10.1%) had other neuroendocrine tumors, including primary hyperparathyroidism in 4 (1 with multifocal parathyroid adenomas), neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma/ganglioneuroma, C-cell hyperplasia and pancreatic NET. Seven (6.4%) had non-neuroendocrine tumors such as renal oncocytoma and renal cell carcinoma, and 3 carriers were asymptomatic. Maggacis et al. (2026) summarized the same series of 109 patients from 87 families.

Haider et al. (2025) described a large MEN5 kindred with an extensive history of pheochromocytomas or uncontrolled hypertension. The male propositus developed pituitary gigantism from a mixed growth hormone- and prolactin-secreting pituitary macroadenoma. The tumor was controlled after 2 surgeries, medical therapy and radiotherapy. He later developed bilateral and recurrent pheochromocytomas. After his death an extra-adrenal myelolipoma was found. The authors reported this as the first case of pituitary gigantism associated with a MAX pathogenic variant and described myelolipoma as a new disease association in MEN5.

Charoenngam et al. (2026) performed a systematic review of 45 studies covering 127 patients with germline MAX mutations. The median age at first tumor diagnosis was 29 years, and 37.0% of patients were women. The most frequent tumors were pheochromocytoma (112 patients; median age at diagnosis 31 years), pituitary adenoma (11 patients; median age 33 years) and paraganglioma (10 patients; median age 43 years). Of the pheochromocytomas, 38.3% were unilateral and 27.6% were metastatic, and most secreted norepinephrine.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that MAX-associated pituitary adenomas tend to be functioning and are typically of PIT1 lineage, secreting growth hormone and/or prolactin. MAX-associated lactotroph adenomas tend to respond well to dopamine agonists, whereas somatotrophinomas tend to need multiple lines of therapy. The authors added that the broader MAX phenotype has been hypothesized to include other neural crest-lineage diseases, such as ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, parathyroid adenoma, chondrosarcoma, myelolipoma and lung adenocarcinoma.

### Inheritance

Several reports describe heterozygous germline MAX mutations transmitted within families, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance (Cardot Bauters et al., 2020; Seabrook et al., 2021; Lian et al., 2024; Haider et al., 2025). Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) suggested that MAX mutations are preferentially transmitted through the father. ⚠︎

Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) proposed that susceptibility to PCC/PGL in their family was linked to the MAX variant with incomplete penetrance. The proband's father (II-2) carried the variant but developed no adrenal lesion, and his metanephrines remained normal. Lian et al. (2024) found asymptomatic carriers in 2 of their 3 families: an unaffected 13-year-old son in family A, and a 43-year-old daughter and 59-year-old sister in family C, neither of whom had biochemical or adrenal CT abnormalities. ⚠︎

Lian et al. (2024) noted that only 22 (25.3%) of 87 families with MAX mutations had a family history of PPGL, and that 8 (9.2%) showed a clear paternal family genetic background. They suggested that de novo MAX mutations may be common and estimated a de novo rate of 65.8%.

### Heterogeneity

Schlisio et al. (2008) identified inherited loss-of-function missense mutations in KIF1B-beta in neuroblastomas and pheochromocytomas, and an acquired loss-of-function mutation in a medulloblastoma. They noted that KIF1B-beta maps to chromosome 1p36.2, a region frequently deleted in neural crest-derived tumors, and argued that KIF1B-beta is a pathogenic target of these deletions. Using a shRNA screen, they showed that KIF1B-beta acts downstream of the EglN3 prolyl hydroxylase and is both necessary and sufficient for neuronal apoptosis when nerve growth factor becomes limiting.

In a family in which heritable PCC had previously been attributed to a germline KIF1B variant (c.4442G-A, p.Ser1481Asn), Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) concluded that the predisposition was linked to a MAX variant rather than to KIF1B. The proband's affected brother carried the MAX variant but not the KIF1B variant. The authors suggested that the KIF1B variant may have contributed to the proband's neuroblastoma. They considered her leiomyosarcoma, her hepatic carcinoma and her father's lung adenocarcinoma possibly independent of both MAX and KIF1B. Whole exome sequencing of the proband found maternally inherited variants of uncertain significance in TFAP2E (614428; p.R363C) and TMEM214 (615301; p.G263S). The wild-type TFAP2E allele was lost in her uterine leiomyoma, and the wild-type TMEM214 allele was lost in her leiomyosarcoma metastases. The authors suggested that these variants may contribute to her non-neural crest tumors.

### Molecular Genetics

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) sequenced the exomes of 3 unrelated individuals with hereditary pheochromocytoma who had no mutations in known susceptibility genes, and found mutations in the MAX gene in each. The tumors lacked MAX protein and showed loss of heterozygosity caused by uniparental disomy, supporting a role for MAX alterations in the disease. In a follow-up study of a selected series of 59 patients with pheochromocytoma, the authors found 5 additional MAX mutations. ⚠︎

Taïeb et al. (2018) reported 5 different MAX mutations among 6 patients with pheochromocytoma. In silico analyses predicted that the mutations disrupt the Max/Myc interaction and/or abolish binding to DNA.

In a family with PCC, Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) used a next-generation sequencing endocrine tumor panel to identify a germline heterozygous MAX variant, c.145T-C (p.Ser49Pro), in the proband's brother (III-3). He had bilateral PCC and did not carry the familial KIF1B variant. The MAX variant was also carried by the proband, her grandfather (both with PCC), her father and her uncle. It was classified as a variant of uncertain significance under ACMG criteria. Both of III-3's PCCs showed loss of the wild-type MAX allele, and structural modeling predicted that the variant is likely deleterious. In contrast, the proband's 2 PCCs showed no loss of the wild-type MAX allele, and Sanger sequencing excluded a second somatic MAX mutation. The authors noted that a high proportion of normal tissue in the samples could not be ruled out as the reason LOH was not detected. ⚠︎

In all members of an Australian family (family A) who had pheochromocytoma, and in both children with neuroendocrine tumors, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a germline heterozygous MAX variant, c.200C-A (p.Ala67Asp). The pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity. MAX staining was lost on immunohistochemistry in the pheochromocytomas and in the other neural crest tumors, and in vitro studies confirmed that the variant causes loss of function. In the proband of a second Australian family (family B), the authors identified a truncating germline MAX variant, c.22G-T (p.Glu8Ter).

Lian et al. (2024) used targeted next-generation sequencing of 24 PPGL genes to study 3 Chinese probands and identified a heterozygous MAX mutation in each. In family A they found a nonsense mutation in exon 4 (c.223C-T, p.Arg75Ter), confirmed by Sanger sequencing. In family B they found a novel frameshift mutation in exon 4 (c.242_243dupAC, p.Gln82ThrfsTer89); his parents were not tested. In family C they found a novel missense mutation in exon 5 (c.473A-C, p.Glu158Ala). In their literature review the authors found 53 distinct MAX germline alterations in 87 families, involving all 5 exons but mostly exons 3 and 4. Nonsense mutations made up 47.7%. The most common mutations were R33X (c.97C-T; 21.1%), R75X (c.223C-T; 13.8%) and A67D (c.200C-A; 7.3%), which the authors suggested may reflect family and/or regional aggregation.

In the propositus and in multiple affected and unaffected relatives from a MEN5 kindred, Haider et al. (2025) identified a novel germline heterozygous 1-bp deletion in exon 4 of the MAX gene (c.228delG). The deletion was predicted to cause a frameshift and a truncated protein (p.Asn78ThrfsTer92). The extra-adrenal myelolipoma found in the propositus was negative on MAX immunohistochemistry. ⚠︎

Maggacis et al. (2026) cited a patient with the pathogenic MAX variant c.97C-T (p.Arg33Ter) who had bilateral asynchronous pheochromocytoma and a pituitary adenoma co-secreting prolactin and growth hormone. MAX expression was lost in the adenoma on immunohistochemistry, consistent with loss of heterozygosity in the tumor.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) suggested that MAX mutations were associated with malignant outcome in pheochromocytoma and that loss of MAX function correlated with metastatic potential.

In their review of 109 carriers from 87 families, Lian et al. (2024) compared carriers of missense and non-missense MAX mutations. They found no significant difference in age at diagnosis, or in rates of pheochromocytoma, metastatic disease, paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma or other tumors, and suggested that the genotype-phenotype correlation is not strong.

### Pathogenesis

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) proposed that the MYC-MAX-MXD1 network is involved in the development and progression of neural crest cell tumors. They cited 2 lines of support: rat pheochromocytoma (PC12) cells lack functional MAX, and MYCN is amplified in neuroblastoma.

Charoenngam et al. (2026) found evidence implicating MAX loss in the development of paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, renal oncocytoma, myelolipoma and liver sarcoma. They considered the causal link between MAX loss and primary hyperparathyroidism uncertain.

Maggacis et al. (2026) explained that inactivating MAX variants prevent normal activation of the MYC-MAX heterodimer. This leaves MYC function unregulated and drives cell proliferation, differentiation and angiogenesis. They noted that the increased MYC activity is thought to produce a more aggressive subtype of neural crest chromaffin cells.

### Diagnosis

Taïeb et al. (2018) found that 18F-FDOPA PET/CT detected more lesions than CT/MRI in MAX-related pheochromocytoma (per-lesion sensitivity 90.9% vs 52.4%). 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT detected fewer lesions than 18F-FDOPA in 1 of 3 patients. 18F-FDG PET/CT was only faintly positive in 2 of 4 patients and underestimated extra-adrenal lesions in 1 patient. The authors concluded that 18F-FDOPA PET/CT should be considered the first-line functional imaging modality for these patients.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that both loss of MAX immunohistochemical staining and loss of heterozygosity have been found in MAX-related pheochromocytoma. They suggested that these tests may help when a causative MAX variant is suspected, for example when a germline MAX variant is of uncertain significance.

### Clinical Management

Lian et al. (2024) recommended that all MAX germline mutation carriers have a comprehensive baseline assessment for neural crest-derived diseases, including PPGL, pituitary adenoma, hyperparathyroidism and ganglioneuroma/neuroblastoma. They also recommended lifelong surveillance that takes account of the risk of bilateral and/or metastatic pheochromocytoma.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that there are no formal guidelines for MEN5 surveillance. They cited an international consensus statement on childhood PPGL with recommendations for carriers of a pathogenic germline MAX variant. These were annual clinical assessment from age 10 years, together with annual plasma metanephrines and MRI from neck to pelvis every 2 to 3 years, starting at presentation or from age 15 years.

### Population Genetics

Maggacis et al. (2026) stated that the exact prevalence of MEN5 is unknown but that it is thought to account for 0.8 to 1.9% of PPGL.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, SER49PRO [c.145T>C; p.S49P]

In a 3-generation family with pheochromocytoma (PCC), Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) identified a germline heterozygous c.145T-C transition in the MAX gene, predicting a ser49-to-pro (S49P) substitution. It was found by next-generation sequencing endocrine panel testing in the proband's brother (III-3), who had bilateral PCC. The variant was also carried by the proband and her grandfather, both of whom had PCC, and by her father and uncle. It was classified as a variant of uncertain significance under ACMG criteria. Both of III-3's PCCs showed loss of the wild-type MAX allele, and structural modeling predicted the variant to be likely deleterious. The proband's 2 PCCs showed no LOH and no second somatic MAX mutation, but the authors could not exclude normal-tissue contamination as the reason LOH was not detected. The father carried the variant without developing an adrenal lesion, and the authors proposed incomplete penetrance. ⚠︎

**.0002 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, ALA67ASP [c.200C>A; p.A67D]

In all members of an Australian family (family A) who had pheochromocytoma, and in 2 children with a ganglioneuroma and a neuroblastoma, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a germline heterozygous c.200C-A transversion in the MAX gene, predicting an ala67-to-asp (A67D) substitution. The pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity, and MAX staining was lost in the pheochromocytomas and other neural crest tumors. In vitro studies confirmed loss of function.

In their literature review, Lian et al. (2024) found that A67D accounted for 7.3% of MAX germline mutations.

The proband had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, multiple parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas.

**.0004 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**

The mutation was found by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It had a CADD score of 36, and 3D modeling predicted protein truncation.
