### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the MAX gene on chromosome 14q23.3.

### Description

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is a syndrome of endocrine and nonendocrine tumors caused by germline pathogenic variants or genomic rearrangements of the MAX gene (Haider et al., 2025). The main feature is pheochromocytoma, which is often bilateral and sometimes metastatic, with or without paraganglioma. Other reported tumors include pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (mainly growth hormone- and/or prolactin-secreting), ganglioneuromas and neuroblastomas, possibly parathyroid adenomas, and nonendocrine tumors (Seabrook et al., 2021; Lian et al., 2024; Charoenngam et al., 2026).

### Nomenclature

Seabrook et al. (2021) suggested that MAX is a novel multiple endocrine neoplasia gene and proposed the designation multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5. Nosé et al. (2022) described MEN5 as one of the newly defined familial cancer syndromes in the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors. Sahakian et al. (2024) and Lian et al. (2024) also proposed that MAX germline mutations define a separate MEN syndrome, MEN5. Maggacis et al. (2026) stated that the syndrome was designated MEN5 by Seabrook et al. (2021), after MAX variants had been associated with pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma since 2011.

### Clinical Features

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) studied individuals with hereditary pheochromocytoma who did not have mutations in the known susceptibility genes. In a follow-up series of 59 selected patients with pheochromocytoma, MAX mutations appeared to be associated with a malignant outcome.

Taïeb et al. (2018) reported 6 consecutive patients with MAX-related pheochromocytoma. All but 1 developed bilateral pheochromocytomas during their lifetime, and the tumors were often multiple within the same gland or present on both sides.

Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) reported follow-up of a 3-generation family. The proband developed neuroblastoma of the broad ligament at 17 months of age. At 22 years she developed a right pheochromocytoma, a ganglioneuroma at the site of the original neuroblastoma, and an ileal schwannoma, together with hypertension and high normetanephrine levels. Six years later she had surgery for a left pheochromocytoma associated with a mature ganglioneuroma. She also had a leiomyosarcoma of the mesosigmoid with later cutaneous and other metastases, a hepatic carcinoma at 42 years, and a uterine leiomyoma at 43 years. Her paternal grandfather had bilateral pheochromocytoma at 70 years. Her father had lung adenocarcinoma at 47 years and prostatic cancer at 54 years but never showed an adrenal lesion. Her youngest brother presented at 31 years with a cardiomyopathy complicated by Takotsubo syndrome, which led to the diagnosis of bilateral pheochromocytoma. At 56 years, a paternal uncle was found to have a clinically silent 11-mm hypervascular right adrenal nodule with strong [18F]-DOPA uptake, suggestive of pheochromocytoma or an adrenomedullary nodule. ⚠︎

Seabrook et al. (2021) reported 2 families with germline MAX variants evaluated at university hospitals in Australia. In family A, several individuals had pheochromocytoma, including bilateral and metastatic disease. Two children who had not developed pheochromocytoma had neuroendocrine tumors: one had a paravertebral ganglioneuroma and the other an abdominal neuroblastoma. One individual had acromegaly, and his pheochromocytoma tissue stained positive for growth hormone-releasing hormone. Another individual, who had previously had pheochromocytomas resected, had an enlarged pituitary and elevated IGF1. In family B, the proband had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, multigland parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas. ⚠︎

Lian et al. (2024) reported 3 unrelated families. In family A, the proband was diagnosed with a right pheochromocytoma at 24 years after more than 2 years of hypertension (blood pressure 200/110 mm Hg). A 3.8 x 2.9 cm left adrenal pheochromocytoma was found at 36 years, with plasma norepinephrine of 2,766.00 ng/L. His 13-year-old son also carried the mutation but had no abnormalities on screening. In family B, a 34-year-old man had asymptomatic, simultaneous bilateral pheochromocytomas with normal blood pressure and normal plasma catecholamines and metanephrines. In family C, a 64-year-old woman with occasional paroxysmal headache had a 1.9 x 1.6 cm right pheochromocytoma and plasma norepinephrine of 2,243.7 pmol/L. Her daughter and sister also carried the mutation but showed no abnormalities.

Lian et al. (2024) also reviewed 109 patients from 87 families with germline MAX mutations, including their own. Pheochromocytoma developed in 101 patients (92.7%), with a mean age at diagnosis of 32.8 +/- 12.6 years (range, 13-80) and a male-to-female ratio of 1.3:1. Of these, 59 (58.4%) had bilateral tumors: 47 synchronous and 12 metachronous, with a mean interval of 11.1 +/- 6.7 years in the metachronous group. Nineteen (18.8%) had metastatic pheochromocytoma. Eleven patients (10.9%) also had chest or abdominal paraganglioma, and 1 had a head and neck paraganglioma without pheochromocytoma. Nine patients (8.3%) had pituitary neuroendocrine tumors: 3 had elevated growth hormone alone, 4 had elevated prolactin alone, and 1 had both. Eleven patients had other neuroendocrine tumors, diagnosed between 0.7 and 60 years of age. These included primary hyperparathyroidism in 4 patients, and neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma/ganglioneuroma, C-cell hyperplasia, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor. Seven patients (6.4%) had non-neuroendocrine tumors, including squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue, papillary thyroid carcinoma, prostate cancer, renal cell carcinoma, renal oncocytoma, and chondrosarcoma with lung adenocarcinoma. Three mutation carriers had no clinical phenotype. ⚠︎

Haider et al. (2025) reported a large kindred. The propositus developed pituitary gigantism due to a mixed growth hormone- and prolactin-secreting pituitary macroadenoma, which was controlled after 2 surgeries, medical therapy, and radiotherapy. He later developed bilateral, recurrent pheochromocytomas. After his death, an extra-adrenal myelolipoma was found. The kindred had an extensive history of pheochromocytomas or uncontrolled hypertension. The authors stated that this was the first report of pituitary gigantism with a pathogenic MAX variant, and they added myelolipoma to the MEN5 phenotype.

In a systematic review of 127 patients with germline MAX mutations from 45 studies, Charoenngam et al. (2026) found a median age at first tumor diagnosis of 29 years (23.0-39.0); 37.0% of patients were women. The most frequent tumors were pheochromocytoma (112 patients), pituitary adenoma (11), and paraganglioma (10), with median ages at diagnosis of 31.0, 33.0, and 43.0 years, respectively. Among the pheochromocytomas, 38.3% were unilateral and 27.6% were metastatic. Evidence that MAX loss contributed to tumor formation existed for paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, renal oncocytoma, myelolipoma, and liver sarcoma. Whether MAX loss causes primary hyperparathyroidism remained uncertain.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that pituitary adenomas associated with MAX variants tend to be functioning, typically of PIT1 lineage, with growth hormone and/or prolactin secretion. They cited a patient with the MAX variant p.Arg33* and bilateral asynchronous pheochromocytoma whose prolactin/growth hormone co-secreting pituitary adenoma had lost MAX expression on immunohistochemistry.

### Biochemical Features

In the family reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020), the brother with bilateral pheochromocytoma had urinary normetanephrines of 1979 nmol/mmol of creatinine (8 times the upper limit) and normal total metanephrines of 182 nmol/mmol of creatinine. The authors considered this pattern typical of patients with MAX pathogenic variants. Before surgery, the grandfather had urinary normetanephrines 5 times and chromogranin A 3 times the upper limit.

The reports disagreed on the usual secretory pattern. In their literature review, Lian et al. (2024) found that 61 of 63 patients with pheochromocytoma and reported biochemistry (96.8%) had elevated blood or urine catecholamines or metabolites. Of these, 18 (29.5%) had an isolated increase in normetanephrine/norepinephrine and 43 (70.5%) had mixed increases involving metanephrine/epinephrine, normetanephrine/norepinephrine, and/or dopamine. Lian et al. (2024) therefore concluded that MAX-related tumors mostly have a mixed noradrenaline/adrenaline phenotype. In contrast, Charoenngam et al. (2026) reported that most pheochromocytomas in their systematic review were norepinephrine-secreting. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The reports were consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance. Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) suggested that MAX mutations were transmitted preferentially from the father. In the family reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020), the heterozygous MAX variant was present in 3 generations; the father of the proband carried the variant but had no symptomatic adrenal lesion, which the authors interpreted as incomplete penetrance. In their literature review, Lian et al. (2024) found that only 22 of 87 families (25.3%) had a family history of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma and 8 (9.2%) had a clear paternal family history. They suggested that de novo MAX mutations may be common. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) performed exome sequencing in 3 unrelated individuals with hereditary pheochromocytoma and identified mutations in the MAX gene. MAX protein was absent from the tumors, and loss of heterozygosity caused by uniparental disomy was observed. In a follow-up study of 59 selected patients with pheochromocytoma, they identified 5 additional MAX mutations.

Taïeb et al. (2018) found 5 different MAX mutations in 6 patients with MAX-related pheochromocytoma. In silico analysis predicted that the mutations disrupted the MAX/MYC interaction and/or abolished DNA binding.

In the family reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020), next-generation sequencing of a panel of 11 pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma susceptibility genes identified a heterozygous germline MAX variant, c.145T-C (S49P), in a brother with bilateral pheochromocytoma (see MAX, SER49PRO). The brother did not carry the KIF1B variant previously reported in the family. The proband, her father, her grandfather, and her paternal uncle also carried the MAX variant. By ACMG criteria it was classified as a variant of uncertain significance. Both of the brother's tumors had lost the wildtype MAX allele, whereas no loss of heterozygosity or second somatic MAX mutation was found in the proband's pheochromocytomas. Whole-exome sequencing of the proband excluded pathogenic variants in other pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma genes and in 30 cancer-predisposition genes. The authors concluded that susceptibility to pheochromocytoma in this family was linked to the MAX variant rather than the KIF1B variant. ⚠︎

Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a germline MAX variant, A67D (see MAX, ALA67ASP), in all individuals with pheochromocytoma in family A and in both children with neural crest tumors. Pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity, immunohistochemistry showed loss of MAX staining in the pheochromocytomas and other neural crest tumors, and in vitro studies confirmed that the variant caused loss of function. In the proband of family B they identified a truncating germline MAX variant, E8X (see MAX, GLU8TER).

In 3 families with pheochromocytoma, Lian et al. (2024) used targeted next-generation sequencing of 24 genes and found 3 heterozygous MAX mutations, confirmed by Sanger sequencing: R75X (see MAX, ARG75TER), a 2-bp duplication causing a frameshift, Q82Tfs*89 (see MAX, 2-BP DUP, 242AC), and E158A (see MAX, GLU158ALA). In their review of 109 patients from 87 families, they counted 53 different MAX germline alterations spread across all 5 exons. The most frequent types were nonsense (47.7%) and missense (23.8%) mutations. The 3 most common mutations were R33X (c.97C-T; 21.1%), R75X (c.223C-T; 13.8%), and A67D (c.200C-A; 7.3%).

In an extended kindred, Haider et al. (2025) identified a novel germline 1-bp deletion in exon 4 of the MAX gene, c.228delG, predicted to produce a truncated protein (see MAX, 1-BP DEL, 228G). Several affected and unaffected carriers were identified. The extra-adrenal myelolipoma of the propositus was negative for MAX on immunohistochemistry.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In their review of 109 patients with germline MAX mutations, Lian et al. (2024) compared patients with missense mutations and those with other mutation types. The 2 groups did not differ significantly in age at diagnosis or in the rates of pheochromocytoma, metastatic pheochromocytoma, paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, other neuroendocrine tumors, or non-neuroendocrine tumors (all P greater than 0.05). This suggested that genotype-phenotype correlation is weak or absent.

### Pathogenesis

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) proposed that the MYC-MAX-MXD1 network is involved in the development and progression of neural crest cell tumors. They based this on the absence of functional MAX in rat pheochromocytoma (PC12) cells and on MYCN amplification in neuroblastoma, and suggested that loss of MAX function is linked to metastatic potential. Maggacis et al. (2026) stated that inactivating MAX variants prevent normal activation of the MYC-MAX heterodimer, leaving MYC function unregulated. They suggested that this increased MYC activity produces a more aggressive subtype of neural crest chromaffin cells.

### Diagnosis

Taïeb et al. (2018) studied 6 patients with MAX-related pheochromocytoma. 18F-FDOPA PET/CT visualized the tumors accurately and detected more adrenal and extra-adrenal lesions than CT/MRI (per-lesion sensitivity 90.9% vs 52.4%). The 2 lesions missed by 18F-FDOPA PET/CT were smaller than 1 cm and corresponded to nodular adrenomedullary hyperplasia. 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT detected fewer lesions than 18F-FDOPA PET/CT in 1 of 3 patients, and 18F-FDG PET/CT was only faintly positive in 2 of 4 patients. The authors recommended 18F-FDOPA PET/CT as the first-line functional imaging method.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that both loss of MAX immunohistochemical staining and loss of heterozygosity have been found in MAX-related pheochromocytoma. These tests may therefore help when a causative MAX variant is suspected, for example when a germline MAX variant is of uncertain significance. They also stated that screening with supine plasma metanephrines is essential.

### Clinical Management

Lian et al. (2024) recommended that all MAX germline mutation carriers have a comprehensive baseline assessment for neural crest-derived disease, including pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, hyperparathyroidism, and ganglioneuroma/neuroblastoma, followed by lifelong surveillance that accounts for the risk of bilateral and/or metastatic pheochromocytoma. Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that there were no formal guidelines for comprehensive MEN5 surveillance. However, an international consensus statement on childhood pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma recommended the following for carriers of a pathogenic germline MAX variant: annual clinical assessment from 10 years of age, and annual plasma metanephrines with MRI from neck to pelvis every 2 to 3 years, starting at presentation or at 15 years of age. Maggacis et al. (2026) also noted that MAX-associated lactotroph adenomas tend to respond well to dopamine agonists, whereas MAX-associated somatotroph adenomas tend to need multiple lines of therapy.

### Population Genetics

Lian et al. (2024) stated that MAX mutations account for 0.8 to 1.9% of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma. Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that the exact prevalence of MEN5 is unknown but that it is thought to account for 0.8 to 1.9% of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma.

### History

Schlisio et al. (2008) reported inherited loss-of-function missense mutations in KIF1B-beta in neuroblastomas and pheochromocytomas. KIF1B maps to chromosome 1p36.2, a region often deleted in neural crest-derived tumors, and the authors proposed it as a tumor suppressor. In 2 later reports, Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) followed a family from this earlier work in which predisposition to pheochromocytoma had segregated over 3 generations with a heterozygous KIF1B variant, c.4442G-A (S1481N). After a brother without the KIF1B variant developed bilateral pheochromocytoma, reanalysis found that a MAX variant was shared by the family members with pheochromocytoma. The authors concluded that pheochromocytoma in this family was linked to the MAX variant rather than the KIF1B variant. They suggested that the KIF1B variant might have contributed to the proband's neuroblastoma. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, SER49PRO [NM_002382.5:c.145T>C; p.Ser49Pro]

In a 3-generation family with pheochromocytoma, Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) identified a heterozygous germline c.145T-C transition in the MAX gene, resulting in a ser49-to-pro (S49P) substitution. It was first found by next-generation panel sequencing in a man who developed bilateral pheochromocytoma at 31 years. The variant was also present in his sister, who had neuroblastoma in infancy, bilateral pheochromocytoma, and multiple other tumors; in their grandfather, who had bilateral pheochromocytoma at 70 years; in their father, who had no adrenal lesion; and in a paternal uncle with an [18F]-DOPA-avid adrenal nodule. By ACMG criteria the variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance. Both of the brother's pheochromocytomas had lost the wildtype MAX allele, but no loss of heterozygosity was found in his sister's tumors. Structural modeling predicted that the proline substitution was incompatible with the native conformation of the protein. ⚠︎

**.0002 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, ALA67ASP [c.200C>A; p.Ala67Asp]

In family A, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a germline c.200C-A transversion in the MAX gene, resulting in an ala67-to-asp (A67D) substitution. The variant was present in all family members with pheochromocytoma and in 2 children, one with a paravertebral ganglioneuroma and one with an abdominal neuroblastoma. One affected individual had acromegaly, and another had pituitary enlargement with elevated IGF1. Pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity, immunohistochemistry showed loss of MAX staining in the pheochromocytomas and other neural crest tumors, and in vitro studies confirmed loss of function.

In their literature review, Lian et al. (2024) listed A67D (c.200C-A) as the third most common germline MAX mutation (7.3% of carriers).

**.0003 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, GLU8TER [c.22G>T; p.Glu8Ter]

In the proband of family B, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a truncating germline c.22G-T transversion in the MAX gene, resulting in a glu8-to-ter (E8X) substitution. The proband had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, multigland parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas.

**.0004 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, ARG75TER [NM_002382.5:c.223C>T; p.Arg75Ter]

In a man with metachronous bilateral pheochromocytoma diagnosed at 24 and 36 years of age (family A), Lian et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous c.223C-T transition in exon 4 of the MAX gene, resulting in an arg75-to-ter (R75X) substitution. His 13-year-old son also carried the mutation but had no abnormalities on screening. The CADD score was 36, and modeling predicted a truncated protein. In their literature review, R75X was the second most common germline MAX mutation (13.8% of carriers).

**.0005 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, 2-BP DUP, 242AC [NM_002382.5:c.242_243dupAC; p.Gln82ThrfsTer89]

In a 34-year-old man with asymptomatic, simultaneous bilateral pheochromocytomas (family B), Lian et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous 2-bp duplication (c.242_243dupAC) in exon 4 of the MAX gene, resulting in a frameshift and premature termination (Gln82Thrfs*89). The authors considered the mutation pathogenic given the clinical features, and modeling predicted a shortened C terminus. The parents were not tested.

**.0006 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, GLU158ALA [NM_002382.5:c.473A>C; p.Glu158Ala]

In a 64-year-old woman with a unilateral right pheochromocytoma (family C), Lian et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous c.473A-C transversion in exon 5 of the MAX gene, resulting in a glu158-to-ala (E158A) substitution. The mutation was also found in her daughter and her sister, neither of whom had adrenal or biochemical abnormalities. The CADD score was 25 and the REVEL score 0.528, and modeling predicted a change in the loop domain of the protein.

**.0007 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, 1-BP DEL, 228G [c.228delG; p.Asn78ThrfsTer92]

In an extended kindred with familial pheochromocytoma, Haider et al. (2025) identified a novel germline 1-bp deletion (c.228delG) in exon 4 of the MAX gene, predicted to produce a truncated protein (Asn78Thrfs*92). The propositus had pituitary gigantism due to a mixed growth hormone- and prolactin-secreting macroadenoma, later developed bilateral, recurrent pheochromocytomas, and after his death was found to have an extra-adrenal myelolipoma that was negative for MAX on immunohistochemistry. Several affected and unaffected carriers were identified in the kindred.
