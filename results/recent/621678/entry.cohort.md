### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the MAX gene (154950) on chromosome 14q23.

### Description

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type V (MEN5) is caused by heterozygous inactivating germline mutations in the MAX gene (Charoenngam et al., 2026). It is mainly characterized by highly penetrant pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma, with pituitary adenoma as a cardinal feature. Parathyroid adenomas and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors are less common manifestations (Maggacis et al., 2026). Seabrook et al. (2021) concluded that germline MAX mutations are associated with pheochromocytomas, ganglioneuromas, neuroblastomas, pituitary neuroendocrine tumors, possibly parathyroid adenomas, and also chondrosarcoma and lung adenocarcinoma. Charoenngam et al. (2026) noted that the epidemiology, penetrance, and genotype-phenotype associations of MEN5 had not yet been defined.

### Nomenclature

Seabrook et al. (2021) suggested that MAX is a novel multiple endocrine neoplasia gene. Lian et al. (2024) proposed that MAX germline mutations cause a new type of multiple endocrine neoplasia originating from the neural crest, which might be called MEN5. In a review, Sahakian et al. (2024) noted that recently described families with a MAX mutation, which predisposes to pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma, had a phenotypic spectrum resembling multiple endocrine neoplasia, which suggested the existence of another syndrome, MEN5. In their review of the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors, Nosé et al. (2022) described MEN5 as a newly defined familial cancer syndrome, together with MEN4 and MAFA-related syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) studied 3 unrelated individuals with hereditary pheochromocytoma. They also studied a follow-up series of 59 selected patients with pheochromocytoma, in which 5 additional patients had MAX mutations. The findings suggested an association between MAX mutations and malignant outcome. ⚠︎

Taïeb et al. (2018) reported 6 consecutive patients with MAX-related pheochromocytoma. Four were studied at initial diagnosis and 2 at follow-up evaluation. All but 1 patient developed bilateral pheochromocytomas during their lifetime. The tumors were often multiple within the same gland, and some patients had extra-adrenal lesions. Two pheochromocytomas smaller than 1 cm corresponded to nodular adrenomedullary hyperplasia.

Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) described a family with neural crest-derived tumors. The proband (III-1), a woman, developed neuroblastoma of the broad ligament at 17 months of age, which was treated with surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. At age 22 years she developed a right pheochromocytoma, a ganglioneuroma at the site of the original neuroblastoma, and an ileal schwannoma. Six years later, she underwent surgery for a left pheochromocytoma associated with a mature ganglioneuroma. At the same time, a well-differentiated leiomyosarcoma arising from the mesosigmoid was removed. Her other recorded findings included hypertension, high normetanephrines, metastases of the leiomyosarcoma, hepatic carcinoma, and uterine leiomyoma; the neuroblastoma, leiomyosarcoma, and hepatic carcinoma were attributed to other causes. Her paternal grandfather (I-1) had bilateral pheochromocytoma at 70 years of age. Before surgery he had urinary normetanephrines 5 times and chromogranin A 3 times the upper limit of normal. Her youngest brother (III-3) presented at age 31 years with cardiomyopathy complicated by Takotsubo syndrome, which led to the diagnosis of bilateral pheochromocytoma. At age 56 years, her paternal uncle (II-3) developed an 11-mm hypervascular nodule in the right adrenal gland. The nodule was clinically silent but strongly took up 18F-DOPA on PET, suggesting a pheochromocytoma or adrenomedullary nodule. ⚠︎

Seabrook et al. (2021) reported 2 Australian families. In family A, multiple individuals had pheochromocytoma, including bilateral and metastatic disease. Two children who had not developed pheochromocytoma had neuroendocrine tumors: a paravertebral ganglioneuroma in one and an abdominal neuroblastoma in the other. One family member had acromegaly, and their pheochromocytoma tissue stained positive for growth hormone-releasing hormone on immunohistochemistry. Another, whose pheochromocytomas had been resected, had pituitary enlargement and elevated IGF-1. In family B, the proband had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, multigland parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas.

Lian et al. (2024) reported 3 probands with MAX mutations. The proband of family A, a man, was diagnosed with right pheochromocytoma at age 24 years. He had more than a 2-year history of elevated blood pressure and was eventually diagnosed with metachronous bilateral pheochromocytoma. He had elevated plasma norepinephrine and no pituitary abnormalities or other neuroendocrine tumors. The proband of family B, a 34-year-old man, was admitted with bilateral adrenal lesions and was diagnosed with simultaneous bilateral pheochromocytoma. He had no headache, palpitations, or excessive sweating; his plasma catecholamine, metanephrine, and normetanephrine levels were normal, and he had no pituitary or parathyroid abnormalities. The proband of family C, a 64-year-old woman, had a unilateral right pheochromocytoma with occasional paroxysmal headache and elevated plasma norepinephrine. She had no pituitary, neck, or chest abnormalities.

Lian et al. (2024) reviewed 109 carriers of germline MAX mutations from 87 families, 6 newly reported and 103 previously reported. Of these, 101 (92.7%) developed pheochromocytoma at a mean age of 32.8 years (range, 13 to 80 years); 59 of the pheochromocytomas were bilateral. Nineteen patients (18.8%) had metastatic disease and 11 (10.9%) had a concomitant thoracic or abdominal paraganglioma. Nine carriers (8.3%) had functional growth hormone- and/or prolactin-secreting pituitary neuroendocrine tumors. Eleven (10.1%) had other neuroendocrine tumors, including primary hyperparathyroidism, neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma/ganglioneuroma, C-cell hyperplasia, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor. Seven had non-neuroendocrine tumors whose relation to the mutation was uncertain. ⚠︎

Haider et al. (2025) reported a kindred with a germline MAX mutation. The propositus, a man, developed pituitary gigantism due to a mixed growth hormone- and prolactin-secreting pituitary macroadenoma. The tumor was controlled after 2 surgeries, medical therapy, and radiotherapy. He later developed bilateral and recurrent pheochromocytomas. After his death, an extra-adrenal myelolipoma was identified. The kindred had an extensive history of pheochromocytomas or uncontrolled hypertension. Haider et al. (2025) described this as the first reported case of pituitary gigantism associated with a pathogenic MAX variant. They suggested that comprehensive screening of carriers could identify rare disease associations beyond pheochromocytoma-paraganglioma.

Charoenngam et al. (2026) carried out a systematic review of 45 studies covering 127 patients with germline MAX mutations. Median age at first tumor diagnosis was 29 years, and 37.0% of patients were women. The most frequent tumors were pheochromocytoma (112 patients), pituitary adenoma (11), and paraganglioma (10), with median ages at diagnosis of 31.0, 33.0, and 43.0 years, respectively. Of the pheochromocytomas, 38.3% were unilateral and 27.6% were metastatic, and most of the tumors secreted norepinephrine. ⚠︎

Maggacis et al. (2026) summarized that MAX-associated pituitary adenomas tend to be functioning, typically of PIT1 lineage with growth hormone and/or prolactin secretion, and that pituitary gigantism has been reported. The broader phenotype was hypothesized to include other tumors of neural crest lineage, such as ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, chondrosarcoma, myelolipoma, and lung adenocarcinoma.

### Biochemical Features

Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) observed increased urinary normetanephrines with normal total metanephrines in a MAX variant carrier with bilateral pheochromocytoma, a pattern they noted was reminiscent of MAX-related tumors.

Lian et al. (2024) found catecholamine data for 63 patients with MAX-associated pheochromocytoma. Of these, 61 (96.8%) had positive results, and 70.5% of those showed a mixed noradrenergic/adrenergic secretion pattern rather than isolated elevation of norepinephrine.

### Inheritance

In the families reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020), Seabrook et al. (2021), Lian et al. (2024), and Haider et al. (2025), transmission of MEN5 was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Penetrance was incomplete. In the family reported by Cardot Bauters et al. (2020), the proband's father carried the MAX variant but developed no adrenal lesion over repeated imaging and biochemical follow-up. In the families reported by Lian et al. (2024), a 13-year-old son and a daughter and sister of 2 probands carried the mutation without abnormalities. Haider et al. (2025) described both affected and unaffected carriers in their kindred. ⚠︎

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) suggested that MAX mutations are preferentially transmitted paternally in hereditary pheochromocytoma. Lian et al. (2024) noted that only 22 (25.3%) of 87 pedigrees with MAX mutations had a family history of pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, and suggested that de novo MAX mutations may be common. Three of 109 carriers had no clinical phenotype.

### Molecular Genetics

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) sequenced the exomes of 3 unrelated individuals with hereditary pheochromocytoma and identified heterozygous mutations in the MAX gene. MAX protein was absent from the tumors, and loss of heterozygosity caused by uniparental disomy was found, which supported the involvement of MAX alterations in the disease. A follow-up study of 59 selected patients with pheochromocytoma identified 5 additional MAX mutations. ⚠︎

Taïeb et al. (2018) found 5 different MAX mutations in 6 patients with pheochromocytoma. In silico analyses predicted that the mutations disrupted the Max/Myc interaction and/or abolished interaction with DNA.

Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) studied a family in which heritability of pheochromocytoma had previously been attributed to a KIF1B variant. In the proband's brother (III-3), the only heterozygous variant identified was a missense mutation in the MAX gene (S49P). The KIF1B variant was absent in this patient, and both of his pheochromocytomas showed loss of the wildtype MAX allele. The MAX variant was also present in the proband, her affected grandfather, and her paternal uncle (II-3), who had an adrenal nodule and did not carry the KIF1B variant. The proband and grandfather carried both the MAX variant and the KIF1B variant (S1481N). The proband's 2 pheochromocytomas showed no loss of the wildtype MAX allele. She also carried maternally inherited heterozygous variants in TFAP2E and TMEM214, which the authors classified as of uncertain significance. Structural modeling predicted that the proline substitution was incompatible with the native conformation of MAX. Together with the normetanephrine-predominant secretory pattern, these findings led the authors to conclude that the familial pheochromocytoma was linked to the MAX variant.

In family A, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a heterozygous germline missense mutation in the MAX gene (A67D) in all individuals with pheochromocytoma and in both children with neuroendocrine tumors. Pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity. MAX immunohistochemical staining was lost in pheochromocytomas and other neural crest tumors from this family, and in vitro studies confirmed that the variant causes loss of function. In the proband of family B, they identified a heterozygous truncating germline MAX mutation (E8X). ⚠︎

Lian et al. (2024) identified heterozygous MAX mutations in 3 probands. The proband of family A carried a nonsense mutation (R75X) that was absent from both parents; his unaffected 13-year-old son also carried it. The proband of family B carried a 2-bp duplication causing a frameshift; his parents and daughter were not tested. The proband of family C carried a missense mutation (E158A), which was also present in 1 daughter and 1 sister who had no clinical or biochemical abnormalities. In their review of the literature, the authors tabulated 53 distinct germline MAX alterations involving all 5 exons. The most frequent were R33X (21.1%), R75X (13.8%), and A67D (7.3%), and nonsense mutations predominated (47.7%).

In a kindred with MEN5, Haider et al. (2025) identified a novel heterozygous germline 1-bp deletion in exon 4 of the MAX gene (c.228delG), predicted to produce a truncated protein. The kindred included multiple affected and unaffected carriers of the deletion. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) suggested that MAX mutations were associated with malignant outcome of pheochromocytoma and that loss of MAX function correlated with metastatic potential. ⚠︎

Lian et al. (2024) compared carriers of missense and nonmissense MAX mutations. They found no significant differences in age at diagnosis or in the occurrence of pheochromocytoma, metastasis, paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, or other tumors, suggesting a weak genotype-phenotype correlation.

### Pathogenesis

Comino-Méndez et al. (2011) noted that 2 observations supported involvement of the MYC-MAX-MXD1 network in tumors of neural crest cells: rat pheochromocytoma (PC12) cells lack functional MAX, and MYCN is amplified in neuroblastoma.

Haider et al. (2025) found that an extra-adrenal myelolipoma from the propositus of a MEN5 kindred was negative for MAX on immunohistochemistry. They characterized myelolipoma as a new disease association in MEN5.

Charoenngam et al. (2026) found evidence that loss of MAX contributes to the development of paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, renal oncocytoma, myelolipoma, and liver sarcoma. Whether MAX loss causes primary hyperparathyroidism remained uncertain.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that inactivating MAX variants prevent normal activation of the MYC-MAX heterodimer, leaving MYC function unregulated. They suggested that increased MYC activity may give rise to a more aggressive subtype of neural crest chromaffin cells.

### Diagnosis

Taïeb et al. (2018) found that 18F-FDOPA PET/CT accurately visualized MAX-related pheochromocytomas, which were often multiple or bilateral. Its per-lesion sensitivity was 90.9%, compared with 52.4% for CT/MRI. 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT detected fewer lesions in 1 of 3 patients, and 18F-FDG PET/CT was only faintly positive in 2 of 4 patients. The authors recommended 18F-FDOPA PET/CT as the first-line functional imaging modality for these patients.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that loss of MAX immunohistochemical staining and loss of heterozygosity have been found in MAX-related pheochromocytoma. They suggested that these tests may help when a germline MAX variant of uncertain significance is identified, and noted that the catecholamine phenotype is generally mixed noradrenaline and adrenaline secretion.

### Clinical Management

Lian et al. (2024) recommended a comprehensive baseline assessment of MAX mutation carriers for pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, pituitary adenoma, hyperparathyroidism, and ganglioneuroma/neuroblastoma.

Maggacis et al. (2026) noted that there are no formal consensus surveillance guidelines for MEN5. For carriers of pathogenic germline MAX variants, a pediatric pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma consensus recommended annual clinical assessment from age 10 years, and annual plasma metanephrines with MRI from neck to pelvis every 2 to 3 years starting at presentation or age 15 years. Maggacis et al. (2026) also summarized that MAX-associated lactotroph adenomas respond well to dopamine agonists, whereas somatotrophinomas often require multiple lines of therapy.

### Population Genetics

Maggacis et al. (2026) stated that the exact prevalence of MEN5 is unknown, but that it is thought to account for 0.8 to 1.9% of pheochromocytomas/paragangliomas.

### History

Using an shRNA screen, Schlisio et al. (2008) found that the kinesin KIF1B-beta acts downstream of the EglN3 prolyl hydroxylase and is necessary and sufficient for neuronal apoptosis when nerve growth factor becomes limiting. KIF1B-beta maps to chromosome 1p36.2, a region frequently deleted in neural crest-derived tumors. The authors identified inherited loss-of-function KIF1B-beta missense mutations in neuroblastomas and pheochromocytomas and argued that KIF1B-beta is a pathogenic target of 1p36 deletions. Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) later reexamined a family in which familial pheochromocytoma had been attributed to a KIF1B variant. They concluded that the predisposition was linked to a germline MAX variant instead.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, SER49PRO [c.145T>C; p.S49P]

In a family with pheochromocytoma and other neural crest-derived tumors, Cardot Bauters et al. (2020) identified a heterozygous c.145T-C transition (NM_002382.5) in the MAX gene, resulting in a ser49-to-pro (S49P) substitution. In the proband's brother, who had bilateral pheochromocytoma diagnosed at age 31 years after cardiomyopathy complicated by Takotsubo syndrome, this was the only heterozygous variant identified, and both tumors showed loss of the wildtype MAX allele. The variant was also present in the proband, who had bilateral pheochromocytoma, ganglioneuroma, and schwannoma, as well as in her paternal grandfather, who had bilateral pheochromocytoma at age 70 years. Her paternal uncle carried it as well; he developed an adrenal nodule with strong 18F-DOPA uptake at age 56 years and did not carry a KIF1B variant found in the proband and grandfather. The proband's father carried the variant without developing adrenal lesions. Structural modeling predicted that the proline substitution was incompatible with the native protein conformation.

**.0002 MULTIPLE ENDOCRINE NEOPLASIA, TYPE V**  
MAX, ALA67ASP [c.200C>A; p.A67D]

In an Australian family (family A) with MEN5, Seabrook et al. (2021) identified a heterozygous germline c.200C-A transversion in the MAX gene, resulting in an ala67-to-asp (A67D) substitution. Family members had pheochromocytoma (including bilateral and metastatic disease), ganglioneuroma, neuroblastoma, acromegaly, and pituitary enlargement with elevated IGF-1. The variant was found in all individuals with pheochromocytoma and in both children with neuroendocrine tumors. Pheochromocytoma tissue showed loss of heterozygosity, tumors showed loss of MAX immunohistochemical staining, and in vitro studies confirmed that the variant causes loss of function. ⚠︎

The proband had bilateral and metastatic pheochromocytoma, a prolactin-producing pituitary tumor, multigland parathyroid adenomas, chondrosarcoma, and multifocal pulmonary adenocarcinomas. ⚠︎

MAX, ARG75TER [c.223C>T; p.R75*]

His unaffected 13-year-old son was also a carrier. Lian et al. (2024) noted that R75X was the second most frequent germline MAX alteration in their review of the literature (13.8%).
