### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hypohidrotic/tooth/nail type ectodermal dysplasia-19 (ECTD19) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the IRF6 gene on chromosome 1.

### Description

Hypohidrotic/tooth/nail type ectodermal dysplasia-19 (ECTD19) is an ectodermal dysplasia with oligodontia, hypo- or anhidrosis with absent sweat glands, recurrent skin abscesses resembling hidradenitis suppurativa, poikiloderma, nail dysplasia, and bilateral sensorineural hearing impairment (Schneider et al., 2026). Natal teeth and sparse hair are seen in some patients. Unlike Van der Woude syndrome and popliteal pterygium syndrome, which are also caused by IRF6 mutations, the disorder does not include cleft lip/palate or lip pits (Schneider et al., 2026).

### Nomenclature

Peschel et al. (2025) and Schneider et al. (2026) called the disorder 'ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth' (EDNAT). Zhang et al. (2026) called it 'hidradenitis suppurativa, ectodermal dysplasia and deafness syndrome.' Schneider et al. (2026) also used the term IRF6-associated ectodermal dysplasia. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Peschel et al. (2025) reported a new type of ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth. According to the later account by the same group (Schneider et al., 2026), the report described 2 unrelated patients. Neither patient had cleft lip/palate or lip pits. Both had anhidrosis and had been born with numerous teeth.

Schneider et al. (2026) gave full phenotypic data on the 2 previously reported patients (P1 and P2), unrelated male teenagers aged 17 and 14 years. Both had natal teeth on their first day of life. These teeth were weakly anchored and were lost spontaneously or extracted in early infancy. P1 later developed 7 deciduous teeth with some delay and 12 permanent teeth, 2 of which were removed. Most of his teeth had enamel defects and enamel hypomineralization. Oligodontia had left the alveolar crest of his lower jaw highly atrophic. P2 developed only 9 permanent teeth. From early infancy, both patients had episodes of overheating and difficulty regulating body temperature. Both had recurrent skin abscesses, especially in the genital/gluteal area, behind the ears, in the armpits, and in the mouth. Their skin was very dry, with plantar hyperkeratosis and, in winter, blistering on the backs of the toes. Testing showed anhidrosis, and confocal laser scanning microscopy found no sweat glands on the palms. Some toenails and fingernails were dysplastic, thickened, or deformed, with distal onycholysis. Other features were increased thick mucus in the nose and upper throat and dry cerumen. Both had bilateral sensorineural hearing impairment since infancy, treated with hearing aids. Submucous cleft palate was excluded in both. P1 also had a tendency to allergies, including several anaphylactic reactions to milk protein.

Schneider et al. (2026) also described a 22-year-old woman (P3) who was referred for anhidrosis, dry skin, and intolerance of environmental temperatures above 22 degrees C. She had diffuse xerosis and dilated follicular ostia. She had had several episodes of skin abscesses mimicking hidradenitis suppurativa in the groins, axillae, and intergluteal fold, treated with antibiotics and surgery. Her scalp hair, eyebrows, and eyelashes were normal. She had pincer nails on both big toes and bilateral acquired sensorineural hearing loss. No natal teeth were remembered. Her lips and palate were normal. She reported epistaxis, crusted rhinitis, and insufficient salivation. She had bilateral superficial punctate keratitis with a reduced tear film break-up time. Her tooth eruption was delayed, and she had 16 persisting deciduous teeth and only 2 permanent teeth. The authors found a shared pattern of tooth agenesis in P1, P2, and P3. All 3 had permanent upper incisors but no mandibular incisors or canines and no permanent premolars. Skin histology in P1 and P3 showed focal subcorneal clefting and basal spongiosis, and sweat glands were absent in both.

Schneider et al. (2026) summarized 5 known patients: their own 3 and 2 described by Zhang et al. (2026). All 5 had oligodontia, hypo- or anhidrosis, skin abscesses resembling hidradenitis suppurativa, poikiloderma, bilateral hearing impairment, and nail dysplasia. None had lip pits or cleft lip/palate. Natal teeth were present only in P1 and P2. Sparse hair was present only in the 2 patients of Zhang et al. (2026). The authors noted that natal teeth were therefore less typical of the disorder than they had first thought.

### Inheritance

In the patients reported by Schneider et al. (2026), ECTD19 resulted from heterozygous de novo IRF6 mutations. The patient P3 was mosaic for her mutation.

### Molecular Genetics

Peschel et al. (2025) identified a previously undescribed IRF6 variant in ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth. The same group later stated that this report described a heterozygous missense mutation in exon 6 of IRF6 in 2 unrelated patients. The mutation lies between the regions encoding the DNA-binding domain and the protein-binding domain (Schneider et al., 2026).

In 2 unrelated boys with ECTD19 (P1 and P2), Schneider et al. (2026) identified the same heterozygous c.653T-A transversion in exon 6 of the IRF6 gene, resulting in an ile218-to-asn (I218N) substitution. P1 was studied by high-throughput sequencing, and Sanger sequencing of the parents confirmed that the mutation was de novo. In P2, Sanger sequencing of IRF6 was done after no pathogenic variants had been found in EDA, EDAR, EDARADD, or TP63. The parents' DNA showed no change at this position, so the mutation was considered de novo. In a 22-year-old woman with ECTD19 (P3), whose parents were nonconsanguineous and healthy, trio whole-genome sequencing found a heterozygous de novo c.658T-C transition in IRF6, resulting in a ser220-to-pro (S220P) substitution. The variant allele frequency of 30.5% indicated mosaicism. The authors noted that in silico analyses supported pathogenicity but that the variant was ultimately classified as of unknown significance.

Zhang et al. (2026) reported gain-of-function IRF6 variants as the cause of a hidradenitis suppurativa, ectodermal dysplasia, and deafness syndrome. Schneider et al. (2026) described these as 2 additional patients (P4 and P5) from China. The variants affected the same residues as in their own patients: p.Ile218_Ser220delinsSerSerGlyAla and p.Ile218Asn.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Schneider et al. (2026) noted that the ECTD19 mutations are amino acid changes in exon 6 of IRF6. This exon encodes a region of unknown function between the DNA-binding and protein-binding domains. By contrast, IRF6 missense mutations causing Van der Woude syndrome and popliteal pterygium syndrome are found mostly in exons 3, 4, 7, 8, and 9. Because none of the 5 patients had impaired palatal development, the authors concluded that p.Ile218Asn, p.Ser220Pro, and p.Ile218_Ser220delinsSerSerGlyAla do not limit DNA binding of the mutant protein, at least in the fusing palatal shelves. They cautioned that cleft lip/palate could not be ruled out in other patients with similar mutations.

### Pathogenesis

Schneider et al. (2026) stained skin for IRF6. Keratinocyte nuclei from P1, who carried I218N, stained more strongly than control nuclei. Nuclear staining in the epidermis of P3, who carried S220P, was more patchy, consistent with mosaicism. Keratin-17 staining was normal. In an AlphaFold3 structural model, both point mutations affected a short alpha-helix at the end of the region of unknown function. The authors proposed that the disorder arises through a gain-of-function mechanism, with increased IRF6 activity. They speculated that the hearing impairment and the abscesses resembling hidradenitis suppurativa may result from disruption of the IRF6-p63 regulatory loop. They also noted that Zhang et al. (2026) had suggested a gain-of-function effect of the ile218 substitutions. ⚠︎

IRF6, ILE218ASN [c.653T>A; p.Ile218Asn]

In 2 unrelated male teenagers with ECTD19 and natal teeth, Schneider et al. (2026) identified a heterozygous c.653T-A transversion in exon 6 of the IRF6 gene, resulting in an ile218-to-asn (I218N) substitution. The mutation was predicted to be deleterious. It was de novo in both patients, since neither patient's parents carried a change at this position. Keratinocyte nuclei in the skin of 1 patient showed stronger IRF6 staining than controls. Schneider et al. (2026) also noted that the same substitution had been found in 1 of the 2 patients described by Zhang et al. (2026). ✗
