### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ectodermal dysplasia-19 (ECTD19), hypohidrotic/tooth/nail type, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the IRF6 gene on chromosome 1.

### Description

Ectodermal dysplasia-19 (ECTD19) is a disorder of ectodermal derivatives associated with heterozygous IRF6 variants. Its proposed cardinal features are oligodontia, hypohidrosis or anhidrosis with absent sweat glands, hidradenitis suppurativa-like skin abscesses, poikiloderma, nail dysplasia, and bilateral sensorineural hearing impairment. Some patients have natal teeth or sparse hair. Unlike Van der Woude syndrome and popliteal pterygium syndrome, which are also caused by IRF6 variants, affected individuals do not have cleft lip/palate or lip pits (Schneider et al., 2026).

### Nomenclature

Peschel et al. (2025) first called this disorder ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth (EDNAT), as summarized by Schneider et al. (2026). Schneider et al. (2026) later reported that several patients did not have natal teeth, which suggested that this feature is less typical than first thought. Zhang et al. (2026) called the disorder hidradenitis suppurativa, ectodermal dysplasia, and deafness syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Peschel et al. (2025) reported a new type of ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth caused by an IRF6 variant. Schneider et al. (2026) stated that these were 2 unrelated patients who had numerous teeth at birth and had anhidrosis, but no cleft lip/palate or lip pits.

Schneider et al. (2026) gave full phenotypic data on the 2 patients reported by Peschel et al. (2025). They were unrelated male teenagers, aged 17 years (P1) and 14 years (P2), who were noticed on the first day of life because of natal teeth. The natal teeth were weakly anchored and were either lost spontaneously or extracted in early infancy. P1 later developed 7 deciduous teeth, with some delay. Twelve of his permanent teeth erupted, and 2 of these were removed because of repeated dental fistulas or cysts. Most of his teeth had enamel defects and hypomineralization. As a result of oligodontia, his lower alveolar crest was highly atrophic. P2 developed only 9 permanent teeth, and 2 displaced, impacted molars had to be extracted. From early infancy, both patients had episodes of overheating and difficulty regulating body temperature. Both had recurrent skin abscesses, particularly in the genital/gluteal area, behind the ears, in the armpits, and in the mouth. Their skin was very dry, with plantar hyperkeratosis and, during the winter months, blistering on the backs of the toes. Sweat testing showed anhidrosis, and confocal laser scanning microscopy detected no sweat glands on the palms. Some toenails and fingernails were dysplastic, thickened, or deformed, with distal onycholysis. Other findings included increased production of thick mucus in the nose and upper throat and ears partially filled with dry cerumen. Both patients had had bilateral sensorineural hearing impairment since infancy, treated with hearing aids. A submucous cleft palate was excluded in each. P1 also had a tendency to allergies, including several anaphylactic reactions to milk protein.

Schneider et al. (2026) also reported a 22-year-old woman (P3) with mosaicism for an IRF6 variant. She was referred for anhidrosis, dry skin, and intolerance to environmental temperatures above 22 degrees C. Examination showed diffuse xerosis and dilated follicular ostia. She had had several episodes of skin abscesses mimicking hidradenitis suppurativa in the groins, axillae, and intergluteal fold, which had been treated with antibiotics and surgery. Her scalp hair, eyebrows, and eyelashes were normal, and both big toes had pincer nails. She had no syndactyly, and her breasts and nipples were normal. She had bilateral acquired sensorineural hearing loss, with tonal audiometry thresholds of 1000-2000 Hz at 40-50 dB. Her ear canals were large, and the eardrums tended toward retraction and atelectasis. No one remembered natal teeth. Her lips and palate were normal. She reported epistaxis, crusted rhinitis, and insufficient salivation. Ocular examination showed bilateral superficial punctate keratitis and a reduced tear film break-up time (4-5 s). She had 16 persisting deciduous teeth and only 2 permanent teeth after delayed tooth eruption.

Schneider et al. (2026) compared the dentition of P1, P2, and P3 and found a shared pattern of tooth agenesis. All 3 patients had permanent upper incisors but no incisors or canines in the mandible, and none had any permanent premolars. The missing mandibular teeth led to marked atrophy of the lower alveolar crest.

Zhang et al. (2026) described a hidradenitis suppurativa, ectodermal dysplasia, and deafness syndrome caused by IRF6 variants. According to the summary by Schneider et al. (2026), these 2 patients (P4 and P5) had oligodontia, hypo- or anhidrosis, hidradenitis suppurativa-like skin abscesses, poikiloderma, bilateral hearing impairment, nail dysplasia, and sparse hair. They had no natal teeth. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Schneider et al. (2026) found focal subcorneal clefting and basal spongiosis in skin samples from P1 and P3, and sweat glands were absent in both. On immunofluorescence, keratinocyte nuclei from P1 stained more strongly for IRF6 than control nuclei. The epidermis of P3 showed more patchy nuclear staining, consistent with her genetic mosaicism. Keratin 17 immunostaining of the patients' skin showed no abnormalities.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous IRF6 mutations reported in ECTD19 arose de novo in the patients studied by Peschel et al. (2025) and Schneider et al. (2026). One patient was mosaic for the mutation (Schneider et al., 2026).

### Molecular Genetics

Peschel et al. (2025) linked an IRF6 variant not previously described to a new type of ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth. Schneider et al. (2026) reported that the 2 unrelated patients (P1 and P2) had the same heterozygous c.653T-A transition in exon 6 of IRF6, which results in an ile218-to-asn (I218N) substitution. In P1 the variant was identified by whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing. In P2 it was identified by Sanger sequencing of IRF6 after no pathogenic variants were found in EDA, EDAR, EDARADD, or TP63. The variant was classified as de novo because neither patient's parents carried it. ⚠︎

Schneider et al. (2026) performed trio whole-genome sequencing in P3, whose parents were healthy and nonconsanguineous. They identified a de novo heterozygous c.658T-C transition in IRF6, resulting in a ser220-to-pro (S220P) substitution. The variant allele frequency was 30.5%, indicating mosaicism, and in silico analyses supported pathogenicity. Because some of her symptoms did not match the typical IRF6-associated phenotype, the variant was finally classified as of unknown significance.

Zhang et al. (2026) reported gain-of-function variants in IRF6 as the cause of a hidradenitis suppurativa, ectodermal dysplasia, and deafness syndrome. According to Schneider et al. (2026), the 2 patients (P4 and P5) carried variants affecting the same residues as P1 to P3: an I218N substitution and an in-frame p.Ile218_Ser220delinsSerSerGlyAla variant. Schneider et al. (2026) also stated that tissue culture experiments by Zhang et al. (2026) suggested a key role for residues 216 to 222 in nucleocytoplasmic trafficking and a gain-of-function effect of the mutant IRF6 protein.

### Pathogenesis

Schneider et al. (2026) noted that the ECTD19-associated substitutions lie in exon 6 of IRF6. This exon falls in a region of unknown function between the DNA-binding domain and the protein-binding domain. In AlphaFold3 structural models, both point mutations affected a short alpha-helix at the end of this region. The authors proposed that the variants do not impair DNA binding, at least in the fusing palatal shelves, because none of the patients had abnormal palatal development. They concluded that genotype-phenotype correlations and immunohistochemical data from the 5 known patients support a gain-of-function mechanism. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Schneider et al. (2026) compared ECTD19 with Van der Woude syndrome-1, popliteal pterygium syndrome, and p63-related disorders. ECTD19 differed from Van der Woude syndrome-1 and popliteal pterygium syndrome by the absence of lip pits and cleft lip/palate. It also lacked popliteal pterygia, ankyloblepharon, split hand/foot or syndactyly, and hypoplastic nipples or breasts.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA 19, HYPOHIDROTIC/TOOTH/NAIL TYPE**  
IRF6, ILE218ASN [c.653T>A; p.Ile218Asn]

Peschel et al. (2025) and Schneider et al. (2026) reported 2 unrelated male patients with ECTD19 and natal teeth. Both carried a de novo heterozygous c.653T-A transition in exon 6 of the IRF6 gene, resulting in an ile218-to-asn (I218N) substitution. The substitution was predicted to be deleterious, and the parents did not carry the variant. ⚠︎

Schneider et al. (2026) stated that Zhang et al. (2026) found the I218N substitution in another patient with ECTD19, who did not have natal teeth.

**.0002 ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA 19, HYPOHIDROTIC/TOOTH/NAIL TYPE**  
IRF6, SER220PRO [c.658T>C; p.Ser220Pro]

Schneider et al. (2026) used trio whole-genome sequencing to study a 22-year-old woman with ECTD19. She was mosaic for a de novo heterozygous c.658T-C transition in the IRF6 gene, resulting in a ser220-to-pro (S220P) substitution, with a variant allele frequency of 30.5%. In silico analyses supported pathogenicity, but the authors classified the variant as of unknown significance.
