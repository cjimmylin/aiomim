### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hypohidrotic/tooth/nail-type ectodermal dysplasia-19 (ECTD19) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the IRF6 gene (607199) on chromosome 1q32.2.

### Description

Hypohidrotic/tooth/nail-type ectodermal dysplasia-19 (ECTD19) is an ectodermal dysplasia. Schneider et al. (2026) considered its defining features to be oligodontia, hypo- or anhidrosis, hidradenitis suppurativa-like abscesses, poikiloderma, onychodysplasia, and bilateral hearing impairment. Natal teeth and sparse hair were inconsistent features. Affected individuals have a characteristic pattern of tooth agenesis: permanent upper incisors are present, but mandibular incisors and canines and permanent premolars are absent, with pronounced atrophy of the lower alveolar crest.

### Nomenclature

Schneider et al. (2026) noted that they first called this disorder ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth (EDNAT). They later found that natal teeth were less typical of the disorder than they had first thought.

### Clinical Features

Schneider et al. (2026) described 2 unrelated male teenagers with ectodermal dysplasia who were born with natal teeth: P1, aged 17 years, and P2, aged 14 years. P1 developed 7 deciduous teeth, with delay, and 12 permanent teeth, of which 10 remained. Most of his teeth had enamel defects and hypomineralization, and he had a highly atrophic mandibular alveolar crest due to oligodontia. P2 developed only 9 permanent teeth. Both boys had overheated since infancy and had anhidrosis, with no sweat glands detectable on the palms. Both had recurrent skin abscesses in the genital/gluteal area, behind the ears, in the axillae, and at the mouth. Both also had very dry skin with plantar hyperkeratosis and blistering of the toes in winter, dysplastic thickened nails with distal onycholysis, thick nasal mucus, dry cerumen, and bilateral sensorineural hearing impairment since infancy. Submucous cleft palate was excluded.

Schneider et al. (2026) also described a 22-year-old woman (P3) with anhidrosis, diffuse xerosis, dilated follicular ostia, and heat intolerance. She had recurrent hidradenitis suppurativa-like abscesses of the groins, axillae, and intergluteal fold, and pincer nails of both great toes. Other features were bilateral acquired sensorineural hearing loss, epistaxis, crusted rhinitis, and insufficient salivation. She also had bilateral superficial punctate keratitis with reduced tear film break-up time. Her hair, lips, palate, breasts, and nipples were normal, and natal teeth were not remembered. Eruption of her teeth was delayed, and she had 16 persisting deciduous teeth and only 2 permanent teeth.

Schneider et al. (2026) recognized a common pattern of tooth agenesis in P1, P2, and P3. All 3 patients had permanent upper incisors but lacked mandibular incisors and canines and permanent premolars, and all had pronounced atrophy of the lower alveolar crest.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous IRF6 mutations reported by Schneider et al. (2026) arose de novo in all 3 affected individuals, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. In 1 patient (P3), a variant allele frequency of 30.5% indicated somatic mosaicism. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Peschel et al. (2025) reported a previously undescribed IRF6 variant that caused a new type of ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth.

Zhang et al. (2026) reported that gain-of-function variants in the IRF6 gene cause a syndrome of hidradenitis suppurativa, ectodermal dysplasia, and deafness.

Schneider et al. (2026) studied 2 unrelated males with ectodermal dysplasia with natal teeth (P1 and P2). Both carried the same de novo heterozygous c.653T-A change in exon 6 of the IRF6 gene, resulting in an ile218-to-asn (I218N) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing in P1. In P2, it was found by Sanger sequencing after testing of EDA, EDAR, EDARADD, and TP63 was negative. The variant was absent from parental DNA and was predicted to be deleterious. ⚠︎

In a third patient (P3), Schneider et al. (2026) used trio whole-genome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.658T-C transition in the IRF6 gene, resulting in a ser220-to-pro (S220P) substitution. A variant allele frequency of 30.5% indicated mosaicism. In silico analyses supported pathogenicity, although the variant was formally classified as of unknown significance.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Schneider et al. (2026) compared their 3 patients with 2 previously reported patients who carried IRF6 ile218-to-asn or ile218_ser220delins variants. They considered 6 features to be syndrome-defining because all 5 patients had them: oligodontia, hypo- or anhidrosis, hidradenitis suppurativa-like abscesses, poikiloderma, onychodysplasia, and bilateral hearing impairment. Natal teeth (2 of 5) and sparse hair (2 of 5) were irregular features. None of the patients had lip pits, clefting, pterygia, or syndactyly, which distinguished the disorder from Van der Woude syndrome-1 (VWS1) and popliteal pterygium syndrome (PPS). ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Schneider et al. (2026) found focal subcorneal clefting, basal spongiosis, and absent sweat glands in skin biopsies from P1 and P3. Keratinocyte nuclei from P1 showed stronger IRF6 immunostaining than those from controls. P3 showed patchy nuclear staining, consistent with mosaicism. Keratin 17 staining was normal. Based on these results and the genotype-phenotype data, the authors suggested a gain-of-function mechanism. AlphaFold3 modeling placed both substitutions in a short alpha-helix at the end of the region between the DNA-binding and protein-binding domains.
