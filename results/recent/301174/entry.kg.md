### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with or without macrocephaly (NEDMAC) is caused by hemizygous or heterozygous mutation in the SMARCA1 gene (300012) on chromosome Xq25-q26.1.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with or without macrocephaly (NEDMAC) is an X-linked disorder caused by de novo or maternally inherited SMARCA1 variants. Mirzaa et al. (2025) described it in 35 individuals from 26 families. Intellectual disability/developmental delay ranged from mild to severe. Other features included delayed or regressive speech, features of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), facial dysmorphism and other variable findings. Macrocephaly was highly penetrant in individuals with truncating variants.

### Clinical Features

Karaca et al. (2015) described a male patient (BAB4453) with microcephaly, spasticity and intellectual disability. His dysmorphic features resembled Coffin-Siris syndrome and included a coarse face, bushy eyebrows, facial hypertrichosis and long eyelashes. Brain imaging showed severe cortical atrophy. The family history was negative.

Lopes et al. (2016) studied 19 Portuguese patients (16 girls and 3 boys) whose clinical presentation overlapped significantly with Rett syndrome. The patients were evaluated by array CGH, whole-exome sequencing, MRI and muscle biopsy. ⚠︎

Nishimura et al. (2025) reported 2 unrelated males with X-linked intellectual disability. Patient 1 had macrocephaly, behavioral difficulty and learning disability. Patient 2 had ataxia and speech delay.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) studied a 6-generation family with 4 affected individuals who had mild to severe intellectual disability/developmental delay and brain overgrowth. The proband was a 12-year-old boy with occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) of +6 SD and severe intellectual disability without language. He also had ASD, hydrocephalus treated with a shunt and hepatosplenomegaly, and his height and weight were +5 SD. His brother had OFC of +4.3 SD, mild intellectual disability, hydrocephalus and splenomegaly. Two maternal cousins, a 14-year-old girl and an 8-year-old boy, had OFC of +4.9 and +5.5 SD, respectively. Both had mild learning disability, obesity and a normal brain MRI. Their carrier mothers had OFC of +1 and +1.5 SD and no learning disability.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) reported 35 affected individuals (26 males and 9 females) from 26 families, aged 2 to 49 years. The features were: - intellectual disability/developmental delay: 27 of 35 - speech delay and/or regression: 29 of 35 - OFC of +2 SD or more and/or reported macrocephaly: 17 of 35 - motor delay: 20 of 34 - seizures: 8 of 35 - hypotonia: 15 of 35 - behavioral difficulties: 19 of 35 - ASD: 11 of 35 - dysmorphic facial features: 19 of 35 Height of +2 SD or more was less frequent (29%), which the authors said argued against a generalized overgrowth syndrome. Common facial features included frontal bossing, a long face, slanted palpebral fissures, a flat nasal root with a short bulbous nose, anteverted nares and a thin upper lip.

Among the 9 affected females, Mirzaa et al. (2025) found truncating variants in 7. Three of these 7 had macrocephaly and 4 did not. Macrocephaly was discordant in 2 mother-daughter pairs, and learning disabilities appeared milder in females. X-inactivation studies were available for only 3 females, and the results did not match the phenotype.

### Other Features

Mirzaa et al. (2025) noted other recurring features in their cohort of 35 individuals: - urogenital/kidney anomalies: 9 individuals - gastrointestinal problems: 9 - eye/vision deficits: 8 - hepatomegaly/splenomegaly: 3 Recurrent dysmorphic features included ear anomalies (11 individuals) and micrognathia (5).

### Biochemical Features

Mirzaa et al. (2025) performed genome-wide DNA methylation analysis of peripheral blood from 19 individuals with SMARCA1 variants. Compared with controls, they found a predominantly hypomethylated profile defined by 232 probes. Males and females each also showed sex-specific hypomethylated profiles. The authors concluded that these changes were not robust enough to count as an episignature.

### Inheritance

Nishimura et al. (2025) found that both affected males in their report had inherited their SMARCA1 variants from unaffected, healthy mothers. They suggested that hemizygous variants impairing SMARCA1 function can cause X-linked intellectual disability with variable features in males.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) studied 26 index cases. Seventeen had maternally inherited SMARCA1 variants and 4 had de novo variants. Segregation was not determined in 5, and 4 of these were presumed to be maternally inherited. Three-quarters of affected individuals were hemizygous males. Heterozygous females made up 25% and often had milder phenotypes, consistent with X-linked inheritance. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Mirzaa et al. (2025) searched DECIPHER and found 72 CNVs encompassing SMARCA1: 44% were deletions and 56% were duplications. Carriers often had developmental delay, intellectual disability or delayed speech. However, the phenotype was hard to attribute to SMARCA1 because most CNVs were larger than 10 Mb. Individuals with deletions did not have macrocephaly.

### Molecular Genetics

Karaca et al. (2015) studied a male patient (BAB4453) with microcephaly, spasticity, intellectual disability and Coffin-Siris-like dysmorphic features. By whole-exome sequencing, they identified a hemizygous null variant in the SMARCA1 gene (c.7C-T, Q3X; NM_003069). SMARCA1 encodes a member of the SWI/SNF protein family and is part of the ATP-dependent CECR2-containing remodeling factor (CERF). A figure legend in the paper describes the same variant as a homozygous stop gain.

Lopes et al. (2016) performed whole-exome sequencing in 19 Portuguese patients with Rett-like phenotypes. In 26% of patients, they found variants in 5 novel candidate neurodevelopmental disorder genes, including a de novo variant in SMARCA1. Network analysis showed that these genes interact through protein interactions with each other and with known Rett syndrome genes.

By exome sequencing, Nishimura et al. (2025) identified hemizygous SMARCA1 mutations in 2 unrelated males with X-linked intellectual disability. Patient 1 had a nonsense mutation (Q599X) that led to nonsense-mediated decay. Patient 2 had a missense mutation (R448L), which structural modeling suggested might destabilize interactions between SMARCA1 and nucleosomal DNA. Both mutations were inherited from unaffected mothers. ⚠︎

In the 6-generation family with 4 affected individuals and brain overgrowth, Mirzaa et al. (2025) used exome sequencing to identify a nonsense mutation in SMARCA1 (R91X). Affected males were hemizygous and the affected female was heterozygous. Both mothers were heterozygous carriers, and the paternal grandfather and a maternal uncle did not carry the mutation. The same mutation was found in an unrelated 6-year-old boy with macrocephaly, ASD and ADHD, who had inherited it from his mother. ⚠︎

Mirzaa et al. (2025) identified SMARCA1 variants in 35 individuals from 26 families by trio exome sequencing (23 families) or genome sequencing (3 families). Families were ascertained partly through MatchMaker Exchange. There were 25 unique variants: 7 nonsense, 3 frameshift and 15 missense. All were predicted to be deleterious. Each was very rare or absent in gnomAD (allele frequency 8.303 x 10(-7) or lower); M432T was seen in 4 alleles. Sixteen variants lay in the ATPase (12) or HSS (4) domains. Mirzaa et al. (2025) considered SMARCA1 variants in 7 additional families likely non-causative: - V384M (c.1150G-A), found in 3 families. It had previously been reported in familial schizophrenia and was present in 16 gnomAD alleles, including 7 hemizygotes. - P537L (c.1610C-T), found in 2 families. It also had a high gnomAD frequency. - G38V, found together with a de novo 5p15 duplication. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Mirzaa et al. (2025) found macrocephaly in 70% of individuals with truncating SMARCA1 variants. However, 3 individuals with truncating variants lacked brain overgrowth and 4 with missense variants had it, so the feature was not restricted to putative loss-of-function variants. Three of the 4 missense variants associated with macrocephaly were in the HSS domain. Individuals with CNV deletions that included SMARCA1 did not have macrocephaly.

### Pathogenesis

Mirzaa et al. (2025) expressed FLAG-tagged SMARCA1 variants in U2OS cells. The R253X, R259G, R751Q and E771K variants all produced stable proteins that localized to the nucleus. R253X produced a stable 35-kD truncated protein at a somewhat lower level, and this protein was also found in the cytoplasm. The authors suggested that some truncating variants generate truncated proteins with hypomorphic or dominant-negative activity.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) proposed that SMARCA1 variants associated with macrocephaly lack remodeling activity and act in a dominant-negative fashion. Variants not associated with macrocephaly, in contrast, may be functional hypomorphs. In the authors' view, all of these variants alter NURF composition and function in the developing brain.

### Animal Model

Mirzaa et al. (2025) found that Smarca1-null mice were viable and showed little change in brain size, with only slight reductions in a few regions. Progenitor populations and cortical lamination were normal. The mice were more active in the dark cycle but otherwise behaved normally on tests of locomotion, social interaction, anxiety, learning and memory. In Smarca1-null cerebellum, SMARCA5 coimmunoprecipitated with CECR2, which the authors suggested reflects functional compensation by SMARCA5.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) compared mice with single and double knockouts of the NURF components Smarca1, Smarca5 and Bptf. Bptf knockout caused the most severe cortical hypoplasia, and no double mutant was more severely affected. Smarca1ex6del mice express an internally truncated protein and had a total brain area 10% larger than controls. This enlargement was lost when Smarca5 was also ablated. The authors proposed that Smarca5 compensates for complete loss of Smarca1 but not when a mutant SMARCA1 protein is produced, which may act as a dominant negative.
