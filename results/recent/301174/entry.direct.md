### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with or without macrocephaly (NEDMAC) is caused by hemizygous or heterozygous mutation in the SMARCA1 gene on chromosome X.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with or without macrocephaly (NEDMAC) is an X-linked disorder caused by SMARCA1 variants. Its severity varies widely. Features include mild to severe intellectual disability or developmental delay, delayed or regressive speech development, autism spectrum disorder (ASD) features, facial dysmorphism, and other variable features. About half of affected individuals have macrocephaly or brain overgrowth, and it is more common in those with truncating variants. Most affected individuals are hemizygous males with maternally inherited variants. Heterozygous females can also be affected and often have a milder phenotype (Mirzaa et al., 2025; Nishimura et al., 2025).

### Nomenclature

Mirzaa et al. (2025) referred to this disorder as SMARCA1-related neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD).

### Clinical Features

Karaca et al. (2015) reported a male patient (BAB4453) with microcephaly, spasticity, and intellectual disability. He also had dysmorphic features very similar to those of Coffin-Siris syndrome: a coarse face, bushy eyebrows, facial hypertrichosis, and long eyelashes. Brain imaging showed severe cortical atrophy. His family history was negative.

Lopes et al. (2016) studied 19 Portuguese patients (16 girls and 3 boys) whose clinical presentation overlapped substantially with Rett syndrome. One of these patients had a de novo SMARCA1 variant.

Nishimura et al. (2025) reported 2 unrelated males with X-linked intellectual disability. Patient 1 had macrocephaly, behavioral difficulties, and learning disability. Patient 2 had ataxia and speech delay.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) first studied a large 6-generation family in which 4 individuals had mild to severe intellectual disability or developmental delay and brain overgrowth. The proband, a 12-year-4-month-old boy, had an OFC of +6 SD, no language, severe intellectual disability, and ASD. He also had hydrocephalus that required shunting, hepatosplenomegaly, and overgrowth, with height and weight at +5 SD. His 8-year-old brother had an OFC of +4.3 SD, speech delay, and mild intellectual disability. The brother also had hydrocephalus that did not require shunting, splenomegaly, and overgrowth (height +3 SD, weight +3.7 SD). Their maternal cousins had mild learning disability and normal brain MRI. One cousin, a 14-year-7-month-old girl, had an OFC of +4.9 SD and severe obesity (BMI 50 kg/m2). The other, an 8-year-9-month-old boy, had an OFC of +5.5 SD and obesity. The two carrier mothers, aged 45 and 49 years, had no reported learning disability and only mildly increased head circumference (+1 SD and +1.5 SD).

Mirzaa et al. (2025) then reported a total of 35 individuals from 26 families: 26 males and 9 females, aged 2 to 49 years at their last assessment. Most had intellectual disability or developmental delay (27 of 35, 77%) and speech delay and/or speech regression (29 of 35, 83%). In 4 individuals, speech regression was noted rather than delay. Seventeen of 35 individuals (49%) had increased head circumference (OFC at or above +2 SD) and/or reported macrocephaly or brain overgrowth. Increased height (at or above +2 SD) was reported less often (29%), which the authors argued was against a generalized overgrowth syndrome. Other common features were: - motor delay (20 of 34, 59%) - hypotonia (15 of 35, 42%) - seizures or epilepsy (8 of 35, 23%) - behavioral difficulties such as ASD, ADHD, and aggression (19 of 35, 54%); 11 of 35 (31%) were diagnosed with ASD, and in some instances (9 of 25) the behavioral difficulties were linked to regression in speech and mental development Nineteen of 35 individuals had dysmorphic facial features, but the clinical descriptions varied. The recurrent features included: - ear anomalies (11 individuals) - micrognathia (5) - slanted palpebral fissures (5) - prominent forehead (4) - anteverted nostrils (4) - bulbous nasal tip (3) In photographs of 7 affected individuals, relatively common features were frontal bossing, a long face, slanted palpebral fissures, a flat nasal root with a short bulbous nose, anteverted nares, and a thin upper lip. Other recurring findings involved the urogenital tract or kidney (9 individuals), the gastrointestinal tract (9), and the eyes or vision (8); 3 individuals had hepatomegaly or splenomegaly. Affected females often had a milder phenotype.

### Inheritance

Mirzaa et al. (2025) and Nishimura et al. (2025) established X-linked inheritance. In the cohort of Mirzaa et al. (2025), three-quarters of affected individuals were hemizygous males, most of whom had inherited the variant from their mothers. Heterozygous females made up the remaining 25% of affected individuals. Among the 26 index cases, 17 variants were maternally inherited and 4 had arisen de novo; segregation was not determined in 5 cases. In the 2 males reported by Nishimura et al. (2025), the variants were inherited from unaffected mothers. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Karaca et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 128 mostly consanguineous families with neurogenetic disorders. In patient BAB4453, they identified a hemizygous null variant in the SMARCA1 gene, a c.7C-T transition resulting in a gln3-to-ter (Q3X) substitution. The figure legend of the same report described this change as a homozygous stop-gain variant.

Lopes et al. (2016) used array comparative genomic hybridization and whole-exome sequencing to study 19 Portuguese patients with Rett syndrome-like phenotypes. They found a de novo SMARCA1 variant in 1 patient and proposed SMARCA1 as a novel candidate gene for neurodevelopmental disorders.

Riazuddin et al. (2017) performed exome sequencing in 121 Pakistani families with intellectual disability, most of them consanguineous. In a Punjabi family (PKMR142), they identified a c.79G-A transition in the SMARCA1 gene (NM_139035.2) resulting in a glu27-to-lys (E27K) substitution. They listed SMARCA1 as a novel candidate intellectual disability gene. The variant was absent from 213 ethnically matched controls, had a CADD score of 22.1, and had an ExAC allele frequency of 35.55 x 10(-6).

Using exome sequencing, Nishimura et al. (2025) identified hemizygous SMARCA1 variants in 2 unrelated males with X-linked intellectual disability. Patient 1 had a nonsense variant (Q599X) that was predicted to cause nonsense-mediated decay. Patient 2 had a missense variant (R448L), which structural modeling suggested might destabilize interactions between SMARCA1 and nucleosomal DNA. In both cases, the variant was inherited from an unaffected mother.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) performed exome sequencing in a 6-generation family with intellectual disability or developmental delay and brain overgrowth. They identified a SMARCA1 nonsense variant (R91X) in the proband, his brother, and 2 maternal cousins. The mothers of the affected individuals were heterozygous for the variant, and the paternal grandfather and a maternal uncle did not carry it. Through the MatchMaker Exchange network and other routes, the authors ascertained 25 more families. The total came to 35 individuals with apparently non-mosaic hemizygous or heterozygous SMARCA1 variants, identified by trio exome sequencing in 23 families and genome sequencing in 3. There were 25 unique variants; a second, unrelated family carried the same variant as the index family. The variants comprised 7 nonsense, 3 frameshift, and 15 missense changes. All were predicted to be deleterious, and all were absent or very rare in gnomAD. Sixteen of the 25 variants were located in the ATPase domain (12) or the HSS domain (4). The authors also identified SMARCA1 variants in 7 further families that they judged likely non-causative or non-explanatory. These included a recurrent V384M variant found in 3 families. This variant had previously been reported in a family with schizophrenia, and it was present in gnomAD in 16 alleles, including 7 hemizygotes.

Mirzaa et al. (2025) also analyzed genome-wide DNA methylation in peripheral blood from 19 individuals with SMARCA1 variants. Compared with matched controls, the samples showed a predominantly hypomethylated profile. The authors considered these changes not robust enough to qualify as an episignature at that time.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Mirzaa et al. (2025) found that macrocephaly had a penetrance of 70% among individuals with truncating variants. Three individuals with truncating variants did not have brain overgrowth, while 4 individuals with missense variants did. Three of the 4 missense variants associated with macrocephaly were located in the HSS domain. Individuals with copy number deletions that included SMARCA1 were not associated with macrocephaly. Among affected females with truncating variants, 3 had macrocephaly and 4 did not. Macrocephaly was discordant within 2 mother-daughter pairs: in one family the daughter was macrocephalic, and in the other the mother was. X-inactivation studies were available for only 3 females, and the results did not match their phenotypes.

### Pathogenesis

Mirzaa et al. (2025) expressed SMARCA1 variants in U2OS cells. The R259G, R751Q, and E771K variants and the R253X nonsense variant all produced stable proteins that localized to the nucleus. The R253X variant produced a 35-kD truncated protein at a somewhat lower level, and this protein was also found in the cytoplasm. Based on these results and their mouse studies, the authors proposed the following model. Variants associated with macrocephaly lack remodeling activity and act in a dominant-negative fashion. Variants not associated with macrocephaly act as functional hypomorphs. Complete loss of function may be compensated by SMARCA5.

### Animal Model

Mirzaa et al. (2025) found that Smarca1 knockout mice lacked detectable Smarca1 protein, were viable, and had no obvious gross developmental defects. Brain size showed only slight reductions, in total brain width, corpus callosum length, and the area of the dentate gyrus granule cell layer. Neocortical progenitors and cortical lamination were normal. In behavioral testing, the knockout mice were more active in the dark cycle but otherwise did not differ from controls. In knockout cerebellum, Smarca5 coimmunoprecipitated with Cecr2, which suggested functional compensation for the loss of Smarca1. By contrast, mice with an in-frame deletion of Smarca1 exon 6 (Smarca1ex6del) had a total brain area 10% larger than controls. This enlargement was reduced when the mice were combined with Smarca5 ablation. None of the double mutants (Smarca1/Smarca5, Smarca5/Smarca1ex6del, or Smarca5/Bptf) had a more severe phenotype than Bptf mutants alone. ⚠︎

SMARCA1, ARG91TER [NM_003069.4:c.271C>T; p.Arg91Ter]

In a 6-generation family, Mirzaa et al. (2025) used exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous c.271C-T transition in the SMARCA1 gene (NM_003069.4), resulting in an arg91-to-ter (R91X) substitution. It was found in a proband, his brother, and 2 maternal cousins, who had intellectual disability or learning disability and macrocephaly (OFC +4.3 to +6 SD). The same variant was maternally inherited in a second, unrelated family, in a 6-year-old boy with macrocephaly, ASD, and ADHD.
