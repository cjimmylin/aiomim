### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is caused by homozygous or other biallelic mutation in the CSF3 gene (138970) on chromosome 17q21.1.

### Description

Autosomal recessive severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is a form of severe congenital neutropenia caused by complete deficiency of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), which is encoded by the CSF3 gene (Khouj et al., 2023; Almannai et al., 2026). Biallelic inactivation of the receptor gene, CSF3R, had already been established as a cause of SCN. Khouj et al. (2023) were the first to attribute a Mendelian disease to CSF3.

### Clinical Features

Khouj et al. (2023) described 3 patients from 2 families with severe congenital neutropenia and biallelic inactivating variants in the CSF3 gene. They noted that the patients' phenotype mirrored the one documented in mouse and zebrafish models of CSF3 deficiency.

Almannai et al. (2026) reported severe congenital neutropenia in a patient homozygous for a loss-of-function variant in the CSF3 gene. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Khouj et al. (2023) proposed that CSF3 deficiency in humans causes a novel autosomal recessive form of SCN. In each of the 2 families they studied, a different biallelic inactivating CSF3 variant segregated with the disorder. Almannai et al. (2026) found a homozygous loss-of-function CSF3 variant in a patient with SCN, which supports autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Khouj et al. (2023) studied 3 patients with SCN from 2 families. Each family segregated a different biallelic inactivating variant in the CSF3 gene. In RT-PCR analysis of RNA from the patients' skin fibroblasts, CSF3 was completely deficient because of nonsense-mediated decay.

Almannai et al. (2026) identified a homozygous pathogenic loss-of-function variant in the CSF3 gene in a patient with severe congenital neutropenia. They described this as the first report to confirm the previously proposed CSF3-related SCN, and as support for the gene-disease relationship in humans. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Lieschke et al. (1994) generated G-CSF-deficient mice by targeted disruption of the G-CSF gene in embryonal stem cells. The G-CSF -/- mice were viable, fertile, and superficially healthy. However, they had chronic neutropenia, with peripheral blood neutrophil levels at 20 to 30% of wildtype. Heterozygous mice had intermediate neutrophil levels, which the authors suggested reflected a gene-dosage effect. In G-CSF -/- marrow, granulopoietic precursor cells were reduced by 50%, and granulocyte, macrophage, and blast progenitor cells were also reduced. Mature neutrophils were still present in blood and marrow. A single dose of G-CSF mobilized fewer neutrophils into the circulation in the knockout mice. G-CSF treatment reversed the defect: 1 day of treatment brought circulating neutrophils up to normal levels, and after 4 days the marrows of knockout and wildtype mice were morphologically indistinguishable. The G-CSF -/- mice had a markedly impaired ability to control Listeria monocytogenes infection, with a diminished rise in blood neutrophils, a delayed rise in monocytes, and reduced infection-driven granulopoiesis. Lieschke et al. (1994) concluded that G-CSF is indispensable for maintaining normal steady-state neutrophil production, and they also implicated it in emergency granulopoiesis during infection.

Khouj et al. (2023) noted that the phenotype of their patients with CSF3 deficiency mirrored that documented in mouse and zebrafish models of CSF3 deficiency.
