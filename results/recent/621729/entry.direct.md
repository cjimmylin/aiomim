### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutation in the CSF3 gene, which encodes colony-stimulating factor 3 (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, G-CSF). ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is a form of severe congenital neutropenia caused by complete deficiency of colony-stimulating factor 3 (CSF3), a key factor in neutrophil production and function. Khouj et al. (2023) noted that the phenotype mirrors that documented in mouse and zebrafish models of CSF3 deficiency.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SCN14 in the families reported by Khouj et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Almannai et al. (2026) reported a further patient with a homozygous loss-of-function variant in CSF3, supporting autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 patients from 2 families with severe congenital neutropenia, Khouj et al. (2023) identified biallelic inactivating variants in the CSF3 gene; each family segregated a different variant. RT-PCR analysis of RNA derived from patient skin fibroblasts demonstrated complete deficiency of CSF3 as a result of nonsense-mediated decay. Khouj et al. (2023) noted that biallelic inactivation of the CSF3 receptor, CSF3R, was already a well-established cause of SCN, but that no Mendelian disease had previously been ascribed to CSF3.

Almannai et al. (2026) reported a patient with severe congenital neutropenia who carried a homozygous pathogenic loss-of-function variant in the CSF3 gene, providing the first confirmatory report of CSF3-related autosomal recessive SCN. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Lieschke et al. (1994) generated mice lacking G-CSF by targeted disruption of the G-CSF gene. G-CSF-null mice were viable, fertile, and superficially healthy but had chronic neutropenia, with peripheral blood neutrophil levels 20% to 30% of those of wildtype mice; heterozygous mice had intermediate neutrophil levels, suggesting a gene-dosage effect. In the marrow of null mice, granulopoietic precursor cells were reduced by 50%, and granulocyte, macrophage, and blast progenitor cells were reduced, although mature neutrophils were still present in blood and marrow. Null mice had reduced numbers of neutrophils available for rapid mobilization by a single dose of G-CSF. G-CSF administration reversed the granulopoietic defect: 1 day of treatment raised circulating neutrophil levels to normal, and after 4 days wildtype and null marrows were morphologically indistinguishable. G-CSF-null mice had a markedly impaired ability to control infection with Listeria monocytogenes, with diminished neutrophil and delayed monocyte increases in the blood and reduced infection-driven granulopoiesis.
