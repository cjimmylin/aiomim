### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is caused by homozygous or biallelic loss-of-function mutation in the CSF3 gene, which encodes colony-stimulating factor 3 (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, G-CSF). ⚠︎

### Description

Severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is an autosomal recessive form of severe congenital neutropenia caused by complete deficiency of colony-stimulating factor 3 (CSF3), a key factor in neutrophil production and function (Khouj et al., 2023). Severe congenital neutropenia is a genetically heterogeneous condition (Almannai et al., 2026). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Khouj et al. (2023) described 3 patients from 2 families with severe congenital neutropenia. They noted that the phenotype mirrored that documented in mouse and zebrafish models of CSF3 deficiency.

Almannai et al. (2026) reported severe congenital neutropenia due to CSF3 deficiency, the first confirmatory report of CSF3-related SCN.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SCN14 in the families reported by Khouj et al. (2023), in which biallelic inactivating CSF3 variants segregated with the disorder, was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Almannai et al. (2026) provided further support for autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 patients from 2 families with severe congenital neutropenia, Khouj et al. (2023) identified a different biallelic inactivating variant in the CSF3 gene in each family; the variants segregated with the disorder. RT-PCR analysis of RNA derived from patient skin fibroblasts demonstrated complete deficiency of CSF3 as a result of nonsense-mediated decay. Khouj et al. (2023) noted that, although biallelic inactivation of the CSF3 receptor gene CSF3R was a well-established cause of SCN, no Mendelian disease had previously been ascribed to CSF3.

Almannai et al. (2026) reported SCN due to a homozygous pathogenic loss-of-function variant in the CSF3 gene, providing the first confirmatory report of this gene-disease relationship.

### Animal Model

Lieschke et al. (1994) generated mice lacking G-CSF by targeted disruption of the G-CSF gene in embryonic stem cells. G-CSF-null mice were viable, fertile, and superficially healthy but had chronic neutropenia, with peripheral blood neutrophil levels 20 to 30% of those of wildtype mice; heterozygous mice had intermediate neutrophil levels, suggesting a gene-dosage effect. In the marrow of null mice, granulopoietic precursor cells were reduced by 50%, and granulocyte, macrophage, and blast progenitor cells were reduced, although mature neutrophils were still present in blood and marrow. Null mice had reduced numbers of neutrophils available for rapid mobilization by a single dose of G-CSF. G-CSF administration reversed the granulopoietic defect: 1 day of administration raised circulating neutrophil levels to normal, and after 4 days the marrows of null and wildtype mice were morphologically indistinguishable. Null mice had a markedly impaired ability to control infection with Listeria monocytogenes, with diminished neutrophil and delayed monocyte increases in the blood and reduced infection-driven granulopoiesis.
