### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is caused by biallelic mutation in the CSF3 gene (138970) on chromosome 17q21.

### Description

Autosomal recessive severe congenital neutropenia-14 (SCN14) is a form of severe congenital neutropenia caused by biallelic inactivating mutations in the CSF3 gene, which encodes granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF). Khouj et al. (2023) proposed CSF3 deficiency as a novel autosomal recessive form of SCN, and Almannai et al. (2026) supported this gene-disease relationship.

### Clinical Features

Khouj et al. (2023) described 3 patients from 2 families with severe congenital neutropenia (SCN). The authors noted that the phenotype in their patients mirrored that documented in mouse and zebrafish models of CSF3 deficiency.

Almannai et al. (2026) reported severe congenital neutropenia in a patient or patients carrying a homozygous loss-of-function variant in CSF3. The number of patients and families was not stated in the available evidence.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SCN14 in the families reported by Khouj et al. (2023) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Almannai et al. (2026) further supported autosomal recessive inheritance of CSF3-related SCN.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 patients from 2 families with SCN14, Khouj et al. (2023) identified biallelic inactivating mutations in the CSF3 gene; each family segregated a different biallelic inactivating variant with the disorder. RT-PCR of RNA derived from patient skin fibroblasts demonstrated complete deficiency of CSF3 resulting from nonsense-mediated decay. The authors concluded that CSF3 deficiency in humans causes a novel autosomal recessive form of SCN.

Almannai et al. (2026) identified a homozygous pathogenic loss-of-function mutation in the CSF3 gene in a patient or patients with SCN14. They described their study as the first confirmatory report of SCN due to a homozygous loss-of-function variant in CSF3, supporting this gene-disease relationship in humans.

### Animal Model

Lieschke et al. (1994) generated mice lacking G-CSF by targeted disruption in embryonic stem cells. The G-CSF-null mice were viable, fertile, and superficially healthy but had chronic neutropenia, with peripheral blood neutrophil levels 20 to 30% of wildtype. Heterozygous mice had intermediate neutrophil levels, suggesting a gene-dosage effect. In null mice, marrow granulopoietic precursor cells were reduced by 50%, and granulocyte, macrophage, and blast progenitor cells were reduced. Mature neutrophils were still present in blood and marrow, indicating that other factors can support neutrophil production in vivo.

Lieschke et al. (1994) found that administration of G-CSF reversed the granulopoietic defect of G-CSF-null mice: 1 day of treatment brought circulating neutrophils to normal levels, and after 4 days the null marrow could not be distinguished morphologically from wildtype marrow. Fewer neutrophils were available for rapid mobilization by a single dose of G-CSF. The null mice were markedly impaired in controlling Listeria monocytogenes infection, with smaller neutrophil increases, delayed monocyte increases in the blood, and less infection-driven granulopoiesis. The authors concluded that G-CSF is indispensable for maintaining normal steady-state neutrophil production and is involved in emergency granulopoiesis.
