### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the FBRSL1 gene on chromosome 12.

### Description

Camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is a neurodevelopmental malformation syndrome. Ufartes et al. (2020) described respiratory insufficiency, feeding difficulties, postnatal growth restriction, microcephaly, global developmental delay with no active speech, contractures, and facial dysmorphism. Some patients also had heart defects, cleft palate, and distinctive skin creases in the first year of life. The phenotype overlaps with the severe form of AUTS2 syndrome but appears to include a wider range of congenital malformations (Pauli et al., 2021).

### Clinical Features

Ufartes et al. (2020) reported 3 unrelated children with a recognizable malformation syndrome. Patients 1 and 2 had a nearly identical phenotype. In the first year of life they had respiratory insufficiency, feeding difficulties, postnatal growth restriction, microcephaly, global developmental delay, no active speech, contractures, heart defects, cleft palate, facial dysmorphism, and distinctive skin creases. The skin creases were most pronounced immediately after birth and regressed with age. Ufartes et al. (2020) noted that, unlike in congenital symmetric circumferential skin creases syndrome, the back rather than the arms and legs was preferentially affected. In patient 2, the skin folds had regressed markedly by age 2.5 years. Facial features included a pronounced neck fold, flat occiput, and dysmorphic auricles; patient 2 also had deep-set eyes, a round face, and temporal indentations. All 3 patients had intellectual disability or global developmental delay, delayed speech, autistic behavior, microcephaly, swallowing difficulty, postnatal growth retardation, skeletal abnormalities, camptodactyly/contractures, facial dysmorphism, and respiratory failure requiring ventilation therapy. Patient 1 had atrial septal defect (ASD) and patent ductus arteriosus, and patient 2 had ASD and ventricular septal defect. Patients 1 and 2 had cleft palate and hearing impairment. Patient 2 had asplenia and patient 1 had an anal abnormality. Patient 3, photographed at 12 years 6 months and 14 years 8 months, had contractures of both hands and fingers and widely spaced teeth, and had no heart defect, cleft palate, or skin creases.

Pauli et al. (2021) reviewed the 3 patients of Ufartes et al. (2020) and compared them with AUTS2 syndrome patients using the AUTS2 syndrome severity scoring system. All 3 patients had short stature, microcephaly, feeding difficulties, intellectual disability, autistic behavior, kyphosis/scoliosis, camptodactyly/contractures, and respiratory insufficiency requiring ventilation therapy. Two of the 3 had low birth weight, cerebral palsy/spasticity, high arched eyebrows, epicanthal folds, a deep or broad nasal bridge, a short or upturned philtrum, low-set ears, and widely spaced teeth. One patient had anal atresia. Pauli et al. (2021) described the congenital skin creases of the 2 more severely affected patients as involving the back, arms, and legs, disappearing during the first year of life. They concluded that patients with FBRSL1 variants had a higher rate and wider spectrum of congenital malformations than patients with AUTS2 syndrome.

Bukvic et al. (2024) reported an Italian girl, the third child of healthy nonconsanguineous parents. The pregnancy was complicated by threatened miscarriage and poor fetal movements. At term birth she weighed 2,860 g (less than 10th centile) and measured 39.5 cm in length (less than 3rd centile), and her head circumference was 34 cm. She had neonatal feeding difficulties due to weak sucking and postnatal growth retardation. Skeletal findings included bilateral contractures of the hands and fingers, bilateral congenital hip dislocation, and irregular growth fronts of the long bones. She was initially diagnosed with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita at 4 months of age. Developmental delay was global: she achieved head control at 7 months, sat without support at 14 months, and walked without support at 46 months. At 48 months she had no active speech and severe intellectual disability (developmental quotient 22), microcephaly (head circumference 45.3 cm, -2.68 SD), and motor stereotypies. Facial dysmorphism included flat occiput, frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia, short philtrum, micrognathia, and microstomia with a 'whistling mouth.' She also had mild scoliosis and slight distal hypertonia of the legs with supinated, varus feet. Hearing was normal and there was no heart defect. Cerebral and spinal MRI at 2 months and 4 years and metabolic studies were normal. From 46 months she had daily drop tonic seizures, later with myoclonic absences. Interictal EEG showed frontocentral spikes and spike-and-wave complexes. Bukvic et al. (2024) reported that no autism spectrum disorder was found, although their comparison table listed autism/autistic behavior as present. Gastrostomy was performed at 6 years of age because of feeding difficulties and poor weight gain. Unlike the patients of Ufartes et al. (2020), she lacked skin creases, cleft palate, heart defects, hearing impairment, and respiratory insufficiency.

Xu et al. (2026) reported a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism. They also analyzed 4 patients with FBRSL1 variants and described an emerging syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by microcephaly, global developmental delay, autism, facial dysmorphism, and skeletal contractures.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous FBRSL1 mutations in patients with CIDFS reported by Ufartes et al. (2020), Bukvic et al. (2024), and Xu et al. (2026) occurred de novo where both parents were tested. In 1 patient of Ufartes et al. (2020), the mutation was absent from the mother but the father was unavailable for testing. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 unrelated children with CIDFS, Ufartes et al. (2020) identified heterozygous truncating mutations in the FBRSL1 gene. In patients 1 and 2, trio exome sequencing excluded pathogenic variants in TUBB, MAPRE2, and AUTS2. Patient 1 carried a de novo c.487C-T transition predicting a gln163-to-ter (Q163X) substitution, and Sanger sequencing confirmed that it was absent in both unaffected parents. In patient 2, no change was initially found in the annotated FBRSL1 transcript. Manual inspection of intronic reads then revealed a 23-bp deletion that affected an alternative exon 3 of a then-hypothetical short N-terminal transcript (c.581_603del). Sanger sequencing showed that the deletion was de novo. In patient 3, single exome sequencing and reverse phenotyping identified a c.332G-A transition predicting a trp111-to-ter (W111X) substitution. This mutation was not found in the mother, and the father was unavailable for testing. None of the variants was present in gnomAD or ExAC. RNA analysis showed that all 3 truncating variants escaped nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. Ufartes et al. (2020) showed by RT-PCR and Western blot that short N-terminal FBRSL1 isoforms (3.1 and 3.2) containing a DNA translocase (FtsK) domain exist, and all 3 mutations affected these isoforms.

In an Italian girl with CIDFS, Bukvic et al. (2024) used trio clinical exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication (c.371dupC; NM_001142641.2) in exon 2 of the FBRSL1 gene. The duplication predicted a frameshift and premature termination (Cys125LeufsTer7), producing a 131-amino acid protein. Sanger sequencing of the parents confirmed de novo occurrence, and no other variants explaining the phenotype were found. An earlier SNP array had shown 2 maternally inherited duplications at 16p13.11 and 1 paternally inherited duplication at 7p11.2, all considered variants of uncertain significance. EpiSign genomewide DNA methylation analysis of peripheral blood gave a profile similar to controls for all episignatures evaluated. An FBRSL1-specific episignature had not been defined.

In a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism, Xu et al. (2026) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a de novo FBRSL1 frameshift variant (Ala128CysfsTer5).

### Pathogenesis

Ufartes et al. (2020) noted that the long FBRSL1 isoform 1 appears tolerant to loss-of-function variants (pLI = 0.01 in gnomAD), so haploinsufficiency of the long transcripts seemed unlikely. In contrast, no truncating variants had been observed in the short N-terminal isoform 3.2. They proposed haploinsufficiency of isoform 3.2, with a resulting imbalance among FBRSL1 isoforms, as a possible mechanism. Because the truncated transcripts escaped nonsense-mediated decay, they also considered a dominant-negative mechanism. N-terminal FBRSL1 isoforms localized to both cytoplasm and nucleus and appeared to associate with centrosomes and kinetochores in dividing cells.

Bukvic et al. (2024) used in silico modeling of the 131-amino acid truncated protein. The model predicted that it retains a domain at residues 81 to 93 that could bind the same region of FBRSL1. They suggested that the truncated protein could interact with and inactivate wildtype FBRSL1, consistent with a possible dominant-negative effect.

### Clinical Management

In the patient reported by Bukvic et al. (2024), seizures initially responded to valproic acid but later relapsed. Ethosuximide and rufinamide were added, giving better but incomplete control. She received multidisciplinary rehabilitation including splinting, orthotics, physiotherapy, hydrotherapy, hippotherapy, and augmentative and alternative communication, and she achieved most of her developmental milestones. A percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy was placed at 6 years of age.

### Animal Model

Ufartes et al. (2020) found that fbrsl1 is expressed maternally and throughout early development in Xenopus laevis embryos. Morpholino knockdown of fbrsl1 caused reduced craniofacial structures and eyes, cartilage hypoplasia, reduced Ncam expression indicating defective brain development, and impaired migration of cranial nerves and motor neurons. Coinjection of the human short N-terminal FBRSL1 isoform 3.1 partially rescued the craniofacial defects in a dose-dependent manner. Neither the long isoform 1 nor isoform 3.1 carrying the Q163X mutation from patient 1 rescued the defects.

Xu et al. (2026) found that zebrafish fbrsl1 knockdown models recapitulated neurodevelopmental abnormalities, epileptiform discharges, and cardiac dysfunction. They also reported high FBRSL1 expression in the developing brain and heart and in neural progenitors of 1-month-old organoids.

In a child (patient 1) with CIDFS, Ufartes et al. **.0002 CAMPTODACTYLY, IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT, AND FACIAL DYSMORPHISM SYNDROME**  
FBRSL1, 23-BP DEL, NT581 [c.581_603del]

In a girl (patient 2) with CIDFS, Ufartes et al. (2020) identified a de novo heterozygous 23-bp deletion in the FBRSL1 gene by manually inspecting intronic reads from trio exome sequencing. The deletion lies in an alternative exon 3 of the short N-terminal transcript (isoform 3.1; XM_005266181.4) and is described as c.581_603del relative to that transcript. It was predicted to cause a frameshift with a premature stop codon at position 356 of isoform 3.1, disrupting the DNA translocase domain, while isoform 1 was unaffected. Sanger sequencing confirmed the deletion in the patient and wildtype sequence in her healthy parents. RT-PCR of lymphocyte cDNA confirmed the heterozygous deletion at the transcript level.
