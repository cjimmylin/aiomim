### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the FBRSL1 gene (620123) on chromosome 12q24.33. ⚠︎

### Description

Camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is a recognizable malformation syndrome. Its features include impaired intellectual development and/or global developmental delay, delayed or absent speech, autistic behavior, microcephaly, feeding difficulties, postnatal growth retardation, skeletal abnormalities, camptodactyly/contractures, and facial dysmorphism (Ufartes et al., 2020; Pauli et al., 2021). Some patients also have neonatal respiratory failure, cleft palate, heart defects, hearing impairment, and marked skin creases at birth (Ufartes et al., 2020; Pauli et al., 2021). Epilepsy has also been reported (Bukvic et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ufartes et al. (2020) reported 3 unrelated children with a recognizable malformation syndrome. All 3 had impaired intellectual development and/or global developmental delay, delayed speech, autistic behavior, microcephaly, swallowing difficulty, postnatal growth retardation, skeletal abnormalities, camptodactyly/contractures, facial dysmorphism, and respiratory failure that needed ventilation therapy. Two patients had heart defects (ASD/PDA and ASD/VSD), and 2 had cleft palate, hearing impairment, and distinctive skin creases. One patient had asplenia and 1 had an anal anomaly. In 2 patients the skin folds were most pronounced right after birth, mainly affected the back rather than the arms and legs, and regressed with age. The authors noted that this pattern differs from that of the congenital symmetric circumferential skin creases syndromes (CSCSC1 and CSCSC2). Other features included a pronounced neck fold, a flat occiput, dysplastic auricles, deep-set eyes, a round face, temporal indentations, contractures of the hands and fingers, and widely spaced teeth.

Pauli et al. (2021) reviewed the 3 unrelated children with truncating FBRSL1 variants. In the newborn period they had breathing failure needing ventilation and feeding difficulties. Later they showed intellectual disability with no active speech, postnatal microcephaly, growth retardation, and contractures. Two of the 3 had cleft palate and heart defects (atrial septal defect with patent ductus arteriosus in one, and atrial and ventricular septal defects in the other). One had asplenia and 1 had anal atresia. The 2 more severely affected children were born with marked skin creases that disappeared during the first year of life. Pauli et al. (2021) compared these patients with AUTS2 syndrome using the features of the AUTS2 syndrome severity scoring system. All 3 had short stature, microcephaly, feeding difficulties, intellectual disability, autistic behavior, kyphosis/scoliosis, and camptodactyly/contractures. Two of the 3 had cerebral palsy/spasticity, high arched eyebrows, epicanthal folds, a deep/broad nasal bridge, a short/upturned philtrum, low-set ears, widely spaced teeth, and hearing impairment. None had hypertelorism, ptosis, strabismus, or hernias. The authors noted a large overlap with severe AUTS2 syndrome, but with a wider range and a higher rate of congenital malformations in the FBRSL1 patients.

Bukvic et al. (2024) reported a girl who was the third child of healthy nonconsanguineous Italian parents; her father was 46 and her mother 38 years old at her birth. The pregnancy was complicated by threatened miscarriage and poor fetal movements. At term she had low birth weight (2860 g) and length below the 3rd centile. She had neonatal feeding difficulties due to weak sucking, postnatal growth retardation, bilateral contractures of the hands and fingers, bilateral congenital hip dislocation, and irregular bone growth fronts of the long bones. She was initially diagnosed with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita at 4 months of age. She had global developmental delay: she achieved head control at 7 months, sitting at 14 months, and unsupported walking at 46 months. At 48 months she had no active speech and severe intellectual disability (developmental quotient 22), with motor stereotypies but no autism spectrum disorder. At that age she also had short stature, low weight, microcephaly, and facial dysmorphism. The facial features included flat occiput, frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia, short philtrum, micrognathia, and microstomia with 'whistling mouth.' A later examination noted a small forehead, bitemporal narrowing, sunken eyes, broad nasal bridge, unusual ears, and thick lips. She also had mild scoliosis, distal limb hypertonia with supinated varus feet, a wide-based gait, and cerebral palsy/spasticity. Her hearing and heart were normal. Brain and spinal MRI at 2 months and 4 years and metabolic studies were also normal. At 46 months she developed epilepsy with daily drop tonic seizures, followed later by myoclonic absences. Ictal EEG showed a high-voltage slow wave followed by voltage attenuation. Interictal EEG showed frontocentral spikes and spike-and-wave complexes, as well as 2-Hz diffuse spike-and-wave complexes. Bukvic et al. (2024) noted that epilepsy, stereotypies, hip dislocation, and mastication problems had not previously been reported in FBRSL1 patients. Conversely, this patient lacked the respiratory insufficiency, cleft palate, heart defects, and hearing impairment reported by Ufartes et al. (2020). ⚠︎

Xu et al. (2026) described a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism. Analyzing 4 patients with FBRSL1 variants, they described an emerging syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by microcephaly, global developmental delay, autism, facial dysmorphism, and skeletal contractures.

### Biochemical Features

Bukvic et al. (2024) performed genome-wide DNA methylation analysis (EpiSign) on peripheral blood from their patient. The profile was similar to controls for all episignatures tested, including the PRC2 episignature (MVP score of zero). FBRSL1 and PRC1.5 episignatures have not been defined.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous FBRSL1 mutations in CIDFS reported by Ufartes et al. (2020), Bukvic et al. (2024), and Xu et al. (2026) occurred de novo in the cases where both parents were tested. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

By SNP array, Bukvic et al. (2024) found 3 duplications in their patient: a paternally inherited duplication of approximately 530 kb at 7p11.2, and 2 maternally inherited duplications at 16p13.11 (about 470 kb and 1.1 Mb). All were classified as variants of uncertain significance. STITCH analysis found no interactions attributable to these duplications that could be linked to the phenotype.

### Molecular Genetics

Ufartes et al. (2020) identified heterozygous FBRSL1 mutations in 3 unrelated children with CIDFS. In patient 1, trio exome sequencing identified a de novo nonsense mutation (c.487C-T; Q163X), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Both unaffected parents carried the wild-type sequence, and the variant was absent from gnomAD and ExAC. The transcript escaped nonsense-mediated decay. Pathogenic variants in TUBB, MAPRE2, and AUTS2 were excluded in this patient. In patient 2, no change was initially found in the canonical FBRSL1 transcript. Manual inspection of intronic reads then revealed a de novo 23-bp deletion (c.581_603del) affecting alternative exon 3 of the short N-terminal isoform 3.1 (XM_005266181.4). The deletion causes a frameshift and a premature stop at position 356 that disrupts the DNA translocase domain. It was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and RT-PCR, and both healthy parents were wild type. In patient 3, single exome sequencing and reverse phenotyping identified a nonsense mutation (c.332G-A; W111X). It was absent from the healthy mother; the father was not available for testing. It was not present in gnomAD or ExAC. In Xenopus laevis, the craniofacial defects caused by Fbrsl1 knockdown were rescued by human short isoform 3.1, but not by isoform 3.1 carrying the Q163X mutation.

Pauli et al. (2021) noted that all 3 reported patients carried truncating variants in the N-terminal part of FBRSL1 that affect the short N-terminal isoforms. Two were stop variants and 1 was a frameshift causing a premature stop codon. By contrast, severe AUTS2 syndrome is linked to variants affecting the AUTS2 C-terminus.

In the Italian girl with CIDFS, Bukvic et al. (2024) used trio clinical exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication in exon 2 of the FBRSL1 gene (c.371dupC, NM_001142641.2). The duplication causes a frameshift and premature stop codon (Cys125LeufsTer7) and is predicted to yield a 131-amino acid protein instead of the 1,045-residue protein. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and was absent from the parents. No other variants explaining the phenotype were found.

In a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism, Xu et al. (2026) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a de novo truncating mutation in the FBRSL1 gene (Ala128CysfsTer5). In zebrafish, fbrsl1 knockdown recapitulated neurodevelopmental abnormalities, epileptiform discharges, and cardiac dysfunction, consistent with the patient's phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Using RT-PCR and Western blot, Ufartes et al. (2020) validated 2 short N-terminal FBRSL1 isoforms, 3.1 (about 66 kDa) and 3.2 (about 55 kDa), in addition to the long isoform 1 (about 110 kDa). The short isoforms contain an FtsK DNA translocase domain, whereas isoform 1 instead contains an AUTS2 domain. All 3 patient variants affected the short isoforms. Isoform 3.1 was expressed mainly in fetal tissues. The N-terminal isoforms localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus and associated with centrosomes and kinetochores. Ufartes et al. (2020) noted that the long FBRSL1 isoform appears tolerant to loss of function (pLI = 0.01), so haploinsufficiency of the long transcripts is unlikely. They suggested 2 possible mechanisms. One was haploinsufficiency of short isoform 3.2, causing an imbalance between isoforms. The other was a dominant-negative effect, because the truncated transcripts escaped nonsense-mediated decay.

Pauli et al. (2021) noted that FBRSL1, like AUTS2, interacts with the Polycomb subcomplexes PRC1.3 and PRC1.5 and competes with AUTS2 for binding to PRC1.5. In HEK293 cells and human fibroblasts, FBRSL1 was found in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm. An N-terminal antibody detected both nuclear and cytoplasmic signal, whereas a C-terminal antibody mainly detected nuclear signal. This suggested that different isoforms have nuclear versus cytoplasmic functions. Pauli et al. (2021) suggested that mutations in the short N-terminal isoforms cause the developmental phenotype. ⚠︎

Bukvic et al. (2024) used in silico modeling of the truncated 131-residue FBRSL1 protein resulting from the c.371dupC mutation. The model predicted a self-binding domain (residues 81 to 93) and 2 nuclear localization signals. The authors hypothesized that the truncated protein could localize to the nucleus and inactivate wildtype FBRSL1, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. ⚠︎

Xu et al. (2026) found that FBRSL1 is highly expressed in the developing brain and heart. Single-cell RNA sequencing showed high expression in neural progenitors of 1-month-old organoids, suggesting a vital role in neurodevelopment.

### Clinical Management

Bukvic et al. (2024) reported that their patient's seizures initially responded well to valproic acid but relapsed months later. Control was better but incomplete after ethosuximide and rufinamide were added. A percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy was placed at 6 years for feeding difficulties and poor weight gain. With multidisciplinary rehabilitation, including augmentative and alternative communication, she became able to sit and stand independently, walk with minimal support, and communicate basic needs.

### Animal Model

Ufartes et al. (2020) knocked down Fbrsl1 in Xenopus laevis embryos with a splice-blocking morpholino. Knockdown caused reduced craniofacial structures and eyes, cartilage hypoplasia (most severe in the branchial arches), reduced Ncam expression in the brain, and disturbed migration of cranial nerves and motor neurons. Human short isoform 3.1 rescued the craniofacial defects in a dose-dependent manner. Neither the long isoform 1 nor isoform 3.1 carrying the Q163X mutation rescued them.

Xu et al. (2026) found that fbrsl1 knockdown in zebrafish recapitulated neurodevelopmental abnormalities, epileptiform discharges, and cardiac dysfunction, consistent with their patient's phenotype.
