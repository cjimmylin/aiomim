### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the FBRSL1 gene on chromosome 12.

### Description

Camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is a neurodevelopmental malformation syndrome. Its main features are global developmental delay, intellectual disability, absent active speech, microcephaly, postnatal growth retardation, contractures or camptodactyly of the hands and fingers, and facial dysmorphism. Some patients also have neonatal respiratory insufficiency, feeding difficulties, cleft palate, heart defects, congenital skin creases, hearing impairment, and seizures (Ufartes et al., 2020; Bukvic et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ufartes et al. (2020) reported 3 unrelated children with an overlapping syndromic phenotype. Two of them (patients 1 and 2) had a nearly identical presentation in the first year of life: respiratory insufficiency, feeding difficulties, postnatal growth restriction and microcephaly, global developmental delay, no active speech, contractures, heart defects, cleft palate, facial dysmorphism, and distinctive skin creases. The skin creases were most pronounced immediately after birth and regressed with age in both children. Unlike the skin phenotype of congenital symmetric circumferential skin creases syndrome, the creases mainly affected the back rather than the arms and legs. Patient 1 had an atrial septal defect and persistent ductus arteriosus, an anal abnormality, and hearing impairment. Patient 2 had an atrial septal defect, a ventricular septal defect, asplenia, and hearing impairment. All 3 patients had intellectual disability and/or global developmental delay, delayed speech and language development, autistic behavior, microcephaly, swallowing difficulty, postnatal growth retardation, skeletal abnormalities, camptodactyly/contractures, facial dysmorphism, and respiratory failure requiring ventilation therapy. Patient 3, examined at ages 12 years 6 months and 14 years 8 months, had contractures of both hands and fingers and widely spaced teeth, but no heart defect, cleft palate, hearing impairment, or skin creases. Reported facial features included a flat back of the head, dysmorphic or dysplastic ears, a pronounced neck fold, deep-set eyes, a round face, and temporal indentations.

Pauli et al. (2021) compared the 3 patients of Ufartes et al. (2020) with patients with AUTS2 syndrome, using the AUTS2 syndrome severity scoring system. Among the 3 patients, 2 had low birth weight, and all 3 had short stature, microcephaly, feeding difficulties, intellectual disability, autism/autistic behavior, and kyphosis/scoliosis. Two had cerebral palsy/spasticity, high arched eyebrows, epicanthal folds, a deep/broad nasal bridge, a short/upturned philtrum, low-set ears, and widely spaced teeth. In their account, the 2 more severely affected patients were born with pronounced congenital skin creases at the back, arms, and legs, which became less pronounced during the first year of life and then disappeared. The authors noted a marked clinical overlap with the severe form of AUTS2 syndrome, with a wider spectrum and higher rate of congenital malformations in patients with FBRSL1 variants.

Bukvic et al. (2024) reported a girl who was the third child of healthy, nonconsanguineous Italian parents. There were threatened miscarriages in the first and second trimesters and poor fetal movements. At birth, her weight was 2,860 g (less than 10th centile), length 39.5 cm (less than 3rd centile), and head circumference 34 cm. She had neonatal feeding difficulties due to weak sucking and postnatal growth retardation. Skeletal findings included bilateral contractures of the hands and fingers, bilateral congenital hip dislocation, and irregular bone growth fronts in the long bones. Arthrogryposis multiplex congenita was diagnosed at age 4 months. She had global developmental delay: she achieved head control at 7 months, sat without support at 14 months, and walked without support at 46 months. At 48 months, she had no active speech and severe intellectual disability (developmental quotient of 22), as well as simple motor and complex arm and hand stereotypies. Her height was 96 cm, her weight 11.4 kg, and she had microcephaly (head circumference 45.3 cm, -2.68 SD). Facial dysmorphism included a flat back of the head, frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia, short philtrum, micrognathia, and microstomia with a 'whistling mouth.' Later described features included a small forehead, bitemporal narrowing, sunken eyes, a broad nasal bridge, unusual earlobes, and thick lips. She also had mild scoliosis and slight distal hypertonia of the legs, with supinated and varus feet. Hearing and cardiac evaluations were normal. From age 46 months she had daily drop tonic seizures and later myoclonic absences, and the interictal EEG showed frequent spikes and spike-and-wave complexes. Brain and spinal MRI at 2 months and 4 years were normal, and metabolic studies were negative. A percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy was placed at age 6 years because of feeding difficulties and poor weight gain. The text reported no autistic spectrum disorder, but the authors' comparison table listed autism/autistic behavior as present in this patient.

Xu et al. (2026) reported a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism. From an analysis of 4 patients with FBRSL1 variants, they described an emerging syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by microcephaly, global developmental delay, autism, facial dysmorphism, and skeletal contractures.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous FBRSL1 mutations found in patients with CIDFS occurred de novo in 2 patients reported by Ufartes et al. (2020) and in patients reported by Bukvic et al. (2024) and Xu et al. (2026), consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. In the third patient of Ufartes et al. (2020), the mutation was absent from the mother, and the father was not available for testing. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 unrelated children with CIDFS, Ufartes et al. (2020) identified heterozygous truncating mutations in the FBRSL1 gene. Trio exome sequencing identified a de novo nonsense mutation (Q163X) in patient 1. In patient 2, manual inspection of intronic reads followed by Sanger sequencing identified a de novo 23-bp deletion. This deletion lies in an alternative exon 3 of a short N-terminal transcript and causes a frameshift and premature stop codon (c.581_603del). In patient 3, single exome sequencing and reverse phenotyping identified a nonsense mutation (W111X). This mutation was absent from the mother; the father was unavailable. None of the variants were present in the gnomAD or ExAC databases. RNA analysis showed that all 3 truncating variants escaped nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. All 3 mutations affected the 2 short N-terminal isoforms (3.1 and 3.2), which the authors validated by RT-PCR and Western blot analysis, and they disrupted a predicted DNA translocase (FtsK) domain. The 23-bp deletion affected only the short isoforms. In Xenopus embryos, the craniofacial defects caused by Fbrsl1 knockdown were rescued by human isoform 3.1 but not by the long isoform 1 or by isoform 3.1 carrying the Q163X mutation. The authors proposed either haploinsufficiency of the short isoform 3.2 or a dominant-negative effect as the disease mechanism.

In an Italian girl with CIDFS, Bukvic et al. (2024) identified a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication in exon 2 of the FBRSL1 gene (c.371dupC), predicted to cause a frameshift and premature stop codon (Cys125LeufsTer7). The mutation was found by trio clinical exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. No other variants explaining the phenotype were found. SNP array analysis showed 2 maternally inherited duplications at 16p13.11 and a paternally inherited duplication at 7p11.2, all classified as variants of uncertain significance. In silico modeling suggested that the 131-residue truncated protein retains a predicted self-binding domain (residues 81 to 93), which the authors suggested could confer a dominant-negative effect. EpiSign genome-wide DNA methylation analysis of the patient's blood gave a profile similar to controls for all episignatures tested, including the PRC2 episignature. Episignatures for FBRSL1 or PRC1.5 were not available.

Using whole-exome sequencing, Xu et al. (2026) identified a de novo FBRSL1 variant (Ala128CysfsTer5) in a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism. Knockdown of fbrsl1 in zebrafish reproduced features of the patient's phenotype.

### Clinical Management

In the patient reported by Bukvic et al. (2024), valproic acid initially controlled the seizures well. After a relapse with daily drop tonic seizures and myoclonic absences, ethosuximide and rufinamide were added, which improved control but did not fully control the seizures. Multidisciplinary rehabilitation began in infancy and included motor stimulation, splinting and orthotics, sensory and cognitive stimulation, hydrotherapy, hippotherapy, and an augmentative and alternative communication system. With this, the patient became able to sit and stand independently, walk with minimal support, and communicate her basic needs.

### Animal Model

Ufartes et al. (2020) found that fbrsl1 is maternally expressed in Xenopus laevis oocytes and throughout early embryonic development. Knockdown of Fbrsl1 with a splice-blocking morpholino caused a significant reduction of craniofacial structures and the eye, as well as cartilage hypoplasia, most severe in the branchial arches. Morphant embryos also showed reduced Ncam expression, indicating defects in brain development, and impaired directional migration of cranial nerves and motor neurons. Human FBRSL1 isoform 3.1 partially rescued the craniofacial defects in a dose-dependent manner. Neither isoform 1 nor isoform 3.1 carrying the patient mutation Q163X rescued them.

Xu et al. (2026) found that fbrsl1 knockdown in zebrafish reproduced neurodevelopmental abnormalities, epileptiform discharges, and cardiac dysfunction. FBRSL1 was highly expressed in the developing brain and heart. Single-cell RNA sequencing showed high FBRSL1 expression in neural progenitors of 1-month-old organoids.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CAMPTODACTYLY, IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT, AND FACIAL DYSMORPHISM SYNDROME**  
FBRSL1, GLN163TER [c.487C>T; p.Gln163*]

In a child with CIDFS (patient 1), Ufartes et al. (2020) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.487C-T transition in the FBRSL1 gene, resulting in a gln163-to-ter (Q163X) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, and both unaffected parents had the wild-type sequence. The mutation was not present in the gnomAD or ExAC databases, and the transcript escaped nonsense-mediated decay. Human FBRSL1 isoform 3.1 carrying the mutation failed to rescue the craniofacial defects in Fbrsl1-depleted Xenopus embryos, whereas wild-type isoform 3.1 rescued them.

**.0002 CAMPTODACTYLY, IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT, AND FACIAL DYSMORPHISM SYNDROME**  
FBRSL1, 23-BP DEL, NT581 [c.581_603del]

In a girl with CIDFS (patient 2), Ufartes et al. (2020) identified a de novo heterozygous 23-bp deletion in the FBRSL1 gene (c.581_603del, numbered relative to short isoform 3.1). The deletion was detected by manual inspection of intronic exome reads and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and both healthy parents had the wild-type sequence. It affects an alternative exon 3 present only in the short N-terminal isoforms 3.1 and 3.2. It causes a frameshift with a premature stop codon at position 356 of isoform 3.1, disrupting the DNA translocase domain. RT-PCR of patient lymphocyte RNA confirmed the heterozygous deletion at the transcript level.

**.0003 CAMPTODACTYLY, IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT, AND FACIAL DYSMORPHISM SYNDROME**  
FBRSL1, TRP111TER [c.332G>A; p.Trp111*]

In a patient with CIDFS (patient 3), Ufartes et al. (2020) used single exome sequencing and reverse phenotyping to identify a heterozygous c.332G-A transition in the FBRSL1 gene, resulting in a trp111-to-ter (W111X) substitution. The mutation was not found in the healthy mother; the father was not available for testing. It was not present in the gnomAD or ExAC databases, and the transcript escaped nonsense-mediated decay.

**.0004 CAMPTODACTYLY, IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT, AND FACIAL DYSMORPHISM SYNDROME**  
FBRSL1, 1-BP DUP, 371C [c.371dupC; p.Cys125Leufs*7]

In an Italian girl with CIDFS, Bukvic et al. (2024) used trio clinical exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication (c.371dupC, NM_001142641.2) in exon 2 of the FBRSL1 gene, predicted to cause a frameshift and premature stop codon (Cys125LeufsTer7). The result is a predicted protein of 131 amino acids. Sanger sequencing of the patient and parents confirmed de novo occurrence.

**.0005 CAMPTODACTYLY, IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT, AND FACIAL DYSMORPHISM SYNDROME**  
FBRSL1, FS, ALA128CYS [p.Ala128Cysfs*5]

In a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism, Xu et al. (2026) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a de novo frameshift variant in the FBRSL1 gene (Ala128CysfsTer5). Knockdown of fbrsl1 in zebrafish reproduced neurodevelopmental abnormalities, epileptiform discharges, and cardiac dysfunction.
