### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the FBRSL1 gene (620123) on chromosome 12q24.33. ⚠︎

### Description

Camptodactyly, impaired intellectual development, and facial dysmorphism syndrome (CIDFS) is a syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder. Features include intellectual disability with absent speech, global developmental delay, postnatal microcephaly, growth retardation, facial dysmorphism, and contractures. Some patients also have congenital malformations such as cleft palate and heart defects, and some have epilepsy (Pauli et al., 2021; Bukvic et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026).

### Clinical Features

Pauli et al. (2021) reviewed 3 previously reported patients with truncating FBRSL1 variants. In the neonatal period these patients had respiratory insufficiency and feeding difficulties. Later they developed intellectual disability without active speech, postnatal microcephaly, growth retardation, and contractures. Two of the patients had cleft palate and heart defects, 1 had asplenia, and 1 had anal atresia. The 2 more severely affected patients had congenital skin creases that disappeared during the first year of life. Pauli et al. (2021) compared these patients with patients with AUTS2 syndrome using the AUTS2 syndrome severity scoring system. They found a remarkable overlap with the severe form of AUTS2 syndrome. However, the FBRSL1 patients had a wider spectrum and a higher rate of congenital malformations.

Bukvic et al. (2024) reported an Italian girl, born to nonconsanguineous parents, who was last reported at age 6 years. The pregnancy was notable for poor fetal movements, and she had low birth weight. As a neonate she had feeding difficulties due to weak sucking. She later had postnatal growth retardation and poor weight gain, and she required gastrostomy tube feeding. Skeletal findings included bilateral contractures of the hands and fingers, arthrogryposis multiplex congenita, bilateral congenital hip dislocation, irregular growth fronts of the long bones, talipes varus, and mild scoliosis. She had global developmental delay. At 48 months of age she had not developed active speech and had severe intellectual disability, with a developmental quotient of 22 on the Griffiths Mental Development Scales. She had motor stereotypies, truncal hypotonia with distal limb hypertonia/spasticity, and a wide-based gait with poor balance. She had short stature and microcephaly. Dysmorphic features included flat occiput, frontal bossing, bitemporal narrowing, midface hypoplasia, deeply set eyes, broad nasal bridge, short philtrum, thick lips, microstomia with a whistling mouth, micrognathia, abnormal ears, and widely spaced teeth. Her seizures included drop tonic seizures, myoclonic absences, and myoclonus of the extremities, and her interictal EEG showed spike-and-wave complexes. Her seizures responded at first to valproic acid but later relapsed, and ethosuximide and rufinamide were added. Brain and spinal MRI and hearing were normal. She had no heart defect, cleft palate, or autism spectrum disorder. Bukvic et al. (2024) noted that several of her features had not been reported in the 3 earlier FBRSL1 cases: epilepsy, stereotypies, hip dislocation, chewing problems, and the need for percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy. ⚠︎

Xu et al. (2026) reported a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism. They analyzed 4 patients with FBRSL1 variants. They described an emerging syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by microcephaly, global developmental delay, autism, facial dysmorphism, and skeletal contractures.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous FBRSL1 mutations in the patients reported by Bukvic et al. (2024) and Xu et al. (2026) occurred de novo. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Pauli et al. (2021) noted that all 3 previously reported patients had truncating variants in the N terminus of FBRSL1: 2 were stop variants and 1 was a frameshift. These variants affect the short N-terminal isoforms of the protein.

In the Italian girl with CIDFS, Bukvic et al. (2024) performed trio clinical exome sequencing. They identified a de novo heterozygous 1-bp duplication (c.371dupC; NM_001142641.2) in exon 2 of the FBRSL1 gene, resulting in a frameshift and a premature stop codon (Cys125LeufsTer7). The variant was absent in both parents by exome and Sanger sequencing. SNP array analysis in this patient also showed a paternally inherited duplication of about 530 kb at 7p11.2 and 2 maternally inherited duplications at 16p13.11. All 3 duplications were classified as variants of uncertain significance. STITCH in silico analysis found no interactions that could explain the phenotype.

In a patient with neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, congenital heart disease, and facial dysmorphism, Xu et al. (2026) identified a de novo heterozygous frameshift mutation in the FBRSL1 gene (Ala128CysfsTer5). ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Pauli et al. (2021) noted that severe AUTS2 syndrome is linked to C-terminal AUTS2 variants. In contrast, the 3 FBRSL1 patients all had truncating variants in the N terminus of FBRSL1, affecting the short N-terminal isoforms. Pauli et al. (2021) described a long 19-exon FBRSL1 transcript (NM_001142641.2) and 2 validated short N-terminal isoforms. The short isoforms contain an alternative exon 3 that leads to a stop codon. They lack the AUTS2 family domain but include a predicted Ftsk DNA translocase domain.

### Pathogenesis

Pauli et al. (2021) noted that FBRSL1, like AUTS2, interacts with the Polycomb subcomplexes PRC1.3 and PRC1.5 and competes with AUTS2 for binding to PRC1.5. FBRSL1 was found in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm of HEK293 cells and human fibroblasts. An N-terminal antibody detected both nuclear and cytoplasmic FBRSL1, whereas a C-terminal antibody detected mainly nuclear FBRSL1.

Bukvic et al. (2024) predicted that the truncated 131-residue FBRSL1 protein encoded by the c.371dupC allele keeps a self-binding domain (residues 81 to 93) and nuclear localization signals. From this they hypothesized that the truncated protein could bind and inactivate wildtype FBRSL1, acting in a dominant-negative manner.

Xu et al. (2026) found that FBRSL1 is highly expressed in the developing brain and heart, which could potentially explain the multisystem phenotype. Single-cell RNA sequencing showed high FBRSL1 expression in neural progenitors of 1-month-old brain organoids.

### Diagnosis

Bukvic et al. (2024) studied the genome-wide DNA methylation profile (EpiSign) in peripheral blood of their patient with a truncating FBRSL1 mutation. The profile matched controls for all episignatures tested, including the PRC2 episignature. They noted that FBRSL1 and PRC1.5 episignatures have not yet been defined.

### Animal Model

Pauli et al. (2021) reviewed data from Xenopus. Morpholino knockdown of Fbrsl1 caused craniofacial defects, cartilage hypoplasia, reduced brain size, and impaired migration of cranial nerves and motor neurons. A human short N-terminal FBRSL1 isoform rescued the craniofacial defects. Neither the long isoform nor a version of the short isoform carrying a patient variant rescued them, suggesting that mutations affecting the short N-terminal isoforms are causative.

Xu et al. (2026) found that knockdown of fbrsl1 in zebrafish recapitulated neurodevelopmental abnormalities, epileptiform discharges, and cardiac dysfunction. These findings were consistent with the phenotype of their patient carrying a de novo FBRSL1 truncating mutation.
