### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that recurrent hydatidiform mole-6 (HYDM6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the KASH5 gene (618125) on chromosome 19q13.33. ⚠︎

### Description

Recurrent hydatidiform mole-6 is a female reproductive disorder in which affected women have repeated molar pregnancies, often with miscarriages. Rezaei et al. (2024) reported biallelic deleterious variants in 6 genes, including KASH5, in patients with recurrent androgenetic hydatidiform moles. Five of these genes are essential for meiosis I, and deficiency of these genes leads to premature ovarian insufficiency. Saharan et al. (2026) reported that HYDM6 can progress to recurrent and metastatic gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN).

### Clinical Features

Rezaei et al. (2024) described a Jordanian woman (patient 439) who had 3 hydatidiform moles and 2 miscarriages.

Saharan et al. (2026) reported a woman with recurrent hydatidiform moles who had recurrent gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN). Her sister also had recurrent moles and died of metastatic GTN. The authors pointed out that the disorder can progress to severe, life-threatening metastatic GTN.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HYDM6 in the patients reported by Rezaei et al. (2024) and Saharan et al. (2026) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Rezaei et al. (2024) found compound heterozygous KASH5 variants in their patient, and Saharan et al. (2026) found a homozygous KASH5 variant in theirs. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

In the same study that reported KASH5 variants, Rezaei et al. (2024) also found biallelic variants in other meiosis or ovarian genes in patients with recurrent hydatidiform moles. In an Iranian proband (1802) born to consanguineous parents who were third-degree cousins, they used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous frameshift variant in the HFM1 gene, c.3124dupT (p.Tyr1042LeufsTer7). The variant lay in a 25-Mb run of homozygosity on chromosome 1. It was also homozygous in her affected sister, who had 3 hydatidiform moles, and heterozygous in both parents. The CHM from the affected sister was androgenetic monospermic. An affected maternal aunt had 1 mole at age 23 and was then unable to conceive. She was compound heterozygous for c.3124dupT and a predicted splice acceptor variant, c.1159-1G-A.

Rezaei et al. (2024) identified a homozygous MAJIN splice donor variant, c.349+1G-T, in an Italian patient (1824) born to consanguineous parents. She had 2 androgenetic monospermic, p57-negative CHMs followed by secondary infertility, and at age 25 her ovarian volumes were markedly reduced (1.1 and 2.5 cm3). Her heterozygous carrier mother had had 1 triploid dispermic partial mole and irregular menses. The authors suggested that monoallelic variants in MAJIN, and perhaps in other meiotic genes, may underlie milder phenotypes.

Rezaei et al. (2024) also identified a homozygous SYCP2 splice acceptor variant (c.2530-2A-G) in an Egyptian patient (1954) with 4 CHMs and primary and secondary infertility. In another Egyptian patient (2136), who had 6 early miscarriages, 3 failed ICSI cycles, 1 hydatidiform mole, and low anti-Mullerian hormone, they found a homozygous MEIOB nonsense variant (c.814C-T; R272X). In a South Asian patient (1690) with 5 CHMs, they found a homozygous FOXL2 missense variant, c.500T-C (F167S), which they classified as a variant of uncertain significance. The authors stated that this single variant did not allow conclusions about a causal role for FOXL2. Saharan et al. (2026) identified a heterozygous FOXL2 variant, G187D, in a woman with recurrent hydatidiform moles. This variant had previously been reported in patients with premature ovarian insufficiency.

Rezaei et al. (2024) screened 310 patients with reproductive loss for monoallelic loss-of-function variants in 494 ovarian and meiosis genes. They found 77 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in 55 genes in 68 patients (21.8%). BRCA2 and ZP3 variants were significantly enriched compared with gnomAD. Genes in the DNA metabolism protein class accounted for 11% of the input gene list (53 of 482) but 25% of genes with validated variants in patients (14 of 57) (P = 0.003). The authors concluded that such monoallelic variants most likely confer genetic susceptibility.

### Molecular Genetics

In a Jordanian woman (patient 439) with 3 hydatidiform moles and 2 miscarriages, Rezaei et al. (2024) identified 2 variants in the KASH5 gene: c.1555C-T, resulting in an arg519-to-ter (R519X) substitution, and c.1604T-A, resulting in a leu535-to-gln (L535Q) substitution (NM_144688). Only R519X was inherited from her mother, so the 2 variants were most likely on different parental chromosomes. L535Q had previously been reported in the homozygous state in 2 infertile Qatari brothers with azoospermia.

In a woman with recurrent hydatidiform moles, Saharan et al. (2026) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a very rare homozygous variant in KASH5, arg196-to-his (R196H), and confirmed it by Sanger sequencing. The patient had recurrent gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, and her sister, who also had recurrent moles, died of metastatic GTN. The authors concluded that their findings further supported an association of KASH5 variants with recurrent hydatidiform mole.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Rezaei et al. (2024) compared 15 patients with androgenetic recurrent hydatidiform mole and biallelic pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in 9 genes with 70 patients who did not have such variants. The patients with biallelic variants had a more severe phenotype, with only 1 live birth and 62 pregnancy losses, compared with 39 live births and 198 pregnancy losses in the variant-negative group (P = 0.0007). ⚠︎

Saharan et al. (2026) reviewed published rates of gestational trophoblastic neoplasia in patients with recurrent hydatidiform moles and compared them across different genetic causes. They noted limitations in the current data that should be taken into account when interpreting overall risk.

### Pathogenesis

Rezaei et al. (2024) suggested that meiosis I defects affecting synapsis and recombination play a major role in the genesis of androgenetic complete moles. They also suggested that recurrent androgenetic complete moles may be a sign of accelerated ovarian aging or a mild form of premature ovarian insufficiency, which would explain why the risk rises with advanced maternal age.
