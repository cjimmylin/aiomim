### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Kruer syndrome (KRRS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the FBXO31 gene on chromosome 16.

### Description

Kruer syndrome is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder caused by a recurrent de novo missense mutation in FBXO31. Its core features are cerebral palsy with mixed hypotonia, spasticity, and dystonia; global developmental delay or intellectual disability; and speech impairment. Some patients have prominent neuropsychiatric features. Neuroimaging often shows a hypoplastic corpus callosum and white matter changes that predominate posteriorly (Jin et al., 2020; Dzinovic et al., 2021; Galaz-Montoya et al., 2026).

### Nomenclature

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) proposed the descriptive term 'autosomal dominant FBXO31-associated neurodevelopmental disorder.' With support from the FBXO31 Foundation, and consistent with the validating laboratory, they also proposed the eponym 'Kruer syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Jin et al. (2020) performed exome sequencing of 250 cerebral palsy (CP) parent-offspring trios. They described 2 unrelated patients, F218 and F699, who both had spastic diplegic CP, intellectual disability, expressive language disorder, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Patient F218 also had gut malrotation, constipation, cleft palate, and strabismus, and brain MRI showed normal morphology. Patient F699 had strabismus and severe constipation, and MRI showed ventricular dilation with a thin corpus callosum. In a later summary of these 2 patients, Dzinovic et al. (2021) listed F218 as a female aged 9 years and F699 as a male aged 10 years at last assessment. Both had global developmental delay and neonatal hypotonia. F218 had mild intellectual disability and F699 had moderate intellectual disability. F218's intestinal malrotation and midgut volvulus required small bowel resection. F699 had decreased fetal movements and mild intrauterine growth restriction prenatally.

Dzinovic et al. (2021) reported a 9-year-old Slovakian boy, the first child of unrelated healthy parents, with a complex nonprogressive movement disorder dominated by spasticity and dystonia. He had hypotonia and delayed milestones in the first months of life. He rolled over at 6 months, sat at 9 months, and walked with support at 20 months. He spoke single words at 12 months but lost speech at 18 months. At 2 years his hypotonia had converted to limb spasticity, more pronounced in the legs, and he had involuntary twisting and abnormal postures of all 4 limbs and sometimes the trunk. He was diagnosed with spastic-dystonic CP. At age 9 years he had generalized dystonia, spasticity that was more severe in the legs than the arms, hyperreflexia, clonus in both legs, an unsteady spastic-dystonic gait, and no expressive language. His intellectual disability was estimated as moderate. He had no distinctive craniofacial features. Brain MRI at age 3 years showed bilateral reduction of parietal and occipital white matter volume and global white matter dysmyelination. Lactate levels in blood and urine were slightly elevated once. Comparing him with the 2 patients of Jin et al. (2020), Dzinovic et al. (2021) found that all 3 had motor and/or speech delay, mild to moderate intellectual disability, abnormal speech, and spasticity that mainly affected the lower limbs.

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) re-evaluated the 3 published patients and described 4 additional probands, who were ascertained through advocacy networks, GeneMatcher, and clinician referral. The core features were CP (mixed hypotonia, spasticity, and dystonia), global developmental delay/intellectual disability, and speech impairment. Neuropsychiatric features were sometimes prominent and included attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, anxiety, stereotypies, autistic features, and behavioral dysregulation. Neuroimaging often showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum and white matter changes that predominated posteriorly, and 1 patient had gray matter heterotopias. The authors noted a subtle but consistent facial gestalt.

### Inheritance

Jin et al. (2020) and Dzinovic et al. (2021) found that the FBXO31 mutation in their patients had occurred de novo. Dzinovic et al. (2021) and Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) described the disorder as autosomal dominant.

### Molecular Genetics

Jin et al. (2020) performed whole-exome sequencing of 250 CP parent-offspring trios. In 2 unrelated patients they found the same de novo mutation in the FBXO31 gene, causing an asp334-to-asn (D334N) substitution. Sanger sequencing of both trios confirmed de novo occurrence. Asp334 is a conserved residue in the cyclin D1-binding pocket of FBXO31, and the substitution was predicted to change the surface electrostatic charge around the binding site. In fibroblasts from both patients, cyclin D levels were reduced compared with controls (P = 0.004). Jin et al. (2020) interpreted this as an apparent gain of function in cyclin D degradation.

In a 9-year-old Slovakian boy with spastic-dystonic CP, Dzinovic et al. (2021) used trio whole-exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.1000G-A transition in the FBXO31 gene, resulting in the D334N substitution. The boy was part of a cohort of about 950 exome-sequenced individuals with dystonic disorders. The mutation was absent from gnomAD and from in-house exomes, and no other potentially disease-relevant variants were found in his exome data.

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) found the recurrent de novo D334N mutation in FBXO31 in all 7 individuals they studied, who comprised 3 previously published patients and 4 additional probands.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jin et al. (2020) noted that a homozygous truncating mutation in FBXO31 had previously been reported in association with intellectual disability. They concluded that the de novo D334N mutation causes a phenotype distinct from the nonsyndromic intellectual disability associated with autosomal recessive truncating mutations. Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) likewise noted that biallelic loss-of-function variants in FBXO31 cause autosomal recessive intellectual disability.
