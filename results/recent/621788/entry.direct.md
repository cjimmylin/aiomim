### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Kruer syndrome (KRRS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the FBXO31 gene on chromosome 16.

### Description

Kruer syndrome is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder. Its core features are cerebral palsy with mixed hypotonia, spasticity, and dystonia; global developmental delay or intellectual disability; and speech impairment. Some patients have prominent neuropsychiatric features, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, anxiety, stereotypies, autistic features, and behavioral dysregulation. Neuroimaging often shows a hypoplastic corpus callosum and white matter changes that are worse posteriorly, and affected individuals share a subtle facial gestalt. The disorder has been caused by a recurrent de novo heterozygous FBXO31 missense mutation, asp334-to-asn (D334N) (Jin et al., 2020; Dzinovic et al., 2021; Galaz-Montoya et al., 2026).

### Nomenclature

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) proposed the descriptive term 'autosomal dominant FBXO31-associated neurodevelopmental disorder.' With support from the FBXO31 Foundation, and consistent with the validating laboratory, they also proposed the eponym 'Kruer syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Jin et al. (2020) performed whole-exome sequencing in 250 parent-offspring trios with cerebral palsy and identified 2 unrelated patients with spastic diplegic cerebral palsy. Both had intellectual disability, expressive language disorder, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. One patient (F218) had gut malrotation and constipation, cleft palate, and strabismus, and brain MRI showed normal morphology. The other patient (F699) had strabismus and severe constipation, and MRI showed ventricular dilation with a thin corpus callosum. The authors noted that this phenotype differed from the nonsyndromic intellectual disability previously reported with an autosomal recessive truncating FBXO31 mutation.

Dzinovic et al. (2021) reported a 9-year-old Slovakian boy, born to unrelated healthy parents, with a complex nonprogressive movement disorder dominated by spasticity and dystonia. In the first months of life he had muscular hypotonia and delayed milestones. He rolled over at 6 months, sat at 9 months, and walked with support at 20 months. He spoke single words at 12 months but lost the ability to speak at 18 months. At age 2 years his hypotonia had converted to limb spasticity, more pronounced in the lower extremities, with involuntary twisting and abnormal postures of all 4 limbs and occasionally the trunk. Spastic-dystonic cerebral palsy was diagnosed. Over the next 7 years his tone and movement abnormalities did not worsen significantly. At age 9 years he had generalized dystonia, spasticity (legs more than arms), hyperreflexia, clonus in both legs, and an unsteady spastic-dystonic gait. He had no expressive language and could follow 2-step commands. His intellectual disability was estimated as moderate, although formal IQ testing had not been done. He had no distinctive craniofacial features. Brain MRI at age 3 years showed reduced white matter volume in both parietal and occipital lobes, with global white matter dysmyelination. Lactate levels in blood and urine were slightly elevated on 1 occasion. Comparing their patient with the 2 cases of Jin et al. (2020), Dzinovic et al. (2021) found that all 3 had global developmental delay, neonatal hypotonia, mild to moderate intellectual disability, speech impairment, and spasticity mainly affecting the lower limbs, and all had been diagnosed with cerebral palsy. Patient F699 of Jin et al. (2020) had decreased fetal movements and mild intrauterine growth restriction, and patient F218 had a midgut volvulus that required small bowel resection.

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) re-evaluated the 3 published cases and ascertained 4 additional probands through advocacy networks, GeneMatcher, and clinician referral. All 7 shared a core neurodevelopmental profile: cerebral palsy with mixed hypotonia, spasticity, and dystonia; global developmental delay or intellectual disability; and speech impairment. Neuropsychiatric features were sometimes prominent and included attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, anxiety, stereotypies, autistic features, and behavioral dysregulation. Neuroimaging often showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum and white matter changes that were worse posteriorly, and 1 individual also had gray matter heterotopias. The authors noted a subtle but consistent facial gestalt. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

In all reported patients, the heterozygous FBXO31 mutation in Kruer syndrome arose de novo (Jin et al., 2020; Dzinovic et al., 2021; Galaz-Montoya et al., 2026). Dzinovic et al. (2021) and Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) described the disorder as autosomal dominant.

### Molecular Genetics

Jin et al. (2020) performed whole-exome sequencing in 250 parent-offspring trios with cerebral palsy and found the same de novo heterozygous mutation in the FBXO31 gene, an asp334-to-asn (D334N) substitution, in 2 unrelated patients. De novo occurrence was confirmed by Sanger sequencing of both trios. Asp334 is a conserved residue in the cyclin D-binding pocket of FBXO31. Fibroblasts from both patients had reduced cyclin D levels compared with controls, which the authors interpreted as an apparent gain of function in cyclin D degradation.

Dzinovic et al. (2021) studied a Slovakian boy with spastic-dystonic cerebral palsy, identified from a cohort of about 950 individuals with dystonic disorders. Trio whole-exome sequencing found a de novo heterozygous c.1000G-A transition in the FBXO31 gene, resulting in an asp334-to-asn (D334N) substitution. The variant was absent from gnomAD and in-house control exomes. No other potentially disease-relevant variants were found. The authors classified the variant as pathogenic and considered their report the first independent replication of this mutation.

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) found the recurrent de novo FBXO31 D334N mutation in all 7 individuals they studied, who included the 3 previously published patients and 4 newly ascertained probands.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jin et al. (2020) and Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) noted that biallelic loss-of-function or truncating FBXO31 mutations had previously been associated with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. Jin et al. (2020) considered the phenotype caused by the de novo D334N mutation to be distinct from that nonsyndromic intellectual disability. Dzinovic et al. (2021) stated that further studies are needed to determine whether monoallelic FBXO31 variants other than D334N cause disease. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 KRUER SYNDROME**  
FBXO31, ASP334ASN [c.1000G>A; p.Asp334Asn]

Jin et al. (2020) found a de novo heterozygous asp334-to-asn (D334N) mutation in the FBXO31 gene in 2 unrelated patients with spastic diplegic cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, expressive language disorder, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. The mutation was identified by whole-exome sequencing of parent-offspring trios. It alters a conserved residue in the cyclin D-binding pocket, and patient fibroblasts had reduced cyclin D levels.

Dzinovic et al. (2021) used trio whole-exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.1000G-A transition in the FBXO31 gene, resulting in an asp334-to-asn (D334N) substitution, in a 9-year-old Slovakian boy with spastic-dystonic cerebral palsy and moderate intellectual disability. The variant was absent from gnomAD and in-house exomes.

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) found the recurrent de novo D334N mutation in 7 individuals with Kruer syndrome: 3 previously reported patients and 4 newly ascertained probands.
