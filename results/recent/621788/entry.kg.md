### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Kruer syndrome (KRRS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the FBXO31 gene (609102) on chromosome 16q24.2.

### Description

Kruer syndrome is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder with prominent motor dysfunction. It is caused by a recurrent de novo missense mutation in FBXO31. Dzinovic et al. (2021) suggested that its emerging hallmarks were delayed psychomotor development, dysfunction of both the pyramidal and extrapyramidal systems (spasticity and dystonia), impaired expressive and receptive language, and intellectual disability. Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) described a core phenotype of cerebral palsy, global developmental delay/intellectual disability, and speech impairment, with neuropsychiatric features and a subtle facial gestalt.

### Nomenclature

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) proposed the descriptive term 'autosomal dominant FBXO31-associated neurodevelopmental disorder' and the eponym 'Kruer syndrome' for the disorder caused by the recurrent de novo FBXO31 asp334-to-asn (D334N) mutation.

### Clinical Features

Jin et al. (2020) described 2 unrelated patients (F218 and F699) from a cohort of 250 cerebral palsy trios. Both had spastic diplegic cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, expressive language disorder, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Patient F218 also had gut malrotation, constipation, cleft palate, and strabismus, and brain MRI was normal. Patient F699 had strabismus and severe constipation, and MRI showed ventricular dilation with a thin corpus callosum.

Dzinovic et al. (2021) reported a 9-year-old Slovakian boy, the first child of unrelated healthy parents, who had a complex, non-progressive movement disorder dominated by spasticity and dystonia. In infancy he had hypotonia and delayed development. He spoke single words at 12 months but lost speech at 18 months. By age 2 years his hypotonia had converted to limb spasticity, worse in the legs, and he had dystonic posturing of all 4 limbs. A working diagnosis of spastic-dystonic cerebral palsy was made. At age 9 years he had generalized dystonia, spasticity, hyperreflexia, leg clonus, an unsteady spastic-dystonic gait, no expressive language, and moderate intellectual disability. He had no dysmorphic features. Brain MRI at age 3 years showed reduced parietal and occipital white matter volume and global white matter dysmyelination.

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) reevaluated 3 previously published individuals and described 4 additional probands with the recurrent de novo FBXO31 D334N mutation. The core phenotype comprised cerebral palsy (mixed hypotonia, spasticity, and dystonia), global developmental delay/intellectual disability, and speech impairment. Neuropsychiatric features were sometimes prominent and included ADHD, anxiety, stereotypies, autistic features, and behavioral dysregulation. The authors noted a subtle but consistent facial gestalt. Neuroimaging often showed a hypoplastic corpus callosum and posterior-predominant white matter changes, and 1 individual also had gray matter heterotopias.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous FBXO31 mutation that causes Kruer syndrome occurred de novo in all affected individuals reported by Jin et al. (2020), Dzinovic et al. (2021), and Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026). Dzinovic et al. (2021) concluded that the disorder is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental syndrome.

### Heterogeneity

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) noted that biallelic loss-of-function FBXO31 variants cause autosomal recessive intellectual disability. This disorder is distinct from the autosomal dominant phenotype caused by the recurrent de novo D334N mutation.

### Molecular Genetics

Jin et al. (2020) performed whole-exome sequencing of 250 cerebral palsy parent-offspring trios. They found the same de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the FBXO31 gene, asp334-to-asn (D334N), in 2 unrelated patients. Sanger sequencing of the mothers, fathers, and probands confirmed that the mutation had arisen de novo. Asp334 is a conserved residue in the FBXO31 binding pocket and is thought to mediate hydrogen bonding to cyclin D1. Structural modeling predicted that the mutation changes the surface electrostatic charge around the cyclin D1-binding site. Fibroblasts from both patients had less cyclin D than control fibroblasts (P = 0.004).

In a Slovakian boy with spastic-dystonic cerebral palsy, Dzinovic et al. (2021) used trio whole-exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.1000G-A transition in the FBXO31 gene, resulting in an asp334-to-asn (D334N) substitution. This was the same mutation previously reported in 2 unrelated patients with spastic diplegic cerebral palsy. The mutation was absent from gnomAD and from in-house exomes. It affects a conserved residue in the domain that interacts with the substrate cyclin D1, and it was classified as pathogenic. No other potentially disease-relevant variants were found.

Galaz-Montoya et al. (2026) reported 7 individuals with Kruer syndrome, all of whom carried the recurrent de novo heterozygous FBXO31 c.1000G-A (D334N) mutation. They concluded that this mutation leads to a recognizable neurodevelopmental syndrome and recommended including FBXO31 in diagnostic algorithms for cerebral palsy and neurodevelopmental disorders.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jin et al. (2020) concluded that the de novo FBXO31 D334N mutation causes a phenotype distinct from the nonsyndromic intellectual disability previously reported with an autosomal recessive homozygous truncating FBXO31 mutation. They suggested that their findings likely represent a phenotypic expansion.

Dzinovic et al. (2021) compared their patient with the 2 patients previously reported with the same mutation. All 3 had motor and/or speech delay, mild to moderate intellectual disability, neonatal hypotonia, and lower-limb-predominant spasticity, and each had been diagnosed with cerebral palsy. Only their patient had dystonia. Other features were each found in 1 or 2 of the 3 patients: congenital malformations (cleft palate and intestinal malrotation in 1), ADHD (2), strabismus or esotropia (2), and brain MRI abnormalities.

### Pathogenesis

Jin et al. (2020) found that fibroblasts from both patients carrying the FBXO31 D334N mutation had less cyclin D than control fibroblasts (P = 0.004). They concluded that the mutation affects the cyclin D interaction site and causes an apparent gain of function in cyclin D degradation.
