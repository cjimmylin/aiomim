### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene (602242) on chromosome 19q13.

### Description

Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (FHH3) is a disorder of extracellular calcium homeostasis. It is marked by elevated serum calcium and reduced urinary calcium excretion. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that FHH1 and FHH2 usually have normal PTH levels, whereas FHH3 is characterized by increased serum PTH concentrations, hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia. FHH is genetically heterogeneous: FHH1 maps to 3q21.1, FHH2 to 19p, and FHH3 to 19q13 (Nesbit et al., 2010).

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) called the familial benign hypercalcemia (FBH) in a kindred from Oklahoma the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok).

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) described a large kindred with familial benign hypercalcemia. Of 46 members studied across 5 generations (ages 1.5 to 91 years), 19 were affected. In the 15 hypercalcemic subjects examined biochemically, serum immunoreactive PTH was measured in 3 separate immunoassays. Levels rose to supranormal values by about age 30 years and appeared to increase further after that. Serum alkaline phosphatase and creatinine were normal, but inorganic phosphate was lower than in unaffected kindred members. Of 5 affected adults older than 40 years who were studied radiographically, 3 had changes suggestive of osteomalacia. In a 51-year-old affected woman, iliac crest biopsy after tetracycline labeling confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. The authors suggested that the disorder may not be totally benign and that osteomalacia, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia, can develop in adulthood.

Trump et al. (1995) studied a 5-generation kindred from Oklahoma, USA, with 19 affected members, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected members. In this family, familial benign (hypocalciuric) hypercalcemia occurred together with a developmental elevation in serum PTH levels.

Hannan et al. (2010) restudied the Oklahoma kindred and investigated 40 members from 5 generations. They diagnosed the disorder from hypercalcemia together with a low calcium/creatinine clearance ratio (less than 0.01). Serum PTH was elevated in some affected individuals older than 30 years, and 3 affected adults older than 40 years had evidence of osteomalacia.

Nesbit et al. (2010) studied a second kindred, in which the proband was a 43-year-old woman. Her corrected serum calcium was 2.74 mmol/l (normal, 2.15-2.55), her serum PTH was 47 pg/ml (normal, 10-65), and her urinary calcium clearance:creatinine clearance ratio was 0.006. She had no CASR mutation in the coding region or splice sites. Of 24 members from 3 generations of her kindred, 16 were hypercalcemic, with normal or elevated serum PTH and mild hypophosphatemia. The authors considered these features consistent with FHH3.

In their series, Nesbit et al. (2013) reported that patients with AP2S1 mutations were aged from 8 days to 78 years. They had elevated serum calcium and PTH and reduced urinary calcium excretion. In the Oklahoma kindred, mean serum calcium was 2.88 mmol/l and mean phosphate was 0.77 mmol/l.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese female infant with FHH3. Her hypercalcemia was found on a routine laboratory test done for poor weight gain on breast feeding. At 49 days of age, her adjusted serum calcium was 13.1 mg/dL and her intact PTH was 27 pg/mL. Her urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg, indicating hypercalciuria rather than hypocalciuria. There was no evidence of hyperparathyroidism, a PTHrP-producing neoplasm, or vitamin D excess.

Howles et al. (2016) described 3 unrelated symptomatic probands with FHH3. The first was a 34-year-old woman with a 12-year history of hypercalcemia. She had fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. The second was a 22-year-old man with hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. The third was a 52-year-old woman with an approximately 20-year history of hypercalcemia. She had headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy.

### Inheritance

Nesbit et al. (2013) found heterozygous AP2S1 mutations in FHH patients and concluded that this was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. They noted that FHH1, FHH2, and FHH3 are all autosomal dominant disorders. Fujisawa et al. (2013) described the AP2S1 mutation in their patient as presumably de novo.

### Mapping

In the Oklahoma kindred, Trump et al. (1995) tested 17 polymorphic markers from candidate regions: the FBH loci on 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3, the MEN1 region on 11q13, and the PTH region on 11p15. Linkage to all of these regions was excluded (lod scores less than -2.00 at theta = 0.05 to 0.25). Analysis of multipoint crossovers and the LINKMAP program confirmed the exclusion.

Lloyd et al. (1999) carried out a genomewide search in the Oklahoma family using microsatellite polymorphisms. They found linkage to 8 loci on chromosome 19q13, with a maximum lod score of 6.67 at a recombination fraction of 0.00 for D19S606. Recombinants placed the locus in an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866.

Hannan et al. (2010) performed linkage analysis in 34 informative members of the Oklahoma kindred, 19 of them affected, using 17 microsatellite markers and 7 SNPs. They confirmed linkage to chromosome 19q13, with a peak 2-point lod score of 6.92 at theta = 0 for D19S606. Recombinants in 3 individuals refined the locus to a 4.1-Mb interval (less than 10 cM) flanked by D19S112 and rs245111. This interval contained 135 protein-coding genes and 24 predicted genes. The authors also examined the region of mouse chromosome 7 that is syntenic to the interval. It contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs genes), but BLAST analysis found no human or primate homologs. The human syntenic region (22.0 Mb) and the mouse region (39.3 Mb) shared 2 conserved segments, which were inverted in man. An 11.6-Mb mouse region containing 35 genes, including the 4 Gprc2a-rs genes, was absent from the human genome. The authors concluded that FHH3 cannot be attributed to Gprc2a-rs abnormalities.

In a second FHH3 kindred, Nesbit et al. (2010) found that the disorder cosegregated with microsatellite and SNP markers from chromosome 19q13.3, with a peak lod score of 5.98 at theta = 0.0 with D19S412. Analysis of recombinants placed the locus in a 3.46-Mb interval between SNP rs1990932 (centromeric) and D19S604 (telomeric). Nesbit et al. (2013) stated that this 3.46-Mb interval contains about 110 genes.

### Heterogeneity

The Oklahoma variant was not linked to markers that segregate with FBH, MEN1, or PTH. Trump et al. (1995) therefore concluded that their results indicated further genetic heterogeneity and a third locus for FBH. Lloyd et al. (1999) noted that linkage of the Oklahoma family to the CaSR region on 3q and to the 19p13.3 FBH locus had been excluded. They suggested that a further CaSR or a mediator of calcium homeostasis is involved.

Nesbit et al. (2010) noted that FHH1, caused by CASR mutations, accounts for more than 65% of FHH patients. They suggested that FHH3 may explain the disorder in FHH patients without CASR mutations. Nesbit et al. (2013) reported that about 65% of FHH patients have FHH1, caused by CASR loss-of-function mutations, and that AP2S1 mutations accounted for more than 20% of the remaining cases.

### Molecular Genetics

Lloyd et al. (1999) sequenced the calmodulin III gene, which lies within the 19q13 interval, in 1 affected member of the Oklahoma family. Sequencing of the coding region, the 5-prime UTR, and part of the promoter showed no abnormalities. The authors concluded that this gene is unlikely to be involved.

Hannan et al. (2010) sequenced the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries of 12 candidate genes from the interval in an affected member of the Oklahoma kindred. The genes were chosen for renal and/or parathyroid expression and included PTH2, PTGIR, GNG8, EMP3, SPHK2, TULP2, NUCB1, HRC, RCN3, CYTH2, RRAS, and ATF5. No abnormalities were found, and RT-PCR excluded splicing defects. These genes were therefore considered unlikely to cause FHH3.

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on 1 affected individual from each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds, one from Oklahoma and one from Northern Ireland. Within the 3.46-Mb FHH3 interval on 19q13.3, the 2 patients shared only 1 variant: a C-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, predicting an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant. Restriction enzyme analysis showed that it cosegregated with the disorder in members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. The authors then sequenced AP2S1 in 50 unrelated FHH patients (23 males and 27 females) in whom CASR mutations had been excluded. They found 11 heterozygous missense mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 were R15C, 3 were R15H, and 4 were R15L. They concluded that AP2S1 arg15 mutations may cause FHH in more than 20% of patients without CASR mutations. In functional studies, all 3 mutations decreased the sensitivity of CaSR-expressing cells to extracellular calcium (see PATHOGENESIS). ⚠︎

Fujisawa et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous arg15-to-leu (R15L) mutation in the AP2S1 gene in a Japanese infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria. The authors described the mutation as presumably de novo. No pathologic mutation was found in CASR or GNA11.

Howles et al. (2016) identified heterozygous AP2S1 mutations in 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic FHH3. One proband carried the R15C mutation, one carried R15H, and one carried R15L.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found that patients with the R15C, R15H, and R15L mutations had similar elevations in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium. This indicated no correlation between mutation and clinical phenotype.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) concluded that the absence of hypocalciuria does not necessarily argue against an AP2S1 mutation. They suggested that their patient's early infantile age played a role in her hypercalciuria.

### Pathogenesis

Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that arg15 is conserved across AP2-sigma-2 orthologs and in human AP sigma paralogs. Crystal structure analysis showed that arg15 forms key contacts with a glutamine at position -4 of the dileucine motifs of cargo proteins. The authors predicted that the R15C and R15L substitutions would reduce affinity for the dileucine motif, and they noted that CaSR contains a C-terminal dileucine motif (RHQPLL, residues 1009-1014).

Nesbit et al. (2013) expressed mutant AP2S1 in HEK293 cells stably expressing CaSR. All 3 mutants (R15C, R15H, and R15L) shifted the concentration-response curves to the right, with a higher EC50 for extracellular calcium than wild type (2.73-2.90 mM vs 2.53 mM). This indicated decreased sensitivity to extracellular calcium. The R15C mutant raised the EC50 above that of vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. Cell-surface CaSR was measured by intact-cell ELISA. After stimulation with extracellular calcium, cells transfected with wild-type AP2S1 had a greater reduction in surface CaSR than cells transfected with the R15C mutant, indicating reduced CaSR endocytosis with the mutant. In separate experiments, replacing the CaSR dileucine motif (Leu-Leu1013-1014 to Ala-Ala) also raised the EC50, indicating that the motif is functionally significant.

### Diagnosis

McMurtry et al. (1992) pointed out that, in their kindred, FBH in adults can be especially difficult to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism because serum PTH levels may be elevated. They suggested that the kindred might represent an unusual variant of FBH.

### Clinical Management

Fujisawa et al. (2013) treated their infant patient with a low-calcium formula containing 2.6 mg/dL of calcium. She showed catch-up growth, and both her serum calcium and her urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio fell. Feeding a 2:1 mixture of low-calcium and standard formula then kept her serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without markedly raising serum PTH. The authors concluded that low-calcium formula is worth trying in infants with FHH.

Howles et al. (2016) studied cells expressing the calcium-sensing receptor together with the Cys15, His15, or Leu15 AP2-sigma mutants. Cinacalcet (10 nM) corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and MAPK responses to extracellular calcium in these cells. This indicated that allosteric modulation of CASR can correct the loss of function caused by AP2S1 mutations. The authors treated their 3 FHH3 probands with oral cinacalcet (30 to 60 mg daily) over 33 to 45 months. Serum calcium fell by more than 20%, and symptoms abated. Serum phosphate increased and PTH decreased, both remaining within the normal range. No nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia developed, although the authors noted that long-term surveillance would be required.

### Population Genetics

Nesbit et al. (2013) found 3 different mutations in 13 unrelated probands, all affecting arg15. They noted that arg15 is significantly conserved in 5,400 human exomes (p less than 10(-10)) and concluded that this residue is likely a mutational hotspot.
