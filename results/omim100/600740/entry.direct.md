### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type III (HHC3) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the AP2S1 gene on chromosome 19q13.

### Description

Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type 3 (FHH3) is an autosomal dominant disorder of extracellular calcium homeostasis that affects the parathyroids, kidneys, and bone. Like the other types of FHH, it is marked by lifelong high serum calcium with inappropriately low urinary calcium excretion. Nesbit et al. (2013) noted that FHH3 also has distinctive features: increased serum parathyroid hormone (PTH), hypophosphatemia, and osteomalacia. Howles et al. (2016) described type 3 as the most clinically severe variant of familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia.

### Nomenclature

Trump et al. (1995) designated this form of familial benign hypercalcemia the Oklahoma variant, FBH(Ok), after the kindred in which it was first identified. Hannan et al. (2010) noted that it had also been called familial benign hypocalciuric hypercalcemia type 3 (FBHH3). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

McMurtry et al. (1992) studied a large kindred with familial benign hypercalcemia. After the proposita was identified, 46 kindred members from 5 generations, aged 1.5 to 91 years, were surveyed biochemically and/or by chart review, and 19 were affected. In 15 hypercalcemic subjects examined biochemically, serum immunoreactive PTH, measured in 3 separate immunoassays, became supranormal by about age 30 years and appeared to rise further after that. These subjects had normal serum alkaline phosphatase activity and creatinine, but their inorganic phosphate levels were lower than those of unaffected kindred members. Of 5 affected adults older than 40 years who were studied radiographically, 3 had changes suggestive of osteomalacia. In one of them, a 51-year-old woman, iliac crest biopsy after tetracycline labeling confirmed defective skeletal mineralization. McMurtry et al. (1992) concluded that in this kindred the disorder could be especially hard to distinguish from primary hyperparathyroidism in adults, and that it might not be entirely benign, because osteomalacia, perhaps due to mild hypophosphatemia, could develop in adulthood.

Hannan et al. (2010) restudied this Oklahoma kindred. Affected status was defined by hypercalcemia with a calcium clearance to creatinine clearance ratio below 0.01. Some affected members older than 30 years had elevated serum PTH, and 3 adults older than 40 years had evidence of osteomalacia.

Nesbit et al. (2010) reported a second kindred. The proband, a 43-year-old woman without a CASR mutation, had a corrected serum calcium of 2.74 mmol/liter (normal, 2.15-2.55 mmol/liter), serum PTH of 47 pg/ml (normal, 10-65 pg/ml), and a urinary calcium clearance to creatinine clearance ratio of 0.006. Of 24 relatives from 3 generations who were studied, 16 were hypercalcemic, with normal or elevated serum PTH and mild hypophosphatemia, which the authors considered consistent with FHH3. Nesbit et al. (2013) later referred to this kindred as FHH3NI. ⚠︎

Fujisawa et al. (2013) reported a Japanese female infant whose hypercalcemia was found on routine laboratory testing for poor weight gain on breast feeding. At 49 days of age, her serum calcium (adjusted by Payne's formula) was 13.1 mg/dL, intact PTH was 27 pg/mL, and the urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio was 1.29 mg/mg. There was no evidence of hyperparathyroidism, a PTHrP-producing neoplasm, or vitamin D excess. Fujisawa et al. (2013) concluded that the absence of hypocalciuria does not necessarily argue against an AP2S1 mutation, and suggested that her early infantile age may have contributed to the hypercalciuria.

Howles et al. (2016) reported 3 unrelated probands with FHH3: - A 34-year-old woman with a 12-year history of hypercalcemia, who presented with fatigue, headaches, and persistent generalized aches that did not resolve after parathyroidectomy. - A 22-year-old man who presented with hypercalcemia, fatigue, and generalized rib pain. - A 52-year-old woman with about a 20-year history of hypercalcemia, who presented with headaches, abdominal pain, vomiting, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain that did not resolve after pamidronate infusion or parathyroidectomy.

### Biochemical Features

Nesbit et al. (2013) summarized biochemical findings in 13 families or patients with AP2S1 mutations, whose ages at diagnosis ranged from 8 days to 78 years. In the Oklahoma kindred (13 affected females and 3 affected males), mean serum calcium was 2.88 mmol/l, mean serum phosphate 0.77 mmol/l, and mean urinary calcium to creatinine clearance ratio 0.003. In the FHH3NI kindred (11 affected females and 5 affected males), the corresponding means were 2.72 mmol/l, 0.95 mmol/l, and 0.006. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The Oklahoma kindred reported by McMurtry et al. (1992) and Trump et al. (1995) showed transmission of familial benign hypercalcemia across 5 generations. Nesbit et al. (2013) found heterozygous AP2S1 mutations in affected individuals, which they considered consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance of FHH3.

### Mapping

Trump et al. (1995) studied the Oklahoma kindred, which included 19 affected members, 2 obligate carriers, and 20 unaffected members. Using 17 polymorphic markers, they excluded linkage to 4 candidate regions: 3q21-q24 and 19p13.3 (the known FBH loci), 11q13 (the MEN1 locus), and 11p15 (the PTH locus). LOD scores were below -2.00 at theta = 0.05-0.25. Multipoint crossover analysis and the LINKMAP program confirmed the exclusion, indicating a third locus for FBH.

Lloyd et al. (1999) performed a genomewide search in the Oklahoma kindred and found linkage between the disorder and 8 loci on chromosome 19q13. The highest LOD score was 6.67 at a recombination fraction of 0.00, with D19S606. Recombinants placed the locus in an interval of less than 12 cM flanked by D19S908 and D19S866. The calmodulin III gene lies within this interval, but sequencing its coding region, 5-prime UTR, and part of its promoter in an affected individual found no abnormalities.

Hannan et al. (2010) studied 24 polymorphic loci in the Oklahoma kindred and found a peak LOD score of 6.92 at 0% recombination with D19S606. Recombinants in 3 individuals narrowed the FBHH3 locus to a 4.1-Mb region flanked centromerically by D19S112 and telomerically by rs245111. The syntenic region on mouse chromosome 7 contains 4 Casr-related sequences (Gprc2a-rs), but no human homologs were found. Comparison of the 22.0-Mb human region with the 39.3-Mb mouse region showed 2 evolutionarily conserved segments that were inverted in humans, and an 11.6-Mb mouse segment containing the 4 Gprc2a-rs genes was absent from the human genome. The authors also sequenced the coding regions and intron-exon boundaries of 12 other genes in the interval that are expressed in the parathyroids and/or kidneys, in an affected individual. They found no abnormalities, and RT-PCR excluded splicing defects.

In a second kindred, Nesbit et al. (2010) found cosegregation of FHH with microsatellite and SNP loci on chromosome 19q13.3. The peak LOD score was 5.98 at 0% recombination with D19S412. Recombinants mapped the disorder to a 3.46-Mbp interval flanked centromerically by rs1990932 and telomerically by D19S604.

### Heterogeneity

Trump et al. (1995) excluded linkage of the Oklahoma variant to the FBH loci on 3q and 19p13.3, indicating further genetic heterogeneity of familial benign hypercalcemia. Hannan et al. (2010) and Nesbit et al. (2010) described 3 genetically distinct types of FHH: type 1, caused by CASR mutations on 3q21.1; type 2, which maps to 19p; and type 3, which maps to 19q13. Nesbit et al. (2010) stated that FHH1 accounts for more than 65% of patients. Howles et al. (2016) stated that type 2 is caused by GNA11 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

Nesbit et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing on one affected individual from each of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds: the Oklahoma kindred and the FHH3NI kindred. Within the 3.46-Mbp FHH3 interval on 19q13.3, both patients carried the same C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, predicting an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution. Dideoxynucleotide sequencing confirmed the change. Restriction enzyme analysis showed that it cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. The authors then sequenced AP2S1 in 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. They found 11 heterozygous missense mutations, all affecting arg15: 4 R15C, 3 arg15-to-his (R15H), and 4 arg15-to-leu (R15L). Based on this, they estimated that arg15 AP2S1 mutations may cause FHH in more than 20% of patients without CASR mutations. Arg15 is conserved in all AP2-sigma-2 orthologs and in all human AP sigma paralogs. In the crystal structure of AP2, it forms key contacts with dileucine-based motifs of cargo proteins. Functional studies in HEK293 cells that stably expressed CASR gave 3 main results: - Expression of each of the 3 mutant proteins shifted the concentration-response curves to the right and significantly raised the EC50, indicating that the cells were less sensitive to extracellular calcium. - The R15C mutant reduced CASR endocytosis compared with wildtype. - Mutating the C-terminal dileucine motif of CASR also reduced the receptor's sensitivity to extracellular calcium.

Fujisawa et al. (2013) studied a Japanese infant with hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria. No pathogenic mutation was found in CASR or GNA11, but she carried a heterozygous R15L mutation in AP2S1, presumably de novo.

Howles et al. (2016) reported 3 previously unreported, unrelated probands with FHH3. Each carried a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation: one R15C, one R15H, and one R15L.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Nesbit et al. (2013) found no correlation between the specific mutation and the clinical phenotype. Patients with the R15C, R15H, and R15L mutations had similar increases in serum calcium and PTH and similar reductions in urinary calcium excretion.

### Clinical Management

Fujisawa et al. (2013) treated their infant with a low-calcium formula containing 2.6 mg/dL calcium. She showed catch-up growth, and both her serum calcium and urinary calcium-to-creatinine ratio fell. Feeding with a 2:1 mixture of low-calcium and standard formula kept serum calcium at about 12 mg/dL without markedly raising serum PTH.

Howles et al. (2016) found in vitro that 10 nM cinacalcet corrected the abnormal intracellular calcium signaling and MAPK responses of cells expressing the R15C, R15H, or R15L mutant proteins. They then gave oral cinacalcet, 30 to 60 mg daily, to 3 probands with FHH3 for 33 to 45 months. Serum calcium fell by more than 20% and symptoms abated. Serum phosphate rose and serum PTH fell, but both stayed within the normal range. No patient developed nausea, vomiting, or hypocalcemia, although the authors noted that long-term surveillance would be needed.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 HYPOCALCIURIC HYPERCALCEMIA, FAMILIAL, TYPE III**  
AP2S1, ARG15CYS [p.Arg15Cys; R15C]

Nesbit et al. (2013) found a heterozygous C-to-T transition in exon 2 of the AP2S1 gene, resulting in an arg15-to-cys (R15C) substitution, in affected members of 2 unrelated FHH3 kindreds: the Oklahoma kindred and the FHH3NI kindred. The mutation was first found by exome sequencing and confirmed by dideoxynucleotide sequencing. Restriction enzyme analysis showed that it cosegregated with the disorder in 32 affected and 23 unaffected members from 4 generations of the 2 kindreds. The same mutation was also present in 4 of 50 unrelated FHH patients without CASR mutations. In HEK293 cells stably expressing CASR, the R15C mutant had 3 effects: - It increased the EC50 for extracellular calcium. - The EC50 was significantly higher than with vector alone, suggesting a possible dominant-negative effect. - It reduced calcium-stimulated CASR endocytosis compared with wildtype.

Howles et al. (2016) reported 3 unrelated probands with symptomatic FHH3, one of whom carried a heterozygous AP2S1 mutation resulting in R15C. Cinacalcet corrected the signaling defect in vitro and reduced serum calcium and symptoms in the patients.
