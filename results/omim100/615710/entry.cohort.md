## MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME; MTCHRS

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene (612659) on chromosome 6q22.1.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder of neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal and duodenal atresia, and gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia (Mitchell et al., 2004). Autopsies have shown a normal-appearing exocrine pancreas with no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin (Smith et al., 2010). Most reported patients died in infancy (Mitchell et al., 2004). Some patients with RFX6 mutations have had diabetes starting in childhood rather than the neonatal period (Sansbury et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Smith et al. (2010) proposed the name Mitchell-Riley syndrome, after the two clinicians who first described it. They distinguished it from Martinez-Frias syndrome, noting that the original Martinez-Frias patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias without diabetes and that no RFX6 mutation was found in one of them. Other authors have reported patients with neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresia, and gallbladder aplasia under the Martinez-Frias syndrome name (Martinovici et al., 2010). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Martínez-Frías et al. (1992) studied 2 sibs born to consanguineous parents. Both had a multiple congenital anomaly pattern of low birthweight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. A search of the Spanish Collaborative Study of Congenital Malformations (ECEMC) data files identified 3 other unrelated infants with intestinal atresias, hypospadias, and low birth weight. The authors suggested that these infants might have a milder form of the same syndrome.

Annerén et al. (1998) described a lethal syndrome of intrauterine growth retardation, intra- and extrahepatic biliary atresia, and esophageal and duodenal atresia.

Gentile and Fiorente (1999) reported a patient with esophageal, duodenal, rectoanal, and biliary atresia, intestinal malrotation, a malformed or hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. They presented this as further evidence of a new distinct syndrome. ⚠︎

Mitchell et al. (2004) reported 5 infants from 3 families: 2 sib pairs, 1 of them born to consanguineous parents, and an isolated case. The infants had neonatal diabetes, a hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder. Four children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. One patient had a milder form and was surviving free of insulin. On pancreatic immunohistochemistry there were a few scattered chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but no insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin at all.

Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) described a female infant born to consanguineous parents. She had low birth weight with intrauterine growth retardation, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, a hypoplastic pancreas, and intestinal malrotation, and she died 60 days after birth. Her chromosomes at the 550- to 600-band level were normal (46,XX). The authors considered that she confirmed the syndrome previously described by Martínez-Frías et al. (1992). ⚠︎

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient born to consanguineous parents who had neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis. This was the first reported case with neonatal diabetes and no demonstrable structural abnormality of the pancreas, which the authors took as showing that a deficit in pancreatic function is involved. They proposed that earlier cases with overlapping features, such as malrotation, biliary atresia, and pancreatic hypoplasia, including those described by Martínez-Frías et al. (1992), might be variations of the same syndrome.

Martinovici et al. (2010) reported a newborn of consanguineous parents with intrauterine growth retardation, intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus, which they diagnosed as Martinez-Frias syndrome. The infant also had neonatal hemochromatosis. The authors suggested that neonatal hemochromatosis may be part of the syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) described infants with neonatal diabetes and small bowel obstruction due to bowel atresia. Some had a reduced pancreatic size, but none had exocrine enzyme deficiency. The infants also had a hypoplastic gallbladder and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement. Autopsies of 2 cases were reported: their proband 1, a girl born to consanguineous parents, and case 3 of a previous report. Both showed a normal-appearing exocrine pancreas with clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. Probands 1, 4, and 5 died in the first few months of life. Proband 2 was alive at age 4.5 years. Proband 3 was alive at age 9 years and was intermittently off insulin.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 Turkish double first cousins, both aged 9 years at last report. Case 1 was a girl born at 32 weeks' gestation with a birth weight of 1.65 kg (42nd centile). She had duodenal atresia, a jejunal web, and Meckel diverticulum. She was hyperglycemic at 2 years of age and presented with diabetic ketoacidosis at 3 years, with low insulin and C-peptide and no islet autoantibodies. She also had gastrointestinal bleeding, gastric heterotopia in the small bowel, and an absent gallbladder, and her liver function tests were normal. Case 2 was a boy born at 34 weeks' gestation with a birth weight of 1.70 kg (7th centile). He had duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation. He had asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia at 5 years of age, and diabetes was diagnosed at 6 years after he presented with hyperglycemia (16.0 mmol/l); he had no islet autoantibodies. He had a history of chronic iron deficiency anemia. No structural abnormality of the hepatobiliary tract was found, and his liver function tests were always normal.

Sansbury et al. (2015) also reviewed 8 previously reported cases. All had duodenal atresia, 7 of 8 had gallbladder agenesis, and 7 of 8 had intrahepatic cholestasis. Five of the 8 children had died, all within the first year of life.

### Inheritance

Martínez-Frías et al. (1992) suggested autosomal recessive inheritance because the syndrome occurred in 2 sibs born to consanguineous parents. Annerén et al. (1998) also considered their lethal syndrome to be autosomal recessive. Mitchell et al. (2004) concluded that the combination of neonatal diabetes, pancreatic hypoplasia, intestinal atresia, and gallbladder hypoplasia probably represents a new autosomal recessive syndrome; they based this on affected sib pairs, one of them born to consanguineous parents. Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) and Chappell et al. (2008) likewise considered the disorder to be autosomal recessive. ⚠︎

Autosomal recessive inheritance was confirmed by Smith et al. (2010), who found homozygous or compound heterozygous RFX6 mutations in affected probands; all mutations were inherited from carrier parents. In the family reported by Sansbury et al. (2015), heterozygous carriers of the RFX6 mutations, including several relatives with adult-onset diabetes, had C-peptide and HbA1c levels no different from those of noncarriers. The authors concluded that their data did not support heterozygosity as a risk factor for diabetes. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) carried out homozygosity mapping in 2 probands, the offspring of first cousins and of second cousins, respectively. They found 3 shared regions of homozygosity totaling 24 Mb, on 2p15-p16, 6q21-q22, and 6q23. Of the 194 genes in these regions, only RFX6 had pancreas-enriched expression; it lay in the largest segment, on 6q21-q22.

### Molecular Genetics

Mitchell et al. (2004) studied 1 affected infant from their consanguineous family. They found no duplication or uniparental isodisomy of PLAGL1 at 6q24, no contiguous gene deletion involving the glucokinase gene, and no mutation in the coding sequences or splice sites of PDX1.

In their patient, Chappell et al. (2008) found no mutations in genes with a recognized role in monogenic diabetes, including KCNJ11, ABCC8, GCK, IPF1, HNF1B, NEUROD1, and TCF7L2. They also found none in HNF6, a candidate gene involved in hepatobiliary and pancreatic development.

Smith et al. (2010) used targeted capture and deep sequencing of the exons in the shared homozygous regions in proband 2. The only novel nonsynonymous variant they found was a homozygous S217P mutation in the RFX6 gene. Direct sequencing of RFX6 then identified mutations in 5 of 6 probands, and all mutations were inherited from carrier parents. Probands 1, 4, and 5, who died in the first few months of life, were homozygous for an intron 2 splice donor mutation (IVS2+2T-C), an out-of-frame deletion in exon 7, and an R181Q missense mutation, respectively. Proband 3 was compound heterozygous for splice-site mutations in intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G) and intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G). The R181Q mutation completely abolished DNA binding, whereas S217P only modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation. No properly spliced RFX6 transcript was detected in autopsy pancreas from the proband homozygous for the intron 2 splice donor mutation. No RFX6 mutation was found in proband 6. Proband 6's DNA was not available; the parents carried no point mutation in RFX6 or NEUROG3, and long-range PCR showed no RFX6 deletion. The authors could not exclude a de novo mutation but considered this case most likely a phenocopy.

In the 2 Turkish double first cousins with childhood-onset diabetes, Sansbury et al. (2015) found compound heterozygosity for 2 nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene: c.2176C-T (R726X) in exon 17 and c.2596C-T (R866X) in exon 18. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X and the mothers for R866X, and both mutations were inherited from the grandmothers. R726X was predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay. R866X lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary and was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay, producing a truncated protein that keeps the full DNA-binding domain and possibly some function. The authors suggested that this might explain the milder phenotype and later onset of diabetes.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) found that homozygous severe RFX6 mutations were associated with death in early infancy and milder genotypes with survival. ⚠︎

Sansbury et al. (2015) reviewed 8 previously reported molecularly confirmed cases of Mitchell-Riley syndrome. In those cases, the mutations either disrupted splicing or the reading frame in exons 2 to 8 or were missense changes clustered in the DNA-binding domain, and diabetes began within the first 2 weeks of life. In contrast, their 2 patients had 3-prime truncating mutations, diabetes beginning in childhood, no liver disease, and longer survival. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) suggested that the syndrome most probably arises from a blastogenetic defect that mainly affects the midline developmental duodenal-biliary-pancreatic junction.

Smith et al. (2010) showed that Rfx6 is expressed broadly in definitive endoderm and later becomes restricted to the gut and pancreatic bud. It is reactivated downstream of Neurogenin-3 in islet progenitors and is absent from the Neurog3-null pancreas. Rfx6 formed a heterodimeric DNA-binding complex with Rfx3 on an X-box site, and the 2 proteins cooperated in activating an X-box promoter. Loss of Rfx6 did not affect formation of primary cilia.

### Diagnosis

Sansbury et al. (2015) recommended considering RFX6 genetic testing in patients with intestinal atresias even when neonatal diabetes is absent, especially when autosomal recessive inheritance is suspected.

### Population Genetics

In genomewide association data, Smith et al. (2010) found no common variants in the RFX6 linkage disequilibrium block that were associated with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) found that Rfx6-null mice were born at mendelian ratios but failed to feed. The mice had gross bowel distension due to small bowel obstruction and died within 2 days of birth, and some had a reduced pancreas size. Their pancreata contained many endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin, while pancreatic polypeptide-expressing cells were increased.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous T-to-C transition at the splice donor site of intron 2 (IVS2+2T-C) of the RFX6 gene in proband 1, a girl with neonatal diabetes born to consanguineous parents. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. She died in the first few months of life. At autopsy, her exocrine pancreas appeared normal, with clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. No properly spliced RFX6 transcript was detected in her pancreas.

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [p.S217P]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) mutation in the RFX6 gene in proband 2, the child of second cousins with neonatal diabetes, who was still alive at 4.5 years of age. Targeted capture and deep sequencing of exons in the shared homozygous regions identified S217P as the only novel nonsynonymous variant, and direct sequencing confirmed it. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. S217P modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation.

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS1AS, A-G, -12

Smith et al. (2010) found an A-to-G transition at position -12 of intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G) of the RFX6 gene in proband 3, who had diabetes and was alive and intermittently off insulin at 9 years of age. The change disrupted the acceptor site. It was in compound heterozygosity with an intron 6 splice donor mutation (IVS6+2T-G), and both mutations were inherited from carrier parents.

**.0004 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2

In proband 3 with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, Smith et al. (2010) found a T-to-G transversion at the splice donor site of intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G) of the RFX6 gene, causing loss of the donor site. It was in compound heterozygosity with IVS1-12A-G. The mutations were inherited from carrier parents. The patient was alive at 9 years of age and was intermittently off insulin.

**.0005 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, EX7DEL, FRAMESHIFT

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7 of the RFX6 gene in proband 4, who had neonatal diabetes and died in the first few months of life. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents.

**.0006 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181GLN [p.R181Q]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous arg181-to-gln (R181Q) mutation in the RFX6 gene in proband 5, who had neonatal diabetes and died in the first few months of life. The mutation affects a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain and was inherited from carrier parents. R181Q completely abolished DNA binding.

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.R726*]

Sansbury et al. (2015) found a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution, in 2 Turkish double first cousins with intestinal atresia and childhood-onset diabetes. In both, it was in compound heterozygosity with R866X. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X. This mutation was predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay.

**.0008 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG866TER [c.2596C>T; p.R866*]

Sansbury et al. (2015) found a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution, in 2 Turkish double first cousins with intestinal atresia and childhood-onset diabetes. In both, it was in compound heterozygosity with R726X. The mothers were heterozygous for R866X. Because the mutation lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary, it was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay and produce a truncated protein that keeps the full DNA-binding domain. The authors suggested that partial retention of function might explain the milder phenotype and later onset of diabetes.
