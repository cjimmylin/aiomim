### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene on chromosome 6q21-q22.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive syndromic form of diabetes in which diabetes, usually beginning in the neonatal period, occurs with intestinal atresias (duodenal and/or jejunal atresia, with or without intestinal malrotation), gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia, and hypoplastic or annular pancreas. Intrahepatic cholestasis and malabsorption that does not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement are common, and many affected infants have died in the first year of life (Mitchell et al., 2004; Smith et al., 2010; Sansbury et al., 2015). Sansbury et al. (2015) reported affected individuals whose diabetes began in childhood rather than in the neonatal period.

### Nomenclature

Smith et al. (2010) noted that their patients with RFX6 mutations resembled cases of the Martinez-Frias syndrome, under which OMIM then listed their cases. They concluded that their cases were distinct from Martinez-Frias syndrome, because the original Martinez-Frias patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias and did not have diabetes, and because they found no RFX6 mutation in the parents of one case of Martinez-Frias syndrome. Smith et al. (2010) proposed the name Mitchell-Riley syndrome, after the 2 clinicians who first described the disorder.

### Clinical Features

Mitchell et al. (2004) described 5 infants (2 sib pairs from 2 families and 1 isolated case) with neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and gallbladder aplasia or hypoplasia. One sib pair was born to consanguineous parents. Four of the children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. One patient with a milder form survived without insulin. Pancreatic immunohistochemistry in 3 infants showed a few scattered chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but no insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. Exocrine histology varied. In 1 patient from the consanguineous family, Mitchell et al. (2004) found no duplication or uniparental isodisomy of PLAGL1 at 6q24, no contiguous gene deletion involving the glucokinase gene, and no mutation in the coding sequence or splice sites of PDX1. They concluded that the disorder was probably a new autosomal recessive syndrome.

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient, born to consanguineous parents, with neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis. They noted that this was the first case with neonatal diabetes but no demonstrable structural abnormality of the pancreas, which indicated that a deficit in pancreatic function was involved. They suggested that cases with overlapping features such as malrotation, biliary atresia, and pancreatic hypoplasia, including those described by Martinez-Frias, might be variants of the same syndrome. Sequencing of KCNJ11, ABCC8, GCK, IPF1, HNF1B, NEUROD1, TCF7L2, and HNF6 found no mutations.

Martinovici et al. (2010) reported a newborn of consanguineous parents with intrauterine growth retardation, intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus, together with neonatal hemochromatosis. They diagnosed the infant with Martinez-Frias syndrome and suggested that neonatal hemochromatosis might be part of that syndrome. Sansbury et al. (2015) later listed this patient among the cases with biallelic RFX6 mutations (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

Smith et al. (2010) summarized the human syndrome as neonatal diabetes with small bowel obstruction due to bowel atresia, a hypoplastic gallbladder, and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement. Pancreatic size was somewhat reduced, but the patients were not deficient in exocrine pancreatic enzymes. Autopsies of 2 cases showed normal-appearing exocrine pancreas containing clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells, but no cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 Turkish double first cousins (their fathers were brothers and their mothers sisters), with no history of parental consanguinity. Intestinal atresias were diagnosed at birth, but diabetes was not diagnosed until ages 3 and 6 years. The first patient, a girl born at 32 weeks' gestation weighing 1.65 kg, had duodenal atresia, a jejunal web, and Meckel diverticulum. Hyperglycemia was noted at age 2 years, and she presented with diabetic ketoacidosis at age 3 years. Insulin was below 2 microIU/ml, and anti-GAD, IAA, and ICA antibodies were negative. Small intestinal resection at age 5 years for recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding showed patches of gastric heterotopia. She had an absent gallbladder with an otherwise normal biliary tract, and her liver function tests had always been normal. Her identical twin had duodenal atresia and died at age 1 month of complications of prematurity and surgery. The second patient, a boy born at 34 weeks' gestation weighing 1.70 kg, had duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation. Diabetes was diagnosed at age 6 years, after asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia had been found a year earlier. He also had chronic iron deficiency anemia of unknown cause, but no structural hepatobiliary abnormality. In both cousins, asymptomatic hyperglycemia lasted at least 1 year before diabetes was formally diagnosed. Both were alive at age 9 years. In a review of 8 previously reported molecularly confirmed cases, Sansbury et al. (2015) found duodenal atresia in all cases, gallbladder agenesis in 7 of 8, and intrahepatic cholestasis in 7 of 8 (ascites in another). Five of the 8 children had died, all within the first year of life.

### Inheritance

Mitchell et al. (2004) and Chappell et al. (2008) suggested autosomal recessive inheritance, based on affected sibs and parental consanguinity. Smith et al. (2010) confirmed it by finding homozygous or compound heterozygous RFX6 mutations in affected individuals, all inherited from carrier parents. Sansbury et al. (2015) found that heterozygous carriers in their family showed no evidence of an increased risk of abnormal HbA1c or C-peptide levels or of diabetes.

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) performed homozygosity mapping in 2 probands, the children of first and second cousins respectively. Only 3 regions of homozygosity by descent were shared by the 2 probands, totaling 24 Mb, at 2p16-p15, 6q21-q22, and 6q23. Of the 194 RefSeq genes in these regions, only RFX6, which lies in the largest segment at 6q21-q22, showed pancreas-enriched expression in the TiGER database.

### Molecular Genetics

Smith et al. (2010) captured and deep-sequenced the exons in the shared homozygous regions from 1 proband (proband 2), the child of second cousins. The only nonsynonymous novel variant was a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) change in the RFX6 gene. Sanger sequencing of all 19 RFX6 exons and splice junctions in 6 probands found mutations in 5. Probands 1, 4, and 5 died in the first few months of life. They were homozygous, respectively, for loss of the intron 2 splice donor site (IVS2+2T-C), an out-of-frame deletion in exon 7, and an arg181-to-gln (R181Q) substitution in the DNA-binding domain. Proband 3, alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin, was compound heterozygous for loss of the intron 6 donor site (IVS6+2T-G) and disruption of the intron 1 acceptor site (IVS1-12A-G). Proband 2, alive at age 4.5 years, was homozygous for S217P. All mutations were inherited from carrier parents. Functional studies showed that the IVS2+2T-C mutation abolished the normally spliced transcript in autopsy pancreas. R181Q completely abolished DNA binding, whereas S217P only modestly reduced it. No RFX6 mutation was found in the parents of a sixth proband, whom the authors considered most likely a phenocopy, or in a case of Martinez-Frias syndrome.

Using targeted next-generation sequencing of 29 monogenic diabetes genes, Sansbury et al. (2015) found that 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes were both compound heterozygous for 2 nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene: c.2176C-T (R726X) in exon 17 and c.2596C-T (R866X) in exon 18. The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X, the mothers were heterozygous for R866X, and both mutations had been inherited from the 2 grandmothers. In their review, Sansbury et al. (2015) linked the patients of Mitchell et al. (2004), Chappell et al. (2008), and Martinovici et al. (2010) to probands of Smith et al. (2010). They tabulated the Martinovici et al. (2010) patient as homozygous for c.542G-A, which they listed as p.Arg181Gly; Smith et al. (2010) had reported this mutation as R181Q. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) noted a genotype-phenotype correlation. The 3 probands who died in the first few months of life were homozygous for a splice site mutation, an exon 7 frameshift deletion, and R181Q, a missense mutation that completely abolished DNA binding. By contrast, the 2 surviving probands were either compound heterozygous for 2 splice site mutations or homozygous for S217P, which lies between the DNA-binding and dimerization domains and only modestly reduced DNA binding.

Sansbury et al. (2015) noted that all mutations in the 8 previously confirmed cases lay in exons 1 through 8 or their introns. Six affected splicing and/or the reading frame, and 3 were missense mutations in or near the DNA-binding domain. Their patients with childhood-onset diabetes carried the first truncating mutations described in exons 17 and 18 of the 19-exon gene. The exon 18 mutation, R866X, was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay because it lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary, so a partially truncated protein with an intact DNA-binding domain might be made. The authors suggested that this partial retention of function could explain the later onset of diabetes and the milder phenotype.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) generated Rfx6-null mice by replacing the first 5 exons, including the DNA-binding domain, with an eGFP-cre cassette. Homozygous mice were born at the expected mendelian ratio but fed poorly, had gross bowel distension due to small bowel obstruction, and died within 2 days of birth. Some had a smaller pancreas. At embryonic day 17.5, mutant pancreata contained many endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin. Pancreatic polypeptide-expressing cells were increased in number. Neurog3 expression was unaffected, whereas expression of downstream factors including Arx, Irx2, Pax6, Mafa, Neurod1, and Pdx1 was reduced. Rfx6 formed a heterodimeric DNA-binding complex with Rfx3, and the 2 factors cooperated in activating an X-box promoter.

### History

Martinez-Frias et al. (1992) described 2 sibs, born to consanguineous parents, with low birth weight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias, and suggested a previously unreported autosomal recessive syndrome. Anneren et al. (1998) reported a lethal autosomal recessive syndrome of intrauterine growth retardation, intra- and extrahepatic biliary atresia, and esophageal and duodenal atresia. Gentile and Fiorente (1999) reported esophageal, duodenal, rectoanal, and biliary atresia, intestinal malrotation, malformed or hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. Galan-Gomez et al. (2007) described a child of consanguineous parents with low birth weight, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and intestinal malrotation, who died 60 days after birth with a normal 46,XX karyotype. They regarded this patient as confirming the autosomal recessive disorder described by Martinez-Frias et al. (1992). Smith et al. (2010) considered Mitchell-Riley syndrome distinct from Martinez-Frias syndrome (see NOMENCLATURE).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2 [IVS2+2T>C; c.380+2T>C]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous T-to-C transition at the intron 2 splice donor site of the RFX6 gene (IVS2+2T-C) in an affected infant (proband 1) born to first-cousin parents. The infant died in the first few months of life. RT-PCR of autopsy pancreas RNA did not detect the normally spliced transcript or any RNA from exons 1 and 2, which the authors attributed probably to nonsense-mediated decay. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this mutation as c.380+2T-C and attributed the proband, together with an affected sib, to the Pakistani family reported by Mitchell et al. (2004).

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [c.649T>C; p.Ser217Pro; S217P]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) substitution in the RFX6 gene in an affected child (proband 2), the offspring of second cousins. The child was alive at age 4.5 years. The mutation was found by deep sequencing of exons in the shared homozygous regions, where it was the only novel nonsynonymous variant, and was confirmed by direct sequencing. It lies between the DNA-binding and dimerization domains. It modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this mutation as c.649T-C and attributed the patient to the report of Chappell et al. (2008).

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, 13-BP DEL, NT776 [c.776_780+8del13]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7 of the RFX6 gene in an affected infant (proband 4) who died in the first few months of life. Sansbury et al. (2015) listed this mutation as c.776_780+8del13 and described the patient as a French boy from a nonconsanguineous family with duodenal atresia, who died at age 2.5 months.

**.0004 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181GLN [c.542G>A; R181Q]

Smith et al. (2010) found a homozygous arg181-to-gln (R181Q) substitution, affecting a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain of RFX6, in an affected infant (proband 5) who died in the first few months of life. The mutant protein completely failed to bind DNA. Sansbury et al. (2015) attributed this patient to the report of Martinovici et al. (2010), an infant with neonatal hemochromatosis. They listed the mutation as c.542G-A, with the protein change given as p.Arg181Gly.

**.0005 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2 [IVS6+2T>G; c.672+2T>G]

Smith et al. (2010) found compound heterozygosity for 2 mutations in the RFX6 gene in an affected child (proband 3) who was alive at age 9 years and intermittently off insulin. One mutation was a T-to-G transversion at the intron 6 donor site (IVS6+2T-G), and the other was an A-to-G transition in intron 1 disrupting the acceptor site (IVS1-12A-G). Sansbury et al. (2015) listed these mutations as c.672+2T-G and c.224-12A-G.

**.0006 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS1AS, A-G, -12 [IVS1-12A>G; c.224-12A>G]

For discussion of the A-to-G transition in intron 1 of the RFX6 gene (IVS1-12A-G; c.224-12A-G), which disrupts the acceptor site and was found in compound heterozygous state with IVS6+2T-G in a patient with Mitchell-Riley syndrome, see RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2 (Smith et al., 2010; Sansbury et al., 2015).

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.Arg726X]

Sansbury et al. (2015) found 2 nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene in compound heterozygous state in 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and diabetes beginning at ages 3 and 6 years: a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution, and a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18 (R866X). The mutations were found by targeted next-generation sequencing. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X, which had been inherited from a grandmother. R726X was predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay.

**.0008 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG866TER [c.2596C>T; p.Arg866X]

In 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes, Sansbury et al. (2015) found compound heterozygosity for a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution, and R726X. The mothers were heterozygous for R866X. Because R866X lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary, it was predicted to escape nonsense-mediated decay and to produce a partially truncated protein with an intact DNA-binding domain. The authors proposed that this might explain the later onset of diabetes.
