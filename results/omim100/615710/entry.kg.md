### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Mitchell-Riley syndrome (MTCHRS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the RFX6 gene (612659) on chromosome 6q22.

### Description

Mitchell-Riley syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder. Its cardinal features are neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, duodenal and jejunal atresia, and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder (Mitchell et al., 2004). Affected infants also have intractable diarrhea that does not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement. Pancreatic islets lack cells staining for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin (Smith et al., 2010). In a review of molecularly confirmed cases, Sansbury et al. (2015) found duodenal atresia in all patients, gallbladder agenesis and intrahepatic cholestasis in most, and death within the first year in more than half. They also described patients with childhood-onset diabetes who survived longer.

### Nomenclature

Smith et al. (2010) noted that OMIM had listed their cases under Martinez-Frias syndrome. They considered their cases distinct, because the original Martinez-Frias syndrome patients had esophageal atresia and hypospadias and did not have diabetes. They proposed the name Mitchell-Riley syndrome, after the 2 clinicians who first described the condition.

### Clinical Features

Martínez-Frías et al. (1992) described 2 Spanish sibs, born to consanguineous parents, with a pattern of multiple congenital anomalies. The pattern included low birth weight, tracheoesophageal fistula, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. The authors searched the data files of the Spanish Collaborative Study of Congenital Malformations (ECEMC) and found 3 other unrelated infants with intestinal atresias, hypospadias, and low birth weight. They suggested that these cases might represent a milder expression of the same syndrome. ⚠︎

Annerén et al. (1998) described a lethal syndrome with intrauterine growth retardation, intra- and extrahepatic biliary atresia, and esophageal and duodenal atresia.

Gentile and Fiorente (1999) reported a patient with esophageal, duodenal, rectoanal, and biliary atresia, intestinal malrotation, a malformed/hypoplastic pancreas, and hypospadias. They considered the case further evidence of a new distinct syndrome. ⚠︎

Mitchell et al. (2004) described 5 infants from 3 families: 2 sib pairs and 1 isolated case. One sib pair was born to consanguineous parents. The infants had neonatal diabetes, hypoplastic or annular pancreas, jejunal atresia, duodenal atresia, and aplasia or hypoplasia of the gallbladder. Four of the children died in the first year of life despite aggressive medical management. One patient with a milder form was surviving without insulin. Pancreatic immunohistochemistry in 3 of the infants showed a few scattered chromogranin A-positive cell clusters but no insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin staining at all. Exocrine histology varied between cases.

Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) described a female infant, born to consanguineous parents, with a pattern of multiple congenital anomalies: low birth weight, duodenal atresia, extrahepatic biliary atresia, hypoplastic pancreas, and intestinal malrotation. She died 60 days after birth. High-resolution chromosome analysis (550-600 bands) was normal (46,XX).

Chappell et al. (2008) reported a patient, born to consanguineous parents, with neonatal diabetes, intestinal atresias, and gallbladder agenesis. This was the first such case with neonatal diabetes and no demonstrable structural abnormality of the pancreas. The authors stated that the case showed that a deficit in pancreatic function is involved.

Martinovici et al. (2010) reported a newborn of consanguineous parents with multiple congenital anomalies: intrauterine growth retardation, intestinal atresia, gallbladder aplasia, and diabetes mellitus. The authors diagnosed Martinez-Frias syndrome. The infant also had neonatal hemochromatosis, and the authors suggested that neonatal hemochromatosis may be part of Martinez-Frias syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) described infants with neonatal diabetes and small bowel obstruction due to bowel atresia. Pancreas size was somewhat reduced, but the patients were not deficient in exocrine pancreatic enzymes. Autopsies of 2 cases showed normal-appearing exocrine pancreata containing clusters of chromogranin A-positive cells, but no cells stained for insulin, glucagon, or somatostatin. The syndrome also included a hypoplastic gallbladder and intractable diarrhea that did not respond to pancreatic enzyme replacement.

Sansbury et al. (2015) reported 2 Turkish double first cousins. The parents were not consanguineous, but the mothers were sisters and the fathers were brothers. Case 1 was a girl born at 32 weeks' gestation (birth weight 1.65 kg) with duodenal atresia, a jejunal web, and Meckel diverticulum. Hyperglycemia was noted at age 2 years. At 3 years she presented with diabetic ketoacidosis, very low insulin and C-peptide, and negative islet autoantibodies, and she was treated with insulin. She had recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding due to gastric heterotopia in the small bowel, which resolved after resection at age 5. She also had an absent gallbladder with normal liver function. Her identical twin had duodenal atresia and died at 1 month. Case 2, her male cousin, was born at 34 weeks (birth weight 1.70 kg) with duodenal atresia and midgut malrotation. He had asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia at age 5 and was diagnosed with diabetes at age 6 years, with undetectable insulin, low C-peptide, and negative autoantibodies. He required insulin. He had chronic iron deficiency anemia of unknown cause and no hepatobiliary abnormality. ⚠︎

Sansbury et al. (2015) also reviewed 8 previously published molecularly confirmed cases of Mitchell-Riley syndrome. All 8 had duodenal atresia, with or without jejunal atresia or midgut malrotation. Seven of 8 had gallbladder agenesis, 7 of 8 had intrahepatic cholestasis, and another had ascites. Five of 8 died within the first year. Compared with these cases, the authors' 2 new patients had childhood-onset diabetes, no liver pathology, and longer survival.

### Inheritance

Martínez-Frías et al. (1992) concluded that the anomalies in their sibs born to consanguineous parents appeared to represent a previously unreported autosomal recessive syndrome. Annerén et al. (1998) also considered their lethal syndrome to be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait.

Mitchell et al. (2004) concluded that the disorder probably represents a new autosomal recessive syndrome. They based this on the affected sib pairs, one of which was born to consanguineous parents. Galán-Gómez et al. (2007), Chappell et al. (2008), and Martinovici et al. (2010) each reported a patient born to consanguineous parents and considered the disorder autosomal recessive. ⚠︎

Smith et al. (2010) described the syndrome as autosomal recessive. The 2 mapped probands were the offspring of consanguineous parents (first and second cousins), and all identified RFX6 mutations were inherited from carrier parents.

In the family they studied, Sansbury et al. (2015) found no evidence that heterozygosity for either RFX6 mutation was associated with abnormal HbA1c or C-peptide levels or with diabetes, although several heterozygous relatives had adult-onset diabetes. They noted that carriers of 5-prime RFX6 mutations might still have an increased risk of early-onset type 2 diabetes.

### Mapping

Smith et al. (2010) performed high-resolution homozygosity mapping in 2 probands, the offspring of first-cousin and second-cousin parents, respectively. Only 3 homozygous-by-descent regions were shared by both probands, totaling 24 Mb and containing 194 RefSeq genes. Of these genes, only RFX6, located in the largest segment at 6q21-q22, showed pancreas-enriched expression. Its expression in fetal pancreas increased between 10 and 20 weeks.

### Heterogeneity

Chappell et al. (2008) noted that other reported cases had overlapping patterns, including malrotation, biliary atresia, and pancreatic hypoplasia (e.g., as described by Martinez-Frias). They proposed that these cases may be variations of the same syndrome.

Smith et al. (2010) did not find an RFX6 mutation in proband 6. No DNA from the proband was available, so they sequenced both parents and found no point mutation in RFX6 or NEUROG3. Long-range PCR detected no RFX6 deletions. The authors considered this case most likely a phenocopy. They also found no RFX6 mutations in a case of Martinez-Frias syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

Mitchell et al. (2004) studied 1 patient from the consanguineous family. They found no duplication or uniparental isodisomy of PLAGL1 at 6q24, no contiguous gene deletion involving the glucokinase gene, and no mutation in the coding sequences or splice sites of PDX1.

In their patient, Chappell et al. (2008) sequenced genes known to cause monogenic diabetes (KCNJ11, ABCC8, GCK, IPF1, HNF1-beta, NeuroD1, and TCF7L2). They also sequenced the candidate gene HNF6, which is involved in hepatobiliary and pancreatic development. They found no mutations.

In proband 2, Smith et al. (2010) used array capture and deep sequencing of the exons in the shared homozygous regions. Of 30 novel sequence variants detected, the only nonsynonymous one was a homozygous S217P substitution in the RFX6 gene. Direct sequencing confirmed this mutation. Further study found RFX6 mutations in 5 of 6 probands, all inherited from carrier parents: - proband 1: homozygous loss of the intron 2 splice donor site; - proband 3: compound heterozygous for 2 splice mutations; - proband 4: homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7; - proband 5: homozygous R181Q missense mutation, which abolished DNA binding. RT-PCR of autopsy pancreas from proband 1 did not detect the properly spliced transcript.

Sansbury et al. (2015) studied 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes. Using targeted next-generation sequencing confirmed by Sanger sequencing, they identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the RFX6 gene: R726X in exon 17 and R866X in exon 18. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X and the mothers for R866X. Both mutations had been inherited from the grandmothers.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Smith et al. (2010) described a genotype-phenotype correlation. Probands homozygous for a splice site mutation, a frameshift deletion, or the R181Q mutation, which abolished DNA binding, died in the first few months of life. In contrast, the compound heterozygous proband 3 was alive at age 9 years. Proband 2, who was homozygous for the modestly hypomorphic S217P mutation, was alive at age 4.5 years.

Sansbury et al. (2015) noted that the 8 previously reported molecularly confirmed cases carried splicing or frameshift mutations in exons 2 to 8, or missense mutations within or near the DNA-binding domain, and had diabetes onset within the first 2 weeks of life. Their own patients carried the first truncating mutations reported at the 3-prime end of the gene (exons 17 and 18). They suggested that escape of the R866X allele from nonsense-mediated decay could allow some RFX6 function to be retained. This would result in a milder phenotype with later-onset diabetes and no liver pathology. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Galán-Gómez et al. (2007) suggested that the syndrome is most probably of blastogenetic origin, mainly affecting the midline developmental duodenal-biliary-pancreatic junction.

Smith et al. (2010) showed in mice that Rfx6 acts downstream of Neurog3. After gastrulation, Rfx6 is expressed broadly in definitive endoderm. Its expression then becomes restricted to the gut and pancreatic bud, is reactivated by Neurog3 in islet progenitor cells, and is finally restricted to the islets. The authors noted that NEUROG3 mutations also cause intractable diarrhea and diabetes, but not small bowel atresia or biliary abnormalities. They attributed this difference to Rfx6 expression in early gut endoderm, where Neurog3 is not expressed. Using EMSA, Smith et al. (2010) showed that Rfx6 and Rfx3 form a heterodimeric complex that binds an X box site. The 2 factors cooperated in activating a promoter containing multimers of this site. The authors concluded that Rfx3 and Rfx6 cooperate in regulating genes involved in islet development but not in cilia formation.

### Diagnosis

Sansbury et al. (2015) recommended RFX6 genetic testing in infants with duodenal or jejunal atresia, with or without intestinal malrotation, even when neonatal diabetes is absent. They considered testing especially indicated when sib recurrence or parental consanguinity suggests autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Animal Model

Smith et al. (2010) generated Rfx6-null mice by replacing the first 5 exons with an eGFP-cre cassette. Homozygous mice were born at the expected mendelian ratio. However, they did not feed normally, had gross bowel distension due to small bowel obstruction, and died within 2 days of birth. Some had reduced pancreas size. At embryonic day 17.5, mutant pancreata contained many chromogranin A-positive endocrine cells, but none expressed insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, or ghrelin. Cells expressing pancreatic polypeptide were increased. Neurog3 expression was unaffected in the mutant pancreata. Expression was reduced for the alpha-cell factors Irx2 and Arx and for the insulin transcription factors Pax6, MafA, NeuroD1, and Pdx1. Nkx2.2 and Nkx6.1 levels were not significantly changed, Pax4 was increased, and Nkx6.1 expression expanded to all endocrine cells. Primary cilia formed normally, in contrast to Rfx3-null mice.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS2DS, T-C, +2

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 1, the offspring of first cousins, who had Mitchell-Riley syndrome and died in the first few months of life. They identified a homozygous T-to-C transition at position +2 of intron 2 of the RFX6 gene (IVS2+2T-C), which abolished the splice donor site. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. RT-PCR of RNA from the proband's autopsy pancreas did not detect the properly spliced transcript. No RNA from exons 1 and 2 was detected either, which the authors attributed probably to nonsense-mediated decay.

**.0002 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, SER217PRO [p.S217P]

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 2, the offspring of second cousins, who had Mitchell-Riley syndrome and was alive at age 4.5 years. Array capture and deep sequencing of the shared homozygous regions identified a homozygous ser217-to-pro (S217P) substitution in the RFX6 gene. This was the only nonsynonymous variant among 30 novel variants detected, and direct sequencing confirmed it. The mutation was inherited from carrier parents. S217P lies between the DNA-binding and dimerization domains. It only modestly reduced DNA binding and did not affect dimer formation.

**.0003 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS6DS, T-G, +2

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 3, who had Mitchell-Riley syndrome and was alive at age 9 years, intermittently off insulin. This patient was compound heterozygous for 2 splice mutations in the RFX6 gene. The first was a T-to-G transversion at position +2 of intron 6 (IVS6+2T-G), which abolished the splice donor site. The second was an A-to-G transition at position -12 of intron 1 (IVS1-12A-G), which disrupted the splice acceptor site. Both mutations were inherited from carrier parents.

**.0004 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, IVS1AS, A-G, -12

Smith et al. (2010) found this mutation in proband 3, who had Mitchell-Riley syndrome and was alive at age 9 years, intermittently off insulin. The mutation is an A-to-G transition at position -12 of intron 1 of the RFX6 gene (IVS1-12A-G), which disrupted the splice acceptor site. It occurred in compound heterozygosity with the IVS6+2T-G splice mutation. Both mutations were inherited from carrier parents.

**.0005 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, DEL, EXON 7, FRAMESHIFT

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 4, who had Mitchell-Riley syndrome and died in the first few months of life. They found a homozygous out-of-frame deletion in exon 7 of the RFX6 gene, inherited from carrier parents.

**.0006 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG181GLN [p.R181Q]

Smith et al. (2010) studied proband 5, who had Mitchell-Riley syndrome and died in the first few months of life. They found a homozygous arg181-to-gln (R181Q) mutation in the RFX6 gene, inherited from carrier parents. The mutation affects a highly conserved arginine in the DNA-binding domain. In EMSA, R181Q completely abolished DNA binding.

**.0007 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG726TER [c.2176C>T; p.R726*]

Sansbury et al. (2015) studied 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes. Both carried a c.2176C-T transition in exon 17 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg726-to-ter (R726X) substitution. The mutation occurred in compound heterozygosity with R866X. It was identified by targeted next-generation sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The fathers were heterozygous for R726X, which had been inherited from the grandmother. R726X was predicted to undergo nonsense-mediated decay. ⚠︎

**.0008 MITCHELL-RILEY SYNDROME**  
RFX6, ARG866TER [c.2596C>T; p.R866*]

Sansbury et al. (2015) studied 2 Turkish double first cousins with Mitchell-Riley syndrome and childhood-onset diabetes. Both carried a c.2596C-T transition in exon 18 of the RFX6 gene, resulting in an arg866-to-ter (R866X) substitution. The mutation occurred in compound heterozygosity with R726X and was inherited from the mothers. Because R866X lies within 50 nucleotides of the last exon-exon boundary, the authors predicted that it escapes nonsense-mediated decay. The result would be a partially truncated protein with a preserved DNA-binding domain that might retain some function. They suggested this could explain the milder phenotype, with later-onset diabetes and no liver pathology. ⚠︎
