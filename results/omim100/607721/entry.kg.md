### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25.2.

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is characterized by Noonan-like facial features, including macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, and short webbed neck, together with reduced growth that is often due to growth hormone (GH) deficiency, cognitive deficits, and hyperactive behavior. The ectodermal findings are distinctive. Patients have easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair, which is loose anagen hair on microscopic examination, and darkly pigmented skin, often with eczema or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies are common, with mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects overrepresented (Mazzanti et al., 2003; Cordeddu et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed the name 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair' for a combination of features that, in their view, did not fit any known condition.

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with Noonan syndrome and loose anagen hair. Her hair was short and blond, had never been cut, and was easily plucked. On trichogram, nearly all bulbs were abnormally shaped anagen bulbs without inner and outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reported 19 patients diagnosed with loose anagen hair (LAH) at a university medical center from 1990 onward: 14 children and 5 adults, aged 8 months to 47 years. Associated conditions included Noonan syndrome in a 5-year-old boy and alopecia areata in a 3-year-old boy. In every case, a trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) without sheaths. Two of the adults were parents of 2 affected children, and the other 3 adults were the only members of their families with LAH. The authors concluded that LAH may be sporadic, may accompany developmental or acquired conditions, or, less often, may be familial. They also found that LAH can begin in adulthood: the 3 sporadic adults had diffuse hair shedding that began suddenly 1 to 3 years before evaluation.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children who shared short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, and mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Two of the children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. All 3 had a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities, including darkly pigmented hairless skin and hair defined as loose anagen hair. The authors concluded that the children's appearance was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome and that, together with the behavioral phenotype, GH deficiency, darkly pigmented hairless skin, and loose anagen hair, it did not fit any known condition. They suggested that the children might represent a distinct, previously unreported syndrome. ⚠︎

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that the 25 individuals with the SHOC2 S2G mutation shared a consistent phenotype. This included Noonan-like facies (macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, short webbed neck, and pectus anomalies), reduced growth frequently with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, and hyperactive behavior that improved with age. The hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow-growing, and loose anagen hair was confirmed microscopically in 12 individuals. Most had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies were common, with mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects overrepresented, and the voice was hypernasal. In infancy, several patients had been thought to resemble Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported that all 8 of their patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that easily pluckable or loose anagen hair is distinctive in SHOC2 mutation-positive patients, whose features partly overlap with those of typical Noonan or CFC syndrome.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a SHOC2 mutation who was delivered prematurely at 32 weeks because of fetal distress. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. An echocardiogram showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed quickly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors stated that his cardiac findings and features were not typical of previously reported individuals with this mutation and therefore expanded the clinical phenotype.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 male patients with Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair and the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All 5 had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, developmental delay, and unusually long eyelashes (dolichocilia). All 4 patients who were assessed had a structural cardiac anomaly. Hypotonia and macrocephaly were each present in 4 of 5 patients. Polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, and feeding tube use were each present in 3 of 5. The authors described distinctive brain abnormalities: relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium (4 of 4). They suggested that this combination accounted for the high rate of cerebellar tonsillar ectopia (3 of 4). When literature cases were included, 3 of 8 patients who had undergone MRI had a Chiari I malformation. ⚠︎

Patient 1 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy born to 37-year-old parents after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and cystic hygroma. He was macrosomic at birth. He had pyloric stenosis, severe failure to thrive requiring a gastrostomy tube, mild pulmonic valve stenosis, low axial tone, small optic nerves, motor delay, and an occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) of +3 SD. At age 2 years 3 months, juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia was suspected. He developed hepatosplenomegaly, and bone marrow biopsy was most consistent with primary myelofibrosis. The karyotype was normal, and there was no monosomy 7 and no JAK2 val617-to-phe mutation. He died at age 2.5 years after a splenectomy performed in preparation for bone marrow transplantation. This was the first hematologic neoplasm reported with a SHOC2 mutation.

Patient 3 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy with biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and right bundle branch block at age 6 months, later followed by aortic insufficiency and mild pulmonic stenosis. His OFC was +5 SD at age 9 years. He had severe aggressive behavior, was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome, and took psychotropic medication.

Patient 4 of Gripp et al. (2013) was a boy of Mexican ancestry with global developmental delay and mild to moderate intellectual disability. He had an atrial septal defect, blond, sparse, easily pluckable hair, and long eyelashes. Brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly, periventricular nodular heterotopia, and a thick, dysplastic corpus callosum. By age 6 years he had developed a Chiari I malformation with platybasia and basilar invagination, along with C3-C4 vertebral fusion and unusual venous sinus anatomy. Periventricular nodular heterotopia had not been reported with a SHOC2 mutation before.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous SHOC2 S2G mutation occurred de novo in patients with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair (Cordeddu et al., 2009; Hoban et al., 2012; Gripp et al., 2013). Hannig et al. (2014) reported a heterozygous SHOC2 variant that a girl with a RASopathy phenotype inherited from her father, who also had subtle features. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent pro49-to-arg (P49R) mutation in the PPP1CB gene. He had sparse, thin, slow-growing hair resembling the ectodermal finding typical of Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair is very similar to Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair.

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) used an in silico protein network analysis, seeded with known mutant proteins of the RAS/MAPK pathway, to prioritize SHOC2 as a candidate Noonan syndrome gene. They sequenced SHOC2 in 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who did not have mutations in the known genes. In 4 unrelated sporadic individuals they identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation was absent from the parents in the 3 families tested, with paternity confirmed, indicating de novo origin. It was present in several tissues, indicating that it was germline. Among a further 410 mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, the authors found the S2G mutation in 21. De novo occurrence was proven in the 12 families in which parental DNA was available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found, which suggested a specific pathogenetic role for S2G. In total, 25 subjects carried the mutation. Functional studies showed that the S2G substitution creates an N-myristoylation site: SHOC2 S2G expressed in Cos-1 cells incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype SHOC2 and a disease-unrelated S2A mutant did not. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They found the previously identified S2G mutation in 8 patients. They identified no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In an infant with Noonan syndrome and fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo SHOC2 missense mutation predicting ser2-to-gly. It was found by clinical testing of genes that cause Noonan syndrome and related disorders.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the recurrent SHOC2 c.4A-G mutation (S2G) in 5 patients with Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair. In patient 1, the mutation was found by postmortem testing of buccal DNA and was absent from both parents. The mutation was also de novo in patient 2. Sequencing of other RASopathy genes was normal in these patients.

Hannig et al. (2014) studied a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry born to nonconsanguineous parents. She had right optic nerve hypoplasia, bilateral nystagmus, speech delay, poor hair growth, and hyperactive behavior. Her stature was average, and she had macrocephaly (98th percentile), sparse slow-growing hair that was not loose, normal skin pigmentation, bilateral ptosis, widely spaced nipples, and clubbed nails. MRI showed multiple irregularities of unknown significance in the midbrain, pons, and medulla. She did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis, or loose anagen hair. Sequencing of a RASopathy gene panel identified a heterozygous c.519G-A variant in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution, which she had inherited from her father. Her father was tall (6 feet 3 inches) and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. Apart from the sparse hair and clubbed nails, his features were very subtle and did not clearly indicate a RASopathy. Echocardiograms and renal ultrasounds were negative in both father and daughter. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. Met173 lies in leucine-rich repeat 4 and is conserved among vertebrate SHOC2 orthologs. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the M173I mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation to only about 10% above basal levels, whereas wildtype SHOC2 rescued it fully. The mutant's half-life, subcellular localization, and binding to M-RAS and RAF1 were unchanged. However, it failed to precipitate PP1c effectively and produced only a mild increase in RAF1 ser338 phosphorylation. The authors concluded that M173I causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT (gln269_his270delinsHisTyr), which was associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Structural analyses indicated that the mutation might affect the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains. In functional studies, the mutant protein showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade, supporting an activating role. Unlike SHOC2 S2G, the mutant was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the patients with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed markedly from patients with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair and the S2G mutation. The proband had Noonan syndrome-like craniofacial features and CFC-like sparse hair but did not have short stature, cardiac defects, or loose anagen hair. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that in starved Cos-1 cells, wildtype SHOC2 was distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus and moved to the nucleus on EGF stimulation; endogenous SHOC2 behaved the same way in primary fibroblasts. In contrast, SHOC2 S2G was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane and failed to move to the nucleus. This membrane targeting depended on myristoylation and was reversed by an NMT inhibitor. The mutant did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2, which excluded a dominant-negative effect. It enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells, indicating cell type-specific activation of the MAPK pathway. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that their patient with myelofibrosis lacked the common somatic JAK2 mutation, which is present in most patients with myelofibrosis. They suggested that germline or somatic SHOC2 mutation may be causally involved in myelofibrosis through activation of the RAS/MAPK pathway.

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS interacts with SHOC2 and protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) to form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme. This complex dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site on RAF kinases (S259 on CRAF and S365 on BRAF). MRAS and SHOC2 acted as PP1 regulatory subunits that made the complex highly specific for RAF. MRAS also acted as a targeting subunit, because the complex needed membrane localization to dephosphorylate RAF efficiently and to regulate the ERK pathway in cells. Noonan syndrome mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB always promoted formation of the complex with the other 2 partners but did not necessarily promote binding to other interactors. The authors concluded that, at the biochemical level, Noonan syndrome in people with SHOC2, MRAS, or PPP1CB mutations is likely driven by enhanced formation of the ternary complex. They also reported that the predicted structure of SHOC2 closely resembles the A subunit of PP2A, and they identified several regions in SHOC2 involved in complex formation.

Motta et al. (2019) concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 functional dysregulation converge on upregulation of MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

Tosti et al. (1997) studied the pathology of hair from 5 patients with loose anagen hair. Apart from frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath, the hair follicles showed no morphologic abnormalities. The authors concluded that pathologic findings are of little diagnostic value in LAH.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid detection system for the SHOC2 S2G mutation based on melting curve analysis, for screening of this apparently common mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that 4 of their 5 patients had originally been suspected of having Costello syndrome. In their subjective assessment, the ectodermal, growth, and developmental features of patients with SHOC2 S2G closely resembled those of Costello syndrome due to the HRAS gly13-to-cys mutation. They supported a gene panel approach to molecular testing.

### Population Genetics

Gripp et al. (2013) combined earlier case series, in which 12 of 188 mutation-negative patients with Noonan or Noonan-like syndrome had SHOC2 mutations. From these data, they estimated that SHOC2 mutations account for about 6% of mutation-negative Noonan syndrome, corresponding to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome.

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that in C. elegans with a sur-8 reduction-of-function and let-60 gain-of-function background, wildtype human SHOC2 could functionally replace SUR-8 but SHOC2 S2G could not. S2G did not act as a dominant negative. When SHOC2 S2G or myristoylation-tagged wildtype SHOC2 was expressed at early L3, it caused protruding vulva, egg-laying defects, and bag-of-worms phenotypes. These resulted from abnormal vulval morphogenesis, with asymmetric invaginations involving P5.p/P7.p descendants in 17 of 48 S2G larvae, without altered specification of vulval cell fate. Both proteins were membrane-targeted in worm cells.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G]

Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 25 subjects with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair. Four were found among 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who did not have mutations in the known genes, and 21 among a further 410 mutation-negative subjects. The mutation occurred de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available, with paternity confirmed in the first 3 families. The substitution created an N-myristoylation site, and the mutant protein was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane in a myristoylation-dependent way. It failed to move to the nucleus on EGF stimulation and enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells. In C. elegans, unlike wildtype human SHOC2, S2G could not functionally replace SUR-8, and its expression caused abnormal vulval morphogenesis. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in other known genes. All 8 had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the S2G mutation in 5 male patients with Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair. The mutation was de novo in patients 1 and 2. Patient 1 developed myelofibrosis and died at age 2.5 years.

**.0002 RASOPATHY (NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE FEATURES WITHOUT LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR)**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with macrocephaly, ptosis, optic nerve hypoplasia, sparse slow-growing hair, and hyperactive behavior, and in her father, who had subtle features, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A variant in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The variant was found by RASopathy gene panel sequencing, was inherited from the father, and was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. Met173 lies in leucine-rich repeat 4 and is conserved among vertebrates. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation to only about 10% above basal levels and failed to precipitate PP1c effectively. The authors concluded that the variant causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway. The girl did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, or loose anagen hair.
