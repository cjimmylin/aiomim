### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene (602775) on chromosome 10q25. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is a RASopathy. Its features include Noonan-like facies with macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, and a short webbed neck; reduced growth, often with growth hormone (GH) deficiency; cognitive deficits; hyperactive behavior; and easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair that is loose anagen hair on microscopy. Most patients have darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies are common, and the voice is hypernasal (Mazzanti et al., 2003; Cordeddu et al., 2009).

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) concluded that the 3 children they studied did not fit any known condition. They proposed that the children might have a distinct, previously unreported syndrome and suggested the name 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.'

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with Noonan syndrome and loose anagen hair. Her hair was short and blond, had never been cut, and was easily plucked. On trichogram, nearly all bulbs were abnormally shaped anagen bulbs without inner and outer root sheaths. Scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

Tosti et al. (1997) reported 19 patients with loose anagen hair (LAH) diagnosed at a university medical center from 1990 onward: 14 children and 5 adults, aged 8 months to 47 years. One of them was a 5-year-old boy with Noonan syndrome. In every case the trichogram showed mostly anagen hairs (80 to 100%) without sheaths. LAH could begin in adulthood: 3 sporadic adults had diffuse hair shedding that began suddenly 1 to 3 years before evaluation. On follow-up, LAH was stable in 4 children and 1 adult, and hair density improved considerably in 2 children and 1 adult.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) described 3 children with short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, a short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, and mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Two of the children had mild dilatation of the pulmonary root. All 3 had an unusual combination of ectodermal abnormalities, including darkly pigmented hairless skin and loose anagen hair.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that the 25 individuals with the SHOC2 S2G mutation shared a consistent phenotype. It included Noonan-like facies (macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears, short webbed neck, pectus anomalies), reduced growth often with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, and hyperactive behavior that improved with age. The hair was easily pluckable, sparse, thin, and slow-growing, and loose anagen hair was confirmed microscopically in 12 patients. Most patients had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies were common, with mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects overrepresented, and the voice was hypernasal. In infancy, several patients had been thought to resemble Costello or cardiofaciocutaneous (CFC) syndrome.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) reported 8 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All 8 had short stature, sparse hair, and atopic skin. Six had severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 had leukocytosis. The authors concluded that easily pluckable/loose anagen hair is distinctive in SHOC2 mutation-positive patients, whose features partially overlap with typical Noonan or CFC syndrome.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a SHOC2 mutation who was delivered prematurely at 32 weeks because of fetal distress. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. The cardiac lesion progressed quickly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors stated that his cardiac and other features were not typical of previously reported patients with this mutation and expanded the clinical phenotype.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 unrelated male patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation. All 5 had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, developmental delay, and unusually long eyelashes (dolichocilia). All 4 who were assessed had a structural cardiac anomaly. Hypotonia and macrocephaly were each present in 4 of 5 patients, and polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, and feeding tube use were each present in 3 of 5. Brain imaging showed relative megalencephaly with enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium (4 of 4). The authors suggested that this combination explained the high rate of cerebellar tonsillar ectopia (3 of 4). Including cases from the literature, 3 of 8 patients who had MRI showed a Chiari I malformation.

Gripp et al. (2013) described their patient 1, a boy born to 37-year-old parents after a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios and cystic hygroma. He was macrosomic at birth. He had pyloric stenosis, severe failure to thrive requiring a gastrostomy tube, mild pulmonic valve stenosis, low axial tone, small optic nerves, motor delay, and an occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) of +3 SD. At age 2 years 3 months, juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia was suspected. He developed hepatosplenomegaly, and bone marrow biopsy was most consistent with primary myelofibrosis. The karyotype was normal, and there was no monosomy 7 and no JAK2 val617-to-phe mutation. He died at age 2.5 years after a splenectomy performed in preparation for bone marrow transplant. This was the first hematologic neoplasm reported in a patient with a SHOC2 mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) described their patient 3, a boy who had biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and right bundle branch block at age 6 months, and later aortic insufficiency and mild pulmonic stenosis. His OFC was +5 SD at age 9 years. He had severe aggressive behavior and was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome, for which he took psychotropic medication.

Gripp et al. (2013) described their patient 4, a boy of Mexican ancestry with global developmental delay and mild to moderate intellectual disability. He had an atrial septal defect, blond, sparse, easily pluckable hair, and long eyelashes. Brain MRI showed mild ventriculomegaly, periventricular nodular heterotopia, and a thick, dysplastic corpus callosum. By age 6 years he had developed a Chiari I malformation with platybasia and basilar invagination, along with C3-C4 vertebral fusion and unusual venous sinus anatomy. Periventricular nodular heterotopia had not previously been reported with a SHOC2 mutation.

### Inheritance

In most reported patients with NSLH1, the heterozygous SHOC2 S2G mutation was sporadic and arose de novo (Cordeddu et al., 2009; Hoban et al., 2012; Gripp et al., 2013). ✗

Among patients with isolated or syndromic loose anagen hair, Tosti et al. (1997) found that 2 affected adults were parents of 2 affected children, whereas 3 other adults were the only affected members of their families. They concluded that LAH may be sporadic, may accompany developmental or acquired conditions, or, less often, may be familial.

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with the recurrent pro49-to-arg (P49R) mutation in the PPP1CB gene. His sparse, thin, slow-growing hair resembled the ectodermal finding typical of Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, and he also had craniosynostosis. The authors concluded that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair closely resembles the SHOC2-associated disorder. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Cordeddu et al. (2009) used an in silico protein network analysis, seeded with known mutant RAS/MAPK proteins, to prioritize SHOC2 as a candidate Noonan syndrome gene. They sequenced SHOC2 in 96 patients with Noonan syndrome who had no mutations in known genes. In 4 unrelated sporadic patients they identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation was absent from the parents in the 3 families tested, with paternity confirmed, and so had arisen de novo. It was present in several tissues, indicating a germline mutation. Analysis of a further 410 mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype found the same change in 21. De novo occurrence was proven in the 12 families with parental DNA available. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found, suggesting a specific pathogenetic role for S2G. The mutation created an N-myristoylation site and caused aberrant targeting of SHOC2 to the plasma membrane. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1, or RAF1. They reported the previously identified S2G mutation in 8 of their patients. They found no SHOC2 mutations in leukemia cells from 82 patients with leukemia.

In a premature male infant with Noonan syndrome and fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in SHOC2 by clinical testing of genes that cause Noonan syndrome and related disorders.

In 5 male patients with Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair, Gripp et al. (2013) identified the recurrent SHOC2 c.4A-G mutation (S2G). In patient 1, the mutation was found by postmortem testing of buccal DNA and was absent from both parents. It was also de novo in patient 2. Sequencing of other RASopathy genes was normal in these patients.

In a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with a RASopathy phenotype and in her father, Hannig et al. (2014) found a heterozygous c.519G-A variant in SHOC2, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution, by sequencing a RASopathy gene panel. The girl had inherited the variant from her father. It was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. Met173 lies in leucine-rich repeat 4 and is conserved among vertebrate SHOC2 orthologs. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the M173I mutant restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation by only about 10% above basal levels, whereas wildtype SHOC2 fully rescued it. The mutant had an unchanged half-life, subcellular localization, and binding to M-RAS and RAF1. However, it failed to precipitate PP1c effectively and produced only a mild increase in RAF1 ser338 phosphorylation. The authors concluded that M173I causes loss of function in the ERK1/2 pathway. The proband had sparse, slow-growing hair that was not loose anagen hair, and she did not have short stature or a cardiac anomaly.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a third RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT, predicting gln269_his270delinsHisTyr. It was associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Structural analyses suggested that the mutation might change the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains. In functional studies, the mutant protein bound MRAS and PPP1CB more strongly and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade, supporting an activating effect. Unlike SHOC2 S2G, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the 2 family members with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed markedly from patients with the S2G mutation. The proband had Noonan syndrome-like craniofacial features and CFC-like sparse hair but no short stature, cardiac defects, or loose anagen hair. Her father was tall (6 feet 3 inches) and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair, and clubbed nails. His features were very subtle and did not clearly point to a RASopathy apart from the sparse hair and clubbed nails. The authors concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that the S2G substitution creates an N-myristoylation site. SHOC2 S2G expressed in Cos-1 cells incorporated [3H]-myristic acid, whereas wildtype SHOC2 and a disease-unrelated S2A mutant did not. In starved Cos-1 cells, wildtype SHOC2 was found in both cytoplasm and nucleus and moved to the nucleus on EGF stimulation; endogenous SHOC2 behaved the same way in primary fibroblasts. In contrast, SHOC2 S2G was constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane and failed to move to the nucleus. This targeting depended on myristoylation and was reversed by an NMT inhibitor. The mutant did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2, excluding a dominant-negative effect. It enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells, indicating cell type-specific MAPK activation. ⚠︎

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that their patient with myelofibrosis did not have the somatic JAK2 mutation found in most patients with myelofibrosis. They suggested that germline or somatic SHOC2 mutation, by activating the RAS/MAPK pathway, may play a causal role in myelofibrosis.

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS, SHOC2, and protein phosphatase-1 (PP1) form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site on RAF kinases (S259 on CRAF, S365 on BRAF). MRAS and SHOC2 acted as PP1 regulatory subunits that make the complex highly specific for RAF. MRAS also served as a targeting subunit, because the complex needed membrane localization to dephosphorylate RAF efficiently and to regulate the ERK pathway in cells. The predicted structure of SHOC2 closely resembles the A subunit of PP2A. Noonan syndrome mutations in SHOC2, MRAS, and PPP1CB always promoted formation of the complex with the other 2 partners but did not necessarily promote binding to other interactors. The authors concluded that Noonan syndrome caused by mutations in these genes is probably driven at the biochemical level by enhanced ternary complex formation.

Motta et al. (2019) concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 functional dysregulation converge on upregulation of MAPK signaling.

### Diagnosis

Tosti et al. (1997) studied hair pathology in 5 patients with LAH. Apart from frequent fragmentation of the inner root sheath, they found no morphologic abnormalities of the hair follicles. They concluded that pathologic findings are of little diagnostic value in LAH.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid detection system for the SHOC2 S2G mutation based on melting curve analysis, for screening of this apparently common mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) noted that 4 of their 5 patients with the SHOC2 S2G mutation had originally been suspected of having Costello syndrome. They observed subjectively that the ectodermal, growth, and developmental features of these patients closely resembled those of Costello syndrome due to the HRAS gly13-to-cys mutation. They supported a gene panel approach to molecular testing.

### Population Genetics

Gripp et al. (2013) combined earlier case series (12 of 188 mutation-negative patients with Noonan or Noonan-like syndrome) to estimate that SHOC2 mutations account for about 6% of mutation-negative Noonan syndrome, or about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome.

### Animal Model

In C. elegans, Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype human SHOC2, but not the S2G mutant, could functionally replace SUR-8 in a sur-8(rf);let-60(gf) background. S2G did not act as a dominant negative. Expression of SHOC2 S2G or of myristoylation-tagged wildtype SHOC2 at early L3 caused protruding vulva, egg-laying defects, and bag-of-worms phenotypes. These resulted from abnormal vulval morphogenesis, with asymmetric invaginations involving P5.p/P7.p descendants in 17 of 48 S2G larvae, and vulval cell fate specification was not altered. Both proteins were membrane-targeted in worm cells. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.S2G]

In 25 patients with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair (NSLH1), Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. Four of the patients came from a screen of 96 mutation-negative patients with Noonan syndrome, and 21 from a further 410 mutation-negative subjects. The mutation arose de novo in all families with parental DNA available. The substitution created an N-myristoylation site. It led to myristoylation-dependent constitutive targeting of SHOC2 to the plasma membrane, loss of nuclear translocation on EGF stimulation, and cell type-specific enhancement of EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation. In C. elegans, the mutant could not replace SUR-8 and caused abnormal vulval morphogenesis. ⚠︎

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who had no mutations in other known genes.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant with Noonan syndrome and fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the S2G mutation in 5 male patients with NSLH1. The mutation was de novo in the 2 patients whose parents were tested. One patient developed myelofibrosis.

**.0002 RASOPATHY (NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE PHENOTYPE WITHOUT LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR)**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with a RASopathy phenotype and in her father, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, predicting a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The girl had inherited the variant from her father. The variant was absent from 6,500 asymptomatic individuals and affects a conserved residue in leucine-rich repeat 4. In SHOC2-depleted Cos1 cells, the mutant only weakly restored EGF-induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation and failed to precipitate PP1c effectively, consistent with loss of function. Neither the girl nor her father had short stature, a cardiac anomaly, or loose anagen hair. ⚠︎

**.0003 RASOPATHY WITH PRENATAL-ONSET HYPERTROPHIC CARDIOMYOPATHY**  
SHOC2, 2-BP DELINS, 807TT [c.807_808delinstt; c.p.gln269_his270delinshistyr; c.q269_h270delinshy; p.Q269]

Motta et al. (2019) reported a c.807_808delinsTT mutation in the SHOC2 gene, predicting gln269_his270delinsHisTyr, in association with a RASopathy with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The mutant protein bound MRAS and PPP1CB more strongly and increased MAPK signaling, supporting an activating effect. Unlike S2G, it was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane.
