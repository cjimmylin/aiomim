### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair-1 (NSLH1) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SHOC2 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair is a RASopathy characterized by facial features resembling those of Noonan syndrome together with distinctive ectodermal abnormalities. Mazzanti et al. (2003) described short stature, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, short neck with redundant skin, severe growth hormone (GH) deficiency, mild psychomotor delay with attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), darkly pigmented and hairless skin, and loose anagen hair. Cordeddu et al. (2009) added hair that is easily pluckable, sparse, thin and slow growing, cardiac anomalies with overrepresentation of mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects, and a characteristically hypernasal voice. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Mazzanti et al. (2003) proposed the name 'Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair' for the disorder. Motta et al. (2019) referred to the disorder caused by the recurrent SHOC2 ser2-to-gly mutation as Mazzanti syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Tosti et al. (1991) reported a 4.5-year-old girl with loose anagen hair and Noonan syndrome. She had short, blond, easily pluckable hair that had never been cut. The trichogram showed an absolute prevalence of abnormally shaped anagen bulbs lacking inner and outer root sheaths, and a scalp biopsy showed marked cleft formation between fragmented inner root sheaths and irregularly shaped hair shafts.

In a review of 14 children and 5 adults diagnosed with loose anagen hair, Tosti et al. (1997) noted Noonan syndrome as an associated disease in a 5-year-old boy. In all cases the trichogram showed a predominance of anagen hairs (80 to 100%) devoid of sheaths.

Mazzanti et al. (2003) reported 3 children with short stature, the same facial phenotype, macrocephaly, enlarged cerebrospinal fluid spaces, short neck with redundant skin, severe GH deficiency, mild psychomotor delay with ADHD, and mild dilatation of the pulmonary root in 2 of them. They also had a unique combination of ectodermal abnormalities, including darkly pigmented and hairless skin and hair defined as loose anagen hair. Their appearance was not completely typical of Noonan syndrome, and the authors postulated that the children represented a distinct, previously unreported syndrome.

Cordeddu et al. (2009) reviewed the clinical features of 25 individuals carrying a SHOC2 mutation and found a consistent phenotype. Facial features seemed typical for Noonan syndrome; common features included macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, low-set/posteriorly rotated ears, short and webbed neck, and pectus anomalies. Patients had reduced growth, frequently associated with proven GH deficiency, cognitive deficits, distinctive hyperactive behavior that improved with age in most, and easily pluckable, sparse, thin, slow-growing hair. In 12 subjects, loose anagen hair was confirmed by microscopic examination of pulled hairs. Most had darkly pigmented skin with eczema or ichthyosis. Cardiac anomalies were observed in the majority, with mitral valve dysplasia and septal defects significantly overrepresented compared with the general Noonan syndrome population, and the voice was characteristically hypernasal. Referring pediatricians had felt that several subjects had features suggestive of Costello syndrome or cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome as newborns or young infants.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found that all 8 patients with the SHOC2 ser2-to-gly mutation in their cohort had short stature, sparse hair and atopic skin. Six showed severe mental retardation and easily pluckable hair, and 1 showed leukocytosis.

Hoban et al. (2012) reported a male infant with a de novo SHOC2 ser2-to-gly mutation who presented with fetal distress requiring premature delivery at 32 weeks. After birth he had dysmorphic features, edema, hepatosplenomegaly, leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and respiratory distress. Echocardiography revealed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction; the cardiac lesion progressed rapidly, and he was discharged home for palliative care. The authors noted that his cardiac findings and features were not typical of those previously reported with this mutation.

Gripp et al. (2013) reported 5 males with the SHOC2 ser2-to-gly mutation. All had skin hyperpigmentation, sparse light-colored hair, increased fine wrinkles, ligamentous laxity, and developmental delay, and 4 of 4 had a structural cardiac anomaly. Hypotonia and macrocephaly occurred in 4 of 5, and 3 of 5 had polyhydramnios, increased birth weight, or required a feeding tube. None had been formally diagnosed with loose anagen hair, but all had sparse, very slow-growing hair of unruly texture and unusually light coloring, together with unusually long eyelashes. Four of the 5 patients had been suspected of having Costello syndrome. Brain abnormalities included relative megalencephaly, enlarged subarachnoid spaces suggestive of benign external hydrocephalus, and a relatively small posterior fossa indicated by a vertical tentorium; cerebellar tonsillar ectopia was present in 3 of 4. One patient (patient 4), whose father was of Mexican ancestry, had periventricular nodular heterotopia and postnatal development of a Chiari I malformation. Patient 1 developed myelofibrosis at age 2 years; bone marrow testing for the JAK2 V617F mutation was negative, and he died at age 2.5 years after splenectomy in preparation for bone marrow transplantation. Patient 3 had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aggressive behavior, and was diagnosed with pervasive developmental disorder and Tourette syndrome at 9 years.

Hannig et al. (2014) reported a 5-year-old girl, the oldest child of nonconsanguineous parents of European ancestry, who was referred for right optic nerve hypoplasia, bilateral nystagmus, speech delay, poor hair growth and hyperactive behavior. She had average stature, macrocephaly, sparse and slow-growing hair that was not loose, normal skin pigmentation, bilateral ptosis, wide-spaced nipples, and clubbed nails; MRI showed multiple irregularities of unknown significance in the midbrain, pons and medulla. Her father, who carried the same SHOC2 variant, was 6 feet 3 inches tall and had intellectual disability, a high-arched palate, sparse hair and clubbed nails. Echocardiograms and renal ultrasounds were negative in both. The authors noted that the girl did not have short stature, a cardiac anomaly, hyperpigmentation, ichthyosis or loose anagen hair, and that her features overlapped those of Noonan syndrome, cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome and Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair.

Motta et al. (2019) reported a RASopathy-causing SHOC2 mutation associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

### Inheritance

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that all 4 cases in their initial cohort were sporadic and showed that the SHOC2 mutation had arisen de novo in 3 families with parental DNA available, and in 12 further families in an extended cohort. Hannig et al. (2014) observed a heterozygous SHOC2 variant in a proband and her mildly affected father, and described the RASopathies as autosomal dominant disorders.

### Heterogeneity

Bertola et al. (2017) reported a 12-year-old Brazilian boy with a Noonan-like phenotype and the recurrent pro49-to-arg (P49R) mutation in the PPP1CB gene. He had sparse, thin, slow-growing hair similar to that of Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair, as well as craniosynostosis. The authors noted that PPP1CB-related Noonan syndrome with loose anagen hair shares great similarity with Noonan syndrome-like disorder with loose anagen hair.

### Molecular Genetics

Using an in silico protein network analysis to prioritize candidate genes, Cordeddu et al. (2009) resequenced SHOC2 in 96 individuals with Noonan syndrome who were negative for mutations in known disease genes and identified a 4A-G transition, predicting a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution, in 4 unrelated individuals. All cases were sporadic; the mutation was absent from the parents in the 3 families with parental DNA available, and it was present in DNA from several tissues, indicating germline origin. In a further cohort of 410 mutation-negative subjects with Noonan syndrome or a related phenotype, they found the same change in 21, with de novo occurrence proven in 12 families. No other disease-associated SHOC2 variant was found. The mutation introduced an N-myristoylation site, resulting in aberrant targeting of SHOC2 to the plasma membrane and impaired translocation to the nucleus upon growth factor stimulation (see PATHOGENESIS).

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) analyzed SHOC2 in 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders who did not have mutations in PTPN11, KRAS, HRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1/2, SOS1 or RAF1, and found the S2G mutation in 8. No SHOC2 mutations were identified in leukemia cells from 82 leukemia patients.

In a male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in SHOC2 by clinical testing of genes causative of Noonan syndrome and related disorders.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the SHOC2 c.4A-G (S2G) mutation in 5 patients; in patients 1 and 2 the mutation was shown to have occurred de novo. In patient 1, the mutation was found postmortem in stored buccal cell DNA and was absent from both parents' buccal cell DNA.

By sequencing of a RASopathy gene panel in a 5-year-old girl with features overlapping Noonan syndrome, cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome and Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. Her father, who had sparse hair, clubbed nails and intellectual disability, was also heterozygous for the variant. In functional studies in Cos1 cells depleted of endogenous SHOC2, the mutant protein restored only about 10% above basal levels of EGF-induced ERK1/2 activity, had impaired interaction with PP1c, and led to only a mild increase in RAF1 ser338 phosphorylation, which the authors interpreted as loss of function.

In a patient with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Motta et al. (2019) identified a SHOC2 mutation, c.807_808delinsTT, resulting in a gln269_his270delinshis-tyr substitution. Functional studies showed enhanced binding of the mutant protein to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased signaling through the MAPK cascade; unlike S2G, the mutant was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hannig et al. (2014) noted that the phenotypic features of the 2 patients with the SHOC2 M173I variant differed significantly from those of patients with the S2G mutation, who usually present with significant short stature, intellectual disability, cardiac anomalies and multiple cutaneous findings. They concluded that SHOC2 mutations can cause a spectrum of RASopathy phenotypes of varying severity.

### Pathogenesis

Cordeddu et al. (2009) showed that SHOC2 S2G, but not wildtype SHOC2 or a disease-unrelated S2A mutant, incorporated [3H]-myristic acid when expressed in Cos-1 cells. Wildtype SHOC2 was distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus in starved cells and restricted to the nucleus after EGF stimulation, whereas SHOC2 S2G was targeted to the cell membrane in both states; treatment with an N-myristoyltransferase inhibitor reduced or abolished the membrane localization. The mutant did not heterodimerize with wildtype SHOC2. SHOC2 S2G expression enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A neuroblastoma cells, but not in Cos-1 or 293T cells. The authors concluded that this acquired N-terminal lipid modification causes constitutive membrane targeting and cell context-specific increase in MAPK activation.

Young et al. (2018) showed that MRAS, SHOC2 and PP1 form a heterotrimeric holoenzyme that dephosphorylates the inhibitory S259 site of RAF kinases. Syndromic mutations in MRAS, SHOC2 and PPP1CB invariably promoted complex formation with each other, suggesting that Noonan syndrome in individuals with these mutations is driven at the biochemical level by enhanced ternary complex formation.

Motta et al. (2019) found that the SHOC2 Q269_H270delinsHY mutant, unlike S2G, was not constitutively targeted to the plasma membrane, but showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased MAPK signaling, and concluded that diverse mechanisms of SHOC2 dysregulation converge toward MAPK signaling upregulation.

### Diagnosis

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) developed a rapid detection system based on melting curve analysis to screen for the S2G mutation in SHOC2.

Noting that 4 of their 5 patients had been suspected of having Costello syndrome, Gripp et al. (2013) supported the use of a panel approach to molecular testing in patients with a RASopathy phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Combining the series of Cordeddu et al. (2009) and Komatsuzaki et al. (2010), Gripp et al. (2013) estimated that SHOC2 mutations occur in about 6% (12 of 188) of mutation-negative individuals with Noonan syndrome, corresponding to about 1.8% of all individuals with Noonan syndrome. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Cordeddu et al. (2009) found that wildtype human SHOC2, but not SHOC2 S2G, could functionally replace the C. elegans ortholog SUR-8. In a wildtype background, induction of SHOC2 S2G at the early L3 stage caused abnormal vulval development with protruding vulva, decreased egg laying and a bag-of-worms phenotype; these neomorphic phenotypes were also produced by wildtype SHOC2 tagged with an N-myristoylation sequence, but not by untagged wildtype SHOC2.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, SER2GLY [c.4A>G; p.Ser2Gly; p.S2G]

In 25 individuals with Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, Cordeddu et al. (2009) identified a 4A-G transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a ser2-to-gly (S2G) substitution. The mutation occurred de novo in all families in which parental DNA was available (3 of 4 in the initial cohort and 12 in the second cohort). The substitution introduced an N-myristoylation site, leading to myristoylation of the mutant protein, its constitutive targeting to the plasma membrane, impaired nuclear translocation upon growth factor stimulation, and enhanced EGF-dependent ERK phosphorylation in Neuro2A cells.

Komatsuzaki et al. (2010) found the S2G mutation in 8 of 92 patients with Noonan syndrome and related disorders without mutations in other known genes.

Hoban et al. (2012) identified a de novo S2G mutation in a premature male infant with fatal hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Gripp et al. (2013) identified the S2G mutation in 5 patients, including 1 with myelofibrosis; the mutation was de novo in patients 1 and 2.

**.0002 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, MET173ILE [c.519G>A; p.M173I]

In a 5-year-old girl of European ancestry with a RASopathy phenotype overlapping Noonan syndrome, cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome and Noonan-like syndrome with loose anagen hair, Hannig et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.519G-A transition in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a met173-to-ile (M173I) substitution in the fourth leucine-rich repeat. Her father, who had sparse hair, clubbed nails and intellectual disability, was also heterozygous. The variant was not found in 6,500 asymptomatic individuals. The mutant protein had impaired interaction with PP1c and could restore only about 10% above basal levels of EGF-induced ERK1/2 activity in SHOC2-depleted cells.

**.0003 NOONAN SYNDROME-LIKE DISORDER WITH LOOSE ANAGEN HAIR 1**  
SHOC2, GLN269_HIS270DELINSHISTYR [c.807_808delinsTT; p.Gln269_His270delinsHisTyr]

In a patient with a RASopathy associated with prenatal-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Motta et al. (2019) identified a c.807_808delinsTT mutation in the SHOC2 gene, resulting in a gln269_his270delinshis-tyr substitution. Structural analyses indicated a possible impact on the relative orientation of the 2 leucine-rich repeat domains, and functional studies showed enhanced binding to MRAS and PPP1CB and increased MAPK signaling. ⚠︎
