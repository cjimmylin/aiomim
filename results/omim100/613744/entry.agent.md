### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene on chromosome 15q21.

### Description

SPG51 is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder resulting from deficiency of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4). Affected individuals have infantile hypotonia that progresses to spasticity, with spastic tetraplegia or spastic paraplegia, together with profound or severe intellectual disability, absent or minimal speech, inability to walk, microcephaly, stereotypic laughter, and drooling; seizures and short stature have been reported in some patients (Moreno-De-Luca et al., 2011; Abou Jamra et al., 2011; Kong et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) proposed that disruption of any one of the 4 subunits of AP-4 abolishes the function of the complex and causes a distinct autosomal recessive disorder, which they termed 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.' Kong et al. (2013) considered patients with mutations in the AP-4 subunit genes to have 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome,' a subtype of hereditary spastic paraplegia. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) reported 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with a condition described as cerebral palsy. Both were born at term after uneventful pregnancies and presented with microcephaly and hypotonia at birth or postnatally. The older sib, a female, developed spastic tetraplegia with hyperreflexia and generalized hypertonia by the first year of life, never developed speech or independent walking, and had been wheelchair-bound since 5 years of age, when generalized tonic-clonic seizures also began. Examination at age 23 showed microcephaly (-3 SD), profoundly impaired cognitive ability, drooling, and outbursts of stereotypic laughter. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss more pronounced in the frontal region and corpus callosum; EEG showed generalized theta slowing. Her brother acquired some dysarthric words and walked with support, but became wheelchair-dependent by age 9 years because of increasing spasticity; seizures began as staring spells and became generalized tonic-clonic at age 15. At age 22 years he had spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia and hyperreflexia, stereotypic laughter, drooling, jerky nystagmus, and severe cognitive deficits. Head CT showed moderate ventriculomegaly. Both sibs had dysmorphic features, including bitemporal narrowing, long narrow face with prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, long nose with wide nasal ridge, short philtrum with everted full upper vermilion, prominent antehelix, and facial hypotonia. Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) noted that the phenotype closely resembled that of a previously reported Moroccan sibship with a homozygous AP4M1 mutation. ⚠︎

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation. One of these families carried a mutation in the AP4E1 gene (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical twin girls born to first-cousin Moroccan parents; neonatal asphyxiation was noted at delivery. At 3 years of age, the twins had marked developmental retardation, microcephaly (head circumference -2 SD and -2.5 SD), inability to walk unaided, abnormal speech, abnormal circadian development, smiling or laughing for no obvious reason, drooling, and muscular hypotonia. Neither had seizures. They had a facial gestalt with a prominent bulbous nose, wide mouth, and coarse features. Neurologic examination showed spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities; both had short stature and low body weight. EEG showed diffuse slow waves, and CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy. Both twins were diagnosed with type I complex HSP. In addition, both had been vaccinated with BCG a few days after birth and presented at 9 months of age with unilaterally enlarged, inflammatory axillary lymph nodes; biopsy showed acid-fast bacilli consistent with mycobacterial infection. No abnormalities of the IL12/IFN-gamma axis or of oxidative burst pathways were found.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 in the families reported by Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) and Kong et al. (2013) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the family of Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011), the mother was a heterozygous carrier of the deletion found in homozygous state in the affected sibs, and the father was inferred to be a carrier; in the family of Kong et al. (2013), both parents were heterozygous for the mutation.

### Heterogeneity

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) found that mutations in the genes encoding other AP-4 subunits, AP4S1 and AP4B1, caused a similar phenotype. Kong et al. (2013) noted that mutations in all 4 AP-4 subunit genes had been identified in patients with autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia, with AP4B1 corresponding to SPG47, AP4M1 to SPG50, AP4E1 to SPG51, and AP4S1 to SPG52.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs, born of consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian parents, with spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy, profound intellectual disability, microcephaly, and epilepsy, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous deletion of chromosome 15q21.2 by chromosomal microarray analysis. High-resolution array analysis defined the deletion as 192 kb; it included the 5-prime ends of 2 genes, AP4E1 and SPPL2A. The deletion was confirmed by FISH. The mother was a heterozygous carrier; a paternal sample was not available, but methylation analysis was consistent with biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, and the father was inferred to be a carrier. Although Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) could not completely rule out a contribution of SPPL2A deletion, they proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main mechanism underlying the phenotype.

Using autozygosity mapping and either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing in 3 consanguineous families with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified 1 mutation in each of 3 genes encoding AP-4 subunits, including a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA), resulting in r.421_542del and p.Glu181GlyfsTer20.

In identical twin girls, born of first-cousin Moroccan parents, with complex hereditary spastic paraplegia and BCG lymphadenitis, Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X) by whole-exome sequencing; the mutation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The parents were heterozygous and a healthy sib was wildtype. The variant was not found in 1,050 controls from 51 ethnic groups or in 69 Moroccan controls. The mutation did not affect AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing in patient EBV-B cells, but AP4E1 protein was barely detectable, with a concomitant decrease in AP4M1 and AP4B1 protein levels; assembled AP-4 complex levels in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells were more than 95% lower than in controls. Kong et al. (2013) found no defects in known Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease genes or in the IL12/IFN-gamma circuit and proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease in these patients. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) subsequently identified a homozygous splice site mutation in the SPPL2A gene (c.733+1G-A) in the Moroccan twins reported by Kong et al. (2013), by whole-exome sequencing and genomewide linkage analysis, and showed that SPPL2a deficiency underlies their mycobacterial disease. In addition to the mycobacterial disease, the twins had hereditary spastic paraplegia due to the homozygous AP4E1 R1105X mutation.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 192-KB DEL [chr15:48835480-49028171 del (hg18)]

In 2 sibs, born of consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian parents, with spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy, profound intellectual disability, microcephaly, and epilepsy, Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) identified a homozygous 192-kb deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 (chr15:48835480-49028171, hg18) that included the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes. The deletion was identified by chromosomal microarray analysis and confirmed by FISH. The mother was heterozygous for the deletion, and the father was inferred to be a carrier.

**.0002 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, 4-BP DEL, 542+1GTAA [NM_007347.3:c.542+1_542+4delGTAA; r.421_542del; p.Glu181Glyfs*20]

In a consanguineous family with severe intellectual disability, absent speech, stereotypic laughter, hypotonia progressing to spastic paraplegia, microcephaly, and growth retardation, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA), resulting in deletion of r.421_542 and a frameshift (Glu181GlyfsTer20). The mutation was identified by a combination of autozygosity mapping and Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. ⚠︎

**.0003 SPASTIC PARAPLEGIA 51, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE**  
AP4E1, ARG1105TER [p.R1105X; p.Arg1105Ter]

In identical twin girls, born of first-cousin Moroccan parents, with complex hereditary spastic paraplegia, Kong et al. (2013) identified a homozygous arg1105-to-ter (R1105X) mutation in the AP4E1 gene by whole-exome sequencing, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was wildtype. The variant was not found in 1,050 controls from 51 ethnic groups or in 69 Moroccan controls. The mutation did not affect mRNA levels, but patient cells showed barely detectable AP4E1 protein, reduced levels of other AP-4 subunits, and more than 95% reduction in assembled AP-4 complex. The twins also had BCG lymphadenitis, which Kong et al. (2018) attributed to a homozygous SPPL2A splice site mutation also carried by the twins.
