### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 (SPG51) is caused by homozygous mutation in the AP4E1 gene (607244) on chromosome 15q21.2.

### Description

Autosomal recessive spastic paraplegia-51 is a complex form of hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP). Kong et al. (2013) noted that 27 patients from 9 kindreds with mutations in genes encoding subunits of the adaptor protein complex-4 (AP-4) (AP4E1, AP4B1, AP4S1, and AP4M1) had the same complex HSP phenotype. It comprised severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, progressive spastic paraplegia, growth retardation, and stereotypic laughter. They called this phenotype 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) described 2 sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family with autosomal recessive spastic tetraplegic cerebral palsy, profound intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, and white matter loss. The sibs were a female (IV-4) examined at age 23 and a male (IV-5) examined at age 22. Both were born at term after uneventful pregnancies, and both had microcephaly and hypotonia from birth or soon after. The hypotonia progressed to spastic tetraplegia with hypertonia, hyperreflexia, and Babinski signs. Both later had generalized tonic-clonic seizures, stereotypic laughter, drooling, absent normal speech, and absent sphincter control, and both became wheelchair-bound. The affected female had dysmorphic features: bitemporal narrowing, a long narrow face with a prominent pointed chin, downslanting palpebral fissures, a long nose with a wide nasal ridge, a short philtrum with a full everted upper vermilion, prominent antehelices, and facial hypotonia. Her brother's dysmorphic features were described as very similar. Her brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, cortical and cerebellar atrophy, reduced hippocampal volume, and diffuse white matter loss, which was most pronounced frontally and in the corpus callosum. Her EEG showed generalized theta slowing of the background. Her brother's CT scan showed ventriculomegaly with prominent cisterns. Eye and hearing evaluations were normal in both sibs.

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) reported 8 affected individuals from 3 consanguineous families. They had severe intellectual disability, absent speech, shy character, stereotypic laughter, and muscular hypotonia that progressed to spastic paraplegia. Other features were microcephaly, foot deformity, decreased muscle mass of the lower limbs, inability to walk, and growth retardation. The authors concluded that these findings defined a clinically recognizable syndrome caused by deficiency of the AP4 complex. In 1 of these families the disorder was caused by mutation in AP4E1, and in the other 2 families it was caused by mutations in other AP4 subunit genes (see HETEROGENEITY).

Kong et al. (2013) reported identical female twins, born to first-cousin Moroccan parents, who had complex hereditary spastic paraplegia beginning in infancy. At age 3 years they had marked developmental delay, microcephaly, inability to walk, absent speech, stereotypic laughter, drooling, and muscular hypotonia. They also had short stature, low body weight, and spastic paraplegia of the lower extremities. They did not have seizures. Their facial features were coarse, with a prominent bulbous nose and a wide mouth. CT showed atrophy of the inferior vermis with cortical atrophy, and EEG showed diffuse slow waves. At 9 months of age, after BCG vaccination, both twins developed unilateral enlarged, inflamed axillary lymph nodes, and biopsy showed acid-fast bacilli. Immune cell counts, immunoglobulins, and complement were normal, as were the IL-12/IFN-gamma axis and the oxidative burst. The authors proposed that the AP4E1 mutation was the most likely cause of the mycobacterial disease. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2018) restudied the same monozygotic twin sisters (P1 and P2) from the consanguineous Moroccan kindred at 8 years of age. Besides hereditary spastic paraplegia, they had BCG disease. After BCG vaccination at birth, they presented at 9 months of age with unilaterally enlarged inflammatory axillary lymph nodes, and biopsy confirmed acid-fast bacilli. Neonatal asphyxiation had been noted during delivery.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SPG51 is autosomal recessive. Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) found a homozygous deletion in affected sibs from a consanguineous family, and the mother and father were heterozygous carriers. Kong et al. (2013) found a homozygous mutation in twins born to consanguineous parents; both parents were heterozygous. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) found a homozygous 192-kb deletion at chromosome 15q21.2 in both affected sibs from a Palestinian-Jordanian family. They confirmed it by metaphase FISH with the RP11-147B19 probe. The mother and the older sister carried the deletion heterozygously, and the younger sib did not carry the deletion.

### Heterogeneity

Abou Jamra et al. (2011) found that the same AP4-deficiency phenotype was caused by mutations in genes encoding other AP4 subunits in 2 consanguineous families. One family carried a nonsense mutation in AP4S1 (c.124C-T, arg42-to-ter), and the other carried a frameshift mutation in AP4B1 (c.487_488insTAT, p.Glu163_Ser739delinsVal). They noted that mutations in AP4M1 and AP4E1 had recently been shown to cause cerebral palsy with severe intellectual disability, which supported a crucial role for AP4-mediated trafficking in brain development and function.

### Molecular Genetics

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) used chromosomal microarray analysis to identify a homozygous deletion on chromosome 15q21.2 in 2 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian-Jordanian family. High-resolution array mapping defined the deletion as 192 kb (chr15:48,835,480-49,028,171, hg18). It removed the 5-prime ends of the AP4E1 and SPPL2A genes, and FISH confirmed the result. FISH showed that the mother was a heterozygous carrier. SNRPN methylation analysis showed biparental inheritance of chromosome 15, which indicated that the father was also a carrier. The authors proposed that AP4E1 deficiency was the main cause of the phenotype, but they could not rule out a contribution from loss of SPPL2A.

In affected members of a consanguineous family with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic paraplegia, Abou Jamra et al. (2011) identified a homozygous splice site mutation in the AP4E1 gene (c.542+1_542+4delGTAA). The mutation caused skipping of sequence (r.421_542del) and a frameshift (p.Glu181GlyfsTer20). It was found by autozygosity mapping combined with either Sanger sequencing of candidate genes or exome sequencing. ⚠︎

Kong et al. (2013) used whole-exome sequencing in twins with SPG51 born to consanguineous Moroccan parents and identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the AP4E1 gene (R1105X). Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation. It segregated with the disorder: both parents were heterozygous, and a healthy sib was wildtype. The mutation was not found in 1,050 CEPH controls or in 69 Moroccan controls. In patient EBV-B cells, the mutation did not affect AP4E1 mRNA levels or splicing. However, AP-4-epsilon protein was barely detectable, and levels of AP-4-mu and AP-4-beta were also reduced. Assembled AP-4 complex was more than 95% lower in patient fibroblasts and EBV-B cells. The AP-4 binding partner tepsin was undetectable in patient fibroblasts.

Kong et al. (2018) studied the same Moroccan monozygotic twin sisters, born in 2009 to first-cousin parents, primarily in the context of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease. They noted that the twins also had hereditary spastic paraplegia due to the homozygous R1105X mutation in AP4E1.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Moreno-De-Luca et al. (2011) compared their family carrying the AP4E1 deletion with a Moroccan family in which Verkerk et al. had reported a homozygous AP4M1 mutation. Patients in both families had infantile hypotonia that progressed to spastic tetraplegia, microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, stereotypic laughter, drooling, ventriculomegaly, and white matter loss. Epilepsy was present in the AP4E1 patients but not in the AP4M1 patients. The authors proposed that disrupting any of the 4 AP-4 subunits abolishes the function of the complex and causes a distinct autosomal recessive 'AP-4 deficiency syndrome.'

Kong et al. (2013) noted that the R1105X mutation in AP4E1 lies close to the normal termination codon and does not trigger nonsense-mediated decay. They speculated that the resulting C-terminally truncated protein might have dominant-negative or gain-of-function activity. This could explain why their patients had mycobacterial disease when other AP-4-deficient patients, most of whom carry probable null mutations, did not. ⚠︎
