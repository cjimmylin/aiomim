### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MEHMO syndrome (MEHMO) is caused by hemizygous mutation in the EIF2S3 gene (300161) on chromosome Xp22.11.

### Description

DeLozier-Blanchet et al. (1999) described MEHMO as a new syndrome made up of mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Skopkova et al. (2017) described MEHMO as a rare X-linked syndrome characterized by profound intellectual disability, epilepsy, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. They linked it to mutations in EIF2S3, which encodes the gamma subunit of translation initiation factor eIF2, and placed it among disorders of translation initiation and the integrated stress response.

### Nomenclature

Steinmüller et al. (1998) designated the condition by the acronym MEHMO, based on its major clinical features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity.

### Clinical Features

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) described 2 brothers with severe postnatal growth retardation and severe mental retardation, extreme microcephaly, obesity developing during infancy, microgonadism, and a characteristic amphora-shaped facies. Neurologic examination showed hypertonia, hyperreflexia, nystagmus, and extremely irritable and agitated behavior. The first child died at 4.5 years; he had also had neonatal hypoglycemia and chronic constipation.

Steinmüller et al. (1998) described a previously unrecognized X-linked mental retardation syndrome in a large 3-generation family. Its clinical hallmarks were mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Affected patients had a life expectancy of less than 2 years.

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) described a child with MEHMO syndrome (mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity) who also had lactic acidosis. Histologic staining of a muscle biopsy showed mitochondrial proliferation and lipid storage. On electron microscopy, the mitochondria were abnormal and enlarged, with concentric cristae and electron-dense bodies.

Borck et al. (2012) described a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry in the maternal line in which 3 males had an X-linked intellectual disability syndrome. The syndrome consisted of moderate-to-severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. Individual features included cleft lip and palate and behavioral impairment (III:1), generalized seizures (III:2), and postpubertal microgenitalism and obesity (II:3). Brain MRI showed a thin corpus callosum and enlarged lateral ventricles. The parents I.1 and I.2 were first cousins.

Moortgat et al. (2016) described 2 unrelated families with suspected X-linked syndromic intellectual disability. The affected males had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy.

Skopkova et al. (2017) reported a Slovak boy (family 1) born to nonconsanguineous parents. Microcephaly was detected prenatally. At birth he had micropenis, cryptorchidism, and peripheral hypertonia. He developed drug-resistant partial complex seizures at 4 months, severe developmental delay, and central obesity. Non-autoimmune insulin-treated diabetes was diagnosed at 10 months. His dysmorphic features included full cheeks, large ears, downturned corners of the mouth, epiblepharon, tapered fingers, and talipes. Brain MRI showed delayed myelination that progressed with acute demyelinating changes and cerebral atrophy. A muscle biopsy showed abnormal mitochondria. Seizures and diabetes control worsened during infections. At 5 years his height was -5 SD and his head circumference was -8.4 SD.

Skopkova et al. (2017) reported a second Slovak boy (family 2) from a nonconsanguineous family. His mother was unaffected and he had no other affected relatives. His head circumference at birth was -5.1 SD. From 6 months he had therapy-resistant seizures, without an EEG correlate. He also had hypogenitalism, severe developmental delay, central obesity, and dysmorphic features. Non-autoimmune insulin-treated diabetes was present from 10 months, and MRI showed delayed myelination. At 3 years he was hypotonic, had no voluntary movements, and showed no social interaction.

Skopkova et al. (2017) restudied the German family originally reported by Steinmüller et al. (1998). The index patient was diagnosed with microcephaly prenatally and was born at 32 weeks. He had therapy-resistant seizures from 7 weeks, hypogenitalism, severe delay, and central obesity. His dysmorphic features included facial telangiectasias, edematous hands and feet, and bilateral talipes. Diabetes was diagnosed at 6 months after a ketoacidotic crisis. He died at 2 years with refractory seizures. Female carriers in the family were unaffected.

Skopkova et al. (2017) also reported a boy (family 4) of Northern European origin living in the US, born to nonconsanguineous parents. He had microcephaly, hypospadias, cryptorchidism, developmental delay, and obesity that followed a period of failure to thrive. He had no epileptic seizures and his EEG was normal. Additional features were hypotonia with peripheral spasticity, chronic lung disease, dysphagia, delayed gastric emptying, grade 5 vesicoureteral reflux, and congenital scoliosis. He did not have diabetes. ⚠︎

Gregory et al. (2019) described 3 affected males from a nonconsanguineous white European family: monozygotic twin brothers (III4, III5) and their maternal cousin (III3). They had severe recurrent hypoglycemia, short stature with GH and TSH deficiencies, and mild learning difficulties. They also had an unusual form of glucose dysregulation that fluctuated between hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and postprandial hyperglycemia. Brain MRI showed variable white matter loss and a small anterior pituitary, with a normal posterior pituitary and stalk. None had severe intellectual disability or microcephaly, and all 3 boys attended mainstream school.

Patient III3 of Gregory et al. (2019) presented with poor feeding and hypoglycemia 18 hours after birth. He had total anomalous pulmonary venous return, severe gastroesophageal reflux requiring Nissen fundoplication, GH deficiency, global developmental delay needing 1:1 support, behavioral problems, and mild hepatomegaly. MRI also showed hypoplasia of the corpus callosum and ventricular asymmetry. An oral glucose tolerance test at 8 years produced symptomatic hypoglycemia at 3 hours with inappropriate insulin, suggesting hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia.

The monozygotic twins (III4, III5) reported by Gregory et al. (2019) had intestinal lymphonodular hyperplasia. At 2 years they had hypoglycemic seizures, GH deficiency, and central hypothyroidism. They had microphallus that enlarged on GH therapy, and III4 had transient undescended testes. They had delayed speech with mild conductive hearing loss, and behavioral and mild learning difficulties. At 10 years they had impaired glucose tolerance with fluctuation between hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia and elevated HbA1c. Hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia persisted at 13.6 years. They progressed through puberty spontaneously. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Gregory et al. (2019) noted that the mother of the twins, who was a heterozygous carrier, had secondary amenorrhea with osteoporosis. Her menarche was at 13 years, her periods stopped at 16 years, and her brain MRI was normal. The mother of III3 also presented with secondary amenorrhea.

### Biochemical Features

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) found markedly reduced activities of complexes I, III, and IV of the mitochondrial electron transport chain in a muscle biopsy from a child with MEHMO syndrome. They concluded that this was the first identification of MEHMO as a mitochondrial disorder and one of the very few X-linked mitochondrial syndromes.

Skopkova et al. (2017) measured respiratory chain activity in permeabilized muscle fibers from the family 1 proband. Complex II activity was reduced (1.22 vs 3.11 nM/min/mg in controls; P = 0.009), and complex IV activity was normal.

### Inheritance

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) noted that the cause of the disorder in 2 affected brothers was unknown. They suggested recessive inheritance, either autosomal or X-linked. They considered an X-linked recessive gene especially tempting because of the importance of the X chromosome in mental retardation. ⚠︎

Steinmüller et al. (1998) described X-linked inheritance in a large 3-generation family. Borck et al. (2012), Moortgat et al. (2016), and Skopkova et al. (2017) found hemizygous EIF2S3 mutations in affected males, with heterozygous female carriers. Gregory et al. (2019) found that an EIF2S3 variant segregated in an X-linked recessive manner over 3 generations.

### Mapping

Steinmüller et al. (1998) used haplotype and 2-point linkage analyses in a large 3-generation family to map the MEHMO disease locus to Xp21.1-p22.13, in a region flanked by CYBB and DXS365. DeLozier-Blanchet et al. (1999) likewise assigned the disease locus to chromosome Xp21.1-p22.13.

Borck et al. (2012) performed targeted linkage analysis with 18 X-chromosomal STR markers in 10 members of a Moroccan Jewish family. They identified a 10.7-Mb candidate region at Xp22.11-p21.1 containing 35 annotated genes.

### Heterogeneity

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) found that the 2 brothers they described most resembled patients with Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome (BFL). However, the brothers' dwarfism and mental retardation were much more severe than in any reported BFL patient, and their neurologic and dysmorphic features differed significantly. The authors considered 2 possibilities: a particularly severe, possibly allelic, variant of BFL, or a previously undescribed disorder.

Skopkova et al. (2017) found no EIF2S3 coding variants in 2 previously reported MEHMO families: those of DeLozier-Blanchet et al. and Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002). The patient of Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) carried a tyr430-to-ter (Y430X) mutation in the SERAC1 gene. For the DeLozier-Blanchet family, the authors suggested that the defect could lie outside the regions covered by exome sequencing, or in another gene that mimics MEHMO.

Gregory et al. (2019) screened 103 patients with congenital hypopituitarism and identified no further EIF2S3 variants. The diagnoses were isolated GH deficiency, septooptic dysplasia, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism/Kallmann syndrome, and multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies. None of these patients had glucose dysregulation similar to that in the index family.

### Molecular Genetics

Borck et al. (2012) studied the 3 affected males of a Moroccan Jewish family with X-linked intellectual disability and microcephaly. By exome sequencing within the linkage region, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, they identified a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in the EIF2S3 gene (300161), resulting in an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with the disorder, and female carriers were heterozygous. It was absent from 188 Moroccan Jewish control X chromosomes, dbSNP132, 1000 Genomes, and more than 8,000 ESP alleles. Ile222 lies in the GTP-binding domain and is strictly conserved in animals. In HeLa cells, the I222T mutant pulled down substantially less eIF2-beta than wildtype. Borck et al. (2012) found no additional EIF2S3 mutations when they sequenced all 12 exons in 60 individuals from families with unspecific, potentially X-linked intellectual disability. They suggested that EIF2S3 mutations confer a specific phenotype.

Borck et al. (2012) also noted an EIF2S3 missense variant, c.451G-C (V151L), that had previously been reported from an X-linked intellectual disability resequencing project. The variant was absent from SNP databases and predicted to be damaging. In yeast, the orthologous V210L mutation lies near the Met-tRNAi binding site. It caused defects in growth, GCN4 expression, and start site selection, and overexpression of tRNAi(Met) suppressed the growth defect, suggesting impaired Met-tRNAi binding.

Moortgat et al. (2016) studied 2 families with syndromic X-linked intellectual disability by X-chromosome exome sequencing. In affected males from 1 family they identified a hemizygous missense mutation in EIF2S3, c.777T-G (I259M). In affected males from the second family they identified a hemizygous frameshift mutation, c.1394_1397del (Ile465SerfsTer4). In both families, obligate carrier females were heterozygous. Morpholino knockdown of the zebrafish ortholog recapitulated the microcephaly and short stature phenotype.

Skopkova et al. (2017) studied affected males from 3 families with MEHMO syndrome: 2 Slovak families and the German family originally reported by Steinmüller et al. (1998). By whole-exome or X-chromosome exome sequencing, they identified the same hemizygous 4-bp deletion in the last exon of EIF2S3 (c.1394_1397delTCAA) in all 3 families. The deletion causes a frameshift (Ile465SerfsTer4) that replaces the last 8 amino acids with 3 different ones. It segregated with the disease, was absent from dbSNP and ExAC, and had a CADD score of 35. Functional studies in a yeast/human chimera and in patient fibroblasts supported pathogenicity.

In the boy from family 4, who had a milder phenotype, Skopkova et al. (2017) used trio exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous missense variant in EIF2S3, c.324T-A (S108R). He had inherited it from his healthy mother. The variant changes a serine that is conserved down to Drosophila, was absent from dbSNP and ExAC, and had a CADD score of 24. However, the equivalent mutation in yeast (Asp167Arg) had no effect, and the healthy brother could not be tested. The authors therefore classified the variant as of unknown significance.

Gregory et al. (2019) studied 3 affected males of a family with hypopituitarism and glucose dysregulation. By X-exome sequencing they identified a hemizygous c.1294C-T transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in a pro432-to-ser (P432S) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the C-terminal domain of eIF2-gamma. The boys inherited the variant from their heterozygous mothers, who were sisters, and it originated from the heterozygous maternal grandmother. It segregated in an X-linked recessive manner over 3 generations, was absent from ExAC and gnomAD, and was predicted to be deleterious. In yeast, the equivalent mutation modestly impaired eIF2 function.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that all 4 index patients with the C-terminal frameshift mutation, including one from a previously reported family, had the full MEHMO syndrome. Three of them developed insulin-dependent diabetes in the first year, and none achieved head control, voluntary movement, or social interaction. Patients with missense variants (S108R, I259M, I222T) were less severely affected and had only subsets of the features. The authors proposed that more severe EIF2S3 mutations cause full MEHMO syndrome and that less deleterious ones cause a milder form.

Gregory et al. (2019) noted that their patients with the P432S variant lacked the microcephaly, epilepsy, and obesity that are prominent in previously reported MEHMO patients, and that their neurodevelopmental phenotype was much milder. They proposed that the milder loss of function caused by P432S produces a phenotype distinct from classic MEHMO syndrome. They also suggested that untreated hypoketotic hypoglycemia may have contributed to the more severe neurodevelopmental impairment and seizures in previously reported cases.

### Pathogenesis

Borck et al. (2012) expressed myc/His6-tagged human eIF2-gamma carrying the I222T mutation in HeLa cells. The mutant pulled down substantially less endogenous eIF2-beta than wildtype without affecting eIF2-alpha binding, indicating disrupted eIF2 complex integrity. In structural modeling on archaeal aIF2, the corresponding residue lay in the hydrophobic cleft that binds helix alpha-1 of eIF2-beta. Borck et al. (2012) also tested the analogous mutations in yeast eIF2-gamma. V281T impaired eIF2-beta binding without affecting growth, whereas V281K nearly abolished eIF2-beta binding and impaired growth. Both mutations increased GCN4-lacZ expression (2.5- and 40-fold, respectively) and raised the UUG/AUG initiation ratio 2- to 3-fold. Overexpression of eIF2-beta partially suppressed these defects. The authors concluded that the mutation impairs eIF2 integrity, ternary complex function, and start codon fidelity. ⚠︎

Skopkova et al. (2017) modeled the Ile465SerfsTer4 mutation in a yeast/human eIF2-gamma chimera. The mutant was expressed at normal protein levels and caused slow growth that was worse at 37 degrees C. It derepressed GCN4-lacZ expression nearly 3-fold and doubled the UUG/AUG initiation ratio, effects comparable to those of the Val281Thr mutation associated with X-linked intellectual disability. These results indicated that the C-terminal mutation impairs eIF2 function. In patient fibroblasts the C-terminally truncated eIF2-gamma protein was produced. In fibroblasts from a family 3 patient, DDIT3 transcript levels were 2.5-fold higher than in controls and CHOP protein was also elevated, indicating activation of the integrated stress response.

Skopkova et al. (2017) proposed that EIF2S3 mutations, like mutations in the EIF2B genes and PPP1R15B, reduce the activity or availability of the eIF2 ternary complex. They suggested that this leads to insufficient protein synthesis and to chronically elevated ATF4 and CHOP, causing apoptosis. They also noted that, as in vanishing white matter disease, the condition of their patient 1 worsened markedly during illness.

Gregory et al. (2019) introduced the yeast equivalent of P432S (P490S, in a locally humanized eIF2-gamma) into yeast. The mutation did not affect growth but caused a modest (about 1.7-fold), significant increase in GCN4-lacZ expression, compared with about 11-fold for I318M, the yeast equivalent of the I259M mutation. It also increased UUG start codon initiation from about 1.6% to 3% of AUG initiation. The authors concluded that P432S modestly impairs eIF2 function and may cause disease by altering the fidelity of start site selection.

By in situ hybridization, Gregory et al. (2019) found EIF2S3 expression at Carnegie stages 20 and 23 in the human embryonic ventral hypothalamus, Rathke's pouch, anterior and posterior pituitary, nasal epithelium progenitors, and retina. EIF2S3 was also expressed in the islets of Langerhans of a 13-week fetal pancreas. In a human pancreatic beta-cell line (1.1B4), shRNA knockdown of EIF2S3 by 82% significantly increased basal and cytokine-stimulated caspase activity, suggesting increased apoptosis.

### Clinical Management

Gregory et al. (2019) treated their patients with recombinant human GH, thyroxine, and diazoxide with chlorothiazide. Diazoxide and chlorothiazide were stopped when the hyperinsulinism resolved at 7 years, but the twins later had postprandial hyperglycemia and variable fasting hypoglycemia. The boys grew well on GH. The authors suggested that early molecular diagnosis may prevent significant morbidity and neurodevelopmental delay.

### Population Genetics

The 3 families carrying the c.1394_1397delTCAA mutation in EIF2S3 reported no common ancestors, and identity-by-descent analysis excluded relationships closer than about fourth-degree cousins. However, Skopkova et al. (2017) found that comparison of sequencing data and haplotypes suggested a founder effect with more distant relatedness.

### Animal Model

Moortgat et al. (2016) found that morpholino-based knockdown of eif2s3, the zebrafish ortholog of EIF2S3, recapitulated the microcephaly and short stature seen in patients. They considered this to support the pathogenicity of the variants they identified.
