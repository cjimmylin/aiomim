### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MEHMO syndrome (MEHMO) is caused by hemizygous mutation in the EIF2S3 gene (300161) on chromosome Xp22.11.

### Description

DeLozier-Blanchet et al. (1999) described MEHMO as a new syndrome made up of mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Skopkova et al. (2017) described MEHMO as a rare X-linked syndrome characterized by profound intellectual disability, epilepsy, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. They linked it to mutations in EIF2S3, which encodes the gamma subunit of translation initiation factor eIF2. They placed it among disorders of translation initiation and the integrated stress response.

### Nomenclature

Steinmüller et al. (1998) named the condition with the acronym MEHMO, taken from its major clinical features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity.

### Clinical Features

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) described 2 brothers with severe postnatal growth retardation, severe mental retardation, extreme microcephaly, obesity that developed during infancy, microgonadism, and a characteristic amphora-shaped facies. Neurologic examination showed hypertonia, hyperreflexia, nystagmus, and extremely irritable and agitated behavior. The first child died at 4.5 years. He had also had neonatal hypoglycemia and chronic constipation.

Steinmüller et al. (1998) described a previously unrecognized X-linked mental retardation syndrome in a large 3-generation family. Its clinical hallmarks were mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity. Affected patients had a life expectancy of less than 2 years.

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) described a child with MEHMO syndrome who also had lactic acidosis. Histologic staining of a muscle biopsy showed mitochondrial proliferation and lipid storage. Electron microscopy showed abnormal, enlarged mitochondria with concentric cristae and electron-dense bodies.

Borck et al. (2012) described a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry in the maternal line. In this family, 3 males had an X-linked intellectual disability syndrome with moderate-to-severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. Individual features differed among the 3 males. Patient III:1 had cleft lip and palate and behavioral impairment, patient III:2 had generalized seizures, and patient II:3 had postpubertal microgenitalism and obesity. Brain MRI showed a thin corpus callosum and enlarged lateral ventricles. The parents I.1 and I.2 were first cousins.

Moortgat et al. (2016) described 2 unrelated families with suspected X-linked syndromic intellectual disability. The affected males had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy.

Skopkova et al. (2017) reported a Slovak boy (family 1) born to nonconsanguineous parents. Microcephaly was detected prenatally. At birth he had micropenis, cryptorchidism, and peripheral hypertonia. Drug-resistant partial complex seizures began at 4 months. He went on to have severe developmental delay and central obesity, and non-autoimmune insulin-treated diabetes was diagnosed at 10 months. His dysmorphic features included full cheeks, large ears, downturned corners of the mouth, epiblepharon, tapered fingers, and talipes. Brain MRI showed delayed myelination that progressed with acute demyelinating changes and cerebral atrophy. Muscle biopsy showed abnormal mitochondria. His seizures and diabetes control worsened during infections. At 5 years his height was -5 SD and his head circumference was -8.4 SD.

Skopkova et al. (2017) reported a second Slovak boy (family 2) from a nonconsanguineous family. His mother was unaffected and he had no other affected relatives. His head circumference at birth was -5.1 SD. Therapy-resistant seizures began at 6 months and had no EEG correlate. He had hypogenitalism, severe developmental delay, central obesity, and dysmorphic features. Non-autoimmune insulin-treated diabetes was present from 10 months, and MRI showed delayed myelination. At 3 years he was hypotonic, had no voluntary movements, and showed no social interaction.

Skopkova et al. (2017) restudied the German family originally reported by Steinmüller et al. (1998). Microcephaly was diagnosed prenatally in the index patient, who was born at 32 weeks. Therapy-resistant seizures began at 7 weeks, and he had hypogenitalism, severe delay, and central obesity. His dysmorphic features included facial telangiectasias, edematous hands and feet, and bilateral talipes. Diabetes was diagnosed at 6 months after a ketoacidotic crisis. He died at 2 years with refractory seizures. Female carriers in the family were unaffected.

Skopkova et al. (2017) also reported a boy (family 4) of Northern European origin living in the US, born to nonconsanguineous parents. He had microcephaly, hypospadias, cryptorchidism, and developmental delay. Obesity developed after a period of failure to thrive. He had no epileptic seizures and his EEG was normal. Other features were hypotonia with peripheral spasticity, chronic lung disease, dysphagia, delayed gastric emptying, grade 5 vesicoureteral reflux, and congenital scoliosis. He did not have diabetes. ⚠︎

Gregory et al. (2019) described 3 affected males from a nonconsanguineous white European family: monozygotic twin brothers (III4 and III5) and their maternal cousin (III3). All 3 had severe recurrent hypoglycemia, short stature with GH and TSH deficiencies, and mild learning difficulties. They also had an unusual form of glucose dysregulation that fluctuated between hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and postprandial hyperglycemia. Brain MRI showed variable white matter loss and a small anterior pituitary, with a normal posterior pituitary and stalk. None of the boys had severe intellectual disability or microcephaly, and all 3 attended mainstream school.

Patient III3 presented with poor feeding and hypoglycemia 18 hours after birth (Gregory et al., 2019). He had total anomalous pulmonary venous return and severe gastroesophageal reflux that required Nissen fundoplication. He also had GH deficiency, global developmental delay needing 1:1 support, behavioral problems, and mild hepatomegaly. MRI also showed hypoplasia of the corpus callosum and ventricular asymmetry. An oral glucose tolerance test at 8 years showed symptomatic hypoglycemia at 3 hours with inappropriate insulin, which suggested hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia.

The monozygotic twins (III4 and III5) had intestinal lymphonodular hyperplasia (Gregory et al., 2019). At 2 years they had hypoglycemic seizures, GH deficiency, and central hypothyroidism. Their microphallus enlarged on GH therapy, and III4 had transiently undescended testes. They had delayed speech with mild conductive hearing loss, as well as behavioral and mild learning difficulties. At 10 years they had impaired glucose tolerance, with fluctuation between hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia and elevated HbA1c. Hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia persisted at 13.6 years. Both twins went through puberty spontaneously. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Gregory et al. (2019) noted that the mother of the twins in their family had secondary amenorrhea with osteoporosis. Her menarche was at 13 years, her periods stopped at 16 years, and her brain MRI was normal. The mother of patient III3 also had secondary amenorrhea.

### Biochemical Features

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) found markedly reduced activities of complexes I, III, and IV of the mitochondrial electron transport chain in a muscle biopsy from a child with MEHMO syndrome. They concluded that this was the first identification of MEHMO as a mitochondrial disorder, and one of the very few X-linked mitochondrial syndromes.

Skopkova et al. (2017) measured respiratory chain activity in permeabilized muscle fibers from the family 1 proband. Complex II activity was reduced (1.22 vs 3.11 nM/min/mg in controls; P = 0.009), whereas complex IV activity was normal.

### Inheritance

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) suggested that the disorder in 2 affected brothers was recessive, either autosomal or X-linked. They considered X-linked recessive inheritance especially tempting because of the importance of the X chromosome in mental retardation. Steinmüller et al. (1998) described an X-linked syndrome in a large 3-generation family. In that family, restudied by Skopkova et al. (2017), female carriers were unaffected. Gregory et al. (2019) found that an EIF2S3 mutation segregated in an X-linked recessive manner over 3 generations of a family.

### Mapping

Steinmüller et al. (1998) performed haplotype and 2-point linkage analyses in a large 3-generation family. They mapped the MEHMO disease locus to Xp21.1-p22.13, in a region flanked by CYBB and DXS365. DeLozier-Blanchet et al. (1999) also assigned the MEHMO locus to Xp21.1-p22.13.

Borck et al. (2012) studied a Moroccan Jewish family with X-linked intellectual disability. Targeted linkage analysis with 18 X-chromosomal STR markers in 10 family members identified a 10.7-Mb candidate region at Xp22.11-p21.1 containing 35 annotated genes.

### Heterogeneity

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) found that the brothers they described most resembled patients with Borjeson-Forssman-Lehmann (BFL) syndrome. However, the brothers' dwarfism and mental retardation were much more severe than in any reported BFL patient, and their neurologic and dysmorphic features differed significantly. The authors considered 2 possibilities: a particularly severe, possibly allelic, variant of BFL syndrome, or a previously undescribed disorder.

Skopkova et al. (2017) found no EIF2S3 coding variants in 2 previously reported MEHMO families: the family of DeLozier-Blanchet et al. and the patient of Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002). The Leshinsky-Silver patient carried a tyr430-to-ter (Y430X) mutation in the SERAC1 gene. For the DeLozier-Blanchet family, the authors suggested that the defect could lie outside the regions covered by exome sequencing, or in another gene that mimics MEHMO.

Gregory et al. (2019) screened 103 patients with congenital hypopituitarism and identified no further EIF2S3 variants. The patients had isolated GH deficiency, septooptic dysplasia, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism/Kallmann syndrome, or multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies. None had glucose dysregulation similar to that in the index family.

### Molecular Genetics

Borck et al. (2012) studied the 3 affected males of a Moroccan Jewish family with X-linked intellectual disability and microcephaly. Exome sequencing within the linkage region identified a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The mutation cosegregated with the disorder, and female carriers were heterozygous. It was absent from 188 Moroccan Jewish control X chromosomes, dbSNP132, 1000 Genomes, and more than 8,000 ESP alleles. Ile222 lies in the GTP-binding domain and is strictly conserved in animals. Borck et al. (2012) sequenced all 12 exons of EIF2S3 in 60 individuals from families with unspecific, potentially X-linked intellectual disability and found no additional mutations. They took this to suggest that EIF2S3 mutations confer a specific phenotype. Borck et al. (2012) also noted an EIF2S3 missense variant, c.451G-C (V151L), previously reported in an X-linked intellectual disability resequencing project. The variant was absent from SNP databases and predicted to be damaging. The orthologous yeast mutation (V210L) lies near the Met-tRNAi binding site and caused defects in growth, GCN4 expression, and start site selection.

Moortgat et al. (2016) used X-chromosome exome sequencing in 2 unrelated families with syndromic X-linked intellectual disability. In affected males from one family, they identified a hemizygous missense mutation in the EIF2S3 gene, c.777T-G (I259M). In affected males from the other family, they identified a hemizygous frameshift mutation, c.1394_1397del (ile465serfsTer4). In both families, obligate carrier females were heterozygous.

Skopkova et al. (2017) studied affected males from 3 families with MEHMO syndrome: 2 Slovak families and the German family originally reported by Steinmüller et al. (1998). Whole-exome or X-chromosome exome sequencing identified the same hemizygous 4-bp deletion in the last exon of EIF2S3 (c.1394_1397delTCAA). The deletion causes a frameshift (ile465serfsTer4) that replaces the last 8 amino acids with 3 different ones. It segregated with the disease, was absent from dbSNP and ExAC, and had a CADD score of 35. Trio exome sequencing in the more mildly affected boy from family 4 identified a hemizygous c.324T-A transversion in EIF2S3, resulting in a ser108-to-arg (S108R) substitution, which he inherited from his healthy mother. Ser108 is conserved down to Drosophila, and the variant was absent from dbSNP and ExAC, with a CADD score of 24. However, the equivalent yeast mutation had no effect, and the healthy brother could not be tested. The authors therefore classified S108R as a variant of unknown significance.

Gregory et al. (2019) used X-exome sequencing in the 3 affected males of a family with hypopituitarism and glucose dysregulation. They identified a hemizygous c.1294C-T transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in a pro432-to-ser (P432S) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the C-terminal domain of eIF2-gamma. The boys inherited the variant from their heterozygous mothers, who were sisters, and it originated from the heterozygous maternal grandmother. It segregated in an X-linked recessive manner over 3 generations, was absent from ExAC and gnomAD, and was predicted to be deleterious. In yeast, the equivalent P490S mutation did not affect growth. It did cause a modest but significant (about 1.7-fold) increase in GCN4-lacZ expression and increased UUG start codon initiation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that all 4 index patients with the C-terminal frameshift mutation had full MEHMO syndrome; these included patients from a previously reported family. Three of them developed insulin-dependent diabetes in the first year, and none achieved head control, voluntary movement, or social interaction. Patients with missense mutations (S108R, I259M, I222T) were less severely affected and had only subsets of the features. The authors proposed that more severe EIF2S3 mutations cause full MEHMO syndrome and less deleterious ones cause a milder form.

Gregory et al. (2019) noted that their patients with the P432S mutation did not have the microcephaly, epilepsy, and obesity that are prominent in previously reported MEHMO patients. Their neurodevelopmental phenotype was also much milder. The authors proposed that the milder loss of function of P432S produces a phenotype distinct from classic MEHMO syndrome. They also suggested that untreated hypoketotic hypoglycemia may have contributed to the more severe neurodevelopmental impairment and seizures in previously reported cases.

### Pathogenesis

Borck et al. (2012) expressed myc/His6-tagged human eIF2-gamma carrying I222T in HeLa cells. The mutant protein pulled down substantially less endogenous eIF2-beta than wildtype, without affecting eIF2-alpha binding, indicating that the mutation disrupts eIF2 complex integrity. Structural modeling based on archaeal aIF2 placed the corresponding residue in the hydrophobic cleft that binds helix alpha-1 of eIF2-beta.

Skopkova et al. (2017) modeled the ile465serfsTer4 mutation in a yeast/human eIF2-gamma chimera. The mutant chimera was expressed at normal protein levels but caused slow growth, which was worse at 37 degrees C. It derepressed GCN4-lacZ expression nearly 3-fold and doubled the UUG/AUG initiation ratio. These effects were comparable to those of the X-linked intellectual disability-associated Val281Thr mutation and indicated that the C-terminal mutation impairs eIF2 function. Patient fibroblasts produced the C-terminally truncated eIF2-gamma protein. In fibroblasts from a family 3 patient, DDIT3 transcript levels were 2.5-fold higher than in controls and CHOP protein was also elevated, indicating activation of the integrated stress response. Skopkova et al. (2017) proposed that EIF2S3 mutations, like mutations in the EIF2B genes and PPP1R15B, reduce the activity or availability of the eIF2 ternary complex. They suggested that this leads to insufficient protein synthesis and chronically elevated ATF4 and CHOP, which cause apoptosis. They also noted that patient 1's condition worsened markedly during illness, as in vanishing white matter disease.

Gregory et al. (2019) studied the human P432S mutation in yeast through its equivalent, P490S, in a locally humanized eIF2-gamma. The mutation increased GCN4-lacZ expression about 1.7-fold, compared with about 11-fold for the equivalent of I259M. It also increased UUG start codon initiation from about 1.6% to 3% of AUG. The authors concluded that P432S modestly impairs eIF2 function and may cause disease by altering the fidelity of start site selection. By in situ hybridization, EIF2S3 was expressed at Carnegie stages 20 and 23 in the human embryonic ventral hypothalamus, Rathke's pouch, anterior and posterior pituitary, nasal epithelium progenitors, and retina. It was also expressed in the islets of Langerhans of a 13-week fetal pancreas. In a human pancreatic beta-cell line (1.1B4), shRNA knockdown of EIF2S3 by 82% significantly increased basal and cytokine-stimulated caspase activity, suggesting increased apoptosis.

### Clinical Management

Gregory et al. (2019) treated their patients with recombinant human GH, thyroxine, and diazoxide with chlorothiazide. Diazoxide and chlorothiazide were stopped when the hyperinsulinism resolved at 7 years. The twins later had postprandial hyperglycemia and variable fasting hypoglycemia. The boys grew well on GH. The authors suggested that early molecular diagnosis may prevent significant morbidity and neurodevelopmental delay.

### Population Genetics

The 3 families carrying the c.1394_1397delTCAA mutation reported no common ancestors, and identity-by-descent analysis excluded relationships closer than about fourth-degree cousins. However, Skopkova et al. (2017) compared sequencing data and haplotypes and found that they suggested a founder effect with more distant relatedness. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Borck et al. (2012) studied yeast eIF2-gamma carrying the V281T mutation, which is analogous to human I222T. V281T impaired eIF2-beta binding without affecting growth, whereas V281K nearly abolished eIF2-beta binding and impaired growth. Both mutations increased GCN4-lacZ expression (2.5- and 40-fold, respectively) and raised the UUG/AUG initiation ratio 2- to 3-fold. Overexpression of eIF2-beta partially suppressed these defects. The authors concluded that the mutation impairs eIF2 integrity, ternary complex function, and start codon fidelity. ⚠︎

Moortgat et al. (2016) found that morpholino-based knockdown of the zebrafish EIF2S3 ortholog (eif2s3) recapitulated the human microcephaly and short stature phenotype, supporting the pathogenicity of the identified variants.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE222THR [c.665T>C; p.I222T]

Borck et al. (2012) studied the 3 affected males of a Moroccan Jewish family with X-linked intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. They identified a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing within the linkage region and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It cosegregated with the disorder, and female carriers were heterozygous. It was absent from 188 Moroccan Jewish control X chromosomes, dbSNP132, 1000 Genomes, and more than 8,000 ESP alleles. Ile222 lies in the GTP-binding domain and is strictly conserved in animals. Tagged human eIF2-gamma carrying I222T, expressed in HeLa cells, pulled down substantially less endogenous eIF2-beta than wildtype, without affecting eIF2-alpha binding.

Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that patients with missense mutations, including I222T, were less severely affected than patients with the C-terminal frameshift mutation and had only subsets of the MEHMO features.

**.0002 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, VAL151LEU [c.451G>C; p.V151L]

Borck et al. (2012) noted an EIF2S3 missense variant, c.451G-C (V151L), previously reported in an X-linked intellectual disability resequencing project. The variant was absent from SNP databases and predicted to be damaging. The orthologous yeast mutation, V210L, lies near the Met-tRNAi binding site. It caused defects in growth, GCN4 expression, and start site selection. Overexpression of tRNAi(Met) suppressed the growth defect, suggesting impaired Met-tRNAi binding.

**.0003 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE259MET [c.777T>G; p.I259M]
