### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that MEHMO syndrome (MEHMO) is caused by hemizygous mutation in the EIF2S3 gene on chromosome Xp22.11.

### Description

MEHMO syndrome is a rare X-linked intellectual disability syndrome whose name stands for its cardinal features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity (Steinmüller et al., 1998; Skopkova et al., 2017). Affected males can also have early-onset insulin-dependent diabetes, growth retardation, and typical facial dysmorphism, and life expectancy may be short (Steinmüller et al., 1998; Skopkova et al., 2017). The syndrome results from mutations in EIF2S3, which encodes the gamma subunit of translation initiation factor eIF2. Missense mutations give a milder phenotype that shows only some of the features (Skopkova et al., 2017; Gregory et al., 2019).

### Nomenclature

Steinmüller et al. (1998) introduced the acronym MEHMO, from the major clinical features: mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity.

### Clinical Features

Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989) described 2 brothers with a severe syndrome of postnatal growth and mental retardation. Features included extreme microcephaly, obesity that developed during infancy, microgonadism, and a characteristic amphora-shaped face. The neurologic examination was highly abnormal, with hypertonia, hyperreflexia, nystagmus, and extremely irritable and agitated behavior. The first child died at 4.5 years of age and had also had neonatal hypoglycemia and chronic constipation. The authors noted that the brothers most resembled patients with Börjeson-Forssman-Lehmann syndrome, but their dwarfism and mental retardation were much more severe, and the authors considered either autosomal or X-linked recessive inheritance.

Steinmüller et al. (1998) described a previously unrecognized X-linked mental retardation syndrome in a large 3-generation family. Its hallmarks were mental retardation, epileptic seizures, hypogonadism and hypogenitalism, microcephaly, and obesity, and patients had a life expectancy of less than 2 years.

Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) reported a child with MEHMO syndrome and lactic acidosis. A muscle biopsy showed markedly reduced activities of complexes 1, 3, and 4 of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Histologic staining showed mitochondrial proliferation and lipid storage, and electron microscopy showed abnormal, enlarged mitochondria with concentric cristae and electron-dense bodies. The authors proposed MEHMO as an X-linked mitochondrial disorder.

Borck et al. (2012) studied a family of Moroccan Jewish ancestry in the maternal line in which 3 males had an intellectual disability syndrome with apparent X-linked inheritance. The maternal grandparents were first cousins. Features included moderate-to-severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, and facial dysmorphism. Each affected individual also had unique features: one had cleft lip and palate and behavioral impairment, one had generalized seizures, and one had postpubertal microgenitalism and obesity. Brain MRI showed a thin corpus callosum and enlarged lateral ventricles. ⚠︎

Moortgat et al. (2016) reported affected males from 2 unrelated families with suspected X-linked inheritance. They had severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy.

Skopkova et al. (2017) studied 4 families with MEHMO syndrome. The index patient in family 1 was born to nonconsanguineous Slovak parents. Microcephaly was diagnosed prenatally at 28 weeks' gestation, and micropenis, cryptorchidism, and peripheral hypertonia were noted at birth. He had partial complex seizures from 4 months of age that resisted anticonvulsants, severe mental and motor delay, central obesity, and dysmorphic features. These included full cheeks, large ears, downturned corners of the mouth, epiblepharon, tapered fingers, and talipes. Non-autoimmune diabetes mellitus was diagnosed at 10 months of age and treated with insulin. Brain MRI showed delayed myelination and, at 19 months, acute demyelinating changes and cerebral atrophy. During intercurrent infections his seizures worsened and his diabetes control deteriorated. Muscle biopsy showed abnormal mitochondria and decreased complex II activity with normal complex IV activity. At 5 years his height was 88 cm (-5 SD) and his head circumference 39 cm (-8.4 SD).

The index patient in family 2 of Skopkova et al. (2017), from a nonconsanguineous Slovak family, had microcephaly and therapy-resistant seizures from 6 months of age. He also had hypogenitalism, severe mental and motor delay, central obesity, dysmorphic features very similar to those of the family 1 proband, and non-autoimmune diabetes treated with insulin from 10 months. Family 3 was the family originally reported by Steinmüller et al. (1998). Its index patient was diagnosed prenatally with microcephaly. He had therapy-resistant seizures from 7 weeks of age, and diabetes was diagnosed at 6 months during a ketoacidotic crisis. He had permanent seizures toward the end of his life at 2 years. Female mutation carriers in family 3 were unaffected. The proband of family 4 was born to nonconsanguineous parents of Northern European origin living in the US. He had microcephaly, hypospadias and cryptorchidism, developmental delay, and obesity, but no epileptic seizures, and his EEG was normal.

Gregory et al. (2019) reported 3 affected males from a nonconsanguineous white European pedigree: monozygotic twin brothers and their maternal cousin. They had severe recurrent hypoglycemia, short stature with growth hormone and TSH deficiencies, and an unusual form of glucose dysregulation that alternated between hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and postprandial hyperglycemia. Brain MRI showed variable white matter loss and a small anterior pituitary. The twins had a microphallus, and in 1 twin the testes were small and undescended but descended spontaneously by 2 years of age. Both twins nevertheless went through puberty spontaneously. The boys did not have severe intellectual disability or microcephaly, and all attended mainstream school. The authors noted that the boys lacked the microcephaly, epilepsy, and obesity typical of previously reported MEHMO patients.

### Inheritance

MEHMO syndrome shows X-linked recessive inheritance. Moortgat et al. (2016) found EIF2S3 mutations in the hemizygous state in affected males and in the heterozygous state in obligate female carriers. Skopkova et al. (2017) found that female mutation carriers in the Steinmüller family were unaffected. In the family of Gregory et al. (2019), the mutation segregated in an X-linked recessive manner across 3 generations.

### Mapping

Steinmüller et al. (1998) performed haplotype and 2-point linkage analyses in a large 3-generation family and mapped the MEHMO locus to Xp21.1-p22.13, in a region flanked by CYBB and DXS365 (see also DeLozier-Blanchet et al., 1999).

In the Moroccan Jewish family, Borck et al. (2012) identified a 10.7-Mb candidate region at Xp22.11-p21.1 containing 35 annotated genes by targeted linkage analysis.

### Heterogeneity

Skopkova et al. (2017) found no EIF2S3 mutation in 2 previously reported families. The patient reported by Leshinsky-Silver et al. (2002) carried a deleterious p.Tyr430X mutation in the SERAC1 gene. In the family reported by Delozier-Blanchet et al. (1989), the authors suggested that the genetic defect might lie outside the EIF2S3 region examined by exome sequencing, or that a mutation in another gene might have caused a phenotype mimicking MEHMO syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

Borck et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in an affected male from the Moroccan Jewish family and identified a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in the EIF2S3 gene, predicted to cause an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution in eIF2-gamma. The mutation cosegregated with the disorder in the family and was absent from 188 X chromosomes from Moroccan Jewish controls. Expressed in HeLa cells, the I222T mutant bound substantially less eIF2-beta than wildtype. Yeast eIF2-gamma carrying mutations at the corresponding residue, val281, also had impaired eIF2-beta binding, derepressed GCN4 expression, and reduced fidelity of start codon selection. Sequencing EIF2S3 in 60 individuals from families with nonspecific, potentially X-linked intellectual disability found no further mutations.

Moortgat et al. (2016) performed X-chromosome exome sequencing in 2 unrelated families. They identified a missense mutation (c.777T-G; I259M) and a frameshift mutation (c.1394_1397del; Ile465SerfsTer4) in the EIF2S3 gene. The mutations were hemizygous in affected males and heterozygous in obligate female carriers. Morpholino knockdown of the zebrafish ortholog reproduced the microcephaly and short stature.

Skopkova et al. (2017) performed whole-exome or X-chromosome exome sequencing in 4 families with MEHMO syndrome. Families 1, 2, and 3 carried the same hemizygous 4-bp deletion in the EIF2S3 gene (c.1394_1397delTCAA), which cosegregated with the disease and was predicted to cause a frameshift and premature stop (Ile465SerfsTer4). The deletion replaces the last 8 amino acids of the protein with 3 different residues. In patient fibroblasts the truncated protein was produced. In a yeast/human chimeric eIF2-gamma, the mutation caused slow growth, derepressed GCN4 expression, and increased initiation at UUG codons. Patient fibroblasts also showed increased DDIT3 transcript and CHOP protein levels. The patient in family 4 carried a c.324T-A change (S108R) inherited from his healthy mother. The corresponding yeast mutation had no detectable effect, and the authors classified this change as a variant of unknown significance. ⚠︎

In 3 affected males from a family with hypopituitarism and glucose dysregulation, Gregory et al. (2019) used X-chromosome exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous c.1294C-T transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in a pro432-to-ser (P432S) substitution. The mutation was inherited from the boys' heterozygous mothers, who were sisters, and was absent from the ExAC and gnomAD databases. The corresponding yeast mutation (P490S) modestly increased GCN4-lacZ expression and UUG initiation. In a pancreatic cell line, EIF2S3 knockdown increased caspase activity. Screening 103 patients with congenital hypopituitarism found no further EIF2S3 variants.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Skopkova et al. (2017) noted that all 4 index patients with the C-terminal frameshift mutation had the full MEHMO phenotype, and 3 of them developed insulin-dependent diabetes in the first year of life. Patients with missense mutations were less severely affected and had only subsets of the features. In yeast, the frameshift mutation impaired eIF2-gamma function more than the missense mutations tested. The authors proposed that more severe EIF2S3 mutations cause the full MEHMO phenotype, whereas less deleterious mutations cause a milder form.

Gregory et al. (2019) concluded that the P432S mutation causes a milder loss of function than previously reported EIF2S3 mutations. They proposed that it gives rise to a phenotype distinct from classical MEHMO syndrome, characterized by hypopituitarism and glucose dysregulation.

### Pathogenesis

Borck et al. (2012) concluded that the I222T mutation disrupts the integrity of the eIF2 complex by impairing eIF2-beta binding to eIF2-gamma. They proposed that partial loss of eIF2 function, and the resulting impairment of translation initiation, underlies the disorder.

Skopkova et al. (2017) found significantly increased DDIT3 transcript (2.5-fold) and CHOP protein levels in fibroblasts from a patient with the frameshift mutation. They concluded that the mutation activates the integrated stress response and that MEHMO syndrome belongs to the group of disorders caused by dysregulation of translation or of the unfolded protein response.

Gregory et al. (2019) found EIF2S3 expression in the developing human hypothalamus, pituitary, and pancreatic islets of Langerhans. They suggested that untreated hypoglycemia in previously reported cases may have contributed to their more severe neurologic impairment and seizures.

### Clinical Management

Gregory et al. (2019) suggested that early identification of patients with EIF2S3 mutations through rapid molecular diagnosis may prevent significant morbidity.

### Population Genetics

The 3 families of Skopkova et al. (2017) that carried the same EIF2S3 4-bp deletion knew of no common ancestors. Identity-by-descent analysis of families 1 and 3 found no shared X-chromosome regions larger than 0.5 cM, which excluded with high probability a relationship closer than fourth-degree cousins. Further comparison of the sequencing data suggested a founder effect with more distant relatedness. ⚠︎

### Animal Model

Moortgat et al. (2016) found that morpholino knockdown of eif2s3, the zebrafish ortholog of EIF2S3, reproduced the microcephaly and short stature seen in affected humans.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE222THR [c.665T>C; p.Ile222Thr]

In 3 affected males from a family of Moroccan Jewish maternal ancestry with intellectual disability, microcephaly, and short stature, Borck et al. (2012) identified a hemizygous c.665T-C transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile222-to-thr (I222T) substitution in the GTP-binding domain of eIF2-gamma. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and cosegregated with the disorder. It was absent from 188 control X chromosomes of Moroccan Jewish origin, dbSNP132, the 1000 Genomes data, and the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project. In HeLa cells, the mutant protein showed reduced binding to eIF2-beta. Mutations at the corresponding yeast residue impaired eIF2-beta binding and translation initiation.

**.0002 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, ILE259MET [c.777T>G; p.Ile259Met]

In affected males from a family with severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy, Moortgat et al. (2016) used X-chromosome exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous c.777T-G transversion in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in an ile259-to-met (I259M) substitution. Obligate female carriers were heterozygous.

Gregory et al. (2019) found that the corresponding yeast mutation (I318M) increased GCN4-lacZ expression nearly 11-fold.

**.0003 MEHMO SYNDROME**  
EIF2S3, 4-BP DEL, 1394TCAA [c.1394_1397delTCAA; p.Ile465Serfs*4]

In affected males from a family with severe intellectual disability, microcephaly, growth retardation, and epilepsy, Moortgat et al. (2016) identified a hemizygous 4-bp deletion (c.1394_1397del) in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in a frameshift (Ile465SerfsTer4). Obligate female carriers were heterozygous.

In affected males from 3 families with MEHMO syndrome, 2 from Slovakia and the family originally reported by Steinmüller et al. (1998), Skopkova et al. (2017) identified a hemizygous 4-bp deletion (c.1394_1397delTCAA) in the last exon of the EIF2S3 gene. The deletion was predicted to cause a frameshift and premature stop (Ile465SerfsTer4) that replaces the last 8 amino acids with 3 different residues. It cosegregated with the disease, was absent from dbSNP147 and ExAC, and had a CADD score of 35. The truncated protein was detected in patient fibroblasts, which also showed increased DDIT3 and CHOP levels. In a yeast/human chimeric eIF2-gamma, the mutation caused slow growth, derepressed GCN4 expression, and increased UUG initiation. ⚠︎

**.0004 MEHMO SYNDROME, MILD FORM WITH HYPOPITUITARISM AND GLUCOSE DYSREGULATION**  
EIF2S3, PRO432SER [c.1294C>T; p.Pro432Ser]

In 3 related males (monozygotic twin brothers and their maternal cousin) from a nonconsanguineous white European family with hypopituitarism, glucose dysregulation, and mild learning difficulties, Gregory et al. (2019) used X-chromosome exome sequencing to identify a hemizygous c.1294C-T transition in the EIF2S3 gene, resulting in a pro432-to-ser (P432S) substitution at a highly conserved residue in the C-terminal domain. The variant was inherited from the boys' heterozygous mothers and was absent from ExAC and gnomAD. In yeast, the corresponding P490S mutation modestly increased GCN4-lacZ expression (about 1.7-fold) and UUG initiation.
