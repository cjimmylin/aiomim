### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Knobloch syndrome-2 (KNO2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PAK2 gene (605022) on chromosome 3q29.

### Description

Menzel et al. (2004) described Knobloch syndrome as a disorder characterized by high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration with retinal detachment, and congenital encephalocele. Antonarakis et al. (2021) studied a family they designated KNO2, with Knobloch type 2 syndrome with intellectual disability, autistic behavior, retinal degeneration, and encephalocele.

### Clinical Features

Wilson et al. (1998) reported 2 sibs with high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration, and occipital encephalocele or scalp lesion. One sib had very early onset of severe eye disease, and the other was notable for a very mild scalp defect. Both sibs also appeared to have an unusual pulmonary lymphatic condition.

Antonarakis et al. (2021) studied a historical pedigree, published in 1998, with 2 affected sibs and normal parents. They designated the disorder in the family KNO2, Knobloch type 2 syndrome with intellectual disability, autistic behavior, retinal degeneration, and encephalocele.

### Inheritance

Menzel et al. (2004) described Knobloch syndrome as autosomal recessive. In contrast, Antonarakis et al. (2021) concluded that KNO2 in the family they studied was dominant. They found a heterozygous variant that was apparently de novo in both affected sibs. This indicated likely germline mosaicism in one of the parents, although deep sequencing of parental blood DNA did not detect mosaicism.

### Heterogeneity

Menzel et al. (2004) analyzed 2 unrelated Knobloch syndrome families, from Hungary and New Zealand. They confirmed that COL18A1 is involved in the disorder and showed that it is genetically heterogeneous. Linkage to the COL18A1 locus was excluded in the New Zealand family, which provided evidence for a second Knobloch syndrome locus. They named this locus KNO2.

### Molecular Genetics

In the New Zealand family with Knobloch syndrome in which linkage to COL18A1 had been excluded, Menzel et al. (2004) used mutation analysis to exclude COL15A1, a multiplexin collagen similar to COL18A1, as the gene responsible for KNO2.

Antonarakis et al. (2021) performed whole-exome sequencing in a family with KNO2, including the 2 affected sibs and their normal parents. They identified a heterozygous glu435-to-lys (E435K) mutation in the PAK2 gene as the causative variant. The mutation was apparently de novo in both sibs, indicating likely germline mosaicism in one of the parents, but deep sequencing of parental blood DNA did not detect mosaicism. The mutation lies in the kinase domain. Structural analysis predicted that it might compromise kinase activity, and in transfected HEK293T cells it caused a partial loss of kinase activity.

### Pathogenesis

Wilson et al. (1998) reviewed the literature on Knobloch syndrome and suggested that mesoderm might be involved in the morphogenesis of the disorder.
