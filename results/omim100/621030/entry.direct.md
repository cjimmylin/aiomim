### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant autoinflammation, panniculitis, and dermatosis syndrome (AIPDSA) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the OTULIN gene.

### Description

Autosomal dominant autoinflammation, panniculitis, and dermatosis syndrome (AIPDSA) is a severe autoinflammatory disorder that begins in infancy. Features include fever, raised inflammatory markers with neutrophilic leukocytosis, panniculitis or erythema nodosum, sterile pustular lesions at sites of trauma, and necrotizing or nonhealing skin and abdominal wall wounds. Some patients have episodes of acute respiratory distress. The disorder responds to corticosteroids and to TNF blockade. It is caused by de novo heterozygous OTULIN mutations that act in a dominant-negative manner. Clinically it resembles the OTULIN-related autoinflammatory syndrome (ORAS) caused by biallelic OTULIN loss-of-function mutations (Davidson et al., 2024; Takeda et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Davidson et al. (2024) reported 2 unrelated patients with an ORAS-like disease. Patient 1 was a 22-month-old boy born to nonconsanguineous Caucasian parents at 31 weeks' gestation. At 4 days of age he developed periumbilical erythema, diagnosed as a presumed abscess, that did not respond to broad-spectrum antibiotics. Surgery showed marked inflammation around a urachal remnant with many neutrophils, but no bacteria were cultured. The surgical closure dehisced, leaving an abdominal wound that failed to close. Over 2 months of healing, the only positive culture was a light growth of methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus. At 8 weeks of age he had an acute respiratory deterioration with extensive body edema, hypotension, and presumed pulmonary edema, which improved immediately on corticosteroids. He later developed a spontaneous cutaneous stoma from the duodenum and an echogenic focus on the tricuspid valve, which was treated as infective endocarditis although no organism was identified. He had repeated areas of swelling and erythema consistent with panniculitis, and lesions thought to represent pathergy: sterile pustular abscesses at injection and cannula sites. Investigations showed CRP of 100 to 200 mg/L, neutrophilia (30-50 x 10(9)/L), monocytosis (2-10 x 10(9)/L), eosinophilia (1-3 x 10(9)/L), and persistent thrombocytopenia (below 100 x 10(9)/L). These continued until inflammation was controlled with corticosteroids. Lymphocyte subsets were largely normal. He had initial hypogammaglobulinemia, which the authors noted may have been related to prematurity. Biopsy of perilesional skin from the abdominal wound showed heavy acute inflammatory exudate with diffuse suppuration and areas of necrosis.

Patient 2 of Davidson et al. (2024) was a full-term Saudi girl born to nonconsanguineous parents. She was asymptomatic until 7 months of age, when she presented with multiple tender skin lesions on the upper and lower limbs, fever (38-39 degrees C), and generalized edema, 2 weeks after routine 6-month vaccinations. She had leukocytosis (45.5 x 10(9)/L) with neutrophilia (40.9 x 10(9)/L) and CRP of 264 mg/L; blood and urine cultures were negative. Despite intravenous antibiotics, some lesions progressed to tender cellulitis-like plaques and then to necrotizing fasciitis with ulceration, requiring debridement and fasciotomy. Biopsies showed mixed neutrophil and plasma cell infiltration with necrosis, and all cultures were negative. At 12 months she again had fever with new tender erythematous plaques and patches on the trunk and lower limbs, leukocytosis (19.9 x 10(9)/L), neutrophilia (12.9 x 10(9)/L), CRP of 393 mg/L, and negative cultures. Skin biopsy showed panniculitis. She improved promptly on corticosteroids. She later had recurrent simple chest infections, which improved with prophylactic antibiotics.

Takeda et al. (2024) reported a 9-year-old Japanese girl born to nonconsanguineous healthy parents at 33 weeks and 1 day of gestation, with a birth weight of 1,436 g. Shortly after birth she developed meningitis with neutrophil-dominant leukocytosis and elevated CRP; blood and cerebrospinal fluid cultures did not identify a pathogen. At 1 month of age she developed omphalitis that did not respond to antibiotics, although cultures grew methicillin-susceptible S. aureus. The inflammation extended over most of the abdominal wall and required surgical debridement. Histopathology showed massive infiltration by leukocytes, mainly neutrophils, with inflammatory granuloma formation, and TUNEL and cleaved caspase-3 staining showed significant cell death. Pustulosis developed at skin puncture and surgical sites and did not respond to antibiotics. From 3 months of age she had 3 episodes of acute respiratory distress syndrome associated with pneumonia and thrombocytopenia, which responded to high-dose dexamethasone. Fever, skin erythema, and nodules recurred whenever steroids were tapered. Febrile episodes were accompanied by leukocytosis (up to 1 x 10(8)/microliter with neutrophil predominance), thrombocytosis (up to 1 x 10(9)/microliter), and CRP of 100 to 500 mg/liter. Serum IL-1-beta and IL-6 were elevated. No autoantibodies were detected. She had severely impaired physical and psychological development. At 1 year of age, biopsy of erythema nodosum showed diffuse infiltration of the dermis by neutrophils and TNF-producing mononuclear cells.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous OTULIN mutations in the 2 patients reported by Davidson et al. (2024) and in the patient reported by Takeda et al. (2024) occurred de novo. Both groups concluded that the mutations act in a dominant-negative manner, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. The patient of Takeda et al. (2024) also carried a maternally inherited OTULIN variant, P152L. Her healthy mother and healthy sister also carried this variant, and it showed wildtype activity. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 unrelated patients with an ORAS-like disease, Davidson et al. (2024) identified de novo heterozygous mutations in the OTULIN gene. Both mutations caused the same cys129-to-ser (C129S) substitution at a catalytic-domain cysteine essential for enzymatic activity. Trio exome sequencing of patient 1 found a c.386G-C transversion, and whole-genome sequencing of patient 2 found a c.385T-A transversion. The variant was absent from gnomAD. The C129S protein was as stable as wildtype protein, and patient fibroblasts expressed normal amounts of OTULIN and LUBAC components. However, the mutant protein could not hydrolyze M1-linked diubiquitin in vitro. Activity-based probe labeling showed that patient fibroblasts expressed wildtype and inactive mutant OTULIN in roughly equal amounts. Patient fibroblasts accumulated linear ubiquitin chains. Expressing C129S OTULIN in HEK293T cells, which contain endogenous OTULIN, caused linear ubiquitin to accumulate and NF-kappa-B to be activated, whereas wildtype OTULIN and the loss-of-function variants Y244C and N341D did not. C129S OTULIN drove NF-kappa-B activity even when expressed at lower levels than wildtype OTULIN, which the authors interpreted as a dominant-negative effect. In patient fibroblasts, the mutant protein bound LUBAC and allowed LUBAC autoubiquitination, and less LUBAC was recruited to the TNFR1 signaling complex. Patient fibroblasts were as sensitive to TNF-induced cell death, mainly apoptosis, as fibroblasts from patients with biallelic ORAS mutations. In contrast, fibroblasts from individuals with OTULIN haploinsufficiency were not sensitive. Patient fibroblasts also had a raised interferon-stimulated gene score, and patient PBMCs showed increased transcription of IFNB1 and IL6. ⚠︎

In a Japanese girl with ORAS, Takeda et al. (2024) used trio whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 heterozygous OTULIN variants on different alleles: c.455C-T (P152L), inherited from the mother, and a de novo c.917G-A transition resulting in an arg306-to-gln (R306Q) substitution. P152L was present in 51 of 1,611,754 alleles in gnomAD and was carried by the patient's healthy sister. R306Q was found in 1 of 1,611,754 alleles. Recombinant R306Q protein had markedly reduced deubiquitinase activity (kcat/KM of 8.92 vs 300.82 x 10(3) M(-1) sec(-1) for wildtype) and failed to bind linear diubiquitin, whereas P152L activity was comparable to wildtype. Patient PBMCs and EBV-transformed B cells had increased linear ubiquitin with normal amounts of OTULIN. Mesenchymal stem cells derived from patient iPSCs showed increased linear ubiquitin and LUBAC autoubiquitination after TNF stimulation and increased TNF-induced apoptosis, while NF-kappa-B activation was unaffected. Base editing of P152L to wildtype in patient iPSCs gave WT/R306Q cells that were as sensitive to TNF-induced death as the patient's cells. Correcting R306Q gave WT/P152L cells that behaved like controls. In OTULIN-null HEK293 cells, R306Q reversed, in a dose-dependent manner, the reduction of linear ubiquitin and the rescue from cell death mediated by wildtype or P152L OTULIN. This indicated a dominant-negative effect.

### Pathogenesis

Davidson et al. (2024) proposed that C129S OTULIN works as an inactive linear-ubiquitin receptor that keeps its stability, cellular levels, and binding to LUBAC. As a result, linear ubiquitin chains build up on LUBAC, LUBAC recruitment to the TNFR1 signaling complex falls, and TNF-induced cell death increases. They considered this cell death to underlie the necrotizing tissue lesions. Takeda et al. (2024) likewise proposed that R306Q acts within the LUBAC complex to enhance LUBAC auto-linear ubiquitination, suppressing LUBAC function and increasing TNF-mediated cell death. They suggested that ORAS severity correlates with residual OTULIN function rather than with the mode of inheritance.

### Clinical Management

In patient 1 of Davidson et al. (2024), corticosteroids improved the respiratory illness and abdominal wound healing. Adalimumab lowered CRP and circulating myeloid cells and allowed the steroid dose to be reduced. The JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib was also tried, but its benefit was unclear. Wound healing was markedly better under broad-spectrum antibiotic prophylaxis. Patient 2 was treated with infliximab and methotrexate, was weaned off corticosteroids, and had been in clinical and biochemical remission for 2 years. In the patient reported by Takeda et al. (2024), etanercept started at 2 years of age completely controlled the inflammation and led to steroid-free remission and catch-up in physical development.
