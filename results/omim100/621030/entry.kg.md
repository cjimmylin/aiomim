### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant autoinflammation, panniculitis, and dermatosis syndrome (AIPDSA) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the OTULIN gene (615712) on chromosome 5p15.2.

### Description

Autosomal dominant autoinflammation, panniculitis, and dermatosis syndrome is an OTULIN-related autoinflammatory syndrome (ORAS)-like disorder with onset in infancy. Its features include recurrent panniculitis, sterile neutrophilic skin inflammation with pathergy, necrotizing skin and soft tissue lesions, fever, edema, and markedly raised C-reactive protein (CRP) with neutrophilia. It is caused by a heterozygous dominant negative OTULIN variant that arose de novo, unlike the biallelic loss-of-function variants reported earlier in ORAS (Davidson et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Davidson et al. (2024) described a 22-month-old Caucasian boy (P1) with nonconsanguineous parents who was born at 31 weeks' gestation. At 4 days of age he developed periumbilical erythema with sterile neutrophilic inflammation around a urachal remnant. After surgery the wound dehisced and did not close, and he later developed a spontaneous cutaneous duodenal stoma. At 8 weeks of age he had an acute respiratory deterioration with body edema, hypotension, and presumed pulmonary edema, which responded to corticosteroids. Other features were recurrent panniculitis and pathergy with sterile pustular abscesses at injection and cannula sites. An echogenic focus on the tricuspid valve was treated as culture-negative endocarditis. Laboratory findings were raised CRP (100-200 mg/L), neutrophilia, monocytosis, eosinophilia, persistent thrombocytopenia, and initial hypogammaglobulinemia. A skin biopsy showed acute inflammatory exudate with suppuration and necrosis.

Davidson et al. (2024) also described a Saudi girl (P2) born at term to nonconsanguineous parents. At 7 months of age, 2 weeks after routine vaccinations, she presented with fever, generalized edema, and multiple tender skin lesions. These progressed to cellulitis-like plaques and to necrotizing fasciitis with ulceration, which required debridement and fasciotomy. She had leukocytosis, neutrophilia, and a high CRP (264 mg/L). All cultures were negative, and biopsy showed infiltration by neutrophils and plasma cells with necrosis. At 12 months of age she had a second attack with fever, erythematous plaques, and a CRP of 393 mg/L, and a skin biopsy showed panniculitis. She responded promptly to corticosteroids.

### Biochemical Features

Davidson et al. (2024) observed markedly increased transcription of IFNB1 and IL6, and to a lesser extent TNF, in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from P1. P1's fibroblasts had a raised interferon-stimulated gene score. This score was similar to that of fibroblasts from patients with biallelic ORAS and higher than that of fibroblasts from healthy donors or from OTULIN-haploinsufficient individuals. THP-1 monocytes stably expressing C129S OTULIN showed a similar inflammatory gene signature, whereas those expressing wildtype OTULIN did not.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous dominant negative OTULIN variant reported by Davidson et al. (2024) arose de novo in 2 unrelated individuals, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. This contrasts with the biallelic loss-of-function OTULIN variants reported earlier in ORAS.

### Molecular Genetics

In P1, the Caucasian boy, trio exome sequencing by Davidson et al. (2024) identified a de novo heterozygous c.386G-C transversion in the OTULIN gene, resulting in a cys129-to-ser (C129S) substitution. The variant was absent from gnomAD. Cys129 lies in the catalytic domain and is essential for OTULIN enzymatic activity.

In P2, the Saudi girl, whole-genome sequencing by Davidson et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous c.385T-A transversion in the OTULIN gene, which results in the same C129S substitution. Testing of the parents confirmed that the variant had arisen de novo.

In functional studies, Davidson et al. (2024) found that C129S OTULIN was as thermostable as wildtype OTULIN, and that levels of OTULIN and of the LUBAC components were normal in patient fibroblasts. However, the mutant protein failed to hydrolyze M1-linked di-ubiquitin. An activity-based probe showed that active wildtype and inactive C129S OTULIN were coexpressed in roughly equal amounts in patient fibroblasts. Surface plasmon resonance showed that C129S OTULIN bound M1-di-ubiquitin with high affinity. M1-linked ubiquitin chains accumulated in patient fibroblasts. HEK293T cells express endogenous OTULIN, and transfecting them with C129S OTULIN caused M1-ubiquitin accumulation and dose-dependent NF-kappa-B activation. Wildtype OTULIN and the loss-of-function variants Y244C and N341D did not have these effects. C129S OTULIN drove NF-kappa-B activity even when cotransfected with wildtype OTULIN at lower ratios, indicating a dominant negative effect. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Davidson et al. (2024) found that fibroblasts from patients with the C129S variant were as sensitive to TNF/cycloheximide-induced cell death as fibroblasts from patients with biallelic ORAS. They were more sensitive than fibroblasts from OTULIN-haploinsufficient patients or healthy donors. The cell death was mainly apoptotic: caspase inhibition reduced it, and necrostatin-1 had little effect. TNF-induced MAPK and NF-kappa-B signaling and IL-6/IL-8 secretion were largely normal in patient fibroblasts.

Davidson et al. (2024) showed that C129S OTULIN binds LUBAC. In cells it promoted ubiquitination of the LUBAC component SHARPIN and, to a lesser extent, HOIP. Patient fibroblasts showed increased OTULIN-LUBAC interaction, and most of the OTULIN bound to LUBAC was catalytically inactive. Patient cells also showed reduced recruitment of HOIP and SHARPIN to the TNFR1 signaling complex and reduced modification of RIPK1. The authors concluded that M1-ubiquitin accumulating on LUBAC reduces its recruitment to TNFR1 and promotes TNF-induced cell death, leading to necrotizing tissue.

### Clinical Management

Davidson et al. (2024) reported that both patients responded to corticosteroids. In P1, adalimumab allowed the steroid dose to be reduced, lowered CRP, and supported wound healing; ruxolitinib was also tried, but its benefit was unclear. P2 was treated with infliximab plus methotrexate, was weaned off steroids, and remained in remission for 2 years. Prophylactic antibiotics improved wound healing in P1 and controlled recurrent chest infections in P2.
