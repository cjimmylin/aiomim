### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant autoinflammation, panniculitis, and dermatosis syndrome (AIPDSA) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the OTULIN gene (615712) on chromosome 5p15.2.

### Description

Autosomal dominant autoinflammation, panniculitis, and dermatosis syndrome is an autoinflammatory disorder that begins in infancy. Features include recurrent panniculitis, pathergy with sterile pustular abscesses, sterile necrotizing inflammation of skin and soft tissues, poor wound healing, fever, and edema. Laboratory findings include markedly raised C-reactive protein (CRP) and neutrophilia. Davidson et al. (2024) described the disorder as resembling OTULIN-related autoinflammatory syndrome (ORAS), which is caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants, but the autosomal dominant form results from a heterozygous dominant negative variant.

### Clinical Features

Davidson et al. (2024) described a 22-month-old Caucasian boy (P1) whose parents were not consanguineous. He was born at 31 weeks' gestation. At 4 days of age he developed peri-umbilical erythema with sterile neutrophilic inflammation around a urachal remnant. After surgery the wound dehisced and did not close, and he later developed a spontaneous cutaneous duodenal stoma. At 8 weeks of age he had an acute respiratory deterioration with body edema, hypotension, and presumed pulmonary edema, which responded to corticosteroids. He had recurrent panniculitis and pathergy, with sterile pustular abscesses at injection and cannula sites. An echogenic focus on the tricuspid valve was treated as culture-negative endocarditis. Laboratory findings included raised CRP (100-200 mg/L), neutrophilia, monocytosis, eosinophilia, persistent thrombocytopenia, and initial hypogammaglobulinemia. A skin biopsy showed acute inflammatory exudate with suppuration and necrosis.

Davidson et al. (2024) also described a Saudi girl (P2) born at term to nonconsanguineous parents. At 7 months of age, 2 weeks after routine vaccinations, she presented with fever, generalized edema, and multiple tender skin lesions. The lesions progressed to cellulitis-like plaques and necrotizing fasciitis with ulceration, which required debridement and fasciotomy. She had leukocytosis, neutrophilia, and a high CRP (264 mg/L). All cultures were negative, and a biopsy showed infiltration by neutrophils and plasma cells, with necrosis. At 12 months of age she had a second attack with fever, erythematous plaques, and a CRP of 393 mg/L. A skin biopsy at that time showed panniculitis. She responded promptly to corticosteroids.

### Biochemical Features

Davidson et al. (2024) found markedly increased transcription of IFNB1 and IL6, and to a lesser extent TNF, in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from P1. Fibroblasts from P1 had an elevated interferon-stimulated gene score. The score was similar to that of fibroblasts from patients with biallelic ORAS and higher than that of fibroblasts from healthy donors or OTULIN-haploinsufficient individuals. THP-1 monocytes stably expressing C129S OTULIN showed a similar inflammatory gene signature, whereas cells expressing wildtype OTULIN did not.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous OTULIN mutation in the 2 unrelated patients reported by Davidson et al. (2024) arose de novo in each. This is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance and contrasts with the biallelic loss-of-function variants previously reported in ORAS.

### Molecular Genetics

In P1, a Caucasian boy with an autoinflammatory disorder (AIPDSA), Davidson et al. (2024) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.386G-C transversion in the OTULIN gene. The mutation results in a cys129-to-ser (C129S) substitution and was not present in the gnomAD database. Cys129 lies in the catalytic domain and is essential for OTULIN enzymatic activity.

In P2, an unrelated Saudi girl with AIPDSA, Davidson et al. (2024) used whole-genome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.385T-A transversion in the OTULIN gene. This mutation results in the same C129S substitution. Testing of the parents confirmed that it had arisen de novo.

Davidson et al. (2024) characterized the C129S mutant functionally. Its thermostability was similar to that of wildtype OTULIN, and levels of OTULIN and of the LUBAC components were normal in patient fibroblasts. However, the mutant could not hydrolyze M1-linked di-ubiquitin. An activity-based probe showed that active wildtype OTULIN and inactive C129S OTULIN were coexpressed in roughly equal amounts in patient fibroblasts. Surface plasmon resonance showed that C129S OTULIN bound M1-di-ubiquitin with high affinity. M1-linked ubiquitin chains accumulated in patient fibroblasts. In HEK293T cells, which express endogenous OTULIN, transfection of C129S OTULIN caused M1-ubiquitin accumulation and dose-dependent NF-kappa-B activation. Wildtype OTULIN and the loss-of-function variants Y244C and N341D did not have these effects. C129S OTULIN still drove NF-kappa-B activity when cotransfected with wildtype OTULIN at lower ratios, indicating a dominant negative effect. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Davidson et al. (2024) found that fibroblasts from patients with the C129S mutation were as sensitive to cell death induced by TNF plus cycloheximide as fibroblasts from patients with biallelic ORAS. They were more sensitive than fibroblasts from OTULIN-haploinsufficient individuals or healthy donors. The cell death was mainly apoptotic: caspase inhibition reduced it, whereas necrostatin-1 had little effect. TNF-induced MAPK and NF-kappa-B signaling and IL-6 and IL-8 secretion were largely normal in patient fibroblasts.

Davidson et al. (2024) showed that C129S OTULIN binds LUBAC. In cells, the mutant promoted ubiquitination of the LUBAC component SHARPIN and, to a lesser extent, HOIP. Patient fibroblasts showed increased interaction between OTULIN and LUBAC, and most of the LUBAC-bound OTULIN was catalytically inactive. In patient cells, recruitment of LUBAC components (HOIP and SHARPIN) to the TNFR1 signaling complex was reduced, as was RIPK1 modification. The authors concluded that M1-ubiquitin accumulating on LUBAC reduces its recruitment to TNFR1 and promotes TNF-induced cell death, leading to necrotizing tissue.

### Clinical Management

Davidson et al. (2024) reported that both of their patients responded to corticosteroids. In P1, adalimumab allowed the steroid dose to be reduced, lowered CRP, and supported wound healing. Ruxolitinib was also tried in P1, but its benefit was unclear. P2 was treated with infliximab plus methotrexate, was weaned off steroids, and remained in remission for 2 years. Prophylactic antibiotics improved wound healing in P1 and controlled recurrent chest infections in P2.

In P1, a Caucasian boy, trio exome sequencing identified a c.386G-C transversion, which was not present in gnomAD.
