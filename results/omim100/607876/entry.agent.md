### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by an expanded, inserted ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat in intron 1 of the STARD7 gene on chromosome 2.

### Description

Familial adult myoclonic epilepsy (FAME) is an autosomal dominant disorder defined by a core triad of cortical tremor, multifocal myoclonus, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (Crompton et al., 2012). Corbett et al. (2019) noted that symptoms of cortical myoclonic tremor usually start in the second decade of life, and that overt myoclonic or generalized tonic-clonic seizures follow. Expressivity varies within and between families. Guerrini et al. (2001) described the chromosome 2-linked disorder in an Italian pedigree as autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME), with complex partial seizures in some patients. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) studied a pedigree with 8 affected individuals. The disorder was nonprogressive and began between ages 12 and 50 years. All patients had predominantly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 had complex partial seizures. Most affected individuals had seizures only rarely and had normal cognition. However, the 3 individuals with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All patients had frontotemporal as well as generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Neurophysiologic study pointed to a cortical origin of the myoclonus. In 5 patients there was EEG-EMG coherence at 8 to 25 Hz over the contralateral rolandic area, with EEG activity preceding EMG activity by 8 to 15 ms. The C-reflex at rest was enhanced, and somatosensory and visual evoked potentials were of high amplitude. On transcranial magnetic stimulation, the resting motor threshold was significantly reduced and the postmotor evoked potential silent period was significantly shortened. The authors suggested that these findings reflected diffuse cortical hyperexcitability. They named the disorder autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME).

De Falco et al. (2003) reported a clinical and electrophysiologic study of 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME).

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. Eleven members, aged 24 to 56 years (mean 39.2 years), had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 of them had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study confirmed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected members. Three presymptomatic members had giant somatosensory evoked potential components and an enhanced long-loop reflex I. All 3 developed hand tremor and upper limb myoclonus after 1.5 years of follow-up.

Madia et al. (2008) studied 5 BAFME families from southern Italy, 1 of them not previously reported. In a preliminary genotype-phenotype analysis, patients carrying the disease haplotype showed only minor clinical differences. The authors took this to suggest that other factors did not markedly influence the expressivity of the founder mutation.

Suppa et al. (2009) studied an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy. Affected members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neuropsychological testing showed visuospatial impairment, which the authors thought might reflect temporal lobe dysfunction. Neurophysiologic findings suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex, with normal sensorimotor integration.

Crompton et al. (2012) studied a 6-generation FAME kindred of European descent, ascertained in New Zealand and Australia. All 55 affected members had fine hand tremor, which typically began in adolescence (median age 15 years; range 4 to 60 years). Forty-four of the 55 (80%) had proximal myoclonus, which began later than the hand tremor (median age 17 years; range 5 to 60 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of the 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range 18 to 76 years). These seizures occurred around sleep onset following severe generalized myoclonus. Neurophysiologic testing confirmed features of cortical reflex myoclonus. The most common phenotype in this family was mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, combined with subtle proximal myoclonus.

De Fusco et al. (2014) described a second ADCME family from Tuscany (family B). This 4-generation kindred had 5 affected members, 4 of them living. All patients had postural myoclonus of the hands and upper limbs, and cortical tremor was the presenting symptom in every case, beginning between ages 18 and 50 years. All but 1 patient had epilepsy, mainly rare generalized tonic-clonic seizures, sometimes triggered by visual stimuli. Two patients also had drug-resistant focal motor or complex partial seizures. One patient had borderline cognitive level, and the others had normal intelligence. All patients had generalized or focal paroxysmal EEG activity. Jerk-locked back-averaged EEG, somatosensory evoked potentials, and long-loop reflexes were consistent with cortical reflex myoclonus. None of the ADCME patients had cardiovascular disease. Two of the oldest mutation carriers developed age-related dementia. The authors also noted that myoclonus often worsened at older ages.

Corbett et al. (2019) summarized the clinical features of 22 FAME2 families. Most were Italian, and the others were of Australian/New Zealand, Iraqi and Israeli Sephardic Jewish, South African, French, French/Spanish, and Syrian origin. The frequency of generalized tonic-clonic seizures ranged from 15 to 100% across families. Seizures were usually controlled with antiepileptic drugs for generalized epilepsies, although a few individuals had drug-resistant epilepsy. In the large Australian/New Zealand family, Corbett et al. (2019) found evidence of anticipation. Median age at onset of any relevant symptom was 30 years in generation III (n = 6), 17 years in generation IV (n = 30), and 12 years in generation V (n = 16).

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) found autosomal dominant inheritance with high penetrance in the family they described. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also observed in the ADCME families studied by De Fusco et al. (2014).

### Mapping

Guerrini et al. (2001) performed genome-wide linkage analysis in their ADCME family. They identified a 12.4-cM critical region on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2, between markers D2S2161 and D2S1897. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for marker D2S2175, and multipoint lod scores reached 3.74 around D2S2175. Linkage to the familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus on chromosome 8q23.3-q24 was excluded.

De Falco et al. (2003) found linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 2 Italian BAFME families. They suggested that the disorder might be allelic with ADCME.

In the 5-generation Italian family that they studied, Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p, with a maximum lod score of 5.9.

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the 2p11.1-q12.2 locus for cortical tremor associated with epilepsy and excluded candidate genes. Only the title of this report was available.

Madia et al. (2008) genotyped 53 affected individuals from 5 southern Italian BAFME families using microsatellite markers spanning chromosomes 2p11.1-q12.2 and 8q23.1-q24.11. Linkage to 2p11.1-q12.2 was significant, with a maximum cumulative lod score of 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388. A haplotype of the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113, and D2S2175 segregated with the disease in all 5 families. The authors concluded that the same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, segregated in these families.

In the Italian family that they studied, Suppa et al. (2009) excluded linkage to the 8q24 locus. Affected members shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the pericentromeric region of chromosome 2.

Using the large Australian/New Zealand family, Crompton et al. (2012) narrowed the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

### Heterogeneity

Guerrini et al. (2001) excluded the chromosome 8q23.3-q24 FAME locus in their family. Together with the different phenotype, this led them to define ADCME as a new epilepsy syndrome. De Falco et al. (2003) later found linkage to the same chromosome 2 region in 2 Italian BAFME families and suggested that the 2 disorders might be allelic. In 2 ADCME families, De Fusco et al. (2014) found an ADRA2B indel but did not find ADRA2B mutations in southern Italian BAFME families linked to the same locus. They therefore suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME might be distinct clinical entities. Corbett et al. (2019), however, found the same STARD7 ATTTC repeat expansion in all chromosome 2-linked families they tested. These included the 2 families carrying the ADRA2B variant. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

De Fusco et al. (2014) sequenced candidate genes in the critical region in the original Tuscan ADCME family (family A) that had been reported by Guerrini et al. (2001). They identified a novel in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene, c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG, which replaces 5 amino acids (HGGAL) with 4 residues (QFGR) in the third intracellular loop (p.H225_L229delinsQFGR). The indel cosegregated completely with the disease. It was absent from 575 unrelated controls from Tuscany and from public databases, a total of 16,080 chromosomes. The same indel was found in a second Tuscan ADCME family (family B), which shared the disease haplotype across the entire critical region. Targeted capture and next-generation sequencing of the coding regions in the critical region in 2 affected members of family A identified no other relevant change. The authors did not detect ADRA2B mutations in southern Italian BAFME pedigrees linked to the same locus. In functional studies, the mutant receptor localized normally to the plasma membrane and was internalized in an arrestin-3-dependent manner. However, it could not form a stable complex with spinophilin after epinephrine stimulation. In Xenopus oocytes, spinophilin did not raise the EC50 for epinephrine with the mutant receptor as it did with wildtype. The authors concluded that the mutation increased the intensity of epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling, a gain-of-function effect. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 2 individuals in the Australian/New Zealand family, 1 from an Italian family, and 3 from a French-Spanish family. They found expansion of an ATTTT repeat and insertion of an ATTTC repeat within the first intron of the STARD7 gene in all FAME samples tested. The ATTTC repeat was absent from whole-genome sequencing data of 69 controls. Using repeat-primed PCR, they detected the expansion in 137 of 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported FAME2 families. Two of 24 relatives without a FAME diagnosis also carried the expansion, and both were from younger generations and likely presymptomatic. Of 72 additional individuals with features similar to FAME, all 20 familial cases from 6 families and 1 of 52 singleton cases carried the expansion. In total, the ATTTC expansion segregated in 158 of 158 typically affected individuals from 22 pedigrees. It was also present in 8 of 13 family members whose FAME diagnosis was uncertain. The ATTTC repeat primer was 100% sensitive for FAME in all families with linkage or suggestive linkage to chromosome 2. These included 2 families with the ADRA2B variant reported by De Fusco et al. (2014), and Corbett et al. (2019) concluded that this variant was likely not causative. Long-read sequencing showed the repeat structure. One individual from the Australian/New Zealand family had a SMRT read with 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats and a nanopore read with 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats, which suggested somatic variation in repeat size. An individual from another family had 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats. In patient-derived fibroblasts, STARD7 transcript and protein levels were unaltered, and RNA-seq showed no reads containing ATTTC repeats.

### Pathogenesis

De Fusco et al. (2014) proposed that the ADRA2B mutation they reported had a gain-of-function effect. In their model, reduced interaction with spinophilin increases receptor activity, and the resulting cortical hyperexcitability might stem from impaired GABAergic inhibition. Corbett et al. (2019) later found that the 2 families carrying this ADRA2B variant also carried the STARD7 ATTTC expansion. Because STARD7 expression was unaffected in patient cells, Corbett et al. (2019) suggested that the repeat sequence itself is pathogenic, independent of any effect on the coding sequence of the gene that contains it. They noted that RNA toxicity has been suggested as the disease mechanism.

### Diagnosis

Corbett et al. (2019) found that repeat-primed PCR with an ATTTC repeat primer diagnosed FAME2 with 100% sensitivity in families with linkage or suggestive linkage to chromosome 2. Variation in the length of the endogenous ATTTT repeat in the normal population made that sequence unsuitable for mutation screening.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found that 5 southern Italian BAFME families shared a disease haplotype, consistent with a founder mutation. De Fusco et al. (2014) noted that their 2 Tuscan ADCME families, although not known to be related, shared the same disease haplotype across the critical region. Corbett et al. (2019) stated that FAME2 is the most frequently observed linked locus in Caucasian individuals with FAME.

De Fusco et al. (2014) identified an in-frame indel in the ADRA2B gene, c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG, which replaces 5 residues (HGGAL) with 4 (QFGR) in the third intracellular loop (p.H225_L229delinsQ225_F_G_R228). They found it in affected members of 2 Tuscan families with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy. The indel cosegregated completely with the disease and was absent from 575 Tuscan controls and from public databases. The mutant receptor failed to form a stable complex with spinophilin after agonist activation and increased epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling. Corbett et al. (2019) later found that the 2 families carrying this variant also carried the STARD7 intronic ATTTC repeat expansion, which strongly suggested that the ADRA2B allele is not causative.

**.0002 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2**  
STARD7, (ATTTC)n INS/(ATTTT)n EXP, INTRON 1

In affected members of 22 FAME2 families of diverse origin, Corbett et al. (2019) identified an inserted, expanded ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat, together with an expanded endogenous ATTTT repeat, in the first intron of the STARD7 gene. On the forward strand of chromosome 2, the repeat had the structure (AAATG)n(AAAAT)n. The expansion was found by whole-genome sequencing and repeat-primed PCR. It segregated in 158 of 158 typically affected individuals and was absent from 69 control genomes and 28 control DNA samples. Long-read sequencing of one patient showed 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats in a SMRT read and 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats in a nanopore read, which suggested somatic instability. Another patient had 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats, 4,645 bp in total. STARD7 mRNA and protein levels were not altered in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎
