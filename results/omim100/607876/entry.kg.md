### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by expansion of an intronic ATTTC repeat in the STARD7 gene (616712) on chromosome 2q11.2.

### Description

Madia et al. (2008) described benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME or FAME) as an autosomal dominant condition characterized by shivering-like tremors of cortical origin, myoclonus, and epilepsy. De Fusco et al. (2014) described autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME) as a familial disorder with cortical reflex myoclonus. It presents as action-induced shivering movements of the hands and jerking of the upper limbs, variably associated with focal and generalized tonic-clonic seizures of variable severity. A minority of patients have borderline or moderately impaired cognition, and myoclonus often worsens in advanced age.

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) described a pedigree in which 8 individuals had a nonprogressive disorder with onset between 12 and 50 years of age. All had mainly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 had complex partial seizures. Most rarely had seizures and had normal cognition, but 3 individuals with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All patients had frontotemporal as well as generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Neurophysiologic studies showed that the myoclonus was cortical in origin. In 5 patients, EEG over the contralateral rolandic area was coherent with EMG at 8 to 25 Hz, and EEG activity preceded EMG activity by 8 to 15 ms; ipsilateral coherence in 2 patients was consistent with transcallosal spread. Patients had an enhanced C-reflex and high-amplitude somatosensory and visual evoked potentials. Compared with controls, they had a reduced resting motor threshold to transcranial magnetic stimulation and a shortened post-MEP silent period. Guerrini et al. (2001) suggested that these findings reflected diffuse cortical hyperexcitability. ⚠︎

Labauge et al. (2002) described a 4-generation European family with 18 members and familial adult myoclonic epilepsy (FAME). Ten living and 3 deceased relatives were affected. In the 10 living patients, mean age at onset was 41 years (range, 30-60 years). Eight of the 13 affected individuals had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Electrophysiologic studies in 5 patients confirmed the diagnosis of FAME.

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. Eleven members, aged 24 to 56 years (mean, 39.2 years), had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 of them had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study confirmed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected members. Three presymptomatic members had giant somatosensory evoked potential components and an enhanced long loop reflex I; after 1.5 years of follow-up, these members developed hand tremor and myoclonus of the upper limbs.

Suppa et al. (2009) described an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy (FCMTE). Clinically affected family members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neuropsychological testing showed visuospatial impairment, which the authors suggested may be due to temporal lobe dysfunction. Neurophysiologic studies suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex with normal sensorimotor integration.

Crompton et al. (2012) described a 6-generation kindred of European descent, ascertained in New Zealand and Australia, with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy. All 55 affected family members had fine hand tremor, usually beginning in adolescence (median age 15 years; range 4-60 years). Proximal myoclonus was present in 44 of 55 (80%) and began later than the hand tremor (median age 17 years; range 5-60 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range 18-76 years). Neurophysiologic testing showed features of cortical reflex myoclonus. Crompton et al. (2012) concluded that the most common phenotype in this family was a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, together with subtle proximal myoclonus; generalized tonic-clonic seizures were uncommon and occurred around sleep onset after severe generalized myoclonus.

De Fusco et al. (2014) described a 4-generation Tuscan family (family B) with 5 affected members. All had postural myoclonus of the hands and upper limbs, and cortical tremor was the presenting symptom, starting between ages 18 and 50 years. All but one (IV:1) had epilepsy with rare generalized tonic-clonic seizures, at times triggered by visual stimuli; 2 patients also had drug-resistant focal motor or complex partial seizures. One patient had borderline cognitive level and the others had normal intelligence. Jerk-locked back-averaged EEG, somatosensory evoked potentials, and long-loop reflex studies were consistent with cortical reflex myoclonus. Brain MRI was normal where performed.

Corbett et al. (2019) summarized the clinical features of 22 FAME families. The families were mainly Italian; others were Australian/New Zealand, Sephardic Jewish (Iraqi and Israeli), South African, French/Spanish, French, and Syrian. Patients had cortical myoclonic tremor and myoclonic and later generalized tonic-clonic seizures, with onset usually in the second to third decade. Expressivity varied within and between families, and the frequency of generalized tonic-clonic seizures ranged from 15% to 100% across families. Some families also had focal seizures. In a large Australian/New Zealand family of European ancestry (Family 1) with 55 affected members, all 55 had myoclonus/cortical tremor, 8 (15%) had tonic-clonic seizures, and 2 (4%) had focal seizures; mean age of onset was 18.6 years, with a range of 4 to 60 years.

### Other Features

De Fusco et al. (2014) found no cardiovascular disease on careful history in any of their ADCME patients. Two of the oldest ADRA2B variant carriers developed age-related dementia.

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) found that inheritance in their ADCME family was autosomal dominant with high penetrance. In the European family studied by Labauge et al. (2002), the pattern of inheritance was consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. Crompton et al. (2012) reported autosomal dominant familial adult myoclonic epilepsy in a 6-generation kindred.

Corbett et al. (2019) found evidence of anticipation in the large Australian/New Zealand FAME2 family: median age of onset was 30 years in generation III (n = 6), 17 years in generation IV (n = 30), and 12 years in generation V (n = 16).

### Mapping

By genomewide linkage analysis in a family with ADCME, Guerrini et al. (2001) identified a 12.4-cM critical region on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 between markers D2S2161 and D2S1897. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for D2S2175, and the multipoint lod score reached 3.74 around D2S2175.

de Falco et al. (2003) carried out a clinical and electrophysiologic study of 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy and found that both families showed linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2.

By linkage analysis of an Italian family with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy, Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p (maximum lod score of 5.9).

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the 2p11.1-q12.2 locus responsible for cortical tremor associated with epilepsy and excluded candidate genes in the region.

Madia et al. (2008) genotyped 53 affected individuals from 5 southern Italian families with BAFME, one not previously reported, using microsatellite markers spanning chromosomes 2p11.1-q12.2 and 8q23.1-q24.11. Multipoint linkage analysis found significant linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2, with a maximum cumulative lod score of 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388.

In an Italian family with FCMTE, Suppa et al. (2009) used linkage analysis to exclude the 8q24 locus. Affected members shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the region of chromosome 2 near the centromere.

In a 6-generation kindred with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy, Crompton et al. (2012) narrowed the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

### Heterogeneity

Guerrini et al. (2001) excluded linkage to the familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus on 8q23.3-q24 in their family. Based on the new chromosome 2 localization and the different phenotype, they defined ADCME as a new epilepsy syndrome.

Labauge et al. (2002) excluded the FAME/BAFME locus on 8q23.3-q24.1 in a European FAME family using fluorescent microsatellites, and concluded that this established genetic heterogeneity of the disorder. They also noted that the family extended the occurrence of FAME to non-Japanese patients.

de Falco et al. (2003) noted that BAFME had been mapped to chromosome 8q24; their finding that 2 Italian families were linked to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 supported genetic heterogeneity and the existence of non-Japanese families with BAFME. They suggested that BAFME linked to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 might be allelic with the recently described ADCME syndrome.

De Fusco et al. (2014) sequenced BAFME pedigrees from southern Italy that map to the ADCME locus and found no ADRA2B mutations; Southern blot excluded large insertions or deletions. They suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME may be 2 distinct clinical entities, although they could not exclude variants in regulatory or noncoding regions. In contrast, Corbett et al. (2019) found the STARD7 ATTTC expansion in 2 families (Families 4 and 15) that carried the ADRA2B variant reported by De Fusco et al. (2014), and stated that this strongly suggested that the ADRA2B allele is not causative.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-generation Tuscan family (family A) with ADCME previously mapped to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2, De Fusco et al. (2014) used candidate gene sequencing to identify a heterozygous in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (104260) that replaced 5 amino acids in the third intracellular loop with 4 new residues. The indel segregated completely with the disease and was absent from 575 Tuscan controls and public databases; targeted capture and next-generation sequencing of the critical region in 2 affected members found it to be the only relevant change. The same indel was found in a second Tuscan family (family B) with no known kinship that shared the disease haplotype across the entire critical region, suggesting a common ancestor. The indel lay on the common ADRA2B allele with 12 consecutive glutamic acid residues. In transfected HeLa cells, the mutant protein localized normally to the plasma membrane and underwent arrestin-dependent internalization after epinephrine stimulation. Corbett et al. (2019) subsequently found the STARD7 ATTTC expansion in 2 families carrying this ADRA2B variant and stated that this strongly suggested the ADRA2B allele is not causative. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from affected members of 3 FAME families (Families 1, 3, and 19) using ExpansionHunter and exSTRa and found, in all FAME samples tested, an expansion of an ATTTT repeat together with an inserted ATTTC repeat within the first intron of the STARD7 gene. The ATTTC repeat was absent from WGS data on 69 controls. Using a repeat-primed PCR assay, they detected the STARD7 ATTTC expansion in all 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported FAME2 families, in all 20 affected individuals from 6 additional multiplex families, and in 1 of 52 unrelated singleton cases, giving 158 of 158 typically affected individuals from 22 families. Two individuals without a FAME diagnosis carried the expansion and were likely presymptomatic, and 8 of 13 individuals with an uncertain diagnosis were carriers. The expansion was not found in 28 controls. Long-read sequencing showed the repeat structure on the forward strand to be (AAATG)n[N](AAAAT)n. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In a preliminary genotype-phenotype analysis of 5 southern Italian BAFME families sharing a founder haplotype, Madia et al. (2008) found only minor clinical differences among patients and suggested that other factors do not markedly influence the expressivity of the founder mutation.

### Pathogenesis

De Fusco et al. (2014) found in co-immunoisolation assays in CosM6 cells that spinophilin bound similar amounts of wildtype and mutant ADRA2B after 5 minutes of epinephrine, but by 10 minutes binding to the mutant had fallen back to basal levels while binding to wildtype stayed elevated, suggesting that the mutant cannot form a stable complex with spinophilin. In Xenopus oocytes, spinophilin significantly raised the EC50 for epinephrine-stimulated Ca(2+)-activated Cl- current with wildtype ADRA2B but not with the mutant receptor. The authors concluded that the mutant increases the intensity of Ca(2+) signaling and proposed a gain-of-function effect through reduced interaction with spinophilin, possibly leading to cortical hyperexcitability through impaired GABAergic inhibition.

In patient-derived fibroblasts, Corbett et al. (2019) found that STARD7 mRNA levels (by qPCR and RNA-seq) and protein levels (by Western blot) were not altered, and RNA-seq detected no reads containing ATTTC repeats. The authors suggested that the repeat sequence itself is pathogenic, independent of any effect on the encompassing gene.

### Diagnosis

Corbett et al. (2019) found that the ATTTC repeat primer was diagnostic for FAME, with 100% sensitivity in all families with linkage or suggestive linkage to chromosome 2. The endogenous ATTTT repeat varied in length naturally and was not suitable for screening.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found that the haplotype '5332' of the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113, and D2S2175 segregated with the disease in all 5 southern Italian BAFME families studied, and concluded that the same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, segregates in these families, indicating a founder effect.

Corbett et al. (2019) stated that the FAME2 locus is the most frequently linked region in Caucasian individuals with FAME, whereas chromosome 8 linkage has so far been limited to Asian individuals.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

In affected members of a 5-generation Tuscan family (family A) with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy, De Fusco et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG), resulting in replacement of 5 amino acids (HGGAL) in the third intracellular loop with 4 new residues (QFGR) (p.H225_L229delinsQFGR). The variant was found by candidate gene sequencing, segregated completely with the disease, and was absent from 575 Tuscan controls, dbSNP, the Exome Variant Server, and 1000 Genomes; targeted capture and next-generation sequencing of the critical region in 2 affected members found it to be the only relevant change. The same indel was found in a second Tuscan family (family B) that shared the disease haplotype across the entire chromosome 2 critical region. In transfected HeLa cells, the mutant protein localized normally to the plasma membrane and was internalized to endosomes in an arrestin-dependent manner after epinephrine stimulation; molecular modeling predicted that the change alters the conformation of the third intracellular loop. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) found the STARD7 ATTTC repeat expansion in 2 Italian families (Families 4 and 15) that carried this ADRA2B variant, and stated that this strongly suggested that the ADRA2B allele is not causative.

In affected members of 3 FAME families (Families 1, 3, and 19), Corbett et al. (2019) identified, by analysis of whole-genome sequencing data with ExpansionHunter and exSTRa, an expansion of an ATTTT repeat together with an inserted ATTTC repeat within the first intron of the STARD7 gene; the ATTTC repeat was absent from WGS data on 69 controls. By repeat-primed PCR, the ATTTC expansion was found in all 137 affected individuals from 16 previously reported FAME2 families, in all 20 affected individuals from 6 additional multiplex families, and in 1 of 52 unrelated singleton cases (158 of 158 typically affected individuals from 22 families), but not in 28 controls. Long-read sequencing showed a forward-strand structure of (AAATG)n[N](AAAAT)n; one patient from Family 1 had a SMRT read with 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats and a nanopore read with 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats, which the authors suggested reflected somatic variation, and a patient from Family 5 had 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats, 4645 bp in total. Patient fibroblasts showed no change in STARD7 mRNA or protein levels. ⚠︎
