### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is caused by expansion of an intronic ATTTC pentanucleotide repeat in the STARD7 gene (616712) on chromosome 2q11.2.

### Description

Familial adult myoclonic epilepsy-2 (FAME2) is an autosomal dominant disorder of familial cortical reflex myoclonus. Patients have a shivering movement of the hands brought on by action, along with jerking of the upper limbs, and these are variably associated with focal and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. In many patients the main feature is a mild postural hand tremor that resembles essential tremor, combined with subtle proximal myoclonus. A minority of patients have borderline or moderately impaired cognition, and the myoclonus often worsens in advanced age. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Guerrini et al. (2001) described a pedigree with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME) in which 8 individuals had a nonprogressive disorder with onset between the ages of 12 and 50 years. All patients had mainly distal, semicontinuous rhythmic myoclonus and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and 3 also had complex partial seizures. Most individuals had seizures only rarely and had a normal cognitive level. However, the 3 individuals with intractable seizures had mild mental retardation. All patients had frontotemporal as well as generalized interictal EEG abnormalities. Electrophysiologic findings included an enhanced C-reflex at rest and high-amplitude somatosensory and visual evoked potentials. In patients, the resting motor threshold to transcranial magnetic stimulation was significantly reduced, and the post-MEP silent period was significantly shortened (101 ms vs 137 ms in controls). In 5 patients, EEG-EMG coherence at 8 to 25 Hz over the contralateral rolandic area, with EEG preceding EMG by 8 to 15 ms, suggested that the myoclonus was of cortical origin. Ipsilateral coherence in 2 patients suggested transcallosal spread. The authors suggested that the disorder involves diffuse cortical hyperexcitability, particularly of frontotemporal circuits. ⚠︎

Labauge et al. (2002) reported a 4-generation European family with familial adult myoclonic epilepsy (FAME) in which 10 living and 3 deceased relatives were affected. The 10 living patients had a mean age at onset of 41 years (range, 30-60 years). Affected individuals had adult-onset myoclonus of the extremities, and 8 of the 13 had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed the diagnosis of FAME in the 5 patients studied. ⚠︎

de Falco et al. (2003) reported a clinical and electrophysiologic study of 2 Italian families with benign adult familial myoclonic epilepsy (BAFME) that showed linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2.

Striano et al. (2005) reported a 5-generation family from Naples, Italy, with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. Eleven members, aged 24 to 56 years (mean, 39.2 years), had hand tremor and myoclonus, and 6 also had generalized seizures. Electrophysiologic study showed cortical reflex myoclonus in all affected family members. Three presymptomatic members had giant somatosensory evoked potential components and an enhanced long loop reflex I. After 1.5 years of follow-up, all 3 had developed hand tremor and upper-limb myoclonus.

Madia et al. (2008) studied 5 families with BAFME from southern Italy, 1 of which had not been reported before, and genotyped DNA from 53 affected individuals. The patients had cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy. ⚠︎

Suppa et al. (2009) reported an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy. Affected members had limb and voice tremor, seizures, and myoclonus of the eyelids during blinking. Neurophysiologic findings suggested increased excitability of the primary motor cortex, with normal sensorimotor integration. Neuropsychological testing showed visuospatial impairment, which the authors suggested might be due to temporal lobe dysfunction.

Crompton et al. (2012) reported a 6-generation FAME kindred of European descent, ascertained in New Zealand and Australia, with 55 affected members. All had fine hand tremor, which typically began in adolescence (median age, 15 years; range, 4-60 years). Proximal myoclonus was present in 44 of 55 (80%) and began later than the hand tremor (median age, 17 years; range, 5-60 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures occurred in 8 of 55 (15%), with a median age at onset of 43.5 years (range, 18-76 years). Neurophysiologic testing confirmed features of cortical reflex myoclonus. The most common phenotype was a mild postural hand tremor resembling essential tremor, combined with subtle proximal myoclonus. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures were uncommon and occurred around sleep onset after severe generalized myoclonus.

De Fusco et al. (2014) studied 2 ADCME families from Tuscany. Family A was the 5-generation family in which the ADCME locus had previously been mapped, and its clinical features closely resembled those of family B. In family B, all affected individuals had postural myoclonus of the hands and upper limbs. Cortical tremor was the presenting symptom in every case and appeared between 18 and 50 years of age. Epilepsy occurred in all affected individuals except 1 (IV:1, a 29-year-old woman with onset at 18 years). Generalized tonic-clonic seizures were seen in individuals I:2, II:4, III:2, and III:5. Two patients (III:2 and III:5) also had drug-resistant focal motor or complex partial seizures. Patient III:2 had a borderline cognitive level, whereas the other affected individuals had normal intelligence. Brain MRI was normal in the 3 patients examined. None of the ADCME patients had evidence of cardiovascular disease. Two of the oldest mutation carriers developed age-related dementia.

Corbett et al. (2019) tabulated clinical data from 21 FAME2 families and 1 singleton case. Fifteen families were Italian. The others were Australian/New Zealand of European ancestry (family 1), Iraqi of Sephardic Jewish ancestry, Israeli of Sephardic Jewish ancestry, South African of European ancestry, French/Spanish, and French, and the singleton case was Syrian. Myoclonus or cortical tremor was present in nearly all affected individuals studied. The proportion with tonic-clonic seizures varied between families, from 8 of 55 in family 1 to all affected members in families 2, 3, 4, 14, and 17. Focal seizures were infrequent and were absent in most families. Age at onset also varied between families: it was 12 years in family 6, 2 to 18 years in family 3, and 41 years in the French/Spanish family 19. In the large Australian/New Zealand family 1, the authors found evidence of anticipation, with a median age at onset of 30 years in generation III, 17 years in generation IV, and 12 years in generation V. ⚠︎

### Other Features

Corbett et al. (2019) noted that increased cerebellar choline on proton MR spectroscopy had previously been found in 11 individuals from 3 Italian families, all of which they showed to carry the STARD7 ATTTC expansion. Because STARD7 transfers phosphatidylcholine, they suggested that this finding may be specific to FAME2. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Guerrini et al. (2001) found that ADCME was inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with high penetrance. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also consistent with the pattern in the families reported by Labauge et al. (2002), Madia et al. (2008), Crompton et al. (2012), and De Fusco et al. (2014). In 1 large family, Corbett et al. (2019) found evidence of anticipation. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Guerrini et al. (2001) performed genomewide linkage analysis in their ADCME family. They identified a 12.4-cM critical region on chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2, between D2S2161 and D2S1897. The maximum 2-point lod score was 3.46 at theta = 0.0 for D2S2175, and the multipoint lod score around D2S2175 was 3.74.

de Falco et al. (2003) found linkage to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 2 Italian families with BAFME.

In a 5-generation Italian family with cortical tremor, myoclonus, and epilepsy, Striano et al. (2005) found significant linkage to chromosome 2p (maximum lod score of 5.9).

Madia et al. (2008) found significant linkage of BAFME to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2 in 5 southern Italian families. The maximum cumulative lod score was 18.5 for markers D2S2161 and D2S388.

Saint-Martin et al. (2008) refined the 2p11.1-q12.2 locus responsible for cortical tremor associated with epilepsy and excluded candidate genes in the region.

In an Italian family with familial cortical myoclonic tremor with epilepsy, Suppa et al. (2009) excluded linkage to the 8q24 locus. Affected members shared a common haplotype spanning 14.5 Mb in the pericentromeric region of chromosome 2.

Using genetic mapping in a large 6-generation family, Crompton et al. (2012) narrowed the FAME2 locus on chromosome 2 to a 13.3-Mb interval containing 99 known protein-coding genes.

### Heterogeneity

Guerrini et al. (2001) excluded linkage to the familial adult myoclonic epilepsy locus on chromosome 8q23.3-q24 in their ADCME family. They concluded that the localization near the chromosome 2 centromere and the different phenotype defined a new epilepsy syndrome.

Labauge et al. (2002) excluded linkage to the FAME/BAFME locus on chromosome 8q23.3-q24.1 in a 4-generation European family with FAME. They concluded that the disorder is genetically heterogeneous and that their family extended the occurrence of FAME to non-Japanese patients.

de Falco et al. (2003) noted that BAFME had been mapped to chromosome 8q24. They concluded that their findings in Italian families supported genetic heterogeneity and suggested that BAFME might be allelic with ADCME. ⚠︎

De Fusco et al. (2014) found no ADRA2B mutations in BAFME pedigrees from southern Italy that mapped to the ADCME locus, and Southern blot analysis excluded large insertions or deletions. The authors suggested that ADCME and BAFME/FAME might be distinct clinical entities, but they could not exclude variants in noncoding regulatory regions.

### Molecular Genetics

De Fusco et al. (2014) studied a 5-generation Tuscan family with ADCME (family A) that had previously been mapped to chromosome 2p11.1-q12.2. In this family they identified a heterozygous in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene (104260): c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG; p.His225_Leu229delinsGlnPheGlyArg. The change replaced 5 amino acids (HGGAL) with 4 new ones (QFGR). The indel segregated completely with the disease and was not found in 575 Tuscan controls or in public databases. Next-generation sequencing of the critical region in 2 affected members found no other relevant change. The same indel was found in a second Tuscan ADCME family (family B). The 2 families had no known kinship but shared the same disease haplotype across the entire critical region, which suggested a common ancestor. In transfected cells, the mutant receptor failed to form a stable complex with spinophilin (see PATHOGENESIS). ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from affected members of 3 FAME families using ExpansionHunter and exSTRa. They identified an expansion of the endogenous ATTTT repeat, with an inserted ATTTC repeat, in the first intron of the STARD7 gene within the FAME2 interval on chromosome 2. The ATTTC repeat was absent from whole-genome sequencing data of 69 controls. The authors developed a repeat-primed PCR assay and used it to detect the expansion in 137 of 137 affected individuals from 16 independently reported FAME2 families worldwide. Overall, the ATTTC expansion segregated in 158 of 158 affected individuals from 22 FAME pedigrees. Two of 24 tested individuals without a FAME diagnosis carried the expansion and were likely presymptomatic. The expansion was not amplified in 28 control DNA samples. Among family members with an uncertain FAME diagnosis, usually tremor alone, 8 of 13 carried the expansion. In family 1, 2 individuals with an uncertain diagnosis who tested negative did not share the affected haplotype. Corbett et al. (2019) also screened 52 unrelated patients with generalized epilepsy and tremor or with adult-onset myoclonic epilepsy and found the expansion in only 1, a Syrian singleton case. Long-read sequencing showed that the pathogenic allele had the structure (AAATG)n(AAAAT)n. In one patient, a SMRT read contained 274 AAATG and 387 AAAAT repeats, whereas a nanopore read contained 345 AAATG and 390 AAAAT repeats, suggesting somatic variation in repeat size. In a second patient (family 5, III-37), a single nanopore read spanned 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats, 4,645 bp in total length. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) found the STARD7 ATTTC expansion in 2 Italian families (families 4 and 15) that carried the ADRA2B indel previously reported by De Fusco et al. (2014). They concluded that this strongly suggested that the ADRA2B allele is not causative.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In preliminary analyses, Madia et al. (2008) found only minor clinical differences among patients carrying the shared disease haplotype. They suggested that other factors do not markedly influence the expressivity of the founder mutation.

### Pathogenesis

In transfected HeLa cells, De Fusco et al. (2014) found that mutant alpha-2B-adrenergic receptor (alpha-2B-AR) localized normally to the plasma membrane and was internalized in an arrestin-3-dependent manner, like the wildtype receptor. In coimmunoprecipitation studies, however, the mutant receptor failed to form a stable complex with spinophilin. After 10 minutes of epinephrine stimulation, spinophilin binding to the mutant receptor fell back to baseline, whereas binding to the wildtype receptor remained elevated. In Xenopus oocytes, spinophilin increased the EC50 for epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling through wildtype alpha-2B-AR but not through the mutant receptor. The authors concluded that the mutation increases the intensity of Ca(2+) signaling, a gain-of-function effect caused by loss of the spinophilin interaction. They proposed that the resulting cortical hyperexcitability might arise from impaired GABAergic inhibition. However, Corbett et al. (2019) later concluded that the ADRA2B allele is not causative (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

Corbett et al. (2019) found that STARD7 transcript and protein levels were not altered in patient-derived fibroblasts, and RNA-seq detected no reads containing ATTTC repeats. They suggested that the repeat sequence itself is pathogenic, independent of any effect on the host gene.

### Diagnosis

Corbett et al. (2019) reported that a repeat-primed PCR assay for the ATTTC repeat was diagnostic for FAME, with 100% sensitivity in families linked or suggestively linked to chromosome 2. They noted that FAME2 is the locus most frequently linked in Caucasian patients.

### Population Genetics

Madia et al. (2008) found that a haplotype ('5332') of the adjacent markers D2S388, D2S2216, D2S113, and D2S2175 segregated with the disease in all 5 southern Italian BAFME families. This indicated that the same mutation, inherited from a common ancestor, segregated in these families.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2 (REPORTED; LATER CONCLUDED NOT CAUSATIVE)**  
ADRA2B, 12-BP DEL/9-BP INS, NT675 [c.675_686deltggtggggctttinsgtttggcag; p.H225]

In affected members of 2 Tuscan families with autosomal dominant cortical myoclonus and epilepsy (ADCME), De Fusco et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous in-frame insertion/deletion in the ADRA2B gene: c.675_686delTGGTGGGGCTTTinsGTTTGGCAG, resulting in p.His225_Leu229delinsGlnPheGlyArg. The change replaced 5 amino acids (HGGAL) with 4 new ones (QFGR). In family A, the indel segregated completely with the disease. It was not found in 575 Tuscan controls or in public databases. The 2 families shared the same disease haplotype, suggesting a common ancestor. In functional studies, the mutant receptor failed to form a stable complex with spinophilin, and spinophilin did not modulate epinephrine-stimulated calcium signaling through the mutant receptor. The authors interpreted this as a gain-of-function effect. ⚠︎

Corbett et al. (2019) found that 2 Italian FAME2 families carrying this ADRA2B variant (families 4 and 15) also carried the STARD7 intronic ATTTC repeat expansion. They concluded that this strongly suggested that the ADRA2B allele is not causative.

**.0002 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2**  
STARD7, (ATTTC)n INS, INTRON 1

Corbett et al. (2019) identified an expansion of the endogenous ATTTT repeat with an inserted ATTTC repeat in the first intron of the STARD7 gene in a large Australian/New Zealand family of European ancestry with FAME2 (family 1). The family had 55 affected members, all of whom had myoclonus or cortical tremor, and 8 had tonic-clonic seizures. Whole-genome sequencing analysis with ExpansionHunter and exSTRa was used to find the expansion, and the ATTTC repeat was absent from whole-genome sequencing data of 69 controls. The expansion was present in all tested affected family members. Two tested-negative individuals with an uncertain diagnosis did not share the affected haplotype.

**.0003 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2**  
STARD7, ATTTC REPEAT EXPANSION

Using a repeat-primed PCR assay, Corbett et al. (2019) found the STARD7 intronic ATTTC repeat expansion in affected members of FAME2 families of Italian, Iraqi Sephardic Jewish, Israeli Sephardic Jewish, South African European, French/Spanish, and French ancestry. They also found it in 1 Syrian singleton case among 52 unrelated patients with generalized epilepsy and tremor or adult-onset myoclonic epilepsy. Overall, the expansion segregated in 158 of 158 affected individuals from 22 FAME pedigrees and was not amplified in 28 control DNA samples. Two of the Italian families (families 4 and 15) also carried the ADRA2B indel previously reported by De Fusco et al. (2014). Long-read sequencing showed that the pathogenic allele had the structure (AAATG)n(AAAAT)n, and differing repeat counts between sequencing reads from one patient suggested somatic variation in repeat size. STARD7 transcript and protein levels were not altered in patient fibroblasts. ⚠︎

**.0004 EPILEPSY, FAMILIAL ADULT MYOCLONIC, 2**  
STARD7, (AAATG)588(AAAAT)340

In an Italian family with FAME2 (family 5), Corbett et al. (2019) found the STARD7 intronic ATTTC repeat expansion in all affected members tested. The family had 25 affected members, all of whom had myoclonus or cortical tremor, and 10 had tonic-clonic seizures. In 1 affected member (III-37), a single Oxford Nanopore read spanned the expanded repeats, which comprised 588 AAATG and 340 AAAAT repeats, 4,645 bp in total length.
