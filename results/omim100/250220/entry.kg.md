### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Sedaghatian type (SMDS) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GPX4 gene (138322) on chromosome 19p13.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia is a neonatal lethal form of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. Smith et al. (2014) described its features as severe metaphyseal chondrodysplasia with mild limb shortening, platyspondyly, cardiac conduction defects, and central nervous system abnormalities.

### Clinical Features

Sedaghatian (1980) reported 2 brothers, born within a year of each other, who had a similar skeletal disorder. It consisted of severe congenital metaphyseal involvement, mild rhizomelic shortness of the upper limbs, and mild platyspondyly. Both brothers died at 3 days of age of cardiorespiratory insufficiency. At autopsy, one infant had pulmonary, renal, and adrenal hemorrhage, along with subendocardial myocarditis and myocardial necrosis. The author stated that it was unclear how these manifestations were related pathogenetically. ⚠︎

Opitz et al. (1987) reported a second Iranian infant with Sedaghatian syndrome, who died on day 4 of life. Histology showed severe epimetaphyseal dysplasia. Opitz et al. (1987) summarized Sedaghatian's (1980) family as 2 brothers and a sister with a neonatally lethal disorder marked by slight rhizomelic limb shortening, mild platyspondyly, and severe metaphyseal dysplasia. One of Sedaghatian's patients also had microphthalmia, asymmetric ears, a depressed nasal bridge, a broad nose, a short neck, a prominent sternum, and short lower extremities. On this basis, Opitz et al. (1987) suggested that the syndrome may affect more systems than the radiographs indicate.

Peeden et al. (1992) reported another infant with spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Sedaghatian type, who had a lethal course. They reviewed the Iranian infant described by Sedaghatian (1980), who died shortly after birth. At autopsy he had subacute myocarditis, cortical necrosis of the kidneys, and adrenal and pulmonary hemorrhage. His skeletal findings were mild rhizomelic shortening of the limbs, platyspondyly, and 'laciness' of the iliac wings. Peeden et al. (1992) noted that the second Iranian infant, reported by Opitz et al. (1987), had a similar perinatal course and similar radiographs.

Elcioglu and Hall (1998) reported the radiologic findings in 2 unrelated cases of spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Sedaghatian type. After reviewing the literature, they identified additional anomalies, including disproportionately long fibulae, dysharmonious maturation, and turricephaly.

Koutouby et al. (2000) reported an infant with spondylometaphyseal dysplasia whose parents were nonconsanguineous and Yemeni. The radiologic findings were consistent with lethal metaphyseal chondrodysplasia, Sedaghatian type. All previously reported patients had died within 4 days of life, but this patient survived 161 days.

English et al. (2006) reported a female infant with Sedaghatian spondylometaphyseal dysplasia whose parents were nonconsanguineous and Caucasian. She had a metaphyseal dysplasia, was hypotonic from birth, and had recurrent cyanotic episodes within a few hours of delivery. Brain imaging showed absence of the corpus callosum and marked frontotemporal pachygyria. She developed seizures on day 14 and died at age 17 days. English et al. (2006) concluded that neuronal migration abnormalities can occur in this disorder, and they suggested agenesis of the corpus callosum as a new association.

Aygun et al. (2012) reported a patient with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia. The patient had metaphyseal dysplasia, congenital atrioventricular block, a simplified gyral pattern, hypogenesis of the corpus callosum, and severe cerebellar hypoplasia. The authors suggested that central nervous system malformations might be a major component of this congenital lethal skeletal dysplasia and should be evaluated in detail.

### Biochemical Features

Of the 2 affected brothers reported by Sedaghatian (1980), only one had laboratory tests before death. These showed low calcium, high phosphorus, and high alkaline phosphatase levels, which were attributed to a renal defect.

### Inheritance

The parents of the affected brothers reported by Sedaghatian (1980) were normal and had recently had an affected baby girl. On this basis the author presumed autosomal recessive inheritance.

Opitz et al. (1987) suggested that Sedaghatian syndrome is an autosomal recessive trait with a high gene frequency in Iranians. Their reasons were that children of both sexes were affected in one family, the parents were normal first cousins, and the disorder had so far been seen only in Iranians. ⚠︎

Peeden et al. (1992) also suggested autosomal recessive inheritance of a single-gene defect, because the Iranian infant reported by Opitz et al. (1987) was born to first-cousin parents.

In a girl with SMDS, Smith et al. (2014) found compound heterozygous GPX4 mutations: one was inherited from her mother and the other was de novo. In a second, unrelated family, both unaffected parents of an affected child carried the same heterozygous GPX4 nonsense mutation. These findings are consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Koutouby et al. (2000) investigated their Yemeni patient with serum calcium, magnesium, zinc, ammonia, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, and parathormone levels; liver and renal function tests; TORCH; metabolic screening; chromosomal studies; muscle enzymes; EEG; CT scan; and brain MRI. They analyzed genomic DNA from the patient and parents but found no gene abnormality.

Smith et al. (2014) performed whole-exome sequencing on a girl with SMDS and her unaffected parents. They identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GPX4 gene. The first was a maternally inherited c.587+5G-A splice site mutation; in vitro studies showed that it caused part of exon 4 to be spliced out, resulting in a frameshift and premature truncation. The second was a de novo c.588-8_588-4del mutation; in vitro studies showed that it caused skipping of exon 5, resulting in a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4. In a second, unrelated child with SMDS, whose DNA was not available, Sanger sequencing showed that both unaffected parents carried the same heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 3 of GPX4: c.381C-A, tyr127-to-ter (Y127X). ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

GPX4 is an antioxidant enzyme that protects cells against membrane lipid peroxidation and is essential for early embryo development. Smith et al. (2014) concluded that the truncating GPX4 mutations they found in 2 families with SMDS supported a pathogenic role for GPX4. They suggested that their findings highlighted the importance of GPX4 in development of the cardiac, nervous, and skeletal systems.

Smith et al. (2014) performed whole-exome sequencing on a girl with Sedaghatian-type spondylometaphyseal dysplasia (SMDS) and her unaffected parents. They identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GPX4 gene. One was a maternally inherited c.587+5G-A splice site mutation; in vitro studies showed that it caused part of exon 4 to be spliced out, resulting in a frameshift and premature truncation. The other was a de novo 5-bp deletion (c.588-8_588-4del).

**.0002 SPONDYLOMETAPHYSEAL DYSPLASIA, SEDAGHATIAN TYPE**  
GPX4, 5-BP DEL, IVS4AS, -8 TO -4 [c.588-8_588-4del]

In a girl with SMDS, Smith et al. (2014) identified a de novo c.588-8_588-4del mutation in the GPX4 gene. The girl also carried a maternally inherited c.587+5G-A mutation. In vitro studies showed that the deletion caused skipping of exon 5, resulting in a frameshift and premature truncation of GPX4.

Smith et al. (2014) studied a child with SMDS whose DNA was not available. Sanger sequencing showed that both unaffected parents carried the same heterozygous c.381C-A nonsense mutation in exon 3 of the GPX4 gene, resulting in a tyr127-to-ter (Y127X) substitution.
