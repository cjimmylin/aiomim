### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in PNPLA8, which encodes the mitochondrial calcium-independent phospholipase A2-gamma (iPLA2-gamma). The first molecularly characterized patient, reported by Saunders et al. (2015), had a suspected mitochondrial myopathy with progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia, seizures, poor weight gain, and lactic acidosis. Subsequent reports broadened the phenotype; Nakamura et al. (2024) described PNPLA8-related neurologic disease as a continuum ranging from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy, at the most severe end with congenital microcephaly, to childhood-onset neurodegeneration, and Burnyte et al. (2023) described a milder adolescent-onset form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy.

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) reported a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described a 23-year-old man with a mitochondrial myopathy who had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that had resulted in cardiac insufficiency 22 years before presentation of the skeletal myopathy. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and severe lactic acidosis developed at one stage of the illness. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and accumulation of neutral lipids, which was confirmed histochemically and by thin-layer chromatography. The metabolic defect was not defined.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl (CMH193) evaluated for a suspected mitochondrial myopathy with dystonia, abnormal gait, and abnormal muscle biopsy. She was asymptomatic in the first year and met developmental milestones, although toe-walking was noted. At age 2 she had a positive Gowers sign, and by age 3 years she could no longer walk. Fine motor skills declined and speech became dysarthric. She had proximal muscle weakness, tight heel cords, and generalized mildly increased tone; weakness and dysmetria progressed, and hypotonicity progressed to spasticity. She later developed complex partial seizures with recurrent left temporal lobe spikes on EEG. She had a very lean body habitus and developed significant lactic acidosis requiring bicarbonate supplementation, with a consistently elevated anion gap since age 5. At age 7, blood lactate (3.8-5.6 mmol/L; normal 0.7-2.1) and pyruvate (0.18-0.32 mmol/L; normal 0.08-0.16) were elevated on multiple occasions, with lactate:pyruvate ratios of 17.5-21.1; creatine phosphokinase and the plasma acylcarnitine profile were normal. CSF studies at age 6 showed deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle increased signal in the medial globus pallidus bilaterally, which was no longer seen 2 years later. Muscle biopsy at age 4 showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic fibers but no ragged red fibers; electron microscopy showed disarray of cristae, globular dense osmiophilic inclusions, and subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggestive of degenerating mitochondria, as well as increased secondary lysosomes.

Shukla et al. (2018) reported 2 unrelated girls. Patient 1, of Indian origin, was the second child of consanguineous parents. She was evaluated on postnatal day 6 for congenital microcephaly and seizures; birth weight was 2410 g (-2 SD), length 48 cm (-1 SD), and head circumference 29 cm (-4 SD). She had multifocal clonic seizures from the second day of life and spasticity of all 4 limbs. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy, and MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. Patient 2, born to nonconsanguineous parents (India/Fiji), developed normally until about 13 months of age, then regressed with episodes of epilepsia partialis continua and difficult-to-control focal seizures. Her development stagnated at an 8-month level, she was nonverbal, required gastrostomy feeding, and developed acquired microcephaly (head circumference 49 cm, -4.7 SD, at age 15 years), choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, and severe contractures. Serial MRI showed progressive volume loss in the cerebellum, brainstem, parietal and occipital lobes, and corpus callosum, with a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak on MR spectroscopy. Muscle biopsy showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria with surrounding membrane whorls and disrupted cristae. Echocardiography showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, blood lactate was elevated on several occasions, and visual evoked potentials showed severely reduced amplitudes with prolonged latencies.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with prenatal-onset severe and progressive neurodegeneration with death in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) described 2 adult sibs, a brother and sister, with slowly progressive cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy. Sibling A, a 49-year-old man, developed weakness and distal muscle atrophy of the lower limbs at age 14 years, accompanied by ataxia and gait disturbance; he could not walk without assistance from age 17 and became nonambulant at age 44. He had tetraparesis, muscle wasting, absent lower limb reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, marked dysmetria, and mild length-dependent hypoesthesia; at 45 years he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus, with a Mini-Mental State Examination score of 30/30. Nerve conduction studies showed sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, and brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. Sibling B developed difficulty walking and lower limb weakness at age 14 years; at 21 years she had distal limb atrophy, absent lower limb reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, severe axial ataxia, and appendicular dysmetria, with normal cognition and axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy. Neither sib had visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver dysfunction, and the authors interpreted the phenotype as milder than those previously reported.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patient 1 had the congenital form of the disease, whereas patients 2 and 3 had progressive microcephaly, infantile-onset seizures, progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors proposed infantile onset as a distinct phenotype, in addition to the previously reported congenital form (congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early lethality), childhood neurodegeneration with progressive microcephaly, and adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with peripheral neuropathy.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals (6 male and 8 female) with neurologic manifestations from 12 unrelated families, aged 3 days to 33 years at last assessment. Common features were central hypotonia (13 of 13), dystonia (8 of 12), seizures (10 of 14), and cerebellar atrophy (12 of 12). Eight patients (families 1-8) had congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), global developmental delay without attainment of milestones (7 of 7), peripheral spasticity (7 of 8), and seizure onset within 7 months of age (8 of 8), with a simplified gyral pattern (6 of 6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7 of 7), and severe progressive cerebral cortical atrophy (5 of 5). In contrast, 4 patients (families 11-12) had relatively mild features: all attained walking, 3 of 4 had ataxia, all had regression of milestones, 3 of 4 had mild or no intellectual disability, and brain MRI showed little or no cerebral cortical volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Patients from families 9 and 10 showed intermediate severity. Combining these patients with 11 previously reported individuals from 8 families (25 individuals from 20 families), the authors concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum ranging from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy to childhood-onset neurodegeneration.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015), oxidative enzyme reactions (SDH and COX) in muscle were normal and electron transport chain function was not affected. The authors suggested that, in the absence of an elevated lactate:pyruvate ratio, the findings indicated mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect.

In patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018), electron transport chain enzyme activities of complexes I-IV in muscle were within normal ranges, and at age 14 years growth and differentiation factor-15 was significantly elevated (1293 pg/ml; normal values less than 750 pg/ml).

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that maximal respiration was significantly lower in skin fibroblasts from their patient 1 than in controls, although mitochondrial morphology in the fibroblasts appeared normal by transmission electron microscopy. The abundance of cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin appeared to be decreased in patient-derived fibroblasts compared with controls, without a significant correlation with phenotypic severity.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis in the families reported by Saunders et al. (2015) and Shukla et al. (2018) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance; in both reports, segregation analysis showed that the variants were inherited from carrier parents. Nakamura et al. (2024) reported pedigrees showing autosomal recessive inheritance in 12 families.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 7-year-old girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4) (NM_001256011.1). The mutations, found by exome sequencing of the patient and her parents and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, were inherited from carrier parents; 2 healthy sibs carried neither variant. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot analysis of patient muscle showed a dramatic decrease of PNPLA8 protein bands (88, 63, and 52 kD).

By whole-exome sequencing in 2 unrelated girls with microcephaly, spasticity, lactic acidosis, and cerebellar and brainstem volume loss, Shukla et al. (2018) identified biallelic loss-of-function variants in PNPLA8. Patient 1, born to consanguineous parents, was homozygous for a c.517G-T transversion resulting in a glu173-to-ter (E173X) substitution (NM_001256007.1); her parents were heterozygous. Patient 2 was compound heterozygous for a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT), resulting in a frameshift (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), and a c.419C-A transversion, resulting in a ser140-to-ter (S140X) substitution. Segregation analysis showed that the variants were inherited from carrier parents.

In a patient with prenatal-onset severe and progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy, Masih et al. (2021) identified by whole-exome sequencing a homozygous missense variant, c.1874A-G, in the patatin domain of PNPLA8.

In 2 adult sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified by whole-exome sequencing compound heterozygous frameshift variants in the last coding exon of PNPLA8 (NM_001256007.3): a novel 1-bp deletion (c.2269del), predicted to result in p.Thr757GlnfsTer10, and the previously reported c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4). The c.2275_2276del variant was maternally inherited; paternal DNA was unavailable, but exome and Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 changes were in trans. Both variants were confirmed in the affected sister by Sanger sequencing, and c.2269del was not present in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, or gnomAD databases.

In 3 patients from 2 families, Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) identified by exome sequencing a loss-of-function variant in PNPLA8, c.1231C-T, resulting in an arg411-to-ter (R411X) substitution, in family I, and a missense variant, c.1559T-A, resulting in a val520-to-asp (V520D) substitution, in family II.

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified biallelic ultra-rare PNPLA8 variants in 14 patients from 12 unrelated families, found by proband-only or trio exome or genome sequencing with Sanger segregation analysis; families 7 and 8 had been reported previously without details, family 7 by Denommé-Pichon et al. (2022). Of 14 variants, 12 were novel: 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic near an exon-intron boundary, and 2 missense, all with population frequencies below 0.001%. In lymphoblastoid cells from patient 1, the c.1625+3_1625+6del variant caused skipping of exons 6 to 8, and his skin fibroblasts showed a significant reduction of the 77-kD PNPLA8 isoform. The splice-site variants c.2075-2A-G and c.1684-2A-G were predicted to abolish splice acceptor sites. Overall, 12 of the 14 individuals carried combinations of nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants, suggesting loss of function. A frameshift identified in a severely affected patient (p.Glu296Lysfs*6) also resulted in a null effect in fibroblasts. The authors also identified 2 individuals from 2 unrelated Iranian families in a non-neurologic cohort, one with optic atrophy and one with nonsyndromic hearing loss, who were homozygous for distinct N-terminal frameshift variants affecting only the largest (88-kD) isoform; in the optic atrophy family, an affected sib was heterozygous, and the authors considered the significance of these variants undetermined. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that the c.2275_2276del variant had been reported in compound heterozygosity with another truncating variant in a patient with severe early-childhood neurodegeneration, whereas their sibs, compound heterozygous for c.2275_2276del and c.2269del, had a less severe phenotype. Because both variants lie in the last coding exon, they suggested that the transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and retain partial enzyme activity.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) suggested that the site of the variant, rather than its type, is strongly correlated with disease onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were associated specifically with the severe end of the spectrum, with congenital microcephaly and pontocerebellar hypoplasia. Patients of intermediate severity carried combinations of a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent C-terminal frameshift p.Leu759Alafs*4, and all patients in the mild group, characterized by late-onset movement disorders, carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757Glnfs*10 or p.Leu759Alafs*4). Fibroblasts from a mildly affected patient with p.Leu759Alafs*4 showed weak expression of the 77-kD PNPLA8 isoform, suggesting residual protein function, consistent with escape from nonsense-mediated decay. The authors concluded that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting the 77- and 74-kD isoforms are sufficient for the severe developmental and progressive phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that a 110-kD immunoreactive band present in control muscle was absent in their patient's muscle along with the iPLA2-gamma bands; mass spectrometry identified this band as heat shock protein HSPA4, which was not part of iPLA2-gamma. They speculated that loss of this chaperone might also contribute to pathogenesis.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that cerebral organoids from CRISPR/Cas9-generated PNPLA8-knockout iPSCs had smaller subventricular zone-like regions, reduced numbers of PAX6+HOPX+ basal radial glial cells, and reduced numbers of SATB2+ upper-layer neurons compared with wildtype organoids, without changes in deep-layer neurons or basal intermediate progenitors. Organoids derived from their patient 1 showed the same defects. Spatial transcriptomics suggested altered fate specification of apical radial glial cells, with enrichment of cell cycle, neural differentiation, and lysophospholipid pathway gene sets. PNPLA8-knockout neural progenitor cells had reduced lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid, but mitochondrial function and morphology were barely affected in these cells. Treatment with lysophosphatidic acid increased the number of basal radial glial cells in patient-derived organoids. The authors concluded that biallelic null variants in PNPLA8 cause microcephaly through reduced abundance of basal radial glial cells. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

In the patient reported by Mastaglia et al. (1980), marked improvement in the myopathy occurred after starting prednisone and thiamine and was maintained after the drugs were withdrawn.

In the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015), treatment with a multivitamin containing iron, carbidopa-levodopa, and leucovorin initially improved symptoms, and CoQ10 supplementation increased appetite without subsequent weight gain.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that iPla2-gamma -/- mice had a markedly elevated hippocampal cardiolipin content with a shift to shorter chain molecular species, alterations in choline and ethanolamine glycerophospholipids, increased oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine, and increased ceramides. Electron microscopy showed enlarged heteromorphic lamellar structures, identified as degenerating mitochondria, and ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. They concluded that loss of iPla2-gamma function results in a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder characterized by degenerating mitochondria, autophagy, and cognitive dysfunction.
