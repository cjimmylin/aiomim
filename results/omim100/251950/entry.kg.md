## MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS; MMLA

### Phenotype-Gene Relationships

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31.1. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is an autosomal recessive mitochondriopathy caused by biallelic variants in PNPLA8, which encodes calcium-independent phospholipase A2-gamma (iPLA2-gamma). Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) described PNPLA8-related disease as a spectrum of overlapping features that line up with different ages of onset. The spectrum runs from a congenital form with congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early death; through infantile- and childhood-onset neurodegeneration with progressive microcephaly; to an adult form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy. Nakamura et al. (2024) reported that frequent features were central hypotonia, dystonia, seizures, and cerebellar atrophy. Lactic acidosis and abnormal muscle mitochondrial ultrastructure were reported in several patients (Saunders et al., 2015; Shukla et al., 2018).

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) described a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described a 23-year-old man with mitochondrial myopathy. He had a high-output circulatory state and a cardiomyopathy that caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy appeared. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and at one stage of the illness he developed severe lactic acidosis. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids. Histochemistry and thin-layer chromatography confirmed the lipid accumulation.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old girl (CMH193) with a suspected mitochondrial myopathy. She was asymptomatic in her first year and met developmental milestones, although toe-walking was noted. She had a positive Gowers sign at age 2 years and could no longer walk by age 3. Her course was progressive. It included proximal muscle weakness, dysmetria, dysarthria, decline in fine motor skills, oral motor dystonia, and hypotonia that progressed to spasticity. She had complex partial seizures with left temporal lobe spikes on EEG, a very lean body habitus without weight gain, and significant lactic acidosis requiring bicarbonate supplementation. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at age 4 years showed variable fiber size with isolated small atrophic type 1 and type 2 fibers, and no ragged red fibers. On electron microscopy, mitochondria showed marked disarray of the cristae and globular dense osmiophilic inclusions. There were also subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes, suggestive of degenerating mitochondria. Secondary lysosomes containing residual bodies were increased. Brain MRI at 33 months showed subtle bilateral increased signal in the medial globus pallidus, which was no longer seen 2 years later. ⚠︎

Shukla et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl (patient 1) born to consanguineous parents. She had congenital microcephaly (head circumference -4 SD at birth), multifocal clonic seizures starting on day 2 of life, and spasticity of all four limbs. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. TORCH testing was negative.

Shukla et al. (2018) also reported a girl (patient 2) of Indian/Fijian origin born to nonconsanguineous parents. She developed normally until about 13 months of age and then regressed, with epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures. She went on to have severe developmental stagnation and acquired microcephaly (-4.7 SD at age 15 years); she was nonverbal and fed by gastrostomy tube. Other features were hypotonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent reflexes, choreiform movements, dystonia, and contractures. She developed progressive atrophy of the cerebrum, cerebellum, and brainstem, and MR spectroscopy showed a persistently elevated lipid-lactate peak. She also had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis, abnormal visual evoked potentials, obstructive sleep apnea, elevated blood lactate, and elevated GDF15.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient with severe neurodegeneration that began before birth, progressed, and led to death in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) described 2 adult Lithuanian sibs, a brother and a sister, with slowly progressive cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. Onset was at age 14 years, and cognition was preserved. The brother was 49 years old at the time of the report. He had lower-limb weakness, distal muscle atrophy, ataxia and gait disturbance, tetraparesis, absent lower-limb tendon reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, dysmetria, and mild distal hypoesthesia. He became nonambulant at age 44 years. At age 45 he had mild dysarthria, horizontal nystagmus, and a normal Mini-Mental State Examination score, and brain MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. His sister had severe axial ataxia, appendicular dysmetria, distal muscle atrophy, areflexia, and bilateral Babinski signs at age 21. EMG showed no myopathic changes. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported 3 patients from 2 families with PNPLA8-related mitochondriopathy. Patient 1 (family I) had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3 (family II) had progressive microcephaly, seizures starting in infancy, progressive cortical atrophy, loss of white matter, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia. The authors noted that only 6 patients with biallelic PNPLA8 variants had been reported before. Those phenotypes comprised a congenital form (congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, and early death), a childhood neurodegeneration form with progressive microcephaly, and an adult-onset form with cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 individuals (6 male, 8 female) from 12 unrelated families with neurological disease and biallelic PNPLA8 variants. Many of the families were consanguineous, and they came from countries including Japan, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Turkey, Lebanon, Egypt, and Iran. Age at last assessment ranged from 3 days to 33 years. Frequent features were central hypotonia (13/13), dystonia (8/12), seizures (10/14), and cerebellar atrophy (12/12). Eight patients (families 1-8) were at the severe end of the spectrum. They had congenital microcephaly (5/7), global developmental delay with no milestones attained (7/7), peripheral spasticity (7/8), and seizure onset within 7 months of age (8/8). Their neuroimaging showed a simplified gyral pattern (6/6), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (7/7), and severe progressive cerebral cortical atrophy (5/5). Four patients (families 11-12) had a relatively mild phenotype. All 4 learned to walk and then lost milestones; 3 of the 4 had ataxia, 3 of the 4 had mild or no intellectual disability, and seizures were unique or absent. Their brain MRI showed little or no cerebral cortical volume loss but progressive cerebellar atrophy. Two further patients (families 9 and 10) were of intermediate severity.

### Biochemical Features

Saunders et al. (2015) found that their patient had a persistently elevated anion gap from age 5 years. At age 7 she had elevated blood lactate (3.8-5.6 mmol/L) and pyruvate, a lactate:pyruvate ratio that was not elevated, and a normal plasma acylcarnitine profile. CSF studies at age 6 showed 5-methyltetrahydrofolate deficiency. Creatine kinase and liver function tests were normal. Mitochondrial DNA tRNA sequencing and mtDNA depletion studies were normal, and muscle SDH and COX activity and electron transport chain function were unaffected. The authors suggested that this pattern indicated mitochondrial dysfunction rather than an electron transport chain defect.

Shukla et al. (2018) found that the muscle biopsy of patient 2 showed enlarged, irregularly shaped mitochondria with surrounding membrane whorls, disrupted cristae, electron-dense deposits, lipid droplets, and glycogen pools. Activities of electron transport chain complexes I through IV were normal. GDF15 was elevated at 1293 pg/ml at age 14 years.

In skin fibroblasts from their patient 1, Nakamura et al. (2024) found significantly lower maximal mitochondrial respiration than in healthy controls, although mitochondrial morphology was normal on electron microscopy. Cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin appeared decreased in patient fibroblasts compared with controls, but the levels did not correlate with phenotypic severity.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis is autosomal recessive. In the patient reported by Saunders et al. (2015), each of the 2 PNPLA8 variants was inherited from a carrier parent. Shukla et al. (2018) likewise found that the biallelic PNPLA8 variants in their 2 patients were inherited from carrier parents.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 7-year-old girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) performed trio exome sequencing and found compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (p.Asn112HisfsX29) and c.1975_1976delAG (p.Leu659AlafsX4). Sanger sequencing confirmed both variants. Each was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither variant. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle showed a marked decrease in the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 (iPLA2-gamma) protein bands compared with 3 control muscles. A 110-kD band present in controls was also absent. Mass spectrometry identified this band as HSPA4, which the authors concluded had reacted nonspecifically with the antibody.

Using whole-exome sequencing, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in PNPLA8, c.517G-T (p.Glu173Ter; NM_001256007.1), in patient 1, the daughter of consanguineous Indian parents. Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents were heterozygous for the mutation. In patient 2, trio whole-exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous mutations in PNPLA8: a frameshift, c.2275_2276delCT (p.Leu759AlafsTer4), and a nonsense mutation, c.419C-A (p.Ser140Ter). Sanger sequencing confirmed both mutations, and each was inherited from a carrier parent. ⚠︎

In a patient with severe neurodegeneration that began before birth, Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous missense variant, c.1874A-G, in the patatin domain of PNPLA8.

In 2 Lithuanian sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in exon 11 of PNPLA8. One was a novel c.2269del (p.Thr757GlnfsTer10), and the other was the previously reported c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4). The c.2269del variant was not found in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, or gnomAD databases. The c.2275_2276del variant was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but sequencing showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles (in trans). Sanger sequencing confirmed both variants in the affected sister.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing in 3 patients from 2 families. In patient 1 (family I), who had the congenital form, they identified a loss-of-function variant in PNPLA8, c.1231C-T (p.Arg411Ter). In patients 2 and 3 (family II), who had infantile-onset disease, they identified a missense variant, c.1559T-A (p.Val520Asp).

In 14 individuals from 12 families with neurological disease, Nakamura et al. (2024) found 14 PNPLA8 variants by exome or genome sequencing, with segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing; 12 of the variants were novel. They comprised 4 nonsense, 5 frameshift, 3 intronic variants near exon-intron boundaries, and 2 missense variants. Every variant had a frequency below 0.001% in population databases (dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, jMorp, and gnomAD). Twelve of the 14 individuals carried combinations of nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants, suggesting loss of function. In patient 1 (family 1), the intronic variant c.1625+3_1625+6del caused skipping of exons 6-8 through aberrant splicing in patient lymphoblastoid cells. The patient's fibroblasts showed a significant reduction of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein, indicating a null effect. Patient 2 had the splice-acceptor variant c.2075-2A-G, and patient 5 had c.1684-2A-G. No cells were available from these patients, but SpliceAI predicted loss of the splice acceptor site, with delta scores of 0.97 and 0.93, respectively. Patient 6, who had a severe phenotype, carried the frameshift variant p.Glu296Lysfs*6. Like the variant in patient 1, it had a null effect on PNPLA8 protein in skin fibroblasts.

Nakamura et al. (2024) also studied 2 unrelated Iranian families from a non-neurological cohort. In these families they identified individuals homozygous for distinct N-terminal PNPLA8 frameshift variants that affect only the largest (88-kDa) isoform. One proband had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss. In the optic atrophy family, an affected sib was only heterozygous for the variant, so the authors considered these variants of undetermined significance.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that the 3 known patients with PNPLA8 loss-of-function variants had similar but variable clinical features. Because so few patients were known, they could not establish a genotype-phenotype correlation.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that c.2275_2276del had previously been reported in compound heterozygous state with another truncating variant in a patient with severe early-childhood neurodegeneration, whereas their patients had a milder phenotype. Both truncating variants in their patients lie in the last coding exon of PNPLA8. The authors therefore suggested that these transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and possibly retain partial enzyme activity.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) proposed infantile onset as a new, distinct phenotype of PNPLA8-related disease. They suggested that age at disease onset correlates strongly with where the variant lies, not with what type of variant it is.

Nakamura et al. (2024) combined their patients with 11 previously reported individuals from 8 families. They found that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were associated with the severe phenotype. All patients with a relatively mild neurodegenerative phenotype and late-onset movement disorders carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (p.Thr757Glnfs*10 or p.Leu759Alafs*4). Intermediate cases carried a loss-of-function variant, missense variants, or the recurrent p.Leu759Alafs*4. In skin fibroblasts, weak residual expression of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein was found in patient 12 (mild phenotype, p.Leu759Alafs*4) and in patient 14 (non-neuronal phenotype, p.Val7*). In contrast, the homozygous missense variant p.Val520Asp from a previously reported patient with a severe phenotype gave protein levels similar to controls. The authors suggested that C-terminal truncating variants escape nonsense-mediated decay, leading to less severe disease.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) did not define the metabolic defect in their patient. They postulated an abnormality involving the Krebs cycle that resulted in overproduction of lactate and in lipid accumulation in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) suggested that PNPLA8-related disease, like PLA2G6-related disease, may result primarily from altered homeostasis of the mitochondrial inner membrane, with effects on electron transport and ATP synthesis being secondary.

Shukla et al. (2018) noted that all PNPLA8 variants reported up to that time introduce premature stop codons and are likely to cause truncated protein or nonsense-mediated decay. They also observed that the abnormal mitochondria with concentric lamellar membranes in their patient 2 resembled findings in Pnpla8-null mice, and that the human phenotype resembled the progressive neurodegeneration seen in those mice.

Nakamura et al. (2024) made cerebral organoids from CRISPR/Cas9 PNPLA8-knockout human iPSCs and from iPSCs of their patient 1. These organoids had a smaller subventricular zone-like region, fewer PAX6+HOPX+ basal radial glial cells, and fewer SATB2+ upper-layer neurons; deep-layer neurons and basal intermediate progenitors were unchanged. Knockout neural progenitors had normal mitochondrial morphology and respiration but reduced lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid. Adding LPA partly restored the number of basal radial glia in patient organoids.

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) observed a marked improvement in their patient's myopathy after he started prednisone and thiamine. The improvement continued after both drugs were withdrawn.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported that their patient's symptoms initially improved with a multivitamin containing iron, carbidopa-levodopa, and leucovorin. CoQ10 supplementation increased her appetite but did not lead to weight gain.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found that iPLA2-gamma-null mice had major changes in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism and mitochondrial phospholipid homeostasis. Shotgun lipidomics showed a markedly raised hippocampal cardiolipin content, with the molecular species shifted toward shorter chains. The mice also had altered choline and ethanolamine glycerophospholipids, including less plasmenylethanolamine, along with more oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species and more ceramides. Electron microscopy showed enlarged, heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. Purification and proteomics identified these structures as degenerating mitochondria. The mice showed autophagy and cognitive dysfunction. The authors concluded that iPLA2-gamma has an obligatory role in neuronal mitochondrial lipid metabolism and membrane structure, and that its loss of function causes a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that the phenotype and mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities of their patient closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice have mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance, as well as dysmorphic mitochondria in the myocardium and cerebellum. The authors suggested that this resemblance supported a causal link between the PNPLA8 variants and the patient's disease. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 334AATT [c.334_337delaatt; p.N112H]

In a 7-year-old girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT) predicted to result in p.Asn112HisfsX29; the other was c.1975_1976delAG, a 2-bp deletion. The mutations were found by trio exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Each was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither variant. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. Western blot analysis of patient muscle showed a marked decrease in the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 protein bands.

**.0002 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 1975AG [c.1975_1976delag; p.L659A]

In a 7-year-old girl with mitochondrial myopathy and lactic acidosis, Saunders et al. (2015) identified a 2-bp deletion in the PNPLA8 gene (c.1975_1976delAG) predicted to result in p.Leu659AlafsX4. The mutation was in compound heterozygous state with a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT). The variants were found by trio exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and each was inherited from a carrier parent. Two healthy sibs carried neither variant, and neither variant was found in the Exome Sequencing Project database or in an internal database of 1,913 samples. Patient muscle showed markedly decreased PNPLA8 protein on Western blot.

**.0003 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, GLU173TER [c.517G>T; p.E173*]

In an Indian girl (patient 1), born to consanguineous parents, with congenital microcephaly, neonatal-onset seizures, spasticity, and an elevated lipid-lactate peak on MR spectroscopy, Shukla et al. (2018) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.517G-T transversion (NM_001256007.1) in the PNPLA8 gene, resulting in a glu173-to-ter (E173X) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents were heterozygous for the mutation.

**.0004 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 2275CT [c.2275_2276del; c.2275_2276delct; p.L759A]

In a girl of Indian/Fijian origin (patient 2) with neurodegeneration beginning at about 13 months of age, Shukla et al. (2018) used trio whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT) predicted to result in p.Leu759AlafsTer4, and the other was a c.419C-A transversion resulting in a ser140-to-ter (S140X) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed both mutations, and each was inherited from a carrier parent. ⚠︎

In 2 Lithuanian sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified the c.2275_2276del (p.Leu759AlafsTer4) mutation in compound heterozygous state with a 1-bp deletion (c.2269del; p.Thr757GlnfsTer10) in exon 11 of PNPLA8. The c.2275_2276del mutation was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but sequencing showed that the variants were in trans.

Nakamura et al. (2024) noted that patients with a relatively mild neurodegenerative phenotype carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants, including p.Leu759Alafs*4, and that intermediate cases also carried the recurrent p.Leu759Alafs*4. Fibroblasts from their patient 12 (mild phenotype, p.Leu759Alafs*4) showed weak residual expression of the 77-kDa PNPLA8 protein.

**.0009 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, VAL520ASP [c.1559T>A; p.V520D]

In 2 patients (patients 2 and 3, family II) with infantile-onset disease, Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) used exome sequencing to identify a c.1559T-A transversion in the PNPLA8 gene, resulting in a val520-to-asp (V520D) substitution.
