### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis (MMLA) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (612123) on chromosome 7q31. ⚠︎

### Description

Mitochondrial myopathy with lactic acidosis caused by PNPLA8 mutation is an autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder. Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) noted that 3 presentations of biallelic PNPLA8 variants had been described: a congenital form with congenital microcephaly, early-onset epileptic encephalopathy and early death; childhood neurodegeneration with progressive microcephaly; and adult-onset cerebellar ataxia with peripheral neuropathy. Nakamura et al. (2024) concluded that PNPLA8-related neurologic disease forms a continuum, ranging from developmental and/or degenerative epileptic-dyskinetic encephalopathy with congenital microcephaly to childhood-onset neurodegeneration.

### Clinical Features

Hackett et al. (1973) reported a metabolic myopathy associated with chronic lactic acidemia, growth failure, and nerve deafness.

Mastaglia et al. (1980) described the clinical, biochemical and pathologic findings in a 23-year-old man with a mitochondrial myopathy. He had had a high-output circulatory state and cardiomyopathy that caused cardiac insufficiency 22 years before the skeletal myopathy presented. Venous lactate levels were elevated, and at one stage of the illness he developed severe lactic acidosis. Electron microscopy of muscle showed various mitochondrial abnormalities and an accumulation of neutral lipids, which was confirmed by histochemistry and by thin-layer chromatography.

Saunders et al. (2015) reported a girl (CMH193) whose toe-walking was noted in her first year. She had a positive Gowers sign at age 2 years and could no longer walk by age 3 years. She was last reported at age 7 years. Her features included progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia, complex partial seizures, poor weight gain, a lean body habitus, dystonia, dysarthria, dysmetria, spasticity, and tight heel cords. Laboratory studies showed lactic acidosis, an elevated anion gap, elevated blood pyruvate, and a deficiency of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate in the CSF. Brain MRI showed increased signal in the medial globus pallidus. On muscle biopsy, fiber size varied and some fibers were atrophic. There were no ragged-red fibers. Electron microscopy showed abnormal mitochondrial cristae, osmiophilic inclusions, and subsarcolemmal aggregates of abnormal mitochondria. Creatine phosphokinase and respiratory chain function were not abnormal.

Shukla et al. (2018) described 2 unrelated girls with biallelic PNPLA8 loss-of-function variants. Their features varied but were similar, and included microcephaly, severe global developmental delay, spasticity, lactic acidosis, and progressive cerebellar atrophy. The authors considered the condition a progressive neurodegenerative mitochondrial disorder. Patient 1 was the second child of consanguineous Indian parents. She had a low birth weight and was evaluated in the neonatal intensive care unit on her sixth day of life for congenital microcephaly and seizures. She had multifocal clonic neonatal seizures, spasticity of all 4 limbs, and overriding cranial sutures. Brain MRI showed microcephaly with a simplified gyral pattern, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, a prominent cisterna magna with a hypoplastic cerebellum, and brainstem atrophy. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak.

Patient 2 of Shukla et al. (2018) was a girl of Indian/Fijian ancestry born to nonconsanguineous parents. She developed normally until 13 months of age and then regressed. She was last reported at age 15 years. She had epilepsia partialis continua and focal seizures, severe global developmental delay, no speech, and required gastrostomy tube feeding. Other findings included central hypotonia, acquired microcephaly, choreiform movements, dystonia, proximal muscle weakness, absent muscle stretch reflexes, spasticity, and joint contractures. EEG showed multifocal epileptiform discharges with background slowing, and visual evoked potentials were abnormal. Brain MRI showed delayed myelination, progressive cerebellar atrophy, brainstem and cerebral atrophy, and a thin corpus callosum. MR spectroscopy showed an elevated lipid-lactate peak. Electron microscopy of muscle showed enlarged, irregular mitochondria, but respiratory chain complex I to IV activities were normal. Echocardiography detected hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with subaortic stenosis. Blood lactate was elevated on several occasions, and GDF15 was also elevated. She also had obstructive sleep apnea and restrictive lung disease.

Masih et al. (2021) reported a patient who had prenatal-onset severe, progressive neurodegeneration and died in infancy.

Burnyte et al. (2023) described a milder phenotype in 2 Lithuanian adult sibs. Both had adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, which advanced markedly in the teens but progressed only slightly in adulthood. Cognition was preserved, and there was no visual, auditory, cardiac, or liver dysfunction. Sibling A, a man last reported at age 49 years, first developed lower limb weakness and distal muscle atrophy at age 14 years. His later findings included axial ataxia, tetraparesis, decreased muscle tone, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, foot deformities, bilateral Babinski signs, dysmetria, length-dependent hypoesthesia in the lower limbs, and a sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. He became nonambulant at age 44 years. At age 45 years he had mild dysarthria and horizontal nystagmus. Imaging showed mild cerebellar atrophy. Sibling B, his sister, was last reported at age 21 years. She developed difficulty walking and weakness of both lower limbs at age 14 years. Her findings included distal muscle atrophy of the upper and lower limbs, absent lower limb tendon reflexes, bilateral Babinski signs, an axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, severe axial ataxia, and appendicular dysmetria that was more pronounced in the lower limbs. She was unable to maintain an upright posture. Her cranial nerve examination and cognitive evaluation were normal, and EMG showed no myopathic changes. ⚠︎

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) reported patients from 2 families. Patient 1, from family I, had the congenital form of the disease. Patients 2 and 3, from family II, had infantile-onset disease with progressive microcephaly and seizures. Their imaging showed progressive cortical atrophy, white matter loss, bilateral degeneration of the basal ganglia, and cystic encephalomalacia.

Nakamura et al. (2024) reported 14 new patients with biallelic PNPLA8 variants and reviewed a total of 25 affected individuals from 20 families. Among the 14 new patients, central hypotonia was present in 13 of 13, dystonia in 8 of 12, seizures in 10 of 14, and cerebellar atrophy in 12 of 12. Eight patients (families 1 through 8) were severely affected. They came from Japan (patients 1 and 2, nonconsanguineous) and from Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Turkey, and Lebanon (patients 3 through 8, all consanguineous). Families 7 and 8 had been reported previously. These patients had congenital microcephaly (5 of 7), progressive microcephaly, no developmental milestones, severe intellectual disability, and spasticity. They had early-onset seizures, beginning at 1 day of age in patients 1, 2, 3, and 8, at 2 days in patient 6, at 1 month in patient 7, at 3 months in patient 5, and at 7 months in patient 4. Imaging showed a simplified gyral pattern, pontocerebellar hypoplasia, and progressive cerebral atrophy. Patient 1 died at age 4 years, patient 2 at 10 months, and patient 8 at 8 months. Two patients (families 9 and 10) had disease of intermediate severity, with cerebellar atrophy that was very mild in patient 10. Four patients (families 11 and 12) had relatively mild features. All 4 attained walking and later regressed, 3 of 4 had ataxia, 3 of 4 had mild or no intellectual disability, and all 4 had a single seizure or none. These mild patients included 3 Iranian sibs from a consanguineous family (patients 11a, 11b, and 11c), who had hypotonia, dystonia, ataxia, and a neuromuscular disorder. Patient 11b had seizures from age 31 years. The fourth was an Egyptian woman born to consanguineous parents (patient 12), last reported at age 21 years, who had developmental delay with regression after walking, mild intellectual disability, hypotonia, dystonia, a neuromuscular disorder, and cerebellar atrophy.

### Biochemical Features

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that cardiolipin and monolysocardiolipin levels appeared lower in patient skin fibroblasts than in control fibroblasts, with no significant correlation with the severity of the phenotype. Fibroblasts from 1 severely affected patient (patient 1) had reduced maximal mitochondrial respiration but normal mitochondrial morphology.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MMLA in the family reported by Saunders et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Their patient was compound heterozygous for PNPLA8 mutations, each inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither variant. Shukla et al. (2018) also found that the variants in their patients were inherited from carrier parents, consistent with an autosomal recessive pattern.

### Heterogeneity

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified homozygous N-terminal frameshift PNPLA8 variants, which affected only the 88-kD isoform, in 2 patients from unrelated Iranian families in a non-neurologic cohort. One patient had optic atrophy and the other had nonsyndromic hearing loss. In the optic atrophy family, an affected sib of the proband was heterozygous for the variant. The authors therefore considered the significance of these variants undetermined.

### Molecular Genetics

In a girl with MMLA, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.334_337delAATT (V1) and c.1975_1976delAG (V2). Segregation analysis showed that each mutation was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither mutation. Western blot analysis of the patient's muscle showed a dramatic decrease of the 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 (iPLA2-gamma) bands compared with control muscle, consistent with a null genotype. Neither variant was present in the Exome Sequencing Project or in an internal database of 1,913 samples.

In an Indian girl born to consanguineous parents, who had congenital microcephaly and neonatal seizures, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the PNPLA8 gene (E173X; V3). Both parents were heterozygous. In an unrelated girl with regression from 13 months of age, they found compound heterozygous PNPLA8 mutations, a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT; V4) and a nonsense mutation (S140X; V5), each inherited from a carrier parent. Shukla et al. (2018) noted that all PNPLA8 variants reported to that date introduced premature stop codons.

In a patient with prenatal-onset severe progressive neurodegeneration who died in infancy, Masih et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing and identified a novel homozygous missense variant in the patatin domain of PNPLA8 (c.1874A-G; V6). ⚠︎

In 2 Lithuanian sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. (2023) identified compound heterozygous frameshift mutations in the PNPLA8 gene: c.2269del (Thr757GlnfsTer10) and the previously described c.2275_2276del (Leu759AlafsTer4; V4). Both mutations lay in the last coding exon. The c.2275_2276del mutation was inherited from the mother. Paternal DNA was not available, but whole-exome and Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 changes were on different alleles (in trans). Sanger sequencing confirmed both variants in the affected sister. ⚠︎

Using exome sequencing, Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) identified a loss-of-function PNPLA8 variant (R411X; V7) in a patient with the congenital form of the disease (family I). In 2 patients with infantile-onset disease (family II) they identified a missense variant (V520D; V8).

Nakamura et al. (2024) identified biallelic PNPLA8 variants in 14 patients. All 14 variants had frequencies below 0.001% in population databases. In lymphoblastoid cells from patient 1, they showed that a c.1625+3_1625+6del variant (V9) caused skipping of exons 6 to 8, and patient 1's skin fibroblasts had markedly reduced levels of the 77-kD PNPLA8 protein, indicating a null effect. They could not test the splicing effects of the c.2075-2A-G (V10) and c.1684-2A-G (V12) variants, found in patients 2 and 5. The frameshift variant Glu296LysfsTer6 (V13), found in patient 6, a severely affected patient, also had a null effect. In contrast, fibroblasts from patient 12, who had a mild phenotype and was homozygous for Leu759AlafsTer4, showed weak expression of the 77-kD protein, suggesting residual protein function. A homozygous missense variant from a previously reported severely affected patient (V520D) gave normal protein levels.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Shukla et al. (2018) concluded that too few patients were known to draw a genotype-phenotype correlation.

Burnyte et al. (2023) noted that both truncating PNPLA8 variants in their sibs lay in the last coding exon. They suggested that such transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay and possibly retain partial enzyme activity. This would give a less severe phenotype than the severe early-childhood neurodegeneration reported when c.2275_2276del occurs in trans with another truncating variant.

Abdel-Hamid et al. (2024) proposed infantile onset as a new and distinct phenotype of PNPLA8-related disease. They suggested that the site of a variant, rather than its type, was strongly correlated with age at onset, and that the PNPLA8-related conditions form a spectrum of overlapping features that line up with different ages of onset.

Nakamura et al. (2024) found that biallelic loss-of-function variants affecting 3 or 4 PNPLA8 protein isoforms were associated with the severe end of the spectrum. All patients with the mild, late-onset movement disorder phenotype carried biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (Thr757GlnfsTer10 or Leu759AlafsTer4). These variants were thought to escape nonsense-mediated decay and leave residual protein.

### Pathogenesis

Mastaglia et al. (1980) did not define the metabolic defect in their patient. They proposed that an abnormality in the Krebs cycle caused both the overproduction of lactate and the accumulation of lipid in muscle. ⚠︎

Saunders et al. (2015) found normal SDH and COX activity and normal electron transport chain function in the patient's muscle. They suggested that altered homeostasis of the inner mitochondrial membrane, rather than a primary respiratory chain defect, underlies PNPLA8-related disease.

Nakamura et al. (2024) studied cerebral organoids made from PNPLA8-knockout and patient-derived iPSCs. The organoids had a smaller subventricular zone, fewer PAX6+/HOPX+ basal radial glial cells, and fewer SATB2+ upper-layer neurons. Deep-layer neurons and basal intermediate progenitors were unchanged, and the authors suggested that S phase was altered in apical radial glia. PNPLA8-knockout neural progenitor cells had reduced lysophosphatidic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine, and phosphatidic acid, but normal mitochondrial function and cardiolipin. Adding lysophosphatidic acid partly restored the number of basal radial glial cells in patient-derived organoids. Spatial transcriptomics of the ventricular zone in patient-derived organoids showed enrichment of gene sets for the cell cycle, neuronal differentiation, and lysophospholipid pathways, and depletion of basal radial glia gene sets. The authors suggested that loss of PNPLA8 alters the fate specification of apical radial glia.

### Clinical Management

Mastaglia et al. (1980) observed a marked improvement in their patient's myopathy after treatment with prednisone and thiamine was started. The improvement persisted after these drugs were withdrawn.

### Population Genetics

Saunders et al. (2015) reported that neither PNPLA8 variant in their patient was present in the Exome Sequencing Project or in an internal database of 1,913 samples, and that no homozygous truncating PNPLA8 variants were present in these sources. Nakamura et al. (2024) found that all 14 PNPLA8 variants identified in their patients had population database frequencies below 0.001%.

### Animal Model

Mancuso et al. (2009) found profound alterations in hippocampal phospholipid metabolism in iPLA2-gamma-null mice. Hippocampal cardiolipin was markedly elevated and shifted toward shorter-chain molecular species. Plasmenylethanolamine content was decreased, while oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species and ceramides were increased. The hippocampus of the null mice contained enlarged heteromorphic lamellar structures undergoing degeneration, together with ubiquitin-positive spheroid inclusion bodies. Purification and proteomic analysis identified these structures as degenerating mitochondria. The authors concluded that loss of iPLA2-gamma causes a mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder with autophagy and cognitive dysfunction.

Saunders et al. (2015) noted that their patient's clinical features and mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities closely resembled those of Pnpla8-null mice. These mice have mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired learning, decreased exercise endurance, a thin body habitus, and cold intolerance.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 4-BP DEL, 334AATT [c.334_337delaatt; p.N112H]

In a girl with MMLA who lost ambulation by age 3 years, Saunders et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT) that caused a frameshift and premature termination (Asn112HisfsTer29). The other was a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG), also causing a frameshift (Leu659AlafsTer4). Each mutation was inherited from a carrier parent, and 2 healthy sibs carried neither. The 88-, 63-, and 52-kD PNPLA8 bands were dramatically decreased in the patient's muscle, consistent with a null genotype. The variant was absent from the Exome Sequencing Project and from an internal database of 1,913 samples.

**.0002 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 1975AG [c.1975_1976delag; p.L659A]

In a girl with MMLA, Saunders et al. (2015) identified a 2-bp deletion (c.1975_1976delAG) in the PNPLA8 gene, which caused a frameshift and premature termination (Leu659AlafsTer4). It occurred in compound heterozygosity with a 4-bp deletion (c.334_337delAATT), and the 2 mutations were inherited from carrier parents. PNPLA8 protein was dramatically reduced in the patient's muscle. The variant was absent from the Exome Sequencing Project and from an internal database of 1,913 samples.

**.0003 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, GLU173TER [c.517G>T; p.E173*]

In an Indian girl born to consanguineous parents, who had congenital microcephaly, neonatal seizures, a simplified gyral pattern, cerebellar hypoplasia, and an elevated lipid-lactate peak on MR spectroscopy, Shukla et al. (2018) identified a homozygous c.517G-T transversion in the PNPLA8 gene (NM_001256007.1), resulting in a glu173-to-ter (E173X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation.

**.0004 MITOCHONDRIAL MYOPATHY WITH LACTIC ACIDOSIS**  
PNPLA8, 2-BP DEL, 2275CT [c.2269del; c.2275_2276del; c.2275_2276delct; p.L759A; p.T757Q]

In a girl of Indian/Fijian ancestry who had regression from 13 months of age, epilepsia partialis continua, progressive cerebellar atrophy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and elevated blood lactate, Shukla et al. (2018) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PNPLA8 gene. One was a 2-bp deletion (c.2275_2276delCT) that caused a frameshift and premature termination (Leu759AlafsTer4). The other was a c.419C-A transversion causing a ser140-to-ter (S140X) substitution. The mutations were inherited from carrier parents.

In 2 Lithuanian sibs with adolescent-onset cerebellar ataxia and sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, Burnyte et al. Both variants lie in the last coding exon, and the authors suggested that these transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated decay. ⚠︎

Nakamura et al. (2024) found biallelic C-terminal frameshift variants (Thr757GlnfsTer10 or Leu759AlafsTer4) in all 4 patients with a mild, late-onset phenotype. These included 3 Iranian sibs born to consanguineous parents and an Egyptian woman, born to consanguineous parents, who was homozygous for Leu759AlafsTer4 and had a mild phenotype. Fibroblasts from the Egyptian patient showed weak expression of the 77-kD PNPLA8 protein, suggesting residual protein function.
