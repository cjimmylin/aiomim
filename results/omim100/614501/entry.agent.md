### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures is an autosomal recessive disorder reported in the Old Order Amish. Its cardinal features are hypotonia, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, and a characteristic craniofacial appearance with skull abnormalities. Variable additional features include congenital heart defects, upper airway abnormalities, feeding difficulties, and brain abnormalities on imaging (Puffenberger et al., 2012; Ammous et al., 2021).

### Nomenclature

Puffenberger et al. (2012) referred to the disorder as 'symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia.' Ammous et al. (2021) used the designation 'SNIP1-related disorder.'

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described a disorder they termed symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia in patients from the Plain (Old Order Amish and Mennonite) population of Pennsylvania; exome sequencing was performed in 3 affected individuals from 2 sibships. Affected neonates were hypotonic and fed poorly. Dysmorphic features that evolved over time included a bulbous nose, wide mouth and tongue, broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. Affected children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak; many retained use of their hands to scoot, maneuver a wheelchair, or gesture. Examination typically revealed a subdued child with strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. MRI showed ventriculomegaly, thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities, and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular skull contour. Multifocal spike-wave discharges typically began by 6 months of age and were accompanied by focal or generalized seizures manifesting as dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, or generalized tonic-clonic events; multiple intractable seizure types could afflict an individual patient. Physical anomalies found in some patients included subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, and hydrocele. ⚠︎

Ammous et al. (2021) evaluated 35 of 51 identified affected individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families (19 males and 16 females, aged 1 month to 26 years), including 2 individuals previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). Six children died between ages 9 months and 11 years as a consequence of infection, sudden cardiopulmonary arrest, or accidental drowning. Substantial phenotypic variation was observed, including within sibships. All had severe, nonverbal global developmental delay, hypotonia, hyporeflexia, seizures, feeding difficulties, abnormal skull shape, high-arched palate, and wide mouth with cupid's bow upper lip. Craniofacial features included midface hypoplasia, wide mouth with downturned corners and thin cupid's bow upper lip, large tongue, microretrognathia (Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate in 3 patients), malocclusion, small upturned bulbous nose, long palpebral fissures, and proptosis in those with craniosynostosis. Skull abnormalities ranged from normocephaly to severe multisuture craniosynostosis with cloverleaf skull in 5 individuals, and included an irregular skull surface with variable calvarial thickness and bony dysplasia. Other dysmorphic features included brachydactyly, broad thumbs, single palmar crease, congenital talipes equinovarus, pes planus, toenail hypoplasia, and pectus excavatum. ⚠︎

In the cohort of Ammous et al. (2021), all affected individuals developed epilepsy, with focal and generalized intractable seizures (myoclonic, absence, tonic-clonic) of infantile or childhood onset. EEG was abnormal in all individuals assessed, without a characteristic pattern. In some infants, seizures initially presented as apnea or breath-holding spells, and subclinical seizures were detected in 6 individuals. Seizures could be triggered or exacerbated by febrile illness, sleep deprivation, and hypoglycemic episodes, and no antiepileptic medication consistently provided effective seizure control. Some individuals had severe developmental regression after status epilepticus, 2 with subsequent permanent hemiparesis. Behavioral diagnoses included autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit disorder. Most achieved independent ambulation (age range 3 to 10 years), and some communicated with signs, gestures, and sounds. Neuroimaging in 18 individuals was abnormal in all, with findings including hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, hypomyelination, irregular cortical ribbon, Chiari malformation, absence of the septum pellucidum, hypoplastic optic nerves, and septo-optic dysplasia.

Additional features in the cohort of Ammous et al. (2021) included congenital cardiac defects in 60% (including hypoplastic left heart syndrome, aortic stenosis and bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation, aortic root dilatation, ASD, VSD, PDA, pulmonary artery stenosis, and mitral valve regurgitation), cardiomyopathy (left ventricular noncompaction) in 12%, and neonatal upper airway difficulties including laryngomalacia, pharyngomalacia, and subglottic stenosis, causing weak cry, stridor, and apnea and occasionally requiring tracheostomy. Poor feeding in the neonatal period was universal, and many required a gastrostomy tube. Gastrointestinal features included gastroesophageal reflux, paraesophageal hernia, intestinal malrotation, hepatomegaly, and umbilical and inguinal hernias. Intrauterine growth retardation and failure to thrive were common. Endocrine abnormalities including hypoglycemia, hypothyroidism, hypothermia, and dyshormonogenesis occurred in a small proportion. Other findings were spinal abnormalities (sacral dimple, tethered cord, scoliosis), strabismus, horizontal nystagmus, myopia, chronic otitis media, and hearing loss; 1 individual had a cerebral arteriovenous malformation.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NEDHCS in the families reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012) and Ammous et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Ammous et al. (2021) found that all parents of affected individuals were heterozygous for the SNIP1 mutation.

### Mapping

By autozygosity mapping with SNP microarrays, Puffenberger et al. (2012) mapped the disorder to a 6.6-Mb region on chromosome 1 containing 126 genes, using 6 samples.

In 4 affected individuals from 2 nuclear families, Ammous et al. (2021) identified a single region of homozygosity greater than 1 Mb common to all 4, a 1.65-Mb region on chromosome 1p34.3 between SNP markers rs6667450 and rs10889902 that contained 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 affected individuals from 2 sibships with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia, Puffenberger et al. (2012) identified homozygosity for a c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. The mutation, which was found by exome sequencing within the mapped interval, was the only novel variant among 34 homozygous variants in the region and was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It was found in 5 carriers among 203 Old Order Amish controls. Glu366 is highly conserved and lies in the C terminus, where SNIP1 interacts with c-Myc, Smad1, and Smad2. When transiently overexpressed in mIMCD3 cells, the corresponding mouse mutant (E353G) localized to the nucleus with a more aggregated appearance than wildtype and was unstable on Western blot, with band density 84.9% lower than wildtype, although transcript levels were comparable to wildtype.

By whole-exome sequencing of 2 affected Old Order Amish individuals, Ammous et al. (2021) identified only 1 potentially pathogenic variant in the 1p34.3 critical interval, the E366G mutation in exon 4 of the SNIP1 gene previously identified by Puffenberger et al. (2012). Targeted analysis confirmed homozygosity for the mutation in 35 individuals; all parents were heterozygous and all unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. Whole-transcriptome sequencing in blood from 6 affected individuals from 4 sibships identified 75 significantly upregulated and 109 significantly downregulated genes compared with sex-matched controls; the most overrepresented pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) recommended elective gastrostomy tube placement to support growth and limit pulmonary aspiration, careful optimization of anticonvulsant medications to treat apnea in infancy, maintain seizure control, and prevent status epilepticus, early EEG in infants with suspected apneic episodes, neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis, monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism, and yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments.

### Population Genetics

Ammous et al. (2021) found that the SNIP1 E366G founder mutation was present in an in-house Amish control database of more than 5,000 individuals at an allele frequency of 0.5% in the Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in the Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish, with no unaffected homozygotes. In gnomAD (v3.1), the variant had an allele frequency of 0.001%, with 11 heterozygotes (10 Amish) and no homozygotes.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER WITH HYPOTONIA, CRANIOFACIAL ABNORMALITIES, AND SEIZURES**  
SNIP1, GLU366GLY [c.1097A>G; p.Glu366Gly; NM_024700.4:c.1097A>G]

In 3 affected individuals from 2 sibships with neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS), Puffenberger et al. (2012) identified homozygosity for a c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution at a highly conserved residue. The mutation was identified by exome sequencing and was found in 5 carriers among 203 Old Order Amish controls. The corresponding mouse mutant protein had a more aggregated nuclear distribution and was unstable when overexpressed in mIMCD3 cells.

Ammous et al. (2021) found homozygosity for the E366G mutation in 35 Old Order Amish individuals with NEDHCS; the mutation segregated with the disorder, with all parents heterozygous. It was present at an allele frequency of 0.5% to 1.4% in Amish controls, and in gnomAD at an allele frequency of 0.001% with no homozygotes.
