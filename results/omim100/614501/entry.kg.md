### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene (608241) on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is a complex autosomal recessive disorder described in the Amish, where it results from a founder variant in SNIP1. Its cardinal features include hypotonia, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, and a characteristic craniofacial appearance with skull abnormalities (Ammous et al., 2021).

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described Old Order Amish children with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Affected neonates were hypotonic and fed poorly. Over time they developed dysmorphic features: bulbous nose, wide mouth and tongue, broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. The children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak; they did not engage socially or follow verbal commands. On examination, affected children typically had strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. Brain MRI showed ventriculomegaly, a thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities (hypomyelination), and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular skull contour. Multifocal spike-wave discharges from central, occipital, and temporal regions typically began by 6 months of age and were accompanied by intractable focal or generalized seizures, including dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, and generalized tonic-clonic events. Some patients had subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, or hydrocele.

Ammous et al. (2021) evaluated 35 of 51 identified individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families (19 males and 16 females, aged 1 month to 26 years) with molecularly confirmed SNIP1-related disorder, including 2 previously reported individuals. All had severe nonverbal global developmental delay, hypotonia, hyporeflexia, seizures, feeding difficulties, abnormal skull shape, high-arched palate, and wide mouth with cupid's bow upper lip. Other features included behavioral problems (75%), upper airway abnormalities (75%), congenital heart defects (60%), cardiomyopathy (12%), nystagmus/strabismus (45%), small for gestational age (54%), hypothyroidism (25%), and hypoglycemia (21%). Six children died between 9 months and 11 years of age, and substantial phenotypic variation was observed, including within sibships.

Craniofacial features reported by Ammous et al. (2021) included midface hypoplasia, wide mouth with downturned corners and thin cupid's bow upper lip, large tongue, high-arched palate, microretrognathia (Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate in 3), malocclusion, small upturned bulbous nose, long palpebral fissures, and proptosis. Skull abnormalities ranged from normocephaly to severe multisuture craniosynostosis with cloverleaf skull in 5 individuals, and included an irregular skull surface with variable calvarial thickness and bony dysplasia. Other features were brachydactyly, broad thumbs, single palmar crease, clubfoot, pes planus, toenail hypoplasia, and pectus excavatum.

All affected individuals in the series of Ammous et al. (2021) developed epilepsy, with focal and generalized intractable seizures (myoclonic, absence, tonic-clonic) of infantile or childhood onset, abnormal EEG without a characteristic pattern, seizures presenting as apnea in some infants, and drug-resistant epilepsy. Some had developmental regression after status epilepticus, 2 with permanent hemiparesis. In contrast to the children described by Puffenberger et al. (2012), who did not learn to walk, most individuals in this series achieved independent ambulation (at 3 to 10 years). Behavioral diagnoses included autism and attention deficit disorder. All 18 individuals with neuroimaging had abnormalities, including hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, hypomyelination, Chiari malformation, absent septum pellucidum, and septo-optic dysplasia. ⚠︎

Ammous et al. (2021) reported congenital cardiac defects in 60% of individuals, including hypoplastic left heart, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, aortic root dilatation, atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, and pulmonary artery stenosis, and left ventricular noncompaction cardiomyopathy in 12%. Neonatal upper airway difficulties (laryngomalacia, pharyngomalacia, subglottic stenosis) sometimes required tracheostomy. Poor neonatal feeding was universal, with many requiring gastrostomy. Other features included intestinal malrotation, intrauterine growth restriction, hypoglycemia, hypothyroidism, strabismus, nystagmus, myopia, and hearing loss.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NEDHCS in the Amish families reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012) and Ammous et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Ammous et al. (2021) found that all parents of affected individuals were heterozygous for the SNIP1 mutation, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype.

### Mapping

By autozygosity mapping with 10K SNP arrays in 6 affected Old Order Amish individuals, Puffenberger et al. (2012) mapped symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia to a 6.6-Mb shared homozygous region on chromosome 1 between rs2153337 and rs2225251. The region contained 126 genes.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed genome-wide SNP homozygosity mapping in 4 affected individuals from 2 Amish nuclear families and identified a single shared 1.65-Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 1p34.3 between rs6667450 and rs10889902, containing 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 Old Order Amish individuals from 2 sibships with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia, Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing. Of 34 homozygous variants in the mapped region, the only novel one was a c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution, which PolyPhen-2 predicted to be probably damaging. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and checked for segregation. Screening of 203 Old Order Amish controls found 5 carriers (2.5%) and no homozygotes. Glu366 is highly conserved and lies in the C-terminal region where SNIP1 interacts with c-Myc, Smad1, and Smad2. In transfected mIMCD3 cells, the equivalent mouse variant, Snip1 glu353-to-gly (c.1058A-G), was expressed at transcript levels comparable to wildtype; wildtype Snip1 localized to the nucleus in a punctate pattern, whereas the mutant protein appeared more aggregated. On Western blotting, the mutant protein was unstable, with band density 84.9% lower than wildtype.

In Amish individuals with NEDHCS, Ammous et al. (2021) identified by whole-exome sequencing the homozygous c.1097A-G (E366G) mutation in exon 4 of the SNIP1 gene, previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012), within the 1p34.3 interval. Homozygosity was confirmed in 35 individuals; all parents were heterozygous, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype. The residue is highly conserved and lies near the C-terminal FHA domain. The variant was found in gnomAD at a frequency of 0.001% (11 heterozygotes, 10 of them Amish, and no homozygotes) and was classified as pathogenic.

### Pathogenesis

Puffenberger et al. (2012) proposed that reduced SNIP1 abundance probably lowers c-Myc activity and raises TGF-beta and NF-kappa-B signaling. They noted that disruption of c-Myc or CBP/p300 signaling in mice can cause abnormal development of the brain, skull, craniofacial bones, and distal limbs, and that increased TGF-beta/Smad2 or NF-kappa-B signaling in mouse brain can each initiate epileptogenesis.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed whole-blood transcriptome sequencing in 6 affected individuals from 4 sibships compared with sex-matched controls and identified 75 significantly upregulated and 109 downregulated genes, many associated with neurologic disease (e.g., ROBO1, the most upregulated gene, as well as CNTNAP2 and SYT1). Reactome analysis showed TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition as the most overrepresented pathway. The authors suggested that the variant disrupts the regulation of multiple signaling pathways, and noted that because Snip1-null mice are embryonic lethal, the variant may be unlikely to cause complete loss of function.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) recommended elective gastrostomy tube placement, careful optimization of anticonvulsants to treat infantile apnea and prevent status epilepticus, early EEG in infants with apneic episodes, neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis, monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism, early supportive therapies, and yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments. No antiepileptic medication consistently provided effective seizure control.

### Population Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) found 5 carriers (2.5%) of the SNIP1 E366G mutation and no homozygotes among 203 Old Order Amish controls.

Ammous et al. (2021) found the SNIP1 c.1097A-G (E366G) founder mutation at an allele frequency of 0.5% in Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish in a control database of more than 5,000 individuals, with no unaffected homozygotes. In total, 51 Old Order Amish individuals from 27 sibships were identified with the disorder, 49 of whom were confirmed homozygous; 12 were deceased (age at death 1 week to 11 years).
