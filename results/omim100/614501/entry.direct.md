### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SNIP1 gene on chromosome 1p34.3.

### Description

Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, craniofacial abnormalities, and seizures (NEDHCS) is an autosomal recessive disorder. It has been described in Old Order Amish people. Its cardinal features are hypotonia, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, seizures, and a characteristic face with skull abnormalities. Seizures begin in infancy or childhood and are often intractable. Some patients also have congenital heart defects, upper airway abnormalities, feeding difficulties, and other congenital malformations (Puffenberger et al., 2012; Ammous et al., 2021).

### Nomenclature

Puffenberger et al. (2012) called this disorder 'symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia.' Ammous et al. (2021) called it 'SNIP1-related disorder' and noted the designation 'psychomotor retardation, epilepsy, and craniofacial dysmorphism' (PMRED).

### Clinical Features

Puffenberger et al. (2012) described Old Order Amish children with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. Affected newborns were hypotonic and fed poorly. Dysmorphic features developed over time and included a bulbous nose, wide mouth and tongue, broad jaw with protuberant angles, short hands, short tapered fingers, and broad thumbs. Affected children had severe psychomotor delay and did not learn to walk or speak. Many could still use their hands to scoot, maneuver a wheelchair, or gesture. Some children responded behaviorally to language, but they did not engage socially or follow verbal commands. On examination, children were typically subdued and had strabismus, slow horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, and weak or absent tendon reflexes. MRI showed ventriculomegaly, a thin corpus callosum, white matter abnormalities (including hypomyelination), and an undulating or 'lumpy' skull surface, with the cortical ribbon following the irregular skull contour. Multifocal spike-wave discharges from central, occipital, and temporal regions typically began by 6 months of age. They were accompanied by focal or generalized seizures, which could appear as dystonic posturing, drop attacks, myoclonic jerks, or generalized tonic-clonic events. A single patient could have multiple types of intractable seizures. Some patients had subglottic stenosis, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, umbilical hernia, or hydrocele.

Ammous et al. (2021) evaluated 35 of 51 identified affected individuals from 21 interrelated Old Order Amish families. There were 19 males and 16 females, aged 1 month to 26 years, and the group included 2 patients previously reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012). Six children died between 9 months and 11 years of age from infection, sudden cardiopulmonary arrest, or accidental drowning. The phenotype varied substantially, even within sibships. All patients had severe global developmental delay and were nonverbal. All had hypotonia, hyporeflexia, seizures, feeding difficulties, abnormal skull shape, high-arched palate, and a wide mouth with a cupid's bow upper lip. Craniofacial features included midface hypoplasia, a wide mouth with downturned corners and thin cupid's bow upper lip, large tongue, microretrognathia, malocclusion, a small upturned bulbous nose, long palpebral fissures, and proptosis (in patients with craniosynostosis). Three patients had Pierre Robin sequence with or without cleft palate. Skull morphology ranged from normocephaly to severe multisuture craniosynostosis, with a cloverleaf skull in 5 patients. Skull abnormalities also included an irregular surface with variable calvarial thickness and bony dysplasia. Other dysmorphic features included brachydactyly, broad thumbs, single palmar crease, clubfoot, pes planus, toenail hypoplasia, and pectus excavatum. Seizures began in infancy or childhood and were focal or generalized (myoclonic, absence, or tonic-clonic) and intractable. EEG was abnormal in all patients tested, without a characteristic pattern. In some infants, seizures first appeared as apnea or breath-holding spells. Seizures could be triggered by febrile illness, sleep deprivation, or hypoglycemia. Some patients had severe developmental regression after status epilepticus, and 2 later had permanent hemiparesis. Most patients learned to walk independently between 3 and 10 years of age. Behavioral problems, including irritability, autistic features, and attention deficit disorder, were present in 75%. All 18 patients with neuroimaging had abnormalities, including hydrocephalus, ventriculomegaly, white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, hypomyelination, irregular cortical ribbon, Chiari malformation, absent septum pellucidum, hypoplastic optic nerves, and septo-optic dysplasia. Congenital heart defects were present in 60%. These included hypoplastic left heart syndrome, aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation, aortic root dilatation, ASD, VSD, PDA, pulmonary artery stenosis, and mitral valve regurgitation. Twelve percent developed left ventricular noncompaction cardiomyopathy. Upper airway abnormalities, including laryngomalacia, pharyngomalacia, and subglottic stenosis, caused weak cry, stridor, and apnea and sometimes required tracheostomy. Other features included intrauterine growth retardation, failure to thrive, gastroesophageal reflux, intestinal malrotation, hernias, and spinal abnormalities (sacral dimple, tethered cord, scoliosis). A small proportion of patients had hypoglycemia, hypothyroidism, or hypothermia. Other findings were strabismus, nystagmus, myopia, chronic otitis media, and hearing loss. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of NEDHCS in the Old Order Amish families reported by Puffenberger et al. (2012) and Ammous et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the families studied by Ammous et al. (2021), all parents were heterozygous for the SNIP1 mutation, and unaffected sibs were heterozygous or wildtype.

### Mapping

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed autozygosity mapping in 6 Old Order Amish patients with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia using SNP microarrays. They mapped the disorder to a 6.6-Mb shared homozygous region on chromosome 1 that contained 126 genes.

Ammous et al. (2021) performed a genomewide SNP screen in 4 affected individuals from 2 Amish nuclear families. They identified a single shared homozygous region larger than 1 Mb: a 1.65-Mb region on chromosome 1p34.3, between markers rs6667450 and rs10889902, that contained 17 genes.

### Molecular Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) performed exome sequencing in 3 affected Old Order Amish individuals from 2 sibships with symptomatic epilepsy and skull dysplasia. In the mapped region on chromosome 1, they identified only one novel homozygous variant: a c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and segregation was checked in available family members. The equivalent mouse mutant protein (E353G) was transiently overexpressed in mIMCD3 cells. It localized to the nucleus but appeared more aggregated than wildtype, and Western blotting showed it to be unstable, with band density 84.9% lower than wildtype.

In 35 Amish individuals with NEDHCS, Ammous et al. (2021) confirmed homozygosity for the E366G mutation in SNIP1. The mutation was first identified in the mapped interval on chromosome 1p34.3 by exome sequencing of 2 patients, confirmed by Sanger sequencing, and then tested in additional patients by targeted analysis. It segregated with the disorder. No unaffected individuals in an Amish control database of more than 5,000 individuals were homozygous. Transcriptome sequencing of blood from 6 affected individuals showed differential expression of 184 genes. Reactome pathway analysis found that the most overrepresented pathway was TGF-beta receptor signaling in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition.

### Clinical Management

Ammous et al. (2021) proposed management guidelines. These included careful optimization of anticonvulsant medications to treat apnea in infancy, maintain seizure control, and prevent status epilepticus; early EEG in infants with suspected apneic episodes; neuroimaging and echocardiography at diagnosis; and elective gastrostomy tube placement to support growth and limit pulmonary aspiration. They also recommended early occupational, speech, and physical therapy; monitoring for hypoglycemia and hypothyroidism; and yearly ophthalmology and audiology assessments.

### Population Genetics

Puffenberger et al. (2012) found that the SNIP1 E366G mutation had a population-specific allele frequency of 1.48% (6 of 406 chromosomes) in Old Order Amish controls. No controls were homozygous.

Ammous et al. (2021) found that the SNIP1 E366G mutation had an allele frequency of 0.5% in the Pennsylvania Amish and 1.4% in the Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin Amish. In gnomAD (v3.1), the mutation had an allele frequency of 0.001%, with 11 heterozygotes (10 of them Amish) and no homozygotes. Ammous et al. (2021) identified a total of 51 affected Old Order Amish individuals in 27 sibships.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

SNIP1, GLU366GLY [NM_024700.4:c.1097A>G; p.Glu366Gly]

In 3 Old Order Amish patients from 2 sibships with NEDHCS, Puffenberger et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.1097A-G transition in the SNIP1 gene, resulting in a glu366-to-gly (E366G) substitution at a highly conserved residue. The mutation was found by exome sequencing within a mapped region of homozygosity and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Its allele frequency in Old Order Amish controls was 1.48%, and no controls were homozygous. In mIMCD3 cells, the corresponding mouse mutant protein (E353G) appeared more aggregated in the nucleus than wildtype and was unstable.

Ammous et al. (2021) confirmed homozygosity for the E366G mutation in 35 Old Order Amish individuals with NEDHCS. The mutation segregated with the disorder, and all parents were heterozygous. In the Amish, its allele frequency ranged from 0.5% (Pennsylvania) to 1.4% (Ohio/Indiana/Wisconsin), and no unaffected individuals were homozygous.
