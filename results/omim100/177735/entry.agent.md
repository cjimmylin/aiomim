### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal dominant pseudohypoaldosteronism type I (PHA1A) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding the mineralocorticoid receptor (MLR, MR; NR3C2).

### Description

Pseudohypoaldosteronism type I (PHA1) is characterized by neonatal renal salt wasting with dehydration, hypotension, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis despite elevated aldosterone levels (Geller et al., 1998). Two forms exist: an autosomal recessive form with severe disease persisting into adulthood, caused by mutations in genes encoding subunits of the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC), and an autosomal dominant or sporadic form, which is milder and remits with age (Geller et al., 1998). The autosomal dominant form is restricted to the kidney (renal PHA1), whereas the recessive form involves multiple mineralocorticoid target organs such as sweat and salivary glands, colonic epithelium, and lung (Hanukoglu, 1991; Tajima et al., 2000).

### Nomenclature

Donnell et al. (1959) used the term 'pseudohypo-adrenalocorticism' for renal sodium loss, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia due to renal tubular insensitivity to mineralocorticoids, and Raine and Roy (1962) used the term 'pseudo-hypoadrenocorticalism' for a salt-losing syndrome in infancy.

### Clinical Features

Cheek and Perry (1958) reported a salt-wasting syndrome in infancy.

Hanukoglu (1991) studied 2 kindreds with a total of 9 patients with type I PHA. In kindred I, the propositus presented in infancy with renal salt wasting (vomiting, failure to thrive, short stature, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia) and responded dramatically to a high-salt diet (2.5 g/day); sodium supplementation was discontinued at age 2 years. In 7 additional family members from 3 generations, clinical expression varied from asymptomatic to moderate. In affected members (propositus, mother, and 2 brothers), hyperaldosteronism persisted over 13 years, whereas plasma renin activity decreased gradually to near-normal values, which Hanukoglu (1991) interpreted as the development of tertiary hyperaldosteronism due to autonomously functioning zona glomerulosa. By contrast, the propositus of kindred II, the product of a consanguineous marriage, had severe renal salt losing from age 9 days with increased salivary and sweat electrolytes, recurrent life-threatening salt wasting beyond age 2 years, and still required high salt supplementation (14 g/day) at age 5 years; this pedigree was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Hanukoglu (1991) concluded that type I PHA includes 2 clinically and genetically distinct entities, with either renal or multiple target organ defects.

Riepe et al. (2006) performed clinical and hormonal characterization of 6 PHA1 patients with renal salt loss.

### Biochemical Features

Armanini et al. (1985) studied 3 patients with pseudohypoaldosteronism, a 28-year-old index case in Melbourne and 2 sibs in Munich aged 8 and 2 years, the younger of whom was less severely affected than his sister. Whereas control monocytes bound [3H]aldosterone with high affinity (Kd approximately 3 nM) and limited capacity (150 to 600 sites per cell), no binding was found in monocytes from the index case and the older sib, and the younger sib had 62 sites per cell. Levels in the parents of the Munich sibs, who were first cousins, were normal. Armanini et al. (1985) concluded that pseudohypoaldosteronism is caused by a complete or partial type I (mineralocorticoid) receptor defect and that transmission may be autosomal recessive.

### Inheritance

Hanukoglu (1991) found that the pedigree of a kindred with renal PHA1 was consistent with autosomal dominant transmission with variable expression. Geller et al. (1998) identified heterozygous mineralocorticoid receptor mutations segregating with PHA1 in 4 dominant kindreds, and in 1 sporadic kindred a de novo mutation. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Viemann et al. (2001) found no mutation in the mineralocorticoid receptor gene in 2 kindreds with autosomal dominant PHA1 and concluded that autosomal dominant or sporadic PHA1 is genetically heterogeneous, involving other, as yet unidentified, genes. Sartorato et al. (2003) found mineralocorticoid receptor mutations in 70% of their familial cases. Pujo et al. (2007) noted that a genocopy may exist in 3 PHA1 kindreds without MR mutations, and suggested that other patients without MR mutations might have different diseases resembling PHA1 in the neonatal period.

### Molecular Genetics

Among 6 dominant and 7 sporadic PHA1 kindreds, Geller et al. (1998) found no ENaC gene mutations. Screening the mineralocorticoid receptor gene (MLR), they identified heterozygous mutations in 1 sporadic and 4 dominant kindreds: 2 frameshift mutations (1 de novo), 2 premature termination codons, and 1 splice donor mutation. The mutations segregated with PHA1 and were not found in unaffected subjects.

In all affected members of a Japanese family with renal PHA1, Tajima et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous missense mutation in the MR gene, leu924-to-pro (L924P), by PCR and direct sequencing. In transfected COS-1 cells, aldosterone increased reporter activity with wildtype receptor but had no effect with the mutant receptor.

In a sporadic case of PHA1, Viemann et al. (2001) identified a frameshift mutation (2871insC) in exon 9 of the MR gene that was not present in asymptomatic family members.

In 14 families with autosomal dominant or sporadic PHA1, Sartorato et al. (2003) detected 6 heterozygous MR mutations: 2 frameshift mutations in exon 2 (insT1354 and del8bp537), a nonsense mutation in exon 4 (C2157A, cys645-to-ter), and 3 missense mutations (G633R, Q776R, and L979P). The DNA-binding domain mutant G633R showed reduced maximal transactivation, whereas the ligand-binding domain mutants Q776R and L979P had reduced or absent aldosterone binding, respectively, with reduced or absent ligand-dependent transactivation. L979P had a transdominant-negative effect on wildtype receptor activity, whereas G633R and Q776R were thought to result in haploinsufficiency.

Riepe et al. (2006) detected 6 heterozygous NR3C2 mutations in 6 PHA1 patients: a previously reported frameshift (c.1131dupT), the frameshift c.2871dupC, 2 novel nonsense mutations, and 2 missense mutations, S818L and the novel E972G. The c.2871dupC mutant showed no aldosterone binding and no transactivation; S818L did not bind aldosterone, activate transcription, or translocate into the nucleus; and E972G showed significantly lower ligand-binding affinity and only 9% of wildtype transcriptional activity.

Pujo et al. (2007) screened the MR gene for nucleotide substitutions in coding regions, intron-exon junctions, and untranslated exons, as well as for large deletions, and found a total of 22 MR gene abnormalities in 33 patients with PHA1. They concluded that MR mutations are extremely frequent in PHA1 patients classified according to aldosterone and potassium levels.

Riepe et al. (2006) found that the c.2871dupC mutant receptor showed no aldosterone binding and no transactivation because of a major change in receptor conformation.
