### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb anomalies, and urogenital malformations (BILU), also known as Hoffman syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOP2B gene (126431) on chromosome 3p24.

### Description

BILU syndrome, also called Hoffman syndrome, is an autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency. Affected individuals have agammaglobulinemia or hypogammaglobulinemia with absent or reduced B cells, together with facial dysmorphism, distal limb anomalies, and urogenital malformations. T-cell and myeloid lineages are normal. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Hügle et al. (2011) proposed the name Hoffman syndrome for the condition in their mother-daughter pair and in the sporadic girl reported by Hoffman et al. (2001). Broderick et al. (2019) also used the term Hoffman syndrome. Papapietro et al. (2020) concluded that BILU syndrome and Hoffman syndrome are manifestations of the same disease, TOP2B deficiency. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hoffman et al. (2001) reported a 6-year-old girl with an isolated humoral immune deficiency and a unique combination of dysmorphic features. Physical findings included microcephaly, micrognathia, sickle-shaped eyebrows, hypoplastic alae nasi, thenar hypoplasia, partial syndactyly of toes 4 and 5, recessed great toes, anterior anus, and hypoplastic labia minora. She also had triphalangeal thumbs, hypoplastic first metatarsals, postnatal growth retardation, and developmental delay; her development was substantially slower than that of her twin. She had recurrent sinopulmonary infections and pneumococcal bacteremia. Laboratory evaluation showed hypogammaglobulinemia, absent B cells, and a 46,XX karyotype. A review of the literature and the London Dysmorphology Database found no recognizable syndrome that matched her findings, and the authors suggested that she might represent a unique syndrome of unknown etiology. ⚠︎

Edery et al. (2001) described a 3-generation family with B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb abnormalities, and urogenital malformations.

Tischkowitz et al. (2004) reported a second family with features similar to those of a previously described family that had an unusual combination of B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb abnormalities, genitourinary malformations, and mild dysmorphic features. They gave a further description of the BILU phenotype in females.

Hügle et al. (2011) reported a 7-year-old girl with almost complete loss of B cells, facial dysmorphism, and malformations of the limbs and genitalia. Her mother had similar dysmorphic features and an attenuated form of the B-cell deficiency. The authors concluded that the mother-daughter pair and the sporadic girl reported by Hoffman et al. (2001) all had the same condition, Hoffman syndrome, and they reviewed the features of these 3 affected individuals.

Broderick et al. (2019) studied 3 families with Hoffman syndrome. Two of them were unrelated families with autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency that the authors had reported previously. Ages at presentation were 7 and 35 years in family 1 and 6 years in family 2. The probands presented with recurrent infections by polysaccharide-encapsulated bacteria, severe hypogammaglobulinemia, and absent CD19+ B cells, and they had facial dysmorphism and limb anomalies. T-cell responses to mitogens were normal. The patient in the third family presented at 5 years of age. This patient had initially been reported as having an atypical presentation of ablepharon-macrostomia syndrome and was later found to have humoral immunodeficiency. The patients had normal numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells but no detectable peripheral B cells, and NK cells were reduced in 2 of them. The patients did not form germinal centers and did not generate protective serologic titers to immunization (tetanus, diphtheria, H. influenzae B). However, detectable amounts of circulating immunoglobulins indicated that some B cells were present below the clinical limits of detection. Malignancy was not observed in the patients.

Papapietro et al. (2020) studied the 2 unrelated families, from Cyprus (family A) and France (family B), in which BILU syndrome had previously been described. The patients had absent or reduced B cells with normal T and myeloid cells. A bone marrow aspirate from patient II:4 of family A showed a complete absence of CD19+ cells, including pro-B, pre-B, immature, and mature B cells, with normal T, NK, and myeloid cell lineages.

### Inheritance

Edery et al. (2001) suggested that the disorder in their 3-generation family might be a novel autosomal dominant syndrome. Tischkowitz et al. (2004) reported a second family that also showed autosomal dominant inheritance, and they suggested that the association was a true syndrome. Hügle et al. (2011) described an affected mother and daughter.

In family A of Papapietro et al. (2020), the TOP2B mutation arose de novo in patient II:4 and was transmitted to her affected daughter, which is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. In 1 of the families studied by Broderick et al. (2019), the TOP2B mutation also arose de novo.

### Molecular Genetics

Tischkowitz et al. (2004) studied affected individuals from 2 families with BILU syndrome. Sequence analysis found no mutation in the candidate gene HOXA13, and there was no evidence of a microdeletion involving HOXA13 or the HOXA cluster as a whole.

Broderick et al. (2019) performed whole-genome sequencing of affected and unaffected members of 2 unrelated families with Hoffman syndrome. In each family they found a heterozygous variant in the TOP2B gene that was present only in affected members: a de novo in-frame deletion (c.1761_1763delAGA, p.EE587E) and a missense mutation (c.1448C-T, S483L). Independent reanalysis of clinical whole-exome sequencing data from the patient in a third family revealed a heterozygous c.1897G-A transition in TOP2B, resulting in a gly633-to-ser (G633S) substitution. All 3 mutations affected the TOPRIM (DNA gating) domain and were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The authors noted that TOP2B has a probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) of 1, which suggested disease through haploinsufficiency or partial genetic dominance. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, chimeric or yeast TOP2 constructs carrying the S483L, EE587E, or G633S mutation failed to complement the temperature-sensitive top2-4 allele and did not support recovery of top2-delta haploid spore clones, indicating loss of topoisomerase II function. Haploid strains heterozygous for the mutations grew more slowly, and their nuclear fractions decatenated kinetoplast DNA less effectively than wildtype, indicating a partial dominant-negative effect. ⚠︎

Broderick et al. (2019) noted a brief clinical report of a patient with progressive microcephaly, developmental delay, and hypotonia who carried a TOP2B variant outside the TOPRIM domain (H58Y). They found no similarities to their own patients, and it was unclear whether that variant was causative.

In BILU family A from Cyprus, Papapietro et al. (2020) identified a heterozygous c.1453G-C transversion in TOP2B, resulting in an ala485-to-pro (A485P) substitution. It was the only mutation in the coding part of the genome that was absent from the healthy parents, and it arose de novo in the affected woman (II:4), who transmitted it to her affected daughter (III:1). Sanger sequencing showed the same mutation in the patients of family B from France. TOP2B protein levels were reduced in patient T cells, fibroblasts, and iPSCs. In HEK-293 cells, the mutant protein produced a degradation product, which suggested that it was less stable, but it still interacted with TOP2A. Recombinant TOP2B A485P had more than 10-fold reduced DNA relaxation and decatenation activity, and coexpression with wildtype TOP2B lowered decatenation activity. The authors interpreted this as a dominant-negative effect on wildtype TOP2B and TOP2A rather than haploinsufficiency.

Erdős et al. (2021) reported an inherited TOP2B mutation and suggested that it might confirm mutational hotspots in the TOPRIM domain of TOP2B.

### Pathogenesis

Broderick et al. (2019) found that Top2b mRNA and protein were expressed more highly in wildtype murine B cells than in T cells. They proposed that the B cell-restricted phenotype reflects a preferential reduction in transcription of long genes when topoisomerase function is lost. They noted that murine B-cell transcription factor genes (EBF 390 kb, Pax5 178 kb, Foxo1 85 kb) are notably longer than T-cell transcription factor genes (Tcf1 30 kb, Notch1 45 kb) or shared ones (Rag1 11 kb, E2A 24 kb). In published ChIP-seq data and etoposide-induced END-seq breaks from murine primary B cells, Top2b, but not Top2a, was present at the promoter and intron 5 enhancer regions of Pax5. This suggested that defective Top2b activity could impair germline transcriptional activity and Igh locus accessibility, with negative effects on V(D)J recombination. Because a bone marrow biopsy had not been clinically indicated in their patients, the stage at which B-cell development fails in humans remained open. However, their murine data suggested a reduced bone marrow B-cell compartment in which the surviving cells had a reduced ability to respond to antigen or general stimuli.

Papapietro et al. (2020) found a complete absence of CD19+ cells in the bone marrow of a patient, indicating a block in B-cell development after the common lymphoid progenitor stage. They noted that the block is leaky, because some patients have immunoglobulins and small numbers of B cells.

### Population Genetics

Papapietro et al. (2020) noted that the A485P mutation was absent from more than 90,000 subjects in gnomAD. In contrast, 42 unaffected gnomAD subjects carried heterozygous loss-of-function TOP2B variants, which argued against haploinsufficiency as the disease mechanism.

### Animal Model

Broderick et al. (2019) found that Top2b -/- mice did not survive the perinatal period. B cell-specific deletion of Top2b in mice (Top2b -/- mb1-cre and Top2b -/- cd19-cre) reduced peripheral CD19+ B cells and altered splenic follicle architecture. It also reduced PrePro B, Pre B, and immature B cells in bone marrow and B cells at every splenic developmental stage, with reduced kappa-deleting recombination excision circles and reduced Pax5 expression but unchanged Rag1 expression.

Broderick et al. (2019) also generated knockin mice carrying the murine equivalent of the patient EE587E mutation. Homozygotes were not viable. Top2b +/EE587E heterozygotes had a reduced spleen-to-body ratio, reduced total serum IgG, impaired antigen-specific antibody responses to ovalbumin and Pneumovax-23, and reduced B cells at most developmental stages. Their B cells showed reduced Pax5 and Foxo1 expression and increased DNA strand breaks. Peripheral B cells were reduced even compared with Top2b +/- mice, whereas T cells were unaffected. After LPS stimulation with or without IL-4, B cells from heterozygous knockin mice secreted less total IgG, IgG3, and IgG2b, and more than 90% failed to advance beyond the first division. Isolated splenic T cells produced normal amounts of IL-2 in response to PMA and ionomycin and proliferated normally, consistent with the normal T-cell mitogen responses seen in the patients.
