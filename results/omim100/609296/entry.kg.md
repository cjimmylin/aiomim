### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb anomalies, and urogenital malformations (BILU), also known as Hoffman syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TOP2B gene (126431) on chromosome 3p24.

### Description

B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb anomalies, and urogenital malformations (BILU), also called Hoffman syndrome, is an autosomal dominant syndromic humoral immunodeficiency. Hügle et al. (2011) stated that Hoffman syndrome was originally described as a sporadic case of humoral immunodeficiency, facial dysmorphism, and limb anomalies in a young girl. Broderick et al. (2019) found that the disorder is caused by autosomal dominant mutations in TOP2B, which encodes a type II topoisomerase that relieves topological stress during DNA replication and gene transcription. They described it as the first defined monogenic syndrome caused by mutation in TOP2B, an essential gene.

### Nomenclature

Broderick et al. (2019) noted that the syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency with facial dysmorphism and limb anomalies previously described in 2 unrelated families with autosomal dominant inheritance had later been named Hoffman syndrome. Its genetic cause was unknown until their study.

Papapietro et al. (2020) noted that other dominant TOP2B mutations in the TOPRIM domain cause Hoffman syndrome (B-cell deficiency, limb abnormalities, and facial dysmorphism). They concluded that BILU syndrome and Hoffman syndrome are manifestations of the same disease, TOP2B deficiency.

### Clinical Features

Hoffman et al. (2001) reported a 6-year-old girl, one of a pair of twins, who had isolated humoral immunodeficiency and a unique combination of dysmorphic features. These included microcephaly, micrognathia, sickle-shaped eyebrows, hypoplastic alae nasi, thenar hypoplasia, partial 4-5 toe syndactyly, recessed great toes, anterior anus, and hypoplastic labia minora. Radiographs showed triphalangeal thumbs and hypoplastic first metatarsals. She had postnatal growth retardation, and her development was substantially slower than her twin's. She had recurrent sinopulmonary infections and pneumococcal bacteremia. Laboratory evaluation showed hypogammaglobulinemia and absent B cells. The authors searched the literature and the London Dysmorphology Database and found no recognizable syndrome matching this combination of features. They suggested that she might represent a unique syndrome of unknown etiology.

Edery et al. (2001) described a 3-generation family with B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb abnormalities, and urogenital malformations.

Tischkowitz et al. (2004) reported a second family with B-cell immunodeficiency, distal limb abnormalities, genitourinary malformations, and mild dysmorphic features, similar to the previously described family. They further delineated the phenotype in females and concluded that this was a true syndromic association.

Hügle et al. (2011) reported a 7-year-old girl with almost complete loss of B cells, facial dysmorphism, and malformations of the limbs and genitalia. Her mother had similar dysmorphic features and a milder form of the B-cell deficiency. The authors believed that these 2 patients had the same condition as the girl originally reported by Hoffman et al. (2001), and they reviewed the features of the 3 affected individuals.

Broderick et al. (2019) restudied 2 previously reported unrelated families with autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency. The proband in each family had recurrent infections with polysaccharide-encapsulated bacteria, severe hypogammaglobulinemia, and absent CD19+ B cells, but normal T-cell responses to mitogens. A third patient had initially been reported with an atypical presentation of ablepharon-macrostomia syndrome and was later found to have humoral immunodeficiency. The authors tabulated immunologic values at diagnosis in 4 patients from 3 families, with ages at presentation of 7 years, 35 years, 6 years, and 5 years. CD19+ B cells were absent or markedly reduced (0, 48, 0, and 0 cells/microliter), total IgG was low (232, 631, 73, and 242 mg/dL), and titers to tetanus, diphtheria, and H. influenzae B were deficient or undetectable. CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T-cell counts and mitogen stimulation were normal. Two patients had reduced numbers of CD16+ NK cells. The patients did not form germinal centers and did not generate protective serologic titers after immunization. Because circulating immunoglobulins were detectable, the authors inferred that some B cells were present but below the clinical limits of detection. No malignancy was observed in the patients.

Papapietro et al. (2020) studied 2 previously described unrelated families with autosomal dominant BILU syndrome, one from Cyprus (family A) and one from France (family B). The patients had absent or reduced B cells but normal T and myeloid cells. Multicolor flow cytometry of a bone marrow aspirate from patient II:4 of family A showed complete absence of CD19+ cells, including pro-B, pre-B, immature, and mature B cells, while the T, NK, and myeloid lineages were normal. This indicated a block at the earliest stages of B-cell development, after the common lymphoid progenitor stage. The authors described the block as leaky, because immunoglobulins and small numbers of B cells were found in the peripheral blood of several patients.

### Inheritance

Edery et al. (2001) suggested that the disorder in their 3-generation family might be a novel autosomal dominant syndrome. Tischkowitz et al. (2004) observed autosomal dominant inheritance in the second reported family, as in the first.

Broderick et al. (2019) found that inheritance was autosomal dominant in 3 families with Hoffman syndrome, in which they identified heterozygous TOP2B mutations. In their family 2 the mutation had arisen de novo. In the BILU family A studied by Papapietro et al. (2020), the TOP2B mutation arose de novo in the affected mother and was transmitted to her affected daughter.

### Cytogenetics

Hoffman et al. (2001) found a normal 46,XX karyotype in their patient.

### Molecular Genetics

Tischkowitz et al. (2004) studied affected individuals from both reported BILU families. Sequence analysis found no mutation in the candidate gene HOXA13, and there was no evidence of a microdeletion involving HOXA13 or the HOXA cluster as a whole.

Broderick et al. (2019) performed whole-genome sequencing of affected and unaffected members of 2 unrelated families with autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency and filtered for novel, dominantly inherited coding variants. TOP2B was the only gene with nonsynonymous changes present only in affected members of both families. In family 1 they found a heterozygous missense mutation (S483L). In family 2 they found a de novo in-frame deletion (c.1761_1763delAGA, EE587E). In a third patient (family 3), first reported as having atypical ablepharon-macrostomia syndrome and later found to have humoral immunodeficiency, independent reanalysis of clinical whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous TOP2B mutation (G633S). This variant was absent from the 1000 Genomes Project, ESP6500SI, ExAC v0.3, and the authors' in-house exome database. All mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. All 3 affected highly conserved amino acids in the TOPRIM (DNA gating) domain. TOP2B had a probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) of 1. Constructs carrying any of the 3 mutations failed to complement topoisomerase II function in yeast. Yeast studies also indicated that these were partially dominant loss-of-function mutations.

Broderick et al. (2019) noted a brief clinical report of a patient with progressive microcephaly, developmental delay, and hypotonia who carried a TOP2B variant outside the TOPRIM domain (H58Y). They found no similarities to their own patients and stated that it was unclear whether that variant was causative.

Papapietro et al. (2020) performed exome sequencing of 4 members of BILU family A (from Cyprus) and identified a heterozygous mutation in the TOP2B gene (A485P). The mutation had arisen de novo in affected individual II:4 and was transmitted to her affected daughter. Sanger sequencing found the same mutation in the patients of family B (from France). The mutation was absent from more than 90,000 subjects in gnomAD. TOP2B protein was reduced in patient T cells, fibroblasts, and iPSCs, and the mutant protein had markedly reduced catalytic activity.

Erdős et al. (2021) reported an inherited TOP2B mutation and suggested that it might confirm mutational hotspots in the TOPRIM domain of TOP2B.

### Pathogenesis

Broderick et al. (2019) tested the patient mutations in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Chimeric yeast-human TOP2B constructs or yeast TOP2 vectors carrying S483L, EE587E, or G633S failed to complement the temperature-sensitive top2-4 allele at the nonpermissive temperature. They also did not allow recovery of top2-delta haploid spore clones. These results indicated that each mutation disrupts topoisomerase II function. Yeast strains carrying 1 wildtype and 1 mutant copy of TOP2 (EE587E or S483L) grew more slowly than wildtype controls. Nuclear fractions from these strains decatenated kinetoplast DNA less effectively than fractions containing a single wildtype copy. The authors concluded that the disease-associated TOP2 variants have a partial dominant-negative effect.

Broderick et al. (2019) showed that wildtype mouse B cells express more Top2b mRNA and protein than T cells, consistent with previous reports in humans and mice. They proposed that the B cell-specific vulnerability reflects a preferential reduction in transcription of long genes when topoisomerases are absent. They noted that murine B-cell transcription factor genes (EBF, 390 kb; Pax5, 178 kb; Foxo1, 85 kb) are longer than T-cell transcription factor genes (Tcf1, 30 kb; Notch1, 45 kb) or shared ones (Rag1, 11 kb; E2A, 24 kb). In published ChIP-seq and END-seq data from murine primary B cells, Top2b, but not Top2a, was present at the Pax5 promoter and the intron 5 enhancer.

Papapietro et al. (2020) found that expressing TOP2B A485P in wildtype HEK293 cells lowered decatenation activity compared with wildtype alone, as did coexpressing it with wildtype TOP2B in knockout cells. They concluded that the mutant exerts a dominant-negative effect on wildtype TOP2B and TOP2A rather than causing haploinsufficiency. They noted that gnomAD contains 42 unaffected individuals with heterozygous loss-of-function TOP2B variants.

### Animal Model

Because Top2b-null mice do not survive the perinatal period, Broderick et al. (2019) bred Top2b-flox2 mice to cd19-cre and mb1-cre lines. Compared with wildtype littermates, B cell-specific Top2b-deficient mice had fewer peripheral blood CD19+ B cells and altered splenic follicle structure, with reduced marginal zone and follicular B-cell zones. They also had fewer PrePro B, Pre B, and immature B cells in bone marrow, fewer splenic B cells at every developmental stage, and fewer kappa-deleting recombination excision circles (KRECs). Pax5 expression was significantly reduced, whereas Rag1 expression was unchanged. Top2b-/- mb1-cre mice failed to produce ovalbumin-specific IgG after immunization. Their anti-Pneumovax-23 IgG3 response was impaired and their IgG2b response significantly reduced, whereas PPV23-specific IgM and B-1 cells were unaffected. In vitro, LPS-stimulated Top2b-deficient B cells failed to proliferate, showed increased cell death by LDH release, and upregulated MHC II less robustly. The authors considered these findings consistent with the patients' failure to respond to vaccines.

Broderick et al. (2019) generated a constitutive knockin mouse carrying the murine equivalent of the patient EE587E mutation. Like Top2b-/- mice, Top2b EE587E/EE587E homozygotes were not viable. Top2b +/EE587E heterozygotes, which mirror the patients' genotype, had a reduced spleen-to-body ratio, reduced total serum IgG, and significantly lower antigen-specific antibody titers after ovalbumin or Pneumovax-23 immunization. They had fewer B cells at most stages of B-cell development in bone marrow and spleen. Pax5 and Foxo1 expression was reduced, whereas Rag1, E2A, Notch1, and Tcf1 expression was unchanged or normal. Heterozygous knockin mice had fewer peripheral blood B cells than Top2b +/- mice, with no effect on peripheral blood T cells. They also had reduced KRECs with unaffected TRECs. The authors interpreted these results as supporting a dominant-negative rather than a purely haploinsufficient mechanism. In comet assays, splenic B cells from the knockin mice showed increased DNA strand breaks, whereas T cells were unaffected. When stimulated in vitro with LPS, with or without IL-4, knockin B cells secreted less total IgG, IgG3, and IgG2b than littermate controls. They were also fewer in number and released more LDH, and more than 90% failed to advance beyond the first division after 5 days. In contrast, splenic T cells produced normal IL-2 in response to PMA and ionomycin and proliferated normally, consistent with the normal T-cell mitogen responses in patients. No malignancy was observed in the mice.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 B-CELL IMMUNODEFICIENCY, DISTAL LIMB ANOMALIES, AND UROGENITAL MALFORMATIONS**  
TOP2B, SER483LEU [c.1448C>T; p.S483L]

In affected members of family 1 with autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency (Hoffman syndrome), Broderick et al. (2019) identified a heterozygous c.1448C-T transition in the TOP2B gene, resulting in a ser483-to-leu (S483L) substitution in the TOPRIM (DNA gating) domain. They found the mutation by whole-genome sequencing and confirmed it by Sanger sequencing. It was present only in affected family members. In yeast, constructs carrying S483L failed to complement the temperature-sensitive top2-4 allele and did not allow recovery of top2-delta spore clones. Strains carrying 1 wildtype and 1 S483L copy grew more slowly and had reduced decatenation activity, indicating a partial dominant-negative effect.

**.0002 B-CELL IMMUNODEFICIENCY, DISTAL LIMB ANOMALIES, AND UROGENITAL MALFORMATIONS**  
TOP2B, 3-BP DEL, NT1761 [c.1761_1763delaga]

In the affected member of family 2 with autosomal dominant syndromic B-cell immunodeficiency (Hoffman syndrome), Broderick et al. (2019) identified a de novo heterozygous in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.1761_1763delAGA) in the TOP2B gene, designated EE587E, affecting the TOPRIM (DNA gating) domain. They found the mutation by whole-genome sequencing and confirmed it by Sanger sequencing. In yeast, the EE587E mutation failed to complement topoisomerase II function. Strains heterozygous for the mutation grew more slowly and had reduced decatenation activity, indicating a partial dominant-negative effect. Knockin mice heterozygous for the murine equivalent had reduced B cells, reduced serum IgG, and impaired antigen-specific antibody responses, with B-cell deficits greater than those in Top2b +/- mice. Homozygous knockin mice were not viable.

**.0003 B-CELL IMMUNODEFICIENCY, DISTAL LIMB ANOMALIES, AND UROGENITAL MALFORMATIONS**  
TOP2B, GLY633SER [c.1897G>A; p.G633S]

Broderick et al. (2019) studied a patient (family 3) with humoral immunodeficiency who had initially been reported as having atypical ablepharon-macrostomia syndrome. Reanalysis of clinical whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous c.1897G-A transition in the TOP2B gene, resulting in a gly633-to-ser (G633S) substitution in the TOPRIM domain. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It was absent from the 1000 Genomes Project, ESP6500SI, ExAC v0.3, and the authors' in-house exome database. In yeast, constructs carrying G633S failed to complement the temperature-sensitive top2-4 allele and did not allow recovery of top2-delta spore clones.

**.0004 B-CELL IMMUNODEFICIENCY, DISTAL LIMB ANOMALIES, AND UROGENITAL MALFORMATIONS**  
TOP2B, ALA485PRO [c.1453G>C; p.A485P]

In affected members of 2 unrelated families with BILU syndrome, one from Cyprus (family A) and one from France (family B), Papapietro et al. (2020) identified a heterozygous c.1453G-C transversion in the TOP2B gene, resulting in an ala485-to-pro (A485P) substitution. In family A, exome sequencing of 4 family members showed that the mutation arose de novo in affected individual II:4 and was transmitted to her affected daughter. Sanger sequencing identified the same mutation in the patients of family B. The mutation was absent from more than 90,000 subjects in gnomAD. It affected an alanine that is conserved in eukaryotic and prokaryotic type II topoisomerases and was predicted to destabilize an alpha helix in the catalytic TOPRIM domain. TOP2B protein was reduced in patient T cells, fibroblasts, and iPSCs. In TOP2B-knockout HEK293 cells, the mutant protein showed a degradation product but still interacted with TOP2A. Purified recombinant A485P protein had more than 10-fold reduced DNA relaxation and decatenation activity. Lysates of patient T-cell blasts also had reduced decatenation activity. Expressing the mutant in wildtype cells, or coexpressing it with wildtype TOP2B, lowered decatenation activity, indicating a dominant-negative effect.
