### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that vitelliform macular dystrophy-4 (VMD4) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the IMPG1 gene (602870) on chromosome 6q14.1.

### Description

Vitelliform macular dystrophies (VMD) are inherited retinal dystrophies with yellow, round deposits seen on fundus examination. These deposits occur in juvenile Best macular dystrophy and in adult-onset vitelliform macular dystrophy. Many cases have mutations in BEST1 or PRPH2, but the genetic cause is unknown in many affected individuals (Manes et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Manes et al. (2013) reported that most patients with IMPG1-related VMD had a normal or moderately decreased electrooculogram (EOG) Arden ratio.

Meunier et al. (2014) found that 13 patients from 4 families with IMPG1 or IMPG2 mutations had late-onset vitelliform macular dystrophy (mean age at onset 42 years) with moderate visual impairment (mean visual acuity 20/40); the sporadic presumed recessive case in family 3 had onset at 38 years and visual acuity 20/50. Drusen-like lesions adjacent to the vitelliform deposits were seen in 9 of 13 patients. On spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT), the vitelliform material lay above a well-preserved retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) of normal reflectivity at all stages, located between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines. EOG results were normal or borderline in 9 cases.

Brandl et al. (2017) reported a German boy with a heterozygous IMPG1 mutation who was initially diagnosed at age 12 years. At age 13, he had reduced visual acuity (0.2 and 0.1 logMAR), with central RPE abnormalities and foveal detachment in both fundi.

Brandl et al. (2017) also reported a Turkish boy, homozygous for an IMPG1 mutation, who was diagnosed with VMD at age 2 years and had fluctuating visual acuity of about 0.2 logMAR over 8 years of follow-up. At age 10, multifocal ERG was normal and the EOG Arden ratio was borderline to slightly low. Fundi showed a bilateral singular central vitelliform lesion without satellite drusen. SD-OCT showed a dome-shaped foveal detachment of the neurosensory retina with hyperreflective material that was reabsorbed, leaving an optically empty cavity, elongated photoreceptor outer segments, and preserved Bruch membrane/RPE; fundus autofluorescence hyperfluorescence decreased over time.

Brandl et al. (2017) described strikingly similar phenotypes in 10 patients with IMPG1 or IMPG2 mutations, with presumably consecutive stages: singular central vitelliform lesions with neurosensory detachment and hyperreflective material above a preserved Bruch membrane/RPE; resorption leaving a dome-shaped optically empty cavity; and collapse with central atrophy and RPE loss, causing the most pronounced vision loss. All patients had bilateral singular deposits without drusen-like lesions at the posterior pole.

Gupta et al. (2021) described a 25-year-old woman with symptomatic scotoma and decreased vision who had small yellow spots at the fovea in both eyes. Optical coherence tomography showed disorganized foveal ellipsoid and interdigitation zones with reduced reflectance, worse in the left eye than the right. Fundus autofluorescence showed little, dye angiography showed nothing abnormal, and multifocal electroretinogram showed slightly reduced sensitivity in the central retina of the left eye.

### Inheritance

Manes et al. (2013) concluded that IMPG1 mutations cause both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive VMD. Meunier et al. (2014) observed autosomal dominant transmission of IMPG1-related vitelliform macular dystrophy in families 1 and 2, whereas family 3 was a sporadic case with presumed recessive transmission.

### Mapping

Manes et al. (2013) performed gene mapping in a large family with autosomal dominant VMD, which together with whole-exome sequencing led to the identification of an IMPG1 mutation.

### Heterogeneity

Meunier et al. (2014) identified a c.3230G-T (cys1077-to-phe; C1077F) mutation in the IMPG2 gene in a fourth family with autosomal dominant vitelliform macular dystrophy (2 patients), indicating that vitelliform macular dystrophy can also be caused by IMPG2 mutation.

Brandl et al. (2017) found heterozygous IMPG2 mutations in 8 BEST1/PRPH2-negative VMD patients, including 3 stop (E226X, S522X, Q856X) and 5 missense (A243P, G1008D, F1016S, Y1042C, C1077F) mutations, the last previously associated with VMD. These patients had later onset (mean age at diagnosis 53.1 years) than the IMPG1 patients, and the authors noted that clinical symptoms tended to be more severe with IMPG1 mutations.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large family with autosomal dominant VMD, Manes et al. (2013) used gene mapping and whole-exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.713T-G mutation in the IMPG1 gene, resulting in a leu238-to-arg (L238R) substitution. The same mutation was then found in 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD and the same phenotype. Structural modeling indicated that the substitution destabilizes the conserved SEA1 domain of the SPACR protein.

Manes et al. (2013) screened 144 probands with various forms of macular dystrophy for IMPG1 mutations. In 1 family with autosomal recessive VMD, 2 affected individuals were homozygous for a splice site mutation in IMPG1 (c.807+1G-T). In another family, 2 individuals with VMD were compound heterozygous for 2 IMPG1 mutations: c.461T-C (leu154-to-pro; L154P) and c.1519C-T (arg507-to-ter; R507X).

Meunier et al. (2014) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 49 families with vitelliform macular dystrophy who were negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations, deletions, or rearrangements, from a French national referral center database. IMPG1 was the causal gene in 3 families (11 patients) and IMPG2 in 1 family (2 patients); the authors concluded that IMPG1 and IMPG2 account for 8% of BEST1/PRPH2-negative families. In 2 families (families 1 and 2) with autosomal dominant vitelliform macular dystrophy, they identified the heterozygous L238R mutation in IMPG1. ⚠︎

Brandl et al. (2017) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 106 unrelated patients with Best disease or adult-onset VMD who were negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations, and identified IMPG1 mutations in 2 simplex cases with onset in early childhood. In a German boy (patient 1, family 13-501), they identified a heterozygous c.713T-C transition in exon 7 of IMPG1, resulting in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution in the SEA I domain; he was the only affected family member and parental data were unavailable. In a boy (patient 2) from a consanguineous Turkish family (11-151), they identified a homozygous c.807+5G-A splice site mutation in intron 7 of IMPG1; both parents were heterozygous. In a minigene assay, the c.807+5G-A mutation caused skipping of exon 7 in about half of transcripts.

In a 25-year-old woman with early-onset symptomatic outer retinal disruption, Gupta et al. (2021) found a heterozygous L154P mutation in the IMPG1 gene by genetic testing. The authors concluded that the mutation may cause symptomatic outer retinal disturbance in the heterozygous state and stated that further studies were needed.

Olivier et al. (2021) performed exome sequencing in a family with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (RP) and identified a splice variant in the IMPG1 gene. The same variant was then found in individuals from 2 more families, who had either RP or VMD. Screening of 596 families with RP or VMD by gene panel testing and exome sequencing identified 8 more families carrying different missense or nonsense variants in IMPG1. The authors concluded that monoallelic or biallelic IMPG1 variants are associated with RP. They also revised the clinical diagnosis for an IMPG1 retinopathy-associated variant that had originally been described as benign concentric annular macular dystrophy, reclassifying it as RP with early macular involvement.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Brandl et al. (2017) noted that patients with IMPG2 mutations had later onset (mean age at diagnosis 53.1 years) than patients with IMPG1 mutations, and that clinical symptoms tended to be more severe with IMPG1 mutations.

### Pathogenesis

Manes et al. (2013) noted that IMPG1 encodes SPACR, a component of the extracellular matrix domains of rod and cone photoreceptors, and suggested that impairment of the interphotoreceptor matrix might be a general cause of VMD.

### Diagnosis

Meunier et al. (2014) recommended screening IMPG1 and IMPG2 in adult-onset vitelliform dystrophy with moderate visual impairment, drusen-like lesions, normal RPE reflectivity on SD-OCT, and vitelliform deposits between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines, features not observed in classical BEST1 or PRPH2 vitelliform dystrophies.

Brandl et al. (2017) emphasized the need for diagnostic testing of IMPG1 and IMPG2, especially in VMD patients without EOG abnormalities.

### Animal Model

Olivier et al. (2021) knocked down Impg1 and its paralog Impg2 in medaka fish using morpholinos. The fish showed a phenotype consistent with that seen in the families, including shorter rod and cone photoreceptor outer segments.

In a German boy (patient 1, family 13-501) with VMD4, Brandl et al. IMPG1, IVS7DS, G-A, +5 [c.807+5G>A]

In a boy (patient 2) from a consanguineous Turkish family (11-151) with VMD4 diagnosed at age 2 years, Brandl et al. (2017) identified a homozygous c.807+5G-A splice site mutation in intron 7 of the IMPG1 gene; both parents were heterozygous. In a minigene assay in HEK293 cells, the mutation caused skipping of exon 7 in about half of transcripts, which was predicted to lead to nonsense-mediated decay. The heterozygous parents (aged 35 and 40 years) were asymptomatic with normal multimodal retinal imaging; the authors suggested that the residual correctly spliced transcript is sufficient to prevent subclinical abnormalities in carriers.
