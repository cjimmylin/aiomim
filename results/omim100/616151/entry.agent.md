### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that vitelliform macular dystrophy-4 (VMD4) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the IMPG1 gene.

### Description

Vitelliform macular dystrophies (VMD) are inherited retinal dystrophies characterized by yellow, round deposits visible on fundus examination, seen in juvenile Best macular dystrophy and in adult-onset vitelliform macular dystrophy (Manes et al., 2013). VMD caused by mutation in IMPG1, which encodes the SPACR protein of the photoreceptor extracellular (interphotoreceptor) matrix, can be transmitted in an autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive fashion; most patients have a normal or moderately decreased electrooculogram Arden ratio (Manes et al., 2013). Onset is usually in adulthood with moderate visual impairment (Meunier et al., 2014), although onset in early childhood has been reported (Brandl et al., 2017).

### Clinical Features

Manes et al. (2013) studied a large family with autosomal dominant VMD and 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD and the same phenotype, as well as 2 families with autosomal recessive VMD identified by screening 144 probands with various forms of macular dystrophy. Most cases had a normal or moderately decreased electrooculogram (EOG) Arden ratio.

Meunier et al. (2014) reviewed clinical, imaging, and EOG findings in 49 families with vitelliform macular dystrophy without identified mutation in BEST1 or PRPH2. IMPG1 was the causal gene in 3 families (11 patients) and IMPG2 in a fourth family (2 patients). Patients with IMPG1 or IMPG2 mutations had late onset and moderate visual impairment (mean visual acuity, 20/40; mean age of onset, 42 years), including the sporadic case in IMPG1 family 3 with presumed recessive transmission (age at onset, 38 years; mean visual acuity, 20/50). Drusen-like lesions adjacent to the vitelliform deposits were observed in 9 of 13 patients. The vitelliform material was located above the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) at every stage of the dystrophy, and the RPE was well preserved with classic reflectivity on spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT). EOG results were normal or borderline in 9 cases.

Brandl et al. (2017) described 2 simplex patients with IMPG1-associated VMD with onset in early childhood. Patient 1, a boy from a German family, was the only affected family member; at age 13 years he had reduced visual acuity (0.2 and 0.1 logMAR in the right and left eyes), and fundus examination showed central RPE abnormalities with foveal detachment. Patient 2, a boy from a consanguineous Turkish family, was diagnosed with VMD at age 2 years and had fluctuating visual acuity of about 0.2 logMAR in both eyes over 8 years of follow-up. At age 10, multifocal ERG was normal and the EOG Arden ratio was borderline to slightly abnormally low. Fundus photographs showed a singular vitelliform lesion in the central macula of both eyes, which increased in area during follow-up; SD-OCT showed a bilateral, central, dome-shaped foveal detachment of the neurosensory retina. The underlying hyperreflective material was reabsorbed, leaving an optically empty cavity, photoreceptor outer segments were elongated, and Bruch membrane and RPE appeared well preserved. Fundus autofluorescence showed circular hyperfluorescence that decreased over time. No satellite drusen were seen. His heterozygous parents, aged 35 and 40 years, were asymptomatic with full visual acuity and normal multimodal retinal imaging.

Gupta et al. (2021) reported a 25-year-old woman with symptomatic scotoma and decreased vision who had bilateral small foveal yellow spots. OCT showed disorganization and decreased reflectance of the foveal ellipsoid and interdigitation zones, more in the left eye than the right. Fundus autofluorescence showed minimal findings, dye angiography was unrevealing, and multifocal ERG showed slightly decreased retinal sensitivity in the central retina of the left eye.

### Inheritance

Manes et al. (2013) found that IMPG1 mutations cause both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive forms of VMD. Autosomal dominant transmission was observed in IMPG1 families 1 and 2 of Meunier et al. (2014), and presumed recessive transmission in a sporadic case. Brandl et al. (2017) reported a homozygous IMPG1 mutation in a patient from a consanguineous family and a heterozygous IMPG1 mutation in a simplex patient.

### Heterogeneity

Manes et al. (2013) noted that many cases of Best macular dystrophy and some cases of adult-onset VMD carry mutations in BEST1 or PRPH2. Meunier et al. (2014) found IMPG1 and IMPG2 mutations to be causal in 8% of families negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations, and Brandl et al. (2017) identified heterozygous IMPG2 mutations in 8 VMD patients from a cohort of 106 BEST1/PRPH2-negative patients.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large family with autosomal dominant VMD, Manes et al. (2013) used gene mapping and whole-exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.713T-G transversion in the IMPG1 gene, resulting in a leu238-to-arg (L238R) substitution; the same mutation was found in 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD. Structural modeling indicated that the L238R substitution destabilizes the conserved SEA1 domain of SPACR, the protein encoded by IMPG1. Screening of 144 probands with various forms of macular dystrophy revealed 3 other IMPG1 mutations: 2 individuals from a family with autosomal recessive VMD were homozygous for a splice site mutation (c.807+1G-T), and 2 individuals from another family were compound heterozygous for c.461T-C (L154P) and c.1519C-T (R507X) mutations.

Meunier et al. (2014) systematically screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 49 families with VMD negative for mutations, small deletions, or large rearrangements in BEST1 and PRPH2. IMPG1 was the causal gene in 3 families (11 patients); 2 families with autosomal dominant transmission had the L238R mutation, and family 3 was a sporadic case with presumed recessive transmission.

By next-generation sequencing of IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 106 VMD patients negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations, Brandl et al. (2017) identified 2 IMPG1 mutations in 2 simplex patients with early childhood onset. Patient 1, from a German family, carried a heterozygous c.713T-C transition in exon 7, resulting in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution in the SEA I domain, predicted pathogenic by 3 in silico algorithms; parental data were not available. Patient 2, from a consanguineous Turkish family, was homozygous for a c.807+5G-A splice site mutation in intron 7; homozygosity was confirmed by segregation analysis in the parents. In a minigene assay in HEK293 cells, the mutation caused skipping of exon 7 in about half of the transcripts.

In a 25-year-old woman with early-onset vitelliform dystrophy, Gupta et al. (2021) identified a heterozygous L154P mutation in the IMPG1 gene, and concluded that this mutation may cause symptomatic outer retinal disturbance in the heterozygous state, noting that further studies were necessary.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Olivier et al. (2021) identified an IMPG1 splice variant by exome sequencing in a family with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (RP), and found the same variant in individuals from 2 families with either RP or VMD. Screening of 596 RP and VMD families identified 8 additional families with different missense or nonsense IMPG1 variants. Morpholino-mediated knockdown of Impg1 and its paralog Impg2 in medaka fish produced a phenotype including decreased length of rod and cone photoreceptor outer segments. The authors concluded that monoallelic or biallelic IMPG1 variants are associated with RP.

Brandl et al. (2017) observed that clinical symptoms tended to be more severe in patients with IMPG1 mutations than in those with IMPG2 mutations.

### Diagnosis

Meunier et al. (2014) recommended that IMPG1 and IMPG2 be screened in adult-onset vitelliform dystrophy with moderate visual impairment, drusen-like lesions, normal reflectivity of the RPE line on SD-OCT, and vitelliform deposits located between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines on SD-OCT, characteristics not observed in the classic forms of BEST1 or PRPH2 vitelliform dystrophy. Brandl et al. (2017) emphasized the need for diagnostic testing of these genes particularly in VMD patients without EOG abnormalities.
