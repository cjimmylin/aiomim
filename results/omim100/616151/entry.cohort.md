### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that vitelliform macular dystrophy-4 (VMD4) is caused by heterozygous, homozygous, or compound heterozygous mutation in the IMPG1 gene (602870) on chromosome 6q14.1.

### Description

Vitelliform macular dystrophies are inherited retinal dystrophies marked by yellow, round deposits seen on fundus examination. These deposits occur in juvenile Best macular dystrophy and in adult-onset vitelliform macular dystrophy. When Manes et al. (2013) reported IMPG1 mutations, the genetic cause was unknown in many affected individuals.

### Clinical Features

Manes et al. (2013) studied a large family with autosomal dominant vitelliform macular dystrophy (VMD), 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD and the same phenotype, and 2 families with autosomal recessive VMD, each with 2 affected individuals. Most patients with IMPG1 mutations had a normal or moderately decreased electrooculogram (EOG) Arden ratio.

Meunier et al. (2014) described 11 patients from 3 families with IMPG1-related VMD. Families 1 and 2 had 10 affected members and showed autosomal dominant transmission. Family 3 had a sporadic case with presumed recessive transmission, with onset at age 38 years and a mean visual acuity of 20/50. Across the 13 patients with IMPG1 or IMPG2 mutations in their study, onset was late (mean 42 years) and visual impairment was moderate (mean visual acuity 20/40). Nine of the 13 patients had drusen-like lesions next to the vitelliform deposits, and EOG results were normal or borderline in 9 cases. On spectral-domain OCT, the vitelliform material lay above the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) at all stages, between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines. The RPE was well preserved and of normal reflectivity. Meunier et al. (2014) noted that these features are not seen in classical BEST1 or PRPH2 vitelliform dystrophies.

Brandl et al. (2017) reported 2 simplex male patients with IMPG1 mutations. Patient 1, from a German family and aged 16 years at last report, had reduced visual acuity and central RPE abnormalities with foveal detachment in both eyes. He was the only affected member of his family. Patient 2, from a consanguineous Turkish family, was diagnosed with VMD at age 2 years. Over 8 years of follow-up he had fluctuating visual acuity of about 0.2 logMAR in both eyes. Color fundus images showed a single vitelliform lesion in the central macula of each eye but an otherwise normal fundus, with no satellite drusen. He also had a dome-shaped foveal detachment of the neurosensory retina with elongated photoreceptor outer segments and central circular hyperautofluorescence. At age 10 years, multifocal ERG was normal and the EOG Arden ratio was borderline to slightly low. Brandl et al. (2017) described the same sequence of stages in patients with IMPG1 or IMPG2 mutations. First came single central vitelliform lesions, with the material lying above a preserved Bruch membrane/RPE. The material was then resorbed, leaving an optically empty cavity, and finally there was central atrophy with RPE loss. Clinical symptoms tended to be more severe with IMPG1 mutations.

Gupta et al. (2021) described a 25-year-old woman with a symptomatic scotoma, decreased vision, and small yellow foveal spots in both eyes. OCT showed disorganization and decreased reflectance of the foveal ellipsoid and interdigitation zones, worse in the left eye than the right. Multifocal ERG showed slightly decreased central retinal sensitivity in the left eye.

Olivier et al. (2021) identified IMPG1 variants in families whose members had either retinitis pigmentosa (RP) or VMD. They revised the clinical diagnosis for one IMPG1 retinopathy-associated variant, which had first been described as benign concentric annular macular dystrophy and was reclassified as RP with early macular involvement.

### Inheritance

Manes et al. (2013) showed that VMD4 can follow either an autosomal dominant or an autosomal recessive pattern. They found a heterozygous IMPG1 mutation in families with autosomal dominant VMD and homozygous or compound heterozygous IMPG1 mutations in families with autosomal recessive VMD. Meunier et al. (2014) observed autosomal dominant transmission in 2 families and presumed recessive transmission in a sporadic case. In the consanguineous family reported by Brandl et al. (2017), the patient was homozygous for an IMPG1 mutation and both heterozygous parents were clinically unaffected.

### Heterogeneity

Meunier et al. (2014) found a heterozygous IMPG2 mutation in a family with autosomal dominant VMD (2 patients). In their cohort of VMD patients, Brandl et al. (2017) found heterozygous IMPG2 mutations (3 stop and 5 missense) in 8 patients, who were symptomatic and had a mean age at diagnosis of 53.1 years. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a large family with autosomal dominant VMD, Manes et al. (2013) used gene mapping and whole-exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.713T-G mutation in the IMPG1 gene, resulting in a leu238-to-arg (L238R) substitution. They then found the same mutation in 2 other families with autosomal dominant VMD. They screened 144 probands with various forms of macular dystrophy and found 3 further IMPG1 mutations in 2 families with autosomal recessive VMD. Two affected individuals from one family were homozygous for a splice site mutation (c.807+1G-T). Two affected individuals from the other family were compound heterozygous for a leu154-to-pro (L154P) missense mutation and an arg507-to-ter (R507X) nonsense mutation. Manes et al. (2013) concluded that IMPG1 mutations cause both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive VMD, and suggested that impairment of the interphotoreceptor matrix might be a general cause of VMD.

Meunier et al. (2014) found the L238R mutation in IMPG1 in 2 families with autosomal dominant VMD. They also identified IMPG1 as the causal gene in a third family, which had a sporadic case with presumed recessive transmission.

Brandl et al. (2017) screened IMPG1 and IMPG2 in 106 VMD patients who had tested negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations. They found IMPG1 mutations in 2 simplex cases with onset in early childhood. A German patient carried a heterozygous c.713T-C transition in exon 7, resulting in a leu238-to-pro (L238P) substitution. Brandl et al. (2017) noted that this was the second missense mutation affecting IMPG1 residue Leu238 in a VMD patient, after L238R. A patient from a consanguineous Turkish family was homozygous for a c.807+5G-A splice site mutation, and segregation analysis showed that both parents were heterozygous. In a minigene assay in HEK293 cells, this mutation caused skipping of exon 7 in about half of the transcripts, and the authors suggested that the aberrant mRNA likely undergoes nonsense-mediated decay. The heterozygous parents were asymptomatic and had normal retinal imaging. Brandl et al. (2017) suggested that the correctly spliced transcripts provide enough functional IMPG1 to prevent even subclinical abnormalities in heterozygous carriers. They proposed that defective IMPG1 causes disease through an as yet unknown dominant-negative effect or through haploinsufficiency.

Gupta et al. (2021) found a heterozygous L154P mutation in the IMPG1 gene in a 25-year-old woman with early-onset vitelliform dystrophy. They suggested that this mutation, which has been implicated in adult-onset foveomacular vitelliform dystrophy, may cause symptomatic outer retinal disturbance in the heterozygous state, and noted that further studies were needed.

Olivier et al. (2021) used exome sequencing in a family with autosomal dominant RP and identified a splice variant in IMPG1. They then found the same variant in individuals from 2 families with either RP or VMD. A retrospective study of patients with RP or VMD found 8 more families with different missense or nonsense variants in IMPG1. In all, they screened 596 families with RP or VMD by gene panel testing and exome sequencing. They concluded that monoallelic or biallelic IMPG1 variants are associated with RP, similar to what has been reported for IMPG2.

### Pathogenesis

Manes et al. (2013) noted that IMPG1 encodes SPACR, a component of the extracellular matrix domains of rod and cone photoreceptors. Structural modeling indicated that the L238R substitution destabilizes the conserved SEA1 domain of SPACR.

### Diagnosis

Meunier et al. (2014) recommended screening IMPG1 and IMPG2 in patients with adult-onset vitelliform dystrophy who have moderate visual impairment, drusen-like lesions, normal RPE reflectivity on spectral-domain OCT, and vitelliform deposits between the ellipsoid and interdigitation lines.

### Population Genetics

Meunier et al. (2014) screened 49 families with VMD who were negative for BEST1 and PRPH2 mutations. IMPG1 or IMPG2 was causal in 8% (4 families: 3 with IMPG1 and 1 with IMPG2 mutations).

### Animal Model

Olivier et al. (2021) used morpholinos to knock down Impg1 and its paralog Impg2 in medaka fish. The resulting phenotype matched that seen in the families and included shorter rod and cone photoreceptor outer segments.
