### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.2.

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency (Kilic et al., 2012) caused by tyrosine kinase-2 (TYK2) deficiency (Minegishi et al., 2006). The first reported patient had unusual susceptibility to mycobacteria and viruses and had features of hyper-IgE syndrome, including atopic dermatitis and elevated serum IgE (Minegishi et al., 2006). TYK2-deficient patients reported later did not have hyper-IgE syndrome (Kreins et al., 2015). The cellular defect includes impaired responses to IL-12 and type I interferons, which require TYK2 for signaling (Minegishi et al., 2006).

### Nomenclature

Minegishi et al. (2006) first described TYK2 deficiency in a patient with a homozygous TYK2 mutation who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome and who had unusual susceptibility to mycobacteria. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that hyper-IgE syndrome is not a constant feature of TYK2 deficiency.

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a TYK2-deficient patient with atopic dermatitis and elevated serum IgE. The patient also had unusual susceptibility to mycobacteria and viruses.

Kreins et al. (2015) reported 7 further TYK2-deficient patients from 5 families of 4 different ethnic groups. The patients had mycobacterial disease (BCG and/or tuberculosis) and/or viral disease. None had hyper-IgE syndrome or high serum IgE. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that hyper-IgE syndrome is not a consistent feature of TYK2 deficiency.

### Inheritance

IMD35 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern (Kreins et al., 2015). Minegishi et al. (2006) and Kreins et al. (2015) found homozygous TYK2 mutations in affected individuals. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) found a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells had defective signaling in response to several cytokines, including type I interferons, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23, and showed accelerated Th2 differentiation. Minegishi et al. (2006) concluded that TYK2 deficiency was likely to account for the patient's complex clinical manifestations, including impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation.

Kreins et al. (2015) found homozygous null mutations in TYK2 in further patients with TYK2 deficiency. TYK2 protein was undetectable in cells from all of the patients. The patients' cells had impaired responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta, with reduced IFN-gamma production, and also had impaired responses to IL-23 and IL-10. Responses to IL-6 were normal, as were responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IL-28/29 (IFN-lambda), and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF). Responses to IFN-alpha, IL-12, IL-23, and IL-10 were equally impaired in all TYK2-deficient patients, including the first patient, so only the IL-6 response differed from that patient. Kreins et al. (2015) attributed the lack of hyper-IgE syndrome in their patients to their normal IL-6 responses. They concluded that the IL-10 and IL-23 defects were unlikely to be the cause of hyper-IgE syndrome in the first patient. ⚠︎
