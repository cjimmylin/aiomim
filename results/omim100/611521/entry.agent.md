### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35), also known as tyrosine kinase-2 (TYK2) deficiency, is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is an autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency caused by TYK2 deficiency. Patients are susceptible to intracellular bacteria, such as mycobacteria (including BCG and Mycobacterium tuberculosis), Salmonella and Brucella, and/or to viral infections, mainly herpesviruses. Kreins et al. (2015) attributed these infections to impaired responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta. The first patient reported had features of hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES), including atopic dermatitis, high serum IgE, and staphylococcal infections (Minegishi et al., 2006). Most later patients had none of these features, and Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that HIES is not an intrinsic part of TYK2 deficiency. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

TYK2 deficiency was first described as a form of autosomal recessive hyper-IgE syndrome (Minegishi et al., 2006). Kilic et al. (2012) and Kreins et al. (2015) then described TYK2-deficient patients without HIES. Wu et al. (2020) noted that the International Union of Immunological Societies (IUIS) classification placed TYK2 deficiency among the disorders of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) reported a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. He had unusual susceptibility to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE. Kreins et al. (2015) gave more detail on this patient (P1), a Japanese man born to consanguineous parents. He had atopic dermatitis from age 1 month and recurrent bacterial and viral infections from age 1 year. He developed BCGitis at age 22 months, 6 months after vaccination. He had repeated Staphylococcus aureus skin abscesses, pneumonia without pneumatoceles, otitis and sinusitis. At age 15 years he had sepsis after severe nontyphi Salmonella enterocolitis. His viral infections included parainfluenza virus-3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV infections and molluscum contagiosum, and he had mild oral candidiasis. Tests repeatedly showed hypereosinophilia (701-800/microliter) and high serum IgE (1,430-2,100 IU/ml). At age 30 years he had ocular sarcoidosis.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, born in 1992 to healthy first-cousin parents, who had TYK2 deficiency without HIES. He received BCG vaccine at 2 months of age. Left axillary lymphadenopathy appeared at 8 months, and generalized lymphadenopathy at 21 months. He had fistulous cervical, axillary and inguinal lymphadenopathy, and M. bovis BCG was isolated from the draining cervical sinuses. The PPD skin test was negative. At 8 years of age he developed neurobrucellosis, with Brucella spp. grown from blood and cerebrospinal fluid, after eating unpasteurized cheese. The illness left him with severe cognitive impairment and severe hearing loss, and MRI showed lacunar infarcts. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the right maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve. He had no skeletal or dental features of HIES and no atopy, asthma, candidiasis, boils or cold abscesses. Serum IgE measured at ages 2, 11, 16 and 18 years was normal or slightly high (80, 115, 218 and 155 IU/ml).

Kreins et al. (2015) reported 7 TYK2-deficient patients from 5 unrelated families, all with consanguineous parents. The patients came from Turkey, Morocco, Iran and Argentina. P2 was the Turkish patient reported by Kilic et al. (2012). P3, a Moroccan girl, had disseminated extrapulmonary tuberculosis from age 13 years and died of it. Her younger brother (P4) had meningitis of unknown cause at 8 months of age, recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, and eczema of the ear canal. P5, an Iranian boy, had BCG lymphadenitis and later paravertebral and psoas abscesses with fistulization and pulmonary involvement. His younger sister (P6) had BCG lymphadenopathy and an unknown viral skin infection. P7, a 9-year-old Iranian girl, had M. tuberculosis disease with micronodular lung infiltrates and adenopathies. P8, an 11-year-old boy from Argentina, had recurrent herpes gingivostomatitis, aseptic herpes meningitis, disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions and mild mental disability. None of the 7 had atopy, high serum IgE, staphylococcal or other pyogenic infections, or fungal infections. Infections with weakly virulent intracellular bacteria responded to antibiotics and did not recur. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) studied a TYK2-deficient patient with eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections and IgE levels above 1,000 U/mL. He had no viral or mycobacterial infections.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy born to nonconsanguineous parents. After BCG vaccination on the third day of life he had recurrent abscess and ulceration at the injection site. At 14 months of age he developed BCG lymphadenitis of the left axillary nodes. From 6 months of age he had recurrent respiratory tract infections and diarrhea. Pathogens identified included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, M. bovis BCG and influenza viruses. He had no high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal illness or lymphopenia. He had a small head circumference and began to say simple words at 3 years and 4 months. In a review of 12 reported patients, Wu et al. (2020) found intracellular bacterial infections in 7 (58%) and recurrent viral infections in 7 (58%). None had the facial features, bone abnormalities, delayed shedding of primary teeth or aneurysms seen in STAT3-related HIES. ⚠︎

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 more TYK2-deficient patients with or without hyper-IgE levels and atopy, and with differing patterns of infection.

Zhang et al. (2022) studied an international cohort of 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. Three unrelated Turkish children with TYK2 deficiency were found, aged 2, 4 and 9 years and each born to consanguineous parents. The 2-year-old girl had moderate COVID-19 pneumonia; earlier she had been hospitalized for infection with VZV vaccine and diagnosed with Kawasaki disease at 11 months. The 2 boys had critical COVID-19 pneumonia needing high-flow oxygen in intensive care. The 4-year-old boy had been in intensive care for influenza pneumonia at 3 years of age. His 3-year-old sister had the same genotype; she had critical influenza pneumonia needing intubation and disseminated varicella, but had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 is autosomal recessive. Minegishi et al. (2006) found a homozygous TYK2 mutation in the first patient. In the families reported by Kreins et al. (2015), disease occurred in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes. In the patient of Wu et al. (2020), both parents were heterozygous for the mutation.

### Molecular Genetics

In a patient clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome, Minegishi et al. (2006) found a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene. The patient's cells had defects in signaling by several cytokines, including type I IFN, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12 and IL-23, and these defects were corrected by transduction of intact TYK2. Kreins et al. (2015) reported that this patient (P1) carries a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 (C70HfsX21).

In a Turkish boy with TYK2 deficiency without HIES, Kilic et al. (2012) found a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene, given as c.2302_2310del. The deletion creates a premature termination codon at position 767. No full-length TYK2 protein was detected by Western blotting in the patient's EBV-B cells.

Kreins et al. (2015) studied 7 patients from 5 unrelated families and found homozygous TYK2 mutations. The methods used were linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing (P2), whole-exome sequencing (P3 to P7), or targeted next-generation sequencing (P8). The mutations were a 9-bp deletion in exon 16 (c.2303_2311del, L767X) in P2, the same patient reported by Kilic et al. (2012); c.3318_3319insC (T1106HfsX4) in Moroccan sibs; c.462G-T (E154X) in Iranian sibs; c.149delC (S50HfsX1) in an Iranian girl; and c.1912C-T (R638X) in a boy from Argentina. None of the 5 mutations appeared in public databases. The 130-kD TYK2 protein was undetectable in the patients' cells, which indicated complete TYK2 deficiency. All 8 TYK2-deficient patients, including P1, had impaired but not abolished cellular responses to IL-12, IL-23, IFN-alpha/beta and IL-10, and these were restored by expression of wildtype TYK2. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, IFN-lambda and LIF were normal. ⚠︎

Fuchs et al. (2016) found that the TYK2 mutation in their patient completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in a monocyte assay. It partly reduced IL-10 signaling, together with reduced IL-10R-beta expression, but did not affect IL-6 signaling. NK-cell phenotype and function were normal in the patient.

Using whole-exome sequencing, Wu et al. (2020) found a homozygous c.2395G-A transition in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene in a Chinese boy with BCG disease. The change causes a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution, and both parents were heterozygous. TYK2 mRNA was reduced in the patient's cells, and no TYK2 protein was detected. When the mutant was expressed in HEK293T cells, TYK2 mRNA and protein levels were lower than with wildtype. The authors noted that the variant was listed in gnomAD.

Lv et al. (2022) found novel TYK2 mutations in 5 TYK2-deficient patients by high-throughput sequencing and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. They tested the mutations with qRT-PCR, Western blot and protein stability assays. Responses of the patients' cells to IFN-alpha/beta/gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12 and IL-23 varied from patient to patient.

In 3 unrelated Turkish children with moderate or critical COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) found the same homozygous TYK2 loss-of-function frameshift variant (P216Rfs*14). Family segregation analysis confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. SV40 fibroblasts from a TYK2-deficient patient with the same genotype did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally, even after IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment, and did not induce interferon-stimulated genes.

### Pathogenesis

Kreins et al. (2015) found that leukocytes and fibroblasts from P2 through P8 responded normally to IL-6, whereas cells from P1 did not. In P1's cells, expression of wildtype TYK2 did not restore the IL-6 response, and silencing TYK2 in control cells did not impair it. The authors concluded that IL-6 signaling is TYK2 independent in humans and that P1's impaired IL-6 response was not caused by his TYK2 mutation. They suggested that this impaired IL-6 response may underlie at least the staphylococcal disease of P1. They also found that IL-17+ T cells were present in normal proportions, which they proposed explains the apparent lack of mucocutaneous candidiasis. Residual IL-10 responses were proposed to explain the lack of early-onset colitis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) found almost normal type III IFN signaling and only minimal impairment of virus control in a TYK2-deficient human cell line. They proposed that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may help protect TYK2-deficient patients against viruses.

Minegishi et al. (2006) found a homozygous TYK2 mutation in a Japanese patient with features of hyper-IgE syndrome and susceptibility to viruses, fungi and mycobacteria. Kreins et al. (2015) described the mutation as a homozygous 4-bp deletion in exon 4 that leads to a frameshift (C70HfsX21) and a premature stop codon. In the summary table of Wu et al. **.0002 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 9-BP DEL, EX16 [c.2302_2310del; c.2303_2311del; L767X]

In a Turkish boy born to first-cousin parents, Kilic et al. (2012) found a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene, given as c.2302_2310del. The deletion creates a premature termination codon at position 767. The boy had disseminated BCG infection, neurobrucellosis and herpes zoster but no HIES. No full-length TYK2 protein was detected in his EBV-B cells.

Kreins et al. (2015) reported the same patient (P2) and gave the mutation as c.2303_2311del, resulting in a premature stop codon at position 767 (L767X).

**.0003 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 1-BP INS, 3318C [c.3318_3319insC; T1106HfsX4]

In 2 Moroccan sibs born to first-cousin parents, Kreins et al. (2015) found a homozygous 1-bp insertion in exon 23 of the TYK2 gene (c.3318_3319insC). The insertion causes a frameshift (T1106HfsX4) with a premature stop codon at position 1110. The girl died of disseminated tuberculosis, and her brother had meningitis of unknown origin.

**.0004 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, GLU154TER [c.462G>T; E154X]

In 2 Iranian sibs with BCG disease, born to consanguineous parents, Kreins et al. (2015) found a homozygous c.462G-T transversion in exon 5 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a glu154-to-ter (E154X) substitution. TYK2 protein was undetectable in the patients' cells.

**.0005 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 1-BP DEL, 149C [c.149delC; S50HfsX1]

In a 9-year-old Iranian girl with M. tuberculosis disease, born to consanguineous parents, Kreins et al. (2015) found a homozygous 1-bp deletion in exon 3 of the TYK2 gene (c.149delC). The deletion causes a frameshift and a premature stop codon at position 50 (S50HfsX1).

**.0006 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, ARG638TER [c.1912C>T; R638X]

In an 11-year-old boy from Argentina with disseminated HSV disease, Kreins et al. (2015) found a homozygous c.1912C-T transition in exon 13 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in an arg638-to-ter (R638X) substitution.

**.0007 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, 1-BP DEL, 647C [c.647delC; P216RfsX14; P216Rfs*14]

In the summary table of Wu et al. (2020), the TYK2-deficient Kurdish patient of Fuchs et al. (2016), who had atopy, high IgE and S. aureus infection, is listed with a c.647delC mutation (P216RfsX14) in exon 7. The abstract of Fuchs et al. (2016) does not name the mutation.

Zhang et al. (2022) found the same homozygous TYK2 variant (P216Rfs*14) in 3 unrelated Turkish children born to consanguineous parents. The children had moderate or critical COVID-19 pneumonia. The authors confirmed that the variant causes loss of function. Fibroblasts from a patient homozygous for this variant did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally.

**.0008 IMMUNODEFICIENCY 35**  
TYK2, GLY799ARG [c.2395G>A; p.G799R]

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent respiratory infections, born to nonconsanguineous parents, Wu et al. (2020) found a homozygous c.2395G-A transition in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, resulting in a gly799-to-arg (G799R) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing. TYK2 mRNA was reduced and TYK2 protein was undetectable in the patient's cells. In transfected HEK293T cells, the mutant gave lower TYK2 mRNA and protein levels than wildtype.
