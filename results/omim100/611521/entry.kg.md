### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that immunodeficiency-35 (IMD35) is caused by homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene (176941) on chromosome 19p13.2.

### Description

Immunodeficiency-35 is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by TYK2 deficiency. Kreins et al. (2015) concluded that the core clinical phenotype of complete TYK2 deficiency is mycobacterial and/or viral infections, caused by impaired responses to IL-12 and IFN-alpha/beta. They also concluded that impaired IL-6 responses and hyper-IgE syndrome (HIES) do not appear to be intrinsic features of TYK2 deficiency in humans. Wu et al. (2020) summarized the main features as normal lymphocyte counts with defects in multiple cytokine signaling pathways, susceptibility to viruses or intracellular bacteria, and IgE that may or may not be elevated.

### Clinical Features

Minegishi et al. (2006) described a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. The patient was unusually susceptible to various microorganisms, including viruses, fungi, and mycobacteria, and had atopic dermatitis with elevated serum IgE.

Kilic et al. (2012) reported a Turkish boy, born to first-cousin parents, who developed disseminated BCG disease after vaccination at 2 months of age. Left axillary lymphadenopathy appeared at 8 months. By 21 months he had generalized fistulous cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymphadenopathy with fever. M. bovis BCG was isolated from discharging sinuses, and lymph node histology showed acid-fast bacilli. He recovered without relapse after 18 months of antimycobacterial therapy. At age 8 years he developed neurobrucellosis, with seizures, neck stiffness, and Brucella-positive blood and CSF cultures. It left him with severe sensorineural hearing loss, severe cognitive impairment, and lacunar infarcts on MRI. At 11 years he had herpes zoster of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve, which was treated with acyclovir and did not recur. ⚠︎

Unlike the first reported Japanese TYK2-deficient patient, the patient of Kilic et al. (2012) had no features of hyper-IgE syndrome. He had no atopy, asthma, eczema, staphylococcal or candidal skin infections, and no skeletal or dental abnormalities. His serum IgE ranged from 80 to 218 IU/ml, which was normal or only slightly elevated. Serum IgG, IgM, and IgA levels, lymphocyte subsets, and the NBT test were normal. The authors suggested that TYK2 deficiency should be considered in patients with BCG disease or other intramacrophage infections, particularly those with severe herpesvirus infections, even in the absence of HIES features. ⚠︎

Kreins et al. (2015) reported 7 TYK2-deficient patients (P2-P8) from 5 unrelated families from Turkey, Morocco, Iran, and Argentina. All had mycobacterial and/or viral infections, but none had features of HIES: none had atopy, high serum IgE, staphylococcal disease, documented pyogenic infections, or fungal infections, including mucocutaneous candidiasis. This contrasted with the previously reported Japanese patient (P1), who had atopic dermatitis, high IgE, and staphylococcal abscesses.

P2 of Kreins et al. (2015) was a 23-year-old Turkish man, born to first-cousin parents. He had disseminated BCG lymphadenitis at 21 months, Brucella meningitis at 8 years, and shingles of the maxillary branch of the trigeminal nerve at 11 years.

Kreins et al. (2015) described Moroccan sibs born to first-cousin parents. The girl (P3) developed disseminated extrapulmonary M. tuberculosis infection (abdominal disease, ascites, psoas abscess) at 13 years and died after bacterial meningitis 15 months after onset. Her brother (P4) had meningitis of unknown etiology at 8 months, recurrent otitis and urinary tract infections, asthma, ear canal eczema, and high eosinophils.

Kreins et al. (2015) described Iranian sibs born to consanguineous parents. The boy (P5) developed BCG axillary and cervical lymphadenitis at 2 and 4 months. At 4.5 years he developed paravertebral and psoas abscesses with pulmonary involvement, which were treated with antimycobacterials, surgery, and IFN-gamma. His sister (P6) had BCG lymphadenopathy after vaccination and an unknown viral skin infection at 1 year.

Kreins et al. (2015) described a 9-year-old Iranian girl (P7), born to consanguineous parents, who developed miliary tuberculosis. Findings included diffuse bilateral micronodular lung infiltrations, hilar and mediastinal adenopathy, and cavitary consolidation, and M. tuberculosis was identified on gastric washes.

Kreins et al. (2015) described an 11-year-old Argentinian boy (P8), adopted at 1 day of age. From 18 months he had recurrent HSV disease, including herpes gingivostomatitis, aseptic herpes meningitis, and disseminated cutaneous herpetic lesions, with a good response to acyclovir. He also had mild mental disability of uncertain origin. He had no mycobacterial disease despite BCG vaccination.

Kreins et al. (2015) also updated the clinical history of the original Japanese TYK2-deficient patient (P1), who was born to consanguineous parents. He had atopic dermatitis from 1 month of age, BCGitis, S. aureus skin abscesses, Salmonella sepsis, PIV3 pneumonia, recurrent oral HSV, molluscum contagiosum, mild oral candidiasis, hypereosinophilia, and high serum IgE (1,430-2,100 IU/ml). At 30 years he had ocular sarcoidosis.

Fuchs et al. (2016) reported a patient with TYK2 deficiency who had eczema, skin abscesses, respiratory infections, and serum IgE levels above 1000 U/mL. The patient had no viral or mycobacterial infections, and the authors described the human phenotype as surprisingly mild.

Wu et al. (2020) reported a Chinese boy, the second child of young nonconsanguineous parents, who was vaccinated with BCG on day 3 of life. He had repeated abscess and ulceration at the injection site, which healed by 10 months. At 14 months he developed BCG-associated left axillary lymph node tuberculosis; acid-fast bacilli were seen and Mycobacterium bovis BCG was isolated. From 6 months of age he had recurrent respiratory tract infections every 1 to 2 months, as well as diarrhea. Pathogens identified included Salmonella, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and influenza A and B (IgM positive). He did not have high serum IgE, atopy, staphylococcal disease, lymphopenia, or significant fungal infection. He also had a small head circumference and delayed speech.

Wu et al. (2020) reviewed 12 reported patients with TYK2 deficiency, including their own patient. The mean age at diagnosis was 10.8 years. Seven patients (58%) had intracellular bacterial infections, 2 had M. tuberculosis, and 7 (58%) had recurrent viral infections. Two had rash or atopic dermatitis, 3 had markedly increased serum IgE, and 2 had EBV-related B-cell lymphoma with T-cell lymphopenia. None had the facial, skeletal, dental, or vascular features of STAT3-associated hyper-IgE syndrome.

Lv et al. (2022) described 5 patients with TYK2 deficiency who presented with or without elevated IgE levels and atopy and had distinct pathogen infection profiles. These findings supported the observation that TYK2-deficient patients do not necessarily show features of hyper-IgE syndrome. Lymphocyte homeostasis was disrupted, with altered T- and B-cell differentiation on flow cytometry. ⚠︎

Among 112 children hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia, Zhang et al. (2022) identified 3 unrelated children with AR TYK2 deficiency, each born to a different set of consanguineous Turkish parents. P2, a 2-year-old girl, had moderate COVID-19 pneumonia that did not require oxygen. She had previously had neonatal sepsis, 2 ICU admissions for fever and dyspnea before 5 months of age, Kawasaki disease at 11 months, and a hospitalization for infection with the VZV vaccine at age 2. P3 (age 4) and P4 (age 9), both boys, had critical COVID-19 pneumonia requiring ICU admission and high-flow oxygen. P3 had previously had neonatal sepsis and, at age 3, influenza pneumonia requiring ICU care. P4 had recurrent bronchitis requiring hospital admission. All 3 survived.

### Other Features

Kreins et al. (2015) found normal numbers of central and effector memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in TYK2-deficient patients, unlike in STAT3-mutated AD-HIES patients. They suggested that viral reactivation in TYK2 deficiency results from impaired IFN-alpha/beta responses rather than from a memory T-cell defect. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Fuchs et al. (2016) used a monocyte assay to show that their patient's TYK2 mutation completely abolished the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response. The mutation also partly reduced IL-10-mediated signaling, which was associated with reduced IL-10R-beta expression. IL-6-mediated signaling was not affected.

In the patient reported by Wu et al. (2020), testing at age 1 year 11 months showed normal serum IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE (27.4 IU/ml) and normal eosinophil counts. CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T-cell percentages were normal. The CD19+ B-cell percentage was increased (26.6%) and the NK-cell percentage was decreased (3.6%).

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of IMD35 is autosomal recessive. In all of their kindreds, Kreins et al. (2015) observed clinical disease in all homozygotes and only in homozygotes, and concluded that the patients had autosomal recessive TYK2 deficiency; most of the families were consanguineous. Zhang et al. (2022) confirmed AR inheritance by family segregation analysis.

Zhang et al. (2022) found that the 3-year-old younger sister of their P3 had the same homozygous TYK2 genotype. She had a history of critical influenza pneumonia requiring intubation and ventilation, as well as disseminated varicella. She had not been infected with SARS-CoV-2 and remained seronegative. The authors noted that TYK2 deficiency impairs, but does not abolish, cellular responses to type I IFN. They stated that more TYK2-deficient patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 were needed to estimate penetrance, and suggested that penetrance may be incomplete.

### Molecular Genetics

Minegishi et al. (2006) identified a homozygous mutation in the TYK2 gene in a patient who had been clinically diagnosed with hyper-IgE syndrome. Transducing intact TYK2 restored the defective cytokine signals in the patient's cells.

Woellner et al. (2007) addressed the relationship between hyper-IgE syndrome and mutations in TYK2 in a correspondence. ⚠︎

In a Turkish boy with disseminated BCG disease, Kilic et al. (2012) identified a homozygous 9-bp deletion in exon 16 of the TYK2 gene (c.2302_2310del), which creates a premature termination codon at position 767. Western blot detected no full-length TYK2 protein in the patient's EBV-B cells.

In 7 patients from 5 families with mycobacterial and/or viral infections, Kreins et al. (2015) identified homozygous mutations in the TYK2 gene, using linkage analysis and Sanger sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, or targeted next-generation sequencing. In a Turkish patient (P2), they found a 9-bp deletion in exon 16 (c.2303_2311del; L767X). In Moroccan sibs (P3 and P4), they found a 1-bp insertion in exon 23 (c.3318_3319insC; T1106HfsX4). In Iranian sibs (P5 and P6), they found a c.462G-T substitution in exon 5 (E154X). In an Iranian girl (P7), they found a 1-bp deletion in exon 3 (c.149delC; S50HfsX1). In an Argentinian boy (P8), they found a c.1912C-T substitution in exon 13 (R638X). Western blotting with 4 antibodies against N- or C-terminal epitopes showed that the 130-kD TYK2 protein was undetectable in EBV-B cells, HVS-T cells, and fibroblasts of all patients, indicating complete TYK2 deficiency; no abnormal splicing was detected. TYK2-deficient cells had reduced surface IFN-alpha-R1, IL-10R2, and IL-12R-beta-1, and this was rescued by wildtype TYK2, reflecting loss of the scaffolding role of TYK2.

In a Chinese boy with BCG disease and recurrent respiratory infections, Wu et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a homozygous missense mutation in exon 17 of the TYK2 gene, c.2395G-A (G799R). Both parents were heterozygous for the variant. Patient cells had reduced TYK2 mRNA and no detectable TYK2 protein. When expressed in HEK293T cells, the mutant lowered TYK2 mRNA and protein levels compared with wildtype. The patient also carried heterozygous variants in CFH, LRRC8A, and NIPBL, for which the authors considered the evidence of pathogenicity insufficient.

In 5 patients with TYK2 deficiency, Lv et al. (2022) identified novel TYK2 mutations by high-throughput sequencing and confirmed them by Sanger sequencing. Pathogenicity was supported by qRT-PCR, Western blot, and protein stability assays. Patient PBMCs showed heterogeneous responses to IFN-alpha/beta/gamma, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, and IL-23.

Zhang et al. (2022) studied an international cohort of 112 children under 16 years of age who were hospitalized for COVID-19 pneumonia. Twelve (10.7%) had recessive complete inborn errors of type I IFN immunity: XLR TLR7 deficiency (7 children) and AR deficiency of IFNAR1 (1), STAT2 (1), or TYK2 (3). None of these 15 recessive deficiencies was found in 1,224 SARS-CoV-2-infected controls without pneumonia (P = 1.2 x 10(-11)). In the 3 unrelated consanguineous Turkish children with TYK2 deficiency (P2-P4), whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing identified the same homozygous TYK2 frameshift variant, P216Rfs*14, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The variant had previously been shown to cause loss of expression in patient cells, and the authors confirmed that it was loss-of-function. Eight of the 12 patients in the series were tested for autoantibodies against type I IFNs, and all were negative. The authors identified AR TYK2 deficiency as a new genetic cause of COVID-19 pneumonia.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Kreins et al. (2015) found that cells from their 7 new patients responded normally to IL-6, unlike cells from P1, who had HIES. Re-expressing wildtype TYK2 did not rescue IL-6 hyporesponsiveness in P1 cells, and TYK2 silencing in control or P1 cells did not alter IL-6 responses. These results indicated that the IL-6 defect in P1 was independent of the TYK2 mutation. The authors suggested that this defect may underlie the staphylococcal disease and HIES features of P1, and that P1 may carry a variant in another, unknown component of the IL-6 pathway.

### Pathogenesis

Minegishi et al. (2006) found that the patient's cells had defects in several cytokine signaling pathways, including those for type I interferon, IL6, IL10, IL12, and IL23, and that transducing intact TYK2 restored these signals. They concluded that TYK2 deficiency likely accounted for the patient's complex clinical features, including impaired Th1 differentiation and accelerated Th2 differentiation. They also concluded that in humans TYK2 plays obligatory roles in multiple cytokine signals involved in innate and acquired immunity, and that this differs substantially from TYK2 function in mice.

Kilic et al. (2012) proposed that loss of TYK2 impairs IL-12 signaling and IFN-gamma production, causing susceptibility to BCG and Brucella. They suggested that the viral illnesses are probably related to an impaired cellular response to IFN-alpha/beta.

Kreins et al. (2015) found that TYK2-deficient T and NK cells had impaired, but not abolished, IFN-gamma production in response to IL-12, in contrast to IL-12R-beta-1 deficiency. They suggested that this accounts for the mycobacterial disease and its relatively favorable response to antibiotics; the patient with virulent M. tuberculosis (P3) died. IL-23 responses were impaired but not abolished. IL-17A/F production by naive CD4 T cells was profoundly impaired in vitro, but proportions of circulating IL-17+ T cells were normal ex vivo, probably accounting for the lack of overt chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis.

In response to IFN-alpha/beta, patient cells studied by Kreins et al. (2015) showed reduced but not absent STAT1 phosphorylation and GAF/ISGF3 DNA binding, and absent STAT3 phosphorylation. Patient cells also had low basal ISG expression, and IFN-alpha failed to control VSV replication in EBV-B and HVS-T cells. All of these defects were rescued by wildtype TYK2. The authors suggested that residual responses explain the mild viral phenotype, which resembled partial STAT1 deficiency. Responses to IFN-lambda were normal, although basal IFIT1 levels were low. IL-10 responses (STAT3 phosphorylation, SOCS3 induction, and TNF suppression in macrophages) were impaired but not abolished in all patients and were rescued by wildtype TYK2; the authors suggested that residual IL-10 signaling accounts for the absence of early-onset colitis. Responses to IL-21, IL-27, IFN-gamma, and LIF were normal. ⚠︎

In a TYK2-deficient human cell line, Fuchs et al. (2016) found nearly normal type III IFN signaling and only minimal impairment of virus control. In their patient, NK-cell phenotype and function were normal, including IL-12/IL-18-mediated responses, in contrast to findings in TYK2-deficient mice. The authors suggested that preserved type III IFN responses and normal NK-cell function may help protect against viruses in TYK2 deficiency and may account for the mild human phenotype.

Lv et al. (2022) proposed that TYK2 acts as a multi-tasker that orchestrates various cytokine signaling pathways, and that different combinations of defects account for the varied clinical manifestations of TYK2 deficiency.

Zhang et al. (2022) studied ACE2-transduced SV40 fibroblasts from a TYK2-deficient patient homozygous for P216fs*14. Like STAT2-deficient cells, these cells did not control SARS-CoV-2 replication normally. Pretreatment with IFN-alpha-2b did not block the infection, and the cells failed to induce ISGs (IFIT1, MX1, IFI27) after viral infection or IFN-alpha-2b pretreatment. The authors suggested that these results supported a causal role of TYK2 deficiency in COVID-19 pneumonia.

### Diagnosis

Fuchs et al. (2016) established a simple diagnostic monocyte assay that detects loss of the IFN-alpha-mediated antiviral response in TYK2 deficiency.

Wu et al. (2020) suggested that TYK2 deficiency be considered in patients with repeated intracellular bacterial (including tuberculosis) or viral infections, or with eczema.

### Population Genetics

Kreins et al. (2015) noted that their 5 novel TYK2 mutations were private and were absent from their in-house exome database and from public databases, including 1000 Genomes and ExAC. No database contained homozygotes for TYK2 stop mutations, and only 84 heterozygotes were present. In contrast, Wu et al. (2020) noted that the G799R variant is listed in gnomAD and therefore is not a private mutation; its CADD score was 25.6.
