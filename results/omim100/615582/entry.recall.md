### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding transforming growth factor-beta-3 (TGFB3; 190230) on chromosome 14q24.3, as reported by Kuechler et al. (2015). ⚠︎

### Description

Rienhoff et al. (2013) noted that the features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 overlap with those of Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. They include joint laxity or contractures and craniofacial features such as hypertelorism and bifid uvula (Rienhoff et al., 2013). Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) also reported cleft palate and skeletal overgrowth or other skeletal features. Some patients have aortic or other arterial aneurysm and dissection (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015). Rienhoff et al. (2013) attributed the phenotype to decreased TGF-beta signaling resulting from loss of activity of the TGF-beta ligand TGFB3.

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) described a girl with a syndrome that combined low muscle mass, growth retardation, and distal arthrogryposis with clinical features overlapping those of Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. These included hypertelorism, a bifid uvula with an intact hard palate, and skeletal findings.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) reported a series of patients from several families with heterozygous TGFB3 mutations. The patients had syndromic thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection, and some had abdominal aortic or other arterial aneurysms. Other features included skeletal overgrowth, joint hypermobility, hypertelorism, bifid uvula and cleft palate, and other features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. The authors observed striking intrafamilial and interfamilial clinical variability: typical Loeys-Dietz syndrome features were present in some patients and completely absent in others.

### Inheritance

In the families reported by Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015), transmission was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. In the patient described by Rienhoff et al. (2013), the TGFB3 mutation arose de novo. Because the parents were unaffected and the phenotype overlapped with autosomal dominant syndromes, the authors suggested that a de novo mutation acted as a dominant trait. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) found a de novo heterozygous missense mutation in the TGFB3 gene in a girl with the syndrome. The authors stated that this was the first example of a mutation in the coding portion of TGFB3 implicated in a clinical syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in patients from multiple families with syndromic aortic aneurysm and dissection. In a large family, the mutation segregated with the clinical phenotype. Molecular analyses and predictions indicated that most of the mutations cause loss of function. Even so, the authors observed evidence of paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling in the aortic wall of a patient with a TGFB3 mutation, similar to findings in TGFB2-deficient aortic walls.
