### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also known as Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (190230) on chromosome 14q24.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is an autosomal dominant disorder with clinical features that overlap with Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Reported features include aortic aneurysm and dissection, mitral valve disease, craniofacial findings such as hypertelorism, bifid uvula or cleft palate, and retrognathia, and skeletal findings such as tall stature, arachnodactyly, and joint hypermobility. Severity varies widely within and between families. Some carriers have no cardiovascular involvement, and one reported patient had growth retardation, low muscle mass, and distal arthrogryposis (Rienhoff et al., 2013; Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015; Kuechler et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European-American girl born to nonconsanguineous, healthy parents who had 2 older healthy children. From birth she had distal arthrogryposis with contractures of the fingers and toes, hypotonia, failure to thrive, growth retardation, and delayed gross motor development. Her motor examination showed decreased bulk in all appendicular and axial muscles, strength of 1/5, low tone, and diminished reflexes throughout, and subcutaneous fat was markedly reduced. She had a positive Gower sign and a waddling gait, and muscle biopsy showed type 1 fiber predominance. Other features were bifid uvula, retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, blue sclerae, a metopic ridge, a tubular nose, a midline facial nevus flammeus, long narrow hands and feet with arachnodactyly, hyperextensible large joints, mild pectus excavatum, pes planus, and coxa valga. She had no cardiac defect or aortic dilatation, no intellectual disability, and no scoliosis. ⚠︎

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with clinical features overlapping with Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. Seven family members, aged between 40 and 68 years, had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three died from aortic dissection and rupture. Four members had mitral valve abnormalities, ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation that required surgery. Two had iliac and subclavian artery aneurysms, and 1 bruised easily. Craniofacial abnormalities were rather subtle and included a long face, a high-arched palate, and retrognathia. Some family members had pectus deformity, scoliosis, velvety skin, varices, hiatal hernia, HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease, or celiac disease. Aortic tissue from 2 members showed elastic fiber fragmentation and medial degeneration.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) also described 10 further families (families 2 to 11). Some members had type A or type B aortic dissection, and 1 died from dissection of a cerebral aneurysm. Other cardiovascular findings included mitral valve disease, patent foramen ovale, and atrial or ventricular septal defects. Typical Loeys-Dietz features were common, including hypertelorism, bifid uvula and cleft palate, cervical spine instability, and clubfoot. Other recurrent features were dolichocephaly, high-arched palate, retrognathia, tall stature, joint hypermobility, arachnodactyly, pectus deformity, and inguinal hernia. One patient had short stature, and 2 had early-onset osteoarthritis. The medical records showed no evidence of ectopia lentis, and there was no arterial tortuosity. Aortic wall tissue showed elastic fiber fragmentation with deposition of collagen and proteoglycans. ⚠︎

Across the 11 families, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized 43 patients (23 males and 20 females) with TGFB3 mutations, not all of whom were evaluated for every feature. Type A dissection occurred in 4 patients (median age 51 years), type B dissection in 6 (median age 44.5 years), and aortic aneurysm in 6. Skeletal findings included tall stature (12 patients), arachnodactyly (16), pectus deformity (8), kyphoscoliosis (11), and joint hypermobility (9). Loeys-Dietz features included hypertelorism (14), bifid uvula (11), and cleft palate (5). The authors observed striking variability within and between families. They found no early arterial dissection, no dissection at small aortic dimensions, and no striking arterial tortuosity. Vascular involvement ranged from none at age 64 years to type A or B dissection and death from dissection of a cerebral aneurysm.

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a father and his 2 affected children with a disorder overlapping with Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. All 3 had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula, but none had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation, or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of LDS5 is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) observed this pattern in a large Dutch family, and Kuechler et al. (2015) found the mutation segregating in an affected father and his 2 affected children. De novo mutations were reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013), by Matyas et al. (2014), and in 1 family studied by Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015).

### Mapping

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed linkage analysis in a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms. They found 2 large regions on chromosomes 14 and 15 that were shared by all affected members, and the most prominent candidate gene was TGFB3 on 14q24.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) studied a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, hypertelorism, low muscle mass, and growth retardation. Exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution, and Sanger sequencing confirmed that it was de novo. The mutation was absent from her parents and her 2 unaffected sibs. The patient also carried a de novo nonsense mutation (Q70X) in the CDH2 gene, which the authors considered unlikely to drive the phenotype. Cys409 is the penultimate cysteine of the cysteine knot, a residue that is conserved in all TGF-beta family ligands and is essential for ligand activity. In cultured cells, the C409Y mutant ligand did not activate a TGF-beta reporter. When the mutant was coexpressed with wildtype TGFB3 in Xenopus embryos, pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signaling fell to about 40% and 60% of wildtype, respectively, consistent with a dominant-negative effect. BMP-induced SMAD1 phosphorylation was unaffected. The authors concluded that the allele is hypomorphic.

In a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3.

In a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms and dissections (family 1), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous splice site mutation in the TGFB3 gene (c.754+2T-C). The mutation causes skipping of exon 4 and an in-frame deletion (glu216_lys251del). It segregated with the phenotype and was also present in a 17-year-old family member who had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic manifestations. The authors then screened TGFB3 in 470 probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection. They identified heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 further probands: 4 different missense mutations (D263H, R300W, I322T, and L401P), 1 nonsense mutation (Y365X), and 2 single-base deletions causing a frameshift and a premature stop codon (Leu386ArgfsTer21 and Asn235MetfsTer11). The R300W mutation was found in 4 families, and Y365X occurred de novo. All of the mutations were absent from control databases. A heterozygous A250T variant in the SMAD3 gene was also found in this group, and its significance was considered uncertain. ⚠︎

Kuechler et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 of 3 affected members of a family with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. They identified a novel heterozygous c.898C-G transversion in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and showed that it segregated with the phenotype in the father and his 2 affected children. The authors noted that this is the same codon as a previously reported mutation (R300Q) in a child with overgrowth.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that TGFB3 mutations affecting residue arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

Kuechler et al. (2015) suggested that mutations at codon arg300 of TGFB3 presumably increase TGF-beta signaling. They proposed that the short or tall stature seen in patients with TGFB3 mutations may reflect opposing effects of different mutations on TGF-beta signaling. As examples, they contrasted a loss-of-function mutation (C409Y) in an individual with growth retardation with arg300 mutations in individuals with overgrowth or tall stature.

### Pathogenesis

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found increased pSMAD2, pERK, and TGFB1 mRNA in aortic wall tissue from a patient with the D263H mutation in TGFB3. This indicated paradoxically enhanced canonical and noncanonical TGF-beta signaling, even though the truncating and splice site mutations suggested loss of TGFB3 function.

### Diagnosis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) found that their patient did not meet the diagnostic criteria for Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, or Beals-Hecht syndrome. Distinguishing features were significant growth retardation, absence of cardiovascular findings, and a muscle biopsy without dystrophic changes. No mutations were found in the TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1, or FBN2 genes.

### Clinical Management

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported that a 3-year trial of losartan produced no change in their patient's muscle strength or mass. They took this as further evidence of a pathophysiology distinct from that of mutations that enhance TGF-beta signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended a yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers. They also recommended at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches, and possibly imaging of the brain vessels depending on the family history.
