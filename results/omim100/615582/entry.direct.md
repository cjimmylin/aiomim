### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also known as Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene on chromosome 14q24. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is a connective tissue disorder whose features overlap with Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Marfan syndrome, and Shprintzen-Goldberg syndrome. Craniofacial findings include hypertelorism, bifid uvula, and cleft palate. Skeletal findings include arachnodactyly, joint hypermobility, pectus deformity, scoliosis, and clubfoot. Cardiovascular involvement includes thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysm and dissection and mitral valve disease (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015). Expression varies widely within and between families: some patients have no cardiovascular findings, and stature ranges from short to tall (Rienhoff et al., 2013; Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015; Kuechler et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Kuechler et al. (2015) noted that the emerging connective tissue disorder associated with TGFB3 mutations had tentatively been called Rienhoff syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European American girl born to healthy nonconsanguineous parents. At birth she had contractures of the right hand, most severe in the third and fourth fingers, and of all toes. She also had long, narrow-appearing hands and feet, a midline facial nevus flammeus, and mild hypotonia. Her birth weight was 2.9 kg (5th centile). At 3 months she was evaluated for failure to thrive and delayed gross motor function. At 17 months she weighed 7.5 kg (less than 1st centile) and was 76 cm tall (5th centile). Findings at that age included bilateral pes planus, mild pectus excavatum, hyperextensibility of multiple large joints, mild retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, a bifid uvula with an intact hard palate, a small metopic ridge, and blue sclerae. She had decreased bulk in all appendicular and axial muscles, strength of 1/5, low tone, diminished reflexes, and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. She stood independently with a positive Gower sign at 20 months and walked at 24 months with a 'hip waddle.' Her neurocognitive development was appropriate for age. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. At age 7 years she weighed 15.5 kg (less than 1st centile) and was 115 cm tall (5th centile), with marked hyperextensibility of the elbows and knees. A quadriceps muscle biopsy at that age showed type 1 fiber predominance with mild, focal type 1 fiber disproportion consistent with disuse, without dystrophic or inflammatory changes. Yearly echocardiograms from 18 months onward showed no cardiac defect or dysfunction. At 6.5 years, aortic annulus and aortic root dimensions were normal. The authors noted overlap with Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, and Beals-Hecht syndromes, but the patient did not meet diagnostic criteria for any of them.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. As summarized by Kuechler et al. (2015) and Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015), the patient was a child with tall stature who had cleft palate and/or bifid uvula, without significant vascular involvement at 10.5 years of age.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with clinical features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. Seven family members, aged 40 to 68 years, had aneurysms and dissections mainly involving the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three died from aortic dissection and rupture. Four family members had mitral valve abnormalities, ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation requiring surgery. Craniofacial abnormalities were subtle and included a long face, high-arched palate, and retrognathia. Pectus deformity and scoliosis were frequent. Other recurrent findings were velvety skin, varices, and hiatal hernia. Several family members had autoimmune features, including HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease, and celiac disease. Pathology reports from 2 deceased patients described extensive elastic fiber fragmentation and aortic medial degeneration.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified 10 further families with TGFB3 mutations, giving a total of 43 patients from 11 families. The phenotypes overlapped significantly with Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Vascular involvement ranged from no cardiovascular abnormalities at age 64 years to type A aortic dissection (median age, 51 years; range, 40 to 80) or type B aortic dissection (median age, 44.5 years; range, 30 to 57). It also included abdominal aortic dissection, and 1 patient died from cerebral aneurysm dissection at age 55. The authors observed no early arterial dissections and no dissections at small aortic dimensions. Other cardiovascular features were mitral valve disease, persistent foramen ovale, and atrial or ventricular septal defects. Iliac and subclavian artery aneurysms were found in 2 patients, and no striking arterial tortuosity was seen. Systemic features included tall stature (12 patients), arachnodactyly (16), pectus deformity (8), kyphoscoliosis (11), joint hypermobility (9), hypertelorism (14), bifid uvula (11), and cleft palate (5). Cervical spine instability, clubfoot, dolichocephaly, high-arched palate, retrognathia, and inguinal hernia also occurred. No ectopia lentis was documented, and early-onset osteoarthritis was reported in only 2 individuals. One patient had short stature and received growth hormone during puberty. The aortic wall of 1 patient showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition. The authors noted striking intrafamilial and interfamilial variability: some patients had typical LDS features and others had none.

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a family in which a father and his 2 children were affected. All 3 had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula, without cardiac involvement, aortic dilation, or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

Rienhoff et al. (2013) identified a de novo heterozygous TGFB3 mutation in an affected girl. Matyas et al. (2014) also reported a de novo TGFB3 mutation. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) described a large family with a pattern consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance and found a de novo mutation in another family. Kuechler et al. (2015) reported transmission from an affected father to his 2 affected children.

### Mapping

In a large Dutch family with LDS5, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) performed linkage analysis using SNP genotypes from 6 patients. They identified 2 large genomic regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15, shared by all affected patients. The most prominent candidate gene in these regions was TGFB3 on 14q24.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) found no mutation in TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1, or FBN2 in their patient. Exome sequencing then identified 2 de novo heterozygous changes, in CDH2 and TGFB3. The TGFB3 change was a c.1226G-A transition predicting a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution, and Sanger sequencing confirmed that it was de novo. In transfection assays, the mutant TGFB3 ligand did not activate a TGF-beta reporter. In Xenopus embryos, coexpression of the mutant with wildtype TGFB3 reduced SMAD2 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation without affecting BMP signaling, which the authors interpreted as a dominant-negative effect. The CDH2 change was a nonsense mutation, c.208C-T (Q70X). N-cadherin levels in the patient's fibroblasts did not differ significantly from controls, and the authors concluded that this variant was unlikely to drive the phenotype, although they could not exclude modifying effects.

Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide (LAP) domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and Loeys-Dietz syndrome features. Kuechler et al. (2015) reported this mutation as c.899G-A, predicting an arg300-to-gln (R300Q) substitution.

In the large Dutch family linked to chromosome 14q24, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) first excluded mutations in known thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection genes. Sanger sequencing of TGFB3 then identified a heterozygous splice site mutation, c.754+2T-C, which caused skipping of exon 4 and an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids. The mutation segregated with the phenotype. It was also present in a 17-year-old family member who had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic manifestations. The authors also screened TGFB3 in 470 probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection, by Sanger sequencing in 350 and exome sequencing in 120. They found heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 further probands. Four were missense mutations: D263H, R300W (found in 4 families), I322T, and L401P. One was a nonsense mutation (Y365X), and 2 were single-base deletions leading to frameshifts (L386Rfs*21 and N235Mfs*11). None of the mutations was present in control databases. The Y365X mutation occurred de novo, although that proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance that was inherited from the mother. ⚠︎

In a family with 3 affected members, Kuechler et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 of the affected individuals. They identified a heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and showed that it segregated with the phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) observed that TGFB3 mutations at arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula in their patients and in the patient reported by Matyas et al. (2014). They noted that TGFB3 mutations appeared to have opposing effects on height: some patients, including the patient with C409Y reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013), had short stature, whereas others had tall stature. Kuechler et al. (2015) suggested that mutations at codon arg300 presumably increase TGF-beta signaling, whereas C409Y is a loss-of-function mutation. They proposed that the short or tall stature seen with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of the mutations on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) concluded that the C409Y mutation is hypomorphic. They proposed that reduced TGF-beta signaling during early myogenesis and palatogenesis explained their patient's low muscle mass and bifid uvula, as distinct from the excess TGF-beta signaling seen in Marfan syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) hypothesized that TGFB3 mutations lead to loss of function. Their evidence was the truncating and in-frame splice site mutations and missense changes at residues important for integrin-mediated activation and processing. Nevertheless, immunohistochemical analysis of aortic tissue from a patient with the D263H mutation showed paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling: pSMAD2 and pERK were increased and TGFB1 mRNA was elevated. The authors noted that this contrasted with the decreased signaling reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013) in the Xenopus model.

### Clinical Management

A 3-year trial of losartan at doses up to 2.0 mg/kg/day, started at age 3 years in the patient reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013), produced no change in muscle strength or mass.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers, together with at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches. They suggested that further follow-up be guided by the initial findings and family history, and that imaging of the brain vessels might be indicated.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.Cys409Tyr]

In a 9-year-old girl with LDS5, Rienhoff et al. (2013) used exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene, predicting a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. The affected cysteine is conserved across TGF-beta family ligands and forms part of the 'cysteine knot.' The patient had distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, low muscle mass, and growth retardation, without vascular disease. The mutant ligand did not activate TGF-beta reporter signaling. When coexpressed with wildtype TGFB3 in Xenopus embryos, it reduced SMAD2 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation but did not affect BMP signaling.

**.0002 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLN [c.899G>A; p.Arg300Gln]

Matyas et al. (2014) identified a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and Loeys-Dietz syndrome features. Kuechler et al. (2015) reported the mutation as c.899G-A, predicting an arg300-to-gln (R300Q) substitution, and described the patient as a child with overgrowth.

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, IVS4DS, T-C, +2 [c.754+2T>C; p.Glu216_Lys251del]

In a large Dutch family with LDS5 that mapped to chromosome 14q24, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.754+2T-C mutation in the canonical donor splice site of exon 4 of the TGFB3 gene. The family had aortic aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta, and mitral valve disease. In 2 patients, cDNA sequencing confirmed skipping of exon 4, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 108 nucleotides and a predicted deletion of 36 amino acids (glu216_lys251del). The mutation segregated with the phenotype and was absent from variant databases. ⚠︎

**.0004 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ASP263HIS [p.Asp263His]

In a family with LDS5 (family 3), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous asp263-to-his (D263H) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The mutation disrupts the RGD integrin-binding motif of the latency-associated peptide domain. The aortic wall of the proband, who had an aortic dissection, showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition, together with increased pSMAD2, increased pERK, and elevated TGFB1 mRNA. This proband had short stature and received growth hormone therapy during puberty. ⚠︎

**.0005 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300TRP [p.Arg300Trp]

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous arg300-to-trp (R300W) mutation in the TGFB3 gene in 4 families with LDS5 (families 2, 4, 5, and 11). The mutation affects the last residue of the latency-associated peptide domain, disrupting the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif, and was absent from control databases. In their patients, mutations at arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

**.0006 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, TYR365TER [p.Tyr365*]

In a proband with LDS5 (family 6), Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a de novo heterozygous tyr365-to-ter (Y365X) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T) inherited from the mother, who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement. ⚠︎

**.0007 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLY [c.898C>G; p.Arg300Gly]

In a father and his 2 children with LDS5, Kuechler et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene by whole-exome sequencing, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. All 3 had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula, without cardiac, aortic, or eye involvement. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and its segregation with the phenotype. ⚠︎
