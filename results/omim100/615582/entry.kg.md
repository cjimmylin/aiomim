### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5), also known as Rienhoff syndrome, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene (190230) on chromosome 14q24.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is an autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder whose features overlap with Marfan syndrome and Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Reported features include hypertelorism, bifid uvula or cleft palate, joint hypermobility, arachnodactyly, pectus deformity and kyphoscoliosis, together with aortic aneurysm and dissection and mitral valve disease (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015). Clinical variability is striking both within and between families. Some patients have tall stature, while others have growth retardation and low muscle mass (Rienhoff et al., 2013), and some affected families have no cardiovascular involvement (Kuechler et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) reported a 9-year-old European American girl whose parents were healthy and nonconsanguineous. At birth she had distal arthrogryposis, with contractures of the right hand (most severe in the 3rd and 4th fingers) and of all toes. She also had a midline facial nevus flammeus and mild hypotonia. She failed to thrive and her gross motor development was delayed. At 17 months she had hypertelorism, prominent eyes, blue sclerae, a bifid uvula with an intact hard palate, retrognathia, mild pectus excavatum, pes planus and hyperextensible large joints. She also had reduced muscle bulk with low tone and diminished reflexes, and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. She walked at 24 months with a waddling gait and a positive Gowers sign, and her neurocognitive development was normal. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. Her growth retardation persisted, and at age 7 she weighed below the 1st centile. Yearly echocardiograms from 18 months showed no cardiac defect, and her aortic root dimensions were normal (z score 0.39 at age 6.5 years). A quadriceps biopsy at age 7 showed only mild focal type 1 fiber disproportion consistent with disuse, with no dystrophic or inflammatory changes.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) studied a large Dutch family (family 1) with an autosomal dominant disorder whose features overlapped with Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. Seven members, aged 40 to 68 years, had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta. Three died of aortic dissection and rupture. Four had mitral valve abnormalities ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation. Other features were subtle craniofacial changes (long face, high-arched palate, retrognathia), pectus deformity, scoliosis, velvety skin, varices and hiatal hernia. Autoimmune features included HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease and celiac disease.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) summarized 43 patients (23 male, 20 female) from 11 families with TGFB3 mutations, whose features overlapped with Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Four patients had type A dissection (median age 51 years) and 6 had type B dissection (median age 44.5 years). Other findings were aortic aneurysm, mitral valve disease, septal defects, hypertelorism, bifid uvula, cleft palate, cervical spine instability, clubfoot, tall stature, arachnodactyly, pectus deformity, kyphoscoliosis, joint hypermobility and hernia. The authors observed no early dissection, no dissection at small aortic size, no striking arterial tortuosity and no ectopia lentis. Clinical variability was striking both within and between families.

Kuechler et al. (2015) described a family in which a father and his 2 children had features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. These included tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism and bifid uvula. None of the 3 had cardiac involvement, aortic dilation or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) described autosomal dominant transmission of the disorder in a large Dutch family, and Kuechler et al. (2015) found a heterozygous TGFB3 mutation that segregated with the disorder in a father and his 2 children. Rienhoff et al. (2013) and Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) also observed de novo heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in isolated patients.

### Mapping

Sequencing had excluded all known thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (TAAD) genes in the Dutch family 1. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) then carried out SNP-based linkage analysis in 6 patients from this family. All affected patients shared 2 large genomic regions, on chromosomes 14 and 15, and the TGFB3 gene on 14q24 was the leading candidate in these regions.

### Molecular Genetics

Rienhoff et al. (2013) studied a girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, hypertelorism, low muscle mass and growth retardation. Exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variant was de novo, and it was not present in dbSNP130, the EVS database or 1000 Genomes. No mutations were found in TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1 or FBN2. In functional studies, secreted mutant TGFB3 failed to activate a TGF-beta reporter. In Xenopus embryos, co-injection of mutant and wildtype RNA reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signaling, consistent with a dominant-negative effect. The same patient carried a de novo heterozygous nonsense variant in CDH2 (c.208C-T, Q70X). N-cadherin levels in her fibroblasts were normal, and Cdh2-null heterozygous mice are normal, so the authors concluded that this variant was unlikely to drive the phenotype. They could not exclude modifying effects.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide (LAP) domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found a heterozygous splice donor mutation in intron 4 of the TGFB3 gene (c.754+2T-C) in affected members of the Dutch family 1. The mutation segregated with the phenotype and was absent from variant databases. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) then screened 470 additional probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic TAAD, 350 by Sanger sequencing and 120 by exome sequencing. They found heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 more probands: 4 different missense mutations, 1 nonsense mutation and 2 single-base deletions causing frameshifts. None of the mutations was present in controls in EVS, 1000 Genomes or Genome of the Netherlands. The proband of family 6 had a de novo nonsense mutation (Y365X). This proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T), inherited from a mother who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement; the authors considered its contribution unclear.

Kuechler et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 2 members of a family with a disorder overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes. They identified a heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, which segregated with the phenotype in all 3 affected family members.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that TGFB3 mutations appeared to have opposing effects on height. One patient with the D263H mutation had short stature and received growth hormone, whereas several other patients had tall stature. Mutations affecting arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

Kuechler et al. (2015) noted that a heterozygous loss-of-function TGFB3 mutation (C409Y) had been reported in an individual with growth retardation. In contrast, mutations at codon arg300, which presumably increase TGF-beta signaling, were associated with tall stature or overgrowth. The authors suggested that short or tall stature in patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) concluded that the C409Y mutation is hypomorphic and that reduced TGF-beta signaling likely accounts for the phenotype. They suggested that TGFB3 deficiency during early myogenesis could cause hypomyoplasia, possibly through incomplete epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition of the dermomyotome. This would clinically mimic, but be distinct from, the myopathy caused by excess TGF-beta signaling in Marfan syndrome. They concluded that normal muscle mass and complete palatal fusion require a minimum level of TGFB3 signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found 3 truncating mutations and an in-frame splice site mutation, and on this basis hypothesized that TGFB3 mutations cause loss of function. They examined the dissected aortic wall of a patient with the D263H mutation and found elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition. Immunohistochemistry showed paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling, with increased pSMAD2 (canonical) and pERK (noncanonical) and elevated TGFB1 mRNA, similar to findings in TGFB2-deficient aortas. Pathology reports from 2 patients in family 1 also described extensive elastic fiber fragmentation and aortic medial degeneration. The authors proposed that the young age of previously reported patients (8 and 10.5 years) might explain why those patients lacked obvious cardiovascular disease.

### Clinical Management

Rienhoff et al. (2013) treated their patient with losartan for 3 years, starting at age 3, at doses up to 2.0 mg/kg/day. Her muscle strength and mass did not change. The authors took this as further evidence that her disease mechanism differs from that of disorders with enhanced TGF-beta signaling.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers. They also recommended at least 1 baseline imaging study of the complete aorta and its side branches, and added that imaging of the brain vessels might be indicated.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.C409Y]

Rienhoff et al. (2013) used exome sequencing to study a European American girl with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, hypertelorism, low muscle mass and growth retardation. They identified a de novo heterozygous c.1226G-A transition in the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution. Cys409 is the penultimate cysteine of the cysteine knot and is conserved across TGF-beta family ligands. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variant was de novo, and it was absent from dbSNP130, the EVS database and 1000 Genomes. Secreted mutant TGFB3 failed to activate a TGF-beta reporter in cultured cells. In Xenopus embryos, co-injection of mutant and wildtype RNA at a 1:1 ratio reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals to about 40% and 60% of wildtype levels, respectively, consistent with a dominant-negative effect. The mutant had no effect on BMP/SMAD1 signaling. The authors concluded that the mutation is hypomorphic.

**.0002 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, IVS4DS, T-C, +2 [c.754+2T>C; c.p.glu216_lys251del; p.E216]

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found a heterozygous splice donor mutation in intron 4 of the TGFB3 gene (c.754+2T-C) in affected members of a large Dutch family (family 1) with aortic aneurysms and dissections. The mutation segregated with the phenotype and was absent from variant databases. Analysis of cDNA from 2 patients showed skipping of exon 4, giving an in-frame deletion of 108 nucleotides and a predicted loss of 36 amino acids (Glu216_Lys251del). The mutation was also present in a 17-year-old who had no documented cardiovascular features but had mild systemic findings.

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ASP263HIS [p.D263H]

In family 3, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous asp263-to-his (D263H) mutation in exon 5 of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation changes a highly conserved residue of the latency-associated peptide domain and disrupts the RGD motif needed for integrin binding. In a homology model, asp263 lies in a surface loop that is accessible to integrins. The dissected aortic wall of a patient carrying this mutation showed elastic fiber fragmentation and paradoxically increased TGF-beta signaling, with increased pSMAD2 and pERK. One patient with this mutation had short stature and received growth hormone.

**.0004 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300TRP [p.R300W]

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found a heterozygous arg300-to-trp (R300W) mutation in the TGFB3 gene in 4 families (families 2, 4, 5 and 11). The mutation affects the last amino acid of the latency-associated peptide domain and disrupts the last residue of the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif. Homology modeling predicted that ionic interactions would be lost and steric rearrangements induced. Mutations at arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula.

**.0005 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, TYR365TER [p.Y365*]

In the proband of family 6, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a de novo heterozygous tyr365-to-ter (Y365X) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The proband also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance (A250T), inherited from a mother who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement. The authors considered the contribution of the SMAD3 variant unclear.

**.0006 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, 1-BP DEL, LEU386ARGFSTER21 [p.L386R]

In family 7, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous single-base deletion in the TGFB3 gene that causes a frameshift and premature stop codon (Leu386ArgfsTer21). The authors predicted that it leads to nonsense-mediated decay or a truncated protein that probably loses cytokine activity.

**.0007 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, LEU401PRO [p.L401P]

In family 8, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous leu401-to-pro (L401P) mutation in the TGFB3 gene, at a highly conserved residue in the active cytokine region. The substitution was predicted to change hydrophobic interactions with residues of both the latency-associated peptide and TGFB3 domains.

**.0009 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ILE322THR [p.I322T]

In family 10, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous ile322-to-thr (I322T) mutation in the TGFB3 gene, at a highly conserved residue in the active cytokine region. A homology model predicted that the change would alter the position of arg325, a residue important for binding to TGFBR2, and disrupt hydrophobic contacts with the latency-associated peptide domain.

**.0010 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLY [c.898C>G; p.R300G]

Kuechler et al. (2015) studied a father and his 2 children with tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism and bifid uvula, without cardiac, aortic or eye involvement. Whole-exome sequencing in 2 of the affected individuals identified a heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, which segregated with the phenotype. The authors noted that a different mutation at the same codon (R300Q) had previously been reported in a child with overgrowth. They suggested that arg300 mutations presumably increase TGF-beta signaling.
