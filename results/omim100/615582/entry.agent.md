### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 (LDS5) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TGFB3 gene on chromosome 14q24. ⚠︎

### Description

Loeys-Dietz syndrome-5 is a syndromic form of aortic aneurysm and dissection, with cardiovascular, craniofacial, cutaneous, and skeletal anomalies that overlap with Loeys-Dietz, Shprintzen-Goldberg, and Marfan syndromes (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015). Features include thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysm and dissection, mitral valve disease, cleft palate, bifid uvula, hypertelorism, skeletal overgrowth, cervical spine instability, and clubfoot, with marked intra- and interfamilial variability (Bertoli-Avella et al., 2015). Some patients with TGFB3 mutations have had short stature and others tall stature (Rienhoff et al., 2013; Kuechler et al., 2015).

### Nomenclature

Kuechler et al. (2015) referred to the disorder associated with heterozygous TGFB3 mutations as an emerging connective tissue disorder tentatively called Rienhoff syndrome, after the first report by Rienhoff et al. (2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Rienhoff et al. (2013) described a 9-year-old European-American girl, born to nonconsanguineous healthy parents, with distal arthrogryposis, hypotonia, bifid uvula, and a failure of normal postnatal muscle development. At birth she had contractures of the right hand, most severe in the third and fourth fingers, and of all toes, a midline facial nevus flammeus, and mild hypotonia; birth weight was 2.9 kg (5th centile). She was evaluated for failure to thrive at 3 months. At 17 months she weighed 7.5 kg (less than 1st centile) and was 76 cm tall (5th centile), and had bilateral pes planus, mild pectus excavatum, hyperextensibility of multiple large joints, mild retrognathia, prominent eyes, hypertelorism, blue sclerae, and a bifid uvula with intact hard palate. Muscle bulk was decreased in all appendicular and axial muscles, with low tone, diminished reflexes, and markedly reduced subcutaneous fat. She stood independently with a positive Gowers sign at 20 months and walked at 24 months with a 'hip waddle' gait; neurocognitive development was appropriate for age. Radiographs showed bilateral coxa valga. A quadriceps biopsy at age 7 years showed type 1 fiber predominance with mild, focal type 1 fiber disproportion consistent with disuse. Yearly echocardiograms from 18 months showed no cardiac defect, and at 6.5 years the aortic root measured 1.72 cm (z score 0.39). The patient did not meet diagnostic criteria for Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, or Beals-Hecht syndrome, differing by significant growth retardation, absence of cardiovascular findings, and distinct muscle histopathology.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a patient with overgrowth and features of Loeys-Dietz syndrome. Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that this patient was 10.5 years old and had tall stature.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) reported 43 patients (23 male, 20 female; median age 34 years, range 3 to 74) from 11 families with syndromic aortic aneurysms. In a large Dutch family (family 1) with autosomal dominant inheritance, 7 members aged 40 to 68 years had aneurysms and dissections, mainly of the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta; 3 died from aortic dissection and rupture. Four members had mitral valve abnormalities ranging from mild prolapse to severe regurgitation requiring surgery. Craniofacial abnormalities were subtle, including long face, high-arched palate, and retrognathia; pectus deformity and scoliosis were frequent, and other findings included velvety skin, varices, hiatal hernia, and autoimmune features such as HLA-B27-positive spondyloarthritis, Graves disease, and celiac disease. In the 10 additional families, vascular involvement ranged from no cardiovascular abnormalities at age 64 to type A dissection (median age 51 years, range 40 to 80) or type B dissection (median age 44.5 years, range 30 to 57), abdominal aortic dissection, and death from cerebral aneurysm dissection at age 55. Other cardiovascular features included mitral valve disease, persistent foramen ovale, and atrial or ventricular septal defects. No early arterial dissection, dissection at small aortic dimension, or striking arterial tortuosity was observed. Among the 43 patients, 12 had tall stature, 16 arachnodactyly, 14 hypertelorism, 11 bifid uvula, and 5 cleft palate; cervical spine instability, clubfoot, dolichocephaly, high-arched palate, retrognathia, joint hypermobility, pectus deformity, kyphoscoliosis, and inguinal hernia were also observed. No ectopia lentis was found, and early-onset osteoarthritis was reported in 2 individuals. One patient had short stature and received growth hormone during puberty. The authors noted striking intra- and interfamilial variability. Histologic examination of dissected aortic wall from 1 patient showed elastic fiber fragmentation with increased collagen and proteoglycan deposition.

Kuechler et al. (2015) reported a family in which 2 children and their father had tall stature, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible joints, hypertelorism, and bifid uvula, without cardiac involvement, aortic dilation, or eye involvement.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance was observed in the large Dutch family reported by Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) and in the family reported by Kuechler et al. (2015), in which an affected father and 2 affected children carried the mutation. De novo mutations were found by Rienhoff et al. (2013) and Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015).

### Mapping

By linkage analysis using SNP genotypes from 6 patients of a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified 2 large genomic regions on chromosomes 14 and 15 shared by all affected patients; the most prominent candidate in these regions was the TGFB3 gene on 14q24.

### Molecular Genetics

In a girl with low muscle mass, growth retardation, distal arthrogryposis, and features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, Rienhoff et al. (2013) found no mutation in TGFB2, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, FBN1, or FBN2. Exome sequencing of the proband identified 2 heterozygous de novo changes: a nonsense mutation in CDH2 (c.208C-T, Q70X) and a missense mutation in TGFB3 (c.1226G-A, cys409-to-tyr; C409Y), the latter confirmed as de novo by Sanger sequencing. The concentration of N-cadherin in the patient's fibroblasts did not differ from controls, and the authors concluded that the CDH2 change was unlikely to be driving the phenotype, although modifying effects could not be excluded. The mutant TGFB3 ligand produced in HEK293T cells failed to activate a TGF-beta reporter, and in Xenopus embryos coinjection of mutant and wildtype TGFB3 RNA at a 1:1 ratio reduced pSMAD2 and pERK1/2 signals to approximately 40% and 60%, respectively, without affecting BMP-mediated SMAD1 phosphorylation. Rienhoff et al. (2013) concluded that the mutation was hypomorphic, acting in a dominant-negative manner.

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and Loeys-Dietz syndrome features. According to Kuechler et al. (2015), the mutation was c.899G-A (arg300-to-gln; R300Q).

In a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms in which sequencing of known TAAD genes had been negative, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous splice site mutation in TGFB3 (c.754+2T-C) that segregated with the phenotype; cDNA analysis showed skipping of exon 4, predicting an in-frame deletion of 36 amino acids (glu216-to-lys251del). The mutation was also present in a 17-year-old family member without documented cardiovascular features but with mild systemic manifestations. Screening of TGFB3 in 470 additional probands with syndromic or nonsyndromic thoracic aortic aneurysm (350 by Sanger sequencing and 120 by exome sequencing) identified heterozygous TGFB3 mutations in 10 further probands: 4 different missense mutations (D263H, R300W in 4 families, I322T, and L401P), a nonsense mutation (Y365X), and 2 single-base deletions causing frameshifts (L386Rfs*21 and N235Mfs*11). The Y365X mutation occurred de novo; the proband with this mutation also carried a SMAD3 variant of unknown significance that was present in his or her mother, who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement. All mutations were absent from control databases. On the basis of the truncating and in-frame splice site mutations, the authors hypothesized that the mutations result in loss of function of TGFB3.

By whole-exome sequencing in 2 of 3 affected members of a family with features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, Kuechler et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.898C-G mutation in the TGFB3 gene, predicting an arg300-to-gly (R300G) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation and its segregation with the phenotype.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) noted that TGFB3 mutations appeared to have opposing effects on height: a patient with the D263H mutation had short stature and the patient of Rienhoff et al. (2013) with C409Y had short stature (5th percentile), whereas several patients in their study and the patient of Matyas et al. (2014) had tall stature. Mutations affecting arg300 were associated with cleft palate and/or bifid uvula in their patients and in the patient of Matyas et al. (2014).

Kuechler et al. (2015) proposed that mutations at codon arg300 lead to increased TGF-beta signaling, in contrast to the loss-of-function C409Y mutation in a patient with growth retardation, suggesting that the short or tall stature in patients with TGFB3 mutations may result from opposing effects of mutations on TGF-beta signaling.

### Pathogenesis

Rienhoff et al. (2013) concluded from expression studies that the C409Y mutant TGFB3 ligand decreased canonical and noncanonical TGF-beta signaling, and proposed that the phenotype resulted from a hypomorphic allele. In contrast, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) found evidence of paradoxically enhanced TGF-beta signaling in aortic wall tissue from a patient with the D263H mutation, with increased pSMAD2 and pERK staining and elevated TGFB1 mRNA, similar to findings in aortic walls with mutations in TGFBR1/2, SMAD3, and TGFB2.

### Clinical Management

In the patient reported by Rienhoff et al. (2013), a 3-year trial of losartan beginning at age 3 years, at doses up to 2.0 mg/kg/day, produced no change in muscle strength or mass.

Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) recommended yearly echocardiographic evaluation of the aortic root in all TGFB3 mutation carriers, complemented with at least 1 baseline imaging of the complete aorta and side branches, and suggested that imaging of the brain vessels might be indicated depending on family history.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, CYS409TYR [c.1226G>A; p.Cys409Tyr]

In a 9-year-old European-American girl with low muscle mass, growth retardation, distal arthrogryposis, bifid uvula, and features overlapping Marfan and Loeys-Dietz syndromes, Rienhoff et al. (2013) identified a heterozygous de novo c.1226G-A transition in the last exon of the TGFB3 gene, resulting in a cys409-to-tyr (C409Y) substitution at the penultimate cysteine, which is conserved in all TGF-beta family ligands and forms part of the cysteine knot. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and confirmed as de novo by Sanger sequencing. The mutant ligand had no apparent signaling activity in a reporter assay, and in Xenopus embryos it reduced TGF-beta signaling when coexpressed with wildtype TGFB3.

**.0002 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300GLN [c.899G>A; p.Arg300Gln]

Matyas et al. (2014) reported a de novo mutation in the latency-associated peptide domain of TGFB3 in a patient with overgrowth and Loeys-Dietz syndrome features; Kuechler et al. (2015) cited this mutation as c.899G-A, resulting in an arg300-to-gln (R300Q) substitution, in a child with overgrowth.

**.0003 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, IVS4DS, T-C, +2 [c.754+2T>C; p.Glu216_Lys251del]

In affected members of a large Dutch family with syndromic aortic aneurysms and dissections, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous c.754+2T-C mutation affecting the canonical donor splice site of exon 4 of the TGFB3 gene. The mutation was found by linkage analysis followed by Sanger sequencing, segregated with the phenotype, and was absent from variant databases. cDNA sequencing in 2 patients confirmed skipping of exon 4, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 108 nucleotides and predicting deletion of 36 amino acids (glu216-to-lys251del).

**.0004 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ASP263HIS [p.Asp263His]

In a family (family 3) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous asp263-to-his (D263H) mutation in the TGFB3 gene, disrupting the RGD integrin-binding motif of the latency-associated peptide domain. The proband (3-III:1) had aortic dissection requiring surgery and short stature treated with growth hormone; aortic wall tissue showed elastic fiber fragmentation and evidence of increased TGF-beta signaling.

**.0005 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ARG300TRP [p.Arg300Trp]

In 4 families (families 2, 4, 5, and 11) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous arg300-to-trp (R300W) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. Arg300 is the last amino acid of the latency-associated peptide domain and the last residue of the RKKR furin cleavage recognition motif.

**.0006 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, ILE322THR [p.Ile322Thr]

In a family (family 10) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous ile322-to-thr (I322T) mutation in the TGFB3 gene, affecting a highly conserved residue in the region of the active cytokine; modeling predicted altered positioning of arg325, which is important for binding to TGFBR2.

**.0007 LOEYS-DIETZ SYNDROME 5**  
TGFB3, TYR365TER [p.Tyr365*]

In the proband of a family (family 6) with syndromic aortic aneurysm, Bertoli-Avella et al. (2015) identified a de novo heterozygous tyr365-to-ter (Y365X) mutation in the TGFB3 gene. The proband also carried a SMAD3 variant (A250T) of unknown significance that was present in the proband's mother, who had variable connective tissue findings and mild cardiovascular involvement.

The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and segregated with the phenotype.
