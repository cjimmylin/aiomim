### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A (JEB5A) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding integrin beta-4 (ITGB4). ⚠︎

### Description

Intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A is a mucocutaneous blistering disorder caused by mutation in the ITGB4 gene, which encodes the beta-4 subunit of the hemidesmosomal integrin alpha-6/beta-4. In contrast to the usually fatal form of epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia, affected individuals have milder skin involvement and survive beyond infancy (Mellerio et al., 1998; Jonkman et al., 2002). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mellerio et al. (1998) reported 2 unrelated patients with junctional epidermolysis bullosa associated with pyloric atresia who, unlike the usual course of the disease, which is fatal within the first weeks or months of life even after surgical correction of the intestinal obstruction, survived into early childhood with mild cutaneous involvement.

Jonkman et al. (2002) reported a 49-year-old woman who had had mild blistering of the hands and feet from birth, nail dystrophy with onychogryposis, and enamel hypoplasia. She had no alopecia and no history of pyloric atresia. Electron microscopy and antigen mapping of a skin blister showed an intraepidermal level of separation, low in the basal keratinocytes through the attachment plaque of the hemidesmosome. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed absent binding of monoclonal antibody 450-11A, directed against the third fibronectin type III repeat in the intracellular domain of integrin beta-4, and reduced binding of antibodies recognizing epitopes at the amino- and carboxy-terminal ends of the polypeptide. The authors noted that the phenotype had predominant features of epidermolysis bullosa simplex.

### Inheritance

Junctional epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia was described as an autosomal recessive disorder by Mellerio et al. (1998), who found compound heterozygous ITGB4 mutations in mildly affected patients. Inoue et al. (2000) reported a homozygous ITGB4 missense mutation in non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 unrelated patients with junctional epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia and mild disease, Mellerio et al. (1998) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the ITGB4 gene. The first patient was compound heterozygous for a splice site mutation in exon 30 (3793+1G-A) and a nonsense mutation in exon 36 (W1478X); the second was compound heterozygous for a missense mutation in exon 3 (C38R) and a 1-bp deletion in exon 36 (4776delG). Although the nonsense and deletion mutations were predicted to result in markedly reduced beta-4 integrin mRNA levels, the authors suggested that the missense or splice site mutation on the second allele might allow synthesis of some functional, albeit perturbed, beta-4 polypeptide.

Inoue et al. (2000) reported a homozygous missense mutation, G931D, in the cytoplasmic tail of beta-4 integrin that disrupted hemidesmosome assembly, in non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia. (Only the title of this report was available.)

In a 49-year-old woman with mild blistering and predominant features of epidermolysis bullosa simplex, Jonkman et al. (2002) identified a heterozygous 2-bp deletion (4733delCT) in the ITGB4 gene, resulting in in-frame skipping of exon 36 and a deduced 50-amino acid deletion (residues 1450-1499) within the third fibronectin type III repeat of the cytoplasmic domain. Immunoblot analysis showed a beta-4 polypeptide 5 kD shorter than normal. The patient was expected also to be heterozygous for a null allele, since no full-size protein was detected in vitro and the 450-11A epitope was absent in vivo.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

In patients with the lethal form of epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia, Pulkkinen et al. (1998) found ITGB4 mutations in 5 alleles of 3 patients, 3 of 4 of which were predicted to truncate the polypeptide. By contrast, in 2 patients with mild disease who survived into early childhood, Mellerio et al. (1998) found a missense or splice site mutation on one allele in combination with a nonsense or deletion mutation on the other, and suggested that the missense or splice site allele might permit synthesis of some functional beta-4 polypeptide. Jonkman et al. (2002) concluded that deletion of the third fibronectin type III repeat in the cytoplasmic domain of integrin beta-4, thought to interact with BP180/type XVII collagen, resulted in a mild phenotype with predominant features of epidermolysis bullosa simplex.
