### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A (JEB5A) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ITGB4 gene, which encodes integrin beta-4. ⚠︎

### Description

Intermediate junctional epidermolysis bullosa-5A (JEB5A) is an autosomal recessive mucocutaneous blistering disorder caused by defects in integrin beta-4, a component of the alpha-6/beta-4 integrin of the hemidesmosome. Unlike the usually fatal form of epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia, affected individuals survive beyond infancy and have relatively mild skin involvement. Pyloric atresia may be present (Mellerio et al., 1998) or absent (Inoue et al., 2000; Jonkman et al., 2002).

### Nomenclature

Has et al. (2020) published an updated consensus reclassification of inherited epidermolysis bullosa and other disorders with skin fragility, which reviewed clinical and genetic features, genotype-phenotype correlations, disease-modifying factors, and the natural history of EB.

### Clinical Features

Mellerio et al. (1998) reported 2 unrelated patients with junctional epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia (EB-PA). Although this disorder is usually fatal within the first weeks or months of life, even after surgical correction of the intestinal obstruction, both patients survived into early childhood and had mild cutaneous involvement.

Inoue et al. (2000) described non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia in a patient with a homozygous missense mutation in the cytoplasmic tail of integrin beta-4.

Jonkman et al. (2002) reported a 49-year-old woman who had had mild blistering of the hands and feet from birth, nail dystrophy with onychogryposis, and enamel hypoplasia. She did not have alopecia and had no history of pyloric atresia. The clinical picture was predominantly that of epidermolysis bullosa simplex. Electron microscopy and antigen mapping of a skin blister showed intraepidermal separation low in the basal keratinocytes, through the attachment plaque of the hemidesmosome. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed absent binding of monoclonal antibody 450-11A, which recognizes the third fibronectin type III repeat in the intracellular domain of integrin beta-4, and reduced binding of antibodies recognizing epitopes at the amino- and carboxy-terminal ends of the protein.

### Inheritance

Mellerio et al. (1998) described junctional epidermolysis bullosa with pyloric atresia as autosomal recessive and found compound heterozygous ITGB4 mutations in 2 mildly affected patients. Inoue et al. (2000) identified a homozygous ITGB4 mutation in a patient with non-Herlitz JEB, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 unrelated patients with mild EB-PA who survived into early childhood, Mellerio et al. (1998) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the ITGB4 gene. One patient carried a splice site mutation in exon 30 (3793+1G-A) and a nonsense mutation in exon 36 (W1478X). The other carried a missense mutation in exon 3 (C38R) and a 1-bp deletion in exon 36 (4776delG). The nonsense and deletion mutations were predicted to markedly reduce beta-4 integrin mRNA levels. The authors suggested that the missense or splice site mutation on the second allele might allow synthesis of some functional, though perturbed, beta-4 polypeptide.

In a patient with non-Herlitz junctional epidermolysis bullosa without pyloric atresia, Inoue et al. (2000) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the ITGB4 gene that changed glycine-931 to aspartic acid (G931D) in the cytoplasmic tail of integrin beta-4. They reported that this mutation disrupted hemidesmosome assembly.

In a 49-year-old woman with a mild phenotype that mainly resembled epidermolysis bullosa simplex, Jonkman et al. (2002) found a heterozygous 2-bp deletion in the ITGB4 gene (4733delCT). This deletion caused in-frame skipping of exon 36 and was predicted to delete 50 amino acids (residues 1450-1499) within the third fibronectin type III repeat of the cytoplasmic domain. Immunoblot analysis showed a beta-4 polypeptide that was 5 kD shorter than normal. Because no full-size protein was detected in vitro and the 450-11A epitope was absent in vivo, the patient was also expected to be heterozygous for a null allele. The authors concluded that deleting the third fibronectin type III repeat, which is thought to interact with BP180/type XVII collagen, is pathogenic.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Mellerio et al. (1998) suggested that, in their 2 mildly affected patients with EB-PA, having a missense or splice site mutation on 1 allele might allow production of some functional beta-4 polypeptide, which could explain the milder disease. They noted that identifying the molecular mechanisms in such cases might enable correlation between genotype and phenotype. ⚠︎
