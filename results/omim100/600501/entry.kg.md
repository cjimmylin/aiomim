### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ABCD syndrome (ABCDS) is caused by homozygous mutation in the EDNRB gene (131244) on chromosome 13q22.

### Description

ABCD syndrome is an autosomal recessive neural crest syndrome. Its name comes from its features: albinism, black lock, cell migration disorder of the neurocytes of the gut (Hirschsprung disease), and deafness (Gross et al., 1995; Verheij et al., 2002). Verheij et al. (2002) noted that the phenotype clearly overlaps with Shah-Waardenburg syndrome, which comprises sensorineural deafness, hypopigmentation of the skin, hair and irides, and Hirschsprung disease.

### Clinical Features

Gross et al. (1995) reported a macrosomic newborn girl born to consanguineous Kurdish parents. She had albinism, a black lock of hair at the right temporo-occipital region, retinal depigmentation, and bilateral deafness confirmed by brainstem auditory-evoked potentials. She also had a severe defect of intestinal innervation. Biopsy showed aganglionosis of the large intestine and a total absence of neurocytes and nerve fibers in the small intestine, indicating a total lack of sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation. She died of intestinal dysfunction at 5 weeks of age. She was the 14th child of her parents. Four of her sibs had the same syndrome and died a few days after birth, and the other 9 sibs were well. Gross et al. (1995) considered this combination of albinism, black lock, deafness, and total lack of intestinal neural innervation to be a previously unreported neural crest syndrome and named it ABCD syndrome.

### Inheritance

Gross et al. (1995) concluded that ABCD syndrome shows autosomal recessive inheritance. Their reasoning was that 5 children of consanguineous Kurdish parents were affected while 9 of their sibs were well. Verheij et al. (2002) supported recessive inheritance when they found a homozygous EDNRB mutation in the index patient of this family. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Verheij et al. (2002) found a homozygous EDNRB mutation in ABCD syndrome, and the phenotype overlaps with Shah-Waardenburg syndrome. For these reasons they suggested that ABCD syndrome is an expression of Shah-Waardenburg syndrome rather than a separate entity.

### Molecular Genetics

Verheij et al. (2002) screened the EDNRB gene in the index patient of the ABCD syndrome family reported by Gross et al. (1995). They found a homozygous nonsense mutation in exon 3, arg201-to-ter (R201X). ⚠︎
