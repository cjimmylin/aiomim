### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications (SENEBAC) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NRROS gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications (SENEBAC) is a severe neurodegenerative disorder. Early development is normal or mildly delayed. It is followed by refractory seizures beginning in infancy or the second year of life, and then by developmental regression. Neuroimaging shows intracranial calcification and white matter abnormalities, with progressive brain atrophy. Death has been reported in early childhood (Smith et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2020). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Smith et al. (2020) reported 3 patients from 2 families. Patients 1 and 2 were sisters, the first and third children of nonconsanguineous parents of Maori descent. Patient 3, a boy, was the first child of south Asian parents who were first cousins. All 3 children were born after a normal pregnancy and delivery, and their early development was unremarkable. In the second year of life they developed refractory seizures and neurodegeneration, and they died between 27 and 36 months of age. Metabolic testing, including testing for mitochondrial dysfunction, was noncontributory. Early neuroimaging showed fine calcification at the depths of the cerebral gyri, with normal white matter. On CT, there was widespread spot and linear calcification in the deep and subcortical white matter. As the disease progressed, repeat imaging showed more calcification, severe generalized atrophy with ventricular dilatation, and diffuse signal changes in the cerebral and cerebellar white matter.

Smith et al. (2020) performed a postmortem examination on patient 3 and found abnormalities only in the central nervous system. There was marked cerebral atrophy, with gray and white matter pathology throughout the cerebrum, cerebellum, and brainstem. Focal calcification was present in the neuropil, and there was widespread neuronal loss with reactive gliosis. The most striking finding was an accumulation of foamy macrophages throughout the white matter, mainly in a perivascular distribution. These cells immunoreacted with CD68, MHC class II, and p22phox, but not with CD163, IBA1, NRROS, CD3, P2Y12, or TMEM119. Myelin basic protein expression in the white matter was reduced, although U fibers were preserved. There were occasional axonal spheroids. Parenchymal microglia were markedly abnormal: IBA1-positive cells had disrupted distribution, density, and morphology, P2Y12 staining was lost, and TMEM119 immunoreactivity was weak. There was no indication of immune-mediated inflammation.

Dong et al. (2020) reported 6 individuals from 4 families. All had neurodegenerative disease with refractory epilepsy, developmental regression, and reduced white matter volume with delayed myelination. Development was initially normal or mildly delayed. Seizures began within the first year of life and were followed by developmental regression. Three individuals had intracranial calcification.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SENEBAC in the families reported by Smith et al. (2020) and Dong et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Smith et al. (2020) found homozygous mutations in affected children, one of whom was born to consanguineous parents, and Dong et al. (2020) found biallelic variants. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Smith et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify homozygous mutations in the NRROS gene in 3 patients from 2 families with SENEBAC. The 2 Maori sisters were homozygous for a c.1777C-T transition that resulted in a gln593-to-ter (Q593X) substitution. The south Asian boy was homozygous for a 1-bp deletion (c.1257del) that resulted in a frameshift and premature termination (Gly420AlafsTer14). Both mutations were predicted to truncate the protein. Q593X had not been recorded before, and c.1257del was found on only 1 of 251,438 alleles in gnomAD. No cellular material was available from any patient for functional studies.

Dong et al. (2020) identified biallelic NRROS variants in 6 individuals from 4 families with SENEBAC. The variants affected 2 significant functional domains of NRROS. In HEK293T cells, wildtype and mutant NRROS proteins both colocalized with latent TGF-beta-1 inside the cell on confocal microscopy. However, flow cytometry showed that the mutant proteins failed to anchor latent TGF-beta-1 at the cell surface, unlike wildtype NRROS. Wildtype NRROS rescued the ability of the mutants to present latent TGF-beta-1 at the cell surface. Dong et al. (2020) concluded that loss of NRROS function causes the disorder. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Smith et al. (2020) analyzed published transcriptomic datasets. In human brain, NRROS expression was enriched more than 50-fold in microglia compared with whole brain, and microglia were the major cell type expressing NRROS in the brain parenchyma. In mouse brain, CNS perivascular macrophages expressed significantly more Nrros than parenchymal microglia. In their patient, p22phox was markedly upregulated in perivascular macrophages. Smith et al. (2020) suggested that the absence of functional NRROS might increase p22phox-NOX2 binding and superoxide formation. They concluded that NRROS is indispensable for the early development and/or postnatal preservation of a homeostatic microglial population, and that loss of NRROS function represents a novel microgliopathy. ⚠︎

Dong et al. (2020) noted that NRROS associates with latent TGF-beta-1 and anchors it on the cell surface, which is required to activate TGF-beta-1 in macrophages and microglia. The phenotype of their patients overlapped with the human condition associated with TGF-beta-1 deficiency. They suggested that the disorder has features of a disordered response to inflammation.

### Animal Model

Wong et al. (2017) found that Nrros-deficient mice developed spontaneous neurological disorders, with defects in motor function, and died before 6 months of age. The mice had astrogliosis and lacked normal CD11b-hi/CD45-lo microglia, but showed no detectable demyelination or neuronal loss. Instead, myeloid cells resembling perivascular macrophages populated the central nervous system. Cx3cr1-driven deletion of Nrros showed that it has a crucial role in establishing microglia during early embryonic stages. Normal expression of Sall1 and other microglial genes required NRROS.
