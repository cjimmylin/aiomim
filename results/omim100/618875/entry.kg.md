### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications (SENEBAC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the NRROS gene (615322) on chromosome 3q29.

### Description

Early-onset seizures with neurodegeneration and brain calcifications is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder. After normal or mildly delayed early development, affected children develop refractory seizures in infancy or the second year of life, followed by developmental regression. Neuroimaging shows intracranial calcification, reduced white matter volume or white matter signal change, and progressive cerebral atrophy. Smith et al. (2020) reported death between 27 and 36 months of age and characterized the disorder as a microgliopathy. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Smith et al. (2020) described 3 children from 2 families who had a stereotyped phenotype. Two sisters (P1 and P2) in family F1442 were born to nonconsanguineous parents of Maori descent. A boy (P3) in family F2382 was born to first-cousin South Asian parents. Pregnancy, delivery and early development were normal. In the second year of life the children developed refractory seizures and neurodegeneration, and they died between 27 and 36 months of age. Metabolic testing, including tests for mitochondrial dysfunction, did not identify a cause. In all 3 patients, early neuroimaging showed fine calcification at the depths of the cerebral gyri and spot and linear calcification in the deep and subcortical white matter, with normal white matter signal. As the disease progressed, repeat imaging showed more calcification, rapidly progressive severe generalized cortical and subcortical atrophy with ventricular dilatation, and diffuse signal changes in the cerebral and cerebellar white matter. ⚠︎

At postmortem examination of P3, Smith et al. (2020) found abnormalities only in the central nervous system. These included cerebral atrophy, focal calcification in the neuropil, and widespread neuronal loss with reactive gliosis. The most striking finding was an accumulation of foamy macrophages throughout the cerebral and cerebellar white matter, mainly around blood vessels. These cells were positive for CD68, MHC class II and p22phox and negative for CD163, Iba1, NRROS, CD3, P2Y12 and TMEM119. Myelin basic protein was reduced, the U fibers were preserved, and there were occasional axonal spheroids. Parenchymal microglia were markedly abnormal, with loss of P2Y12 staining and weak TMEM119 staining.

Dong et al. (2020) reported 6 individuals from 4 families who had a neurodegenerative disease. All had refractory epilepsy, developmental regression, and reduced white matter volume with delayed myelination. Development was normal or mildly delayed at first. Seizures began within the first year of life and were followed by developmental regression. Intracranial calcification was found in 3 of the individuals.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of SENEBAC in the families reported by Smith et al. (2020) and Dong et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Smith et al. (2020) found homozygous NRROS mutations in affected individuals from 2 families, one of them consanguineous, and Dong et al. (2020) found biallelic NRROS variants in 4 families. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sisters of Maori descent (family F1442) with SENEBAC, Smith et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous nonsense mutation in the NRROS gene (Q593X). The mutation is predicted to truncate the protein and had not been reported previously. No patient cells were available for functional studies.

In a boy born to consanguineous South Asian parents (family F2382) with SENEBAC, Smith et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp deletion in the NRROS gene (c.1257del). The resulting frameshift is predicted to truncate the protein. The deletion was present on only 1 of 251,438 alleles in the gnomAD database.

In 6 individuals from 4 families with SENEBAC, Dong et al. (2020) identified biallelic variants in the NRROS gene. The abstract does not name the individual variants. The disease-causing variants affected 2 significant functional domains of NRROS. When expressed in HEK293T cells, wildtype and mutant NRROS both colocalized with latent TGF-beta-1 inside the cell on confocal microscopy. However, flow cytometry showed that the mutant proteins, unlike wildtype NRROS, failed to anchor latent TGF-beta-1 at the cell surface. Wildtype NRROS rescued this defect.

### Pathogenesis

Smith et al. (2020) analyzed published transcriptomic datasets. In human brain, NRROS expression was more than 50-fold higher in microglia than in whole brain, which suggested that microglia are the main cell type expressing NRROS in brain tissue. In mouse brain, Nrros was similarly enriched in microglia and was expressed even more highly in central nervous system perivascular macrophages. In P3, p22phox was markedly upregulated in perivascular macrophages. The authors suggested that, in the absence of functional NRROS, p22phox may bind more NOX2, potentially increasing superoxide formation. They found no sign of immune-mediated inflammation. They concluded that NRROS is essential for the early development and/or maintenance of a homeostatic microglial population and that loss of NRROS function causes a new human microgliopathy. ⚠︎

Dong et al. (2020) concluded that loss of NRROS function, through impaired delivery of active TGF-beta-1, causes a severe childhood-onset neurodegenerative condition. They suggested that the features of this condition point to a disordered response to inflammation. The patients' features were consistent with those of the Nrros knockout mouse and overlapped with the human condition associated with TGF-beta-1 deficiency.

### Animal Model

Wong et al. (2017) found that Nrros-deficient mice developed spontaneous neurological disorders, with motor defects and death before 6 months of age. Nrros -/- mice had astrogliosis and lacked normal CD11b-hi/CD45-lo microglia, but showed no detectable demyelination or neuronal loss. Instead, the CNS was populated by myeloid cells that resembled perivascular macrophages. Using Cx3cr1-driven deletion of Nrros, the authors showed that Nrros plays a crucial role in establishing microglia during early embryonic stages. They also found that NRROS, an endoplasmic reticulum transmembrane protein expressed in myeloid cells, is required for normal expression of Sall1 and other microglial genes important for microglial development and function.

Smith et al. (2020) noted that the features of their patients resembled those of Nrros-deficient mice, which show progressive neurological decline beginning at 2 to 3 months of age and die by 6 months. Smith et al. (2020) described the neuropathology of these mice as including neuronal loss, demyelination, axonal pathology, astrogliosis and foamy macrophages, all of which were seen in the patient they examined. The mice also lose homeostatic microglial gene expression, including P2ry12 and Tmem119.

In 2 sisters of Maori descent (family F1442) with SENEBAC, born to nonconsanguineous parents, Smith et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1777C-T transition in the NRROS gene, resulting in a gln593-to-ter (Q593X) substitution. The mutation is predicted to truncate the protein and had not been reported previously. No patient cells were available for functional studies.

**.0002 SEIZURES, EARLY-ONSET, WITH NEURODEGENERATION AND BRAIN CALCIFICATIONS**  
NRROS, 1-BP DEL, NT1257 [c.1257del; p.G420A]

In a boy (P3) with SENEBAC, born to first-cousin South Asian parents (family F2382), Smith et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp deletion (c.1257del) in the NRROS gene. The deletion causes a frameshift and premature termination (Gly420AlafsTer14) and is predicted to truncate the protein. It was present on only 1 of 251,438 alleles in gnomAD. At postmortem examination, the patient's parenchymal microglia were markedly abnormal, and perivascular foamy macrophages in his brain were negative for NRROS.
