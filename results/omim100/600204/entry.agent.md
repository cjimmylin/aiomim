### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple epiphyseal dysplasia-2 (EDM2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding the alpha-2 chain of type IX collagen (COL9A2) on chromosome 1p32.3-p33. ⚠︎

### Description

Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED) is a dominantly inherited chondrodysplasia characterized by mild short stature and early-onset osteoarthrosis (Briggs et al., 1994). The EDM2 subtype has been described as a form of MED in which the hips are not markedly affected at an early age, with childhood onset of pain and stiffness of the knees, ankles, elbows, and finger joints, free articular bodies, and early degenerative joint disease (Fiedler et al., 2002).

### Clinical Features

Versteylen et al. (1988) described 2 families that appeared to contain examples of multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. In both families there was a striking incidence of severe osteochondritis of the knees, which the authors suggested was caused by the fragmented and maldeveloped epiphyses characteristic of epiphyseal dysplasia. The molecular basis in these families was not reported in the abstract.

Briggs et al. (1994) studied a large family with MED, a disorder they characterized as a dominantly inherited chondrodysplasia with mild short stature and early-onset osteoarthrosis.

Fiedler et al. (2002) reported a family of 19 individuals over 4 generations in which 12 members were affected with a variant of multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. Beginning in childhood, the disease led to pain and stiffness of the knees, ankles, elbows, and finger joints. Some adult patients repeatedly suffered from free articular bodies resulting in locking of the joint, and affected individuals were prone to the development of early degenerative joint disease. Electron microscopy showed that the diameter of collagen fibrils from arthroscopically removed free articular bodies of 2 patients was not obviously different from that of normal articular cartilage. The authors noted that in this subtype of MED the hips are not markedly affected at an early age.

### Inheritance

Briggs et al. (1994) described MED as a dominantly inherited chondrodysplasia. The family reported by Fiedler et al. (2002), with 12 affected members over 4 generations and a COL9A2 mutation found in affected members, was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Warman et al. (1994) mapped the COL9A2 gene, which encodes the alpha-2 chain of type IX collagen, to human chromosome 1p32.3-p33 by fluorescence in situ hybridization, and mapped the murine Col9a2 gene to mouse chromosome 4.

In a large family with MED, Briggs et al. (1994) excluded linkage to the chromosome 19 interval to which pseudoachondroplasia and a form of MED had been linked, and identified an MED locus on the short arm of chromosome 1, in a region containing the COL9A2 gene.

### Heterogeneity

Briggs et al. (1994) noted that both mild and severe pseudoachondroplasia as well as a form of MED had been linked to the same interval on chromosome 19; this interval was excluded in their MED family, which mapped instead to chromosome 1p.

### Molecular Genetics

Muragaki et al. (1996) reported that a mutation in the COL9A2 gene causes multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (EDM2).

In a 4-generation family with EDM2, Fiedler et al. (2002) identified a G-C substitution at position -1 of the COL9A2 exon 3/intron 3 splicing region by mutation screening of candidate regions; the authors stated that it most probably alters splicing. Family screening by automated sequencing and BsaWI digestion of amplicons confirmed the substitution in 8 clinically affected family members and in 3 presymptomatic young children. Together with previous reports, the authors concluded that mutations leading to skipping of exon 3 within the COL3 domain of the alpha-2 chain of collagen IX may be relatively common in patients with this subtype of MED.

### Diagnosis

Fiedler et al. (2002) suggested that in patients with this subtype of MED and their families, mutation screening of the candidate regions may help to confirm the diagnosis and support lifestyle advice and genetic counseling.
