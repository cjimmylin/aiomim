### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple epiphyseal dysplasia-2 (EDM2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the COL9A2 gene (120260) on chromosome 1p34.2. ⚠︎

### Description

Briggs et al. (1994) described multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED) as a dominantly inherited chondrodysplasia with mild short stature and early-onset osteoarthrosis. They noted that some forms look clinically like mild pseudoachondroplasia (PSACH). In the EDM2 form, the hips are not markedly affected early in life (Fiedler et al., 2002).

### Clinical Features

Versteylen et al. (1988) described 2 families that appeared to have multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. Severe osteochondritis dissecans of the knees was strikingly common in both families. The authors suggested that the osteochondritis was caused by the fragmented, maldeveloped epiphyses typical of epiphyseal dysplasia.

Fiedler et al. (2002) described a 4-generation family of 19 individuals, 12 of whom had a variant of multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. From childhood, the disease caused pain and stiffness in the knees, ankles, elbows, and finger joints. Some adults had repeated free articular bodies that locked the joint, and affected individuals tended to develop early degenerative joint disease. The hips were not markedly affected at an early age. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Briggs et al. (1994) described multiple epiphyseal dysplasia as dominantly inherited. In the 4-generation family reported by Fiedler et al. (2002), the COL9A2 mutation was found in affected members.

### Mapping

Briggs et al. (1994) studied a large family with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia. Linkage studies with chromosome 19 markers excluded the interval previously linked to PSACH and to one form of MED. Using this family, they identified an MED locus, EDM2, on the short arm of chromosome 1. The region contains COL9A2, which encodes the alpha-2 chain of type IX collagen, a structural component of the cartilage extracellular matrix.

Warman et al. (1994) used fluorescence in situ hybridization to map the COL9A2 gene to human chromosome 1p32.3-p33. Using a single-strand conformational polymorphism in the murine Col9a2 gene, they mapped the mouse gene to chromosome 4.

### Heterogeneity

Briggs et al. (1994) excluded the chromosome 19 interval in a large MED family that mapped instead to chromosome 1p. That interval had previously been linked to mild and severe PSACH and to one form of MED. The finding showed that multiple epiphyseal dysplasia is genetically heterogeneous.

### Molecular Genetics

Muragaki et al. (1996) reported that a mutation in the COL9A2 gene, which encodes the alpha-2 chain of the fibril-associated collagen IX, causes multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (EDM2).

In the 4-generation family with EDM2 reported by Fiedler et al. (2002), screening of candidate regions identified a new G-to-C change at position -1 of the COL9A2 exon 3/intron 3 splice region. The authors thought this change most likely alters splicing. Sequencing and BsaWI digestion confirmed the change in 8 clinically affected family members and in 3 young children who did not yet have symptoms.

### Pathogenesis

Fiedler et al. (2002) examined free articular bodies, removed by arthroscopy from 2 patients, by electron microscopy. Collagen fibril diameter did not differ obviously from that of normal articular cartilage. Taking previous reports into account, they suggested that mutations causing skipping of COL9A2 exon 3, in the COL3 domain of the alpha-2 chain of type IX collagen, may be fairly common in the MED subtype in which the hips are not markedly affected early in life (EDM2).

### Diagnosis

Nakashima et al. (2005) reported that double-layered patella in multiple epiphyseal dysplasia is not exclusive to DTDST mutation. This radiographic sign alone therefore does not indicate the DTDST-related form of MED.
