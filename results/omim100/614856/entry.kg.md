### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene (112264) on chromosome 8p21.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII is an autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder. Its main feature is recurrent fractures, which in some patients begin at or before birth and are numerous enough to cause severe deformity and short stature (Martínez-Glez et al., 2012). Some patients have increased bone mineral density (Asharani et al., 2012; Fahiminiya et al., 2015), but others have normal bone density (Valencia et al., 2014). Some patients have mainly lower extremity fractures together with a mineralization defect in bone tissue (Fahiminiya et al., 2015).

### Clinical Features

Munns et al. (2004) described 3 unrelated children with what appeared to be a novel bone fragility disorder associated with an idiopathic mineralization defect. Recurrent lower limb fractures began when the children started bearing weight. The patients had none of the phenotypic, radiologic, or histomorphometric features classically associated with osteogenesis imperfecta, idiopathic juvenile osteoporosis, or mild autosomal dominant osteopetrosis. Radiographs showed increased metaphyseal trabeculation and normal to increased cortical thickness, with no evidence of rickets or osteomalacia. Areal and volumetric bone mineral density of the lumbar spine showed no major change. In 1 patient, peripheral quantitative CT of the radius showed increased cortical thickness and increased total and trabecular volumetric bone mineral density. On qualitative histology, iliac bone biopsies showed a paucity of the birefringent pattern of normal lamellar bone. Quantitative histomorphometry showed osteomalacia, with a prolonged mineralization lag time and a decreased mineral apposition rate. Munns et al. (2004) concluded that the disorder was a bone fragility disorder of moderate severity that tends to cause lower extremity fractures, and that it involves accumulation of osteoid caused by an intrinsic mineralization defect.

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) described a 15-year-old Egyptian girl (II-3) with severe autosomal recessive OI. Her parents were first cousins. A fracture of the left tibia was noted after birth, and she went on to have 10 to 15 spontaneous fractures per year. She had a triangular face, faint blue sclerae, and normal mental development, and she did not have dentinogenesis imperfecta. She had a large thorax and a large umbilical hernia containing abdominal contents. Other features were hypotonia, generalized hirsutism, severe bone deformities, arachnodactyly, joint hyperextensibility, kyphoscoliosis, pectus carinatum, platyspondyly, wormian bones, and severe short stature (length -11.5 SD). DEXA showed borderline osteoporosis. Her 5-year-old brother (II-6) had bilateral femoral bowing on prenatal ultrasound, and both femora were fractured at birth. He had about 15 fractures per year. His facial features were like his sister's, he had normal mentality, and he did not have dentinogenesis imperfecta. He also had joint hyperextensibility, arachnodactyly, a large umbilical hernia, wormian bones, scoliosis, platyspondyly, and osteoporosis. In both sibs, hearing and cardiovascular examinations were unremarkable and serum calcium and phosphate were normal, but alkaline phosphatase was slightly high. Both sibs were diagnosed with OI Sillence type III.

Asharani et al. (2012) studied a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures.

Valencia et al. (2014) described an additional patient with osteogenesis imperfecta who had normal bone density.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) studied 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder that they had described previously. The main features were lower extremity fractures and a mineralization defect in bone tissue that did not occur at the growth plate. Peripheral quantitative computed tomography showed elevated volumetric cortical bone mineral density in all 4 children.

Cho et al. (2015) described a Korean patient with osteogenesis imperfecta. The patient had an umbilical hernia, frequent fractures, markedly short stature, delayed motor development, and scoliosis. There was also dislocation of the radial head with a bowed radius and ulna.

### Biochemical Features

Munns et al. (2004) found no biochemical evidence of abnormal calcium or phosphate metabolism in their 3 patients.

### Inheritance

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) established autosomal recessive inheritance of OI13. Their 2 affected sibs, from a consanguineous Egyptian family, were homozygous for a BMP1 mutation, and both parents and both unaffected sibs were heterozygous carriers. Asharani et al. (2012), Valencia et al. (2014), Fahiminiya et al. (2015), and Cho et al. (2015) also reported patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous BMP1 mutations.

### Mapping

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) performed genome-wide homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Egyptian family with 2 affected and 2 unaffected sibs. The mapping excluded the known loci for autosomal recessive OI, and sequencing of COL1A1 and COL1A2 found no mutations. Two homozygous regions were shared only by the affected sibs: a 35.4-Mb region on chromosome 3 and a 10.27-Mb region on chromosome 8p. The 8p region contained the BMP1 gene.

### Heterogeneity

In an epidemiologic and genetic study of OI in Victoria, Australia, Sillence et al. (1979) concluded that at least 4 distinct syndromes were then being called OI. They described type III OI as progressively deforming. Two-thirds of these patients had fractures at birth, all were sporadic cases, and their sclerae were less intensely blue than in type I and became less blue with age. The mode of inheritance of type III was not resolved, but the authors considered it likely that this group was heterogeneous, with both dominant and recessive genotypes responsible.

### Molecular Genetics

Munns et al. (2004) found no type I collagen mutations in their 3 patients and stated that the pathogenetic basis of the disorder remained to be elucidated.

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) studied 2 sibs with OI type III from a consanguineous Egyptian family. In both sibs they identified a homozygous mutation in exon 6 of the BMP1 gene (c.747C-G; F249L). Both parents and both unaffected sibs were heterozygous. The variant was absent from 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnicity and from 884 European control chromosomes. In fibroblasts from both patients, procollagen I C-propeptidase activity was diminished, although some residual activity remained. A heterozygous sib's fibroblasts showed slight impairment. The patients' collagen was not overmodified, and BMP1/mTLD protein levels were normal. When overexpressed in NIH3T3 cells and in human primary fibroblasts, wildtype mTLD caused most pro-alpha-1(I) to be C-terminally processed, whereas the F249L mutant did not increase cleavage. Only the wildtype protein rescued processing in patient fibroblasts. The authors concluded that the mutation strongly reduces the proteolytic function of BMP1/mTLD.

Asharani et al. (2012) studied a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and recurrent fractures. Using whole-exome sequencing combined with filtering for homozygous stretches, they identified a homozygous mutation in the signal peptide of the BMP1 gene (c.34G-C). The mutation led to impaired secretion and to altered posttranslational modification.

In a patient with osteogenesis imperfecta and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) identified homozygosity for the recurrent c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene. Western blot analysis of the patient's dermal fibroblasts showed decreased protein levels of both alternatively spliced BMP1 products. Cleavage of the C-terminal propeptide of type I procollagen was abnormal.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 4 unrelated children with bone fragility. Three were homozygous for a BMP1 variant (c.*241T-C) that affects the polyadenylation signal of the transcript encoding the short isoform, BMP1-1. The fourth child was compound heterozygous for the c.*241T-C variant and an exon 15 variant (c.2107G-C) that affected splicing of both BMP1-1 and the long isoform, BMP1-3. Skin fibroblasts from the homozygous individuals had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein. Their fibroblast supernatant contained less free procollagen type I C-propeptide than controls, indicating impaired C-propeptide cleavage. The compound heterozygous patient's fibroblasts also showed impaired C-propeptide cleavage. The authors concluded that isolated lack of BMP1-1 causes bone fragility in children.

In a Korean patient with osteogenesis imperfecta, Cho et al. (2015) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous novel variants in the BMP1 gene. One was a c.808A-G transition resulting in a met270-to-val (M270V) substitution. The other was a c.1297G-T change that caused skipping of exon 10. A zebrafish assay showed that both variants were hypofunctional.

### Pathogenesis

Valencia et al. (2014) studied cultured fibroblasts from 2 patients: their own patient, who was homozygous for the BMP1 c.34G-C mutation, and a previously reported patient, who was homozygous for the c.747C-G mutation. Fluorescence and electron microscopy showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix of these cells. The authors concluded that BMP1 is essential for human type I collagen fibrillogenesis.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) examined iliac bone samples from their patients by histomorphometry and quantitative backscattered electron imaging. Mineralization began late at bone formation sites, but the mineralized matrix was hypermineralized.

### Clinical Management

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) reported that both affected sibs were started on cyclic intravenous bisphosphonate injections.

### Animal Model

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) noted that Bmp1-null mice die perinatally. At birth they have no gross skeletal abnormalities apart from reduced ossification of some skull bones, but they consistently show herniation of the gut. This feature is shared with the large umbilical hernias in their patients.

Asharani et al. (2012) characterized a zebrafish bone mutant with lesions in bmp1a, showing that BMP1 function in osteogenesis is conserved. They used genetic, biochemical, and histologic analyses of this mutant and compared it with a second, similar locus. These studies showed that Bmp1a is critically required in bone for the generation of mature collagen, acting downstream of osteoblast maturation.

In a zebrafish assay, Cho et al. (2015) showed that the BMP1 variants M270V and c.1297G-T (exon 10 skipping) were both hypofunctional.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 OSTEOGENESIS IMPERFECTA, TYPE XIII**  
BMP1, PHE249LEU [c.747C>G; p.F249L]

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) studied 2 sibs with OI type III from a consanguineous Egyptian family. In both sibs they identified a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in exon 6 of the BMP1 gene, resulting in a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution. Phe249 lies in the protease domain and is conserved in all astacin metalloproteases. Both parents and both unaffected sibs were heterozygous. The variant was absent from 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnicity and from 884 European control chromosomes. Patient fibroblasts showed diminished procollagen I C-propeptidase activity, although some residual activity remained, and BMP1/mTLD protein levels were normal. In overexpression studies, the F249L mutant did not increase C-terminal cleavage of pro-alpha-1(I), and unlike wildtype mTLD, it did not rescue processing in patient fibroblasts.

Valencia et al. (2014) showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix of cultured fibroblasts from a previously reported patient who was homozygous for the c.747C-G mutation.
