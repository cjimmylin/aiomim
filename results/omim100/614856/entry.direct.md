### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII is an autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder with recurrent fractures. Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) described sibs with severe progressive bone deformity, short stature and large umbilical hernias, classified as OI type III. Other patients have bone mineral density that is normal or increased rather than reduced (Asharani et al., 2012; Valencia et al., 2014). Fahiminiya et al. (2015) described fractures mainly of the lower limbs with a bone mineralization defect. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Munns et al. (2004) described 3 unrelated children with an apparently novel bone fragility disorder associated with an idiopathic mineralization defect. Recurrent lower limb fractures began when the children started bearing weight. The patients had none of the phenotypic, radiologic or histomorphometric features of known bone fragility disorders such as osteogenesis imperfecta, idiopathic juvenile osteoporosis or mild autosomal dominant osteopetrosis. Radiographs showed increased metaphyseal trabeculation, normal to increased cortical thickness, and no evidence of rickets or osteomalacia. Areal and volumetric bone mineral density of the lumbar spine showed no major alteration. In 1 patient, peripheral quantitative computed tomography of the radius showed increased cortical thickness and increased total and trabecular volumetric bone mineral density. Iliac bone biopsies showed little of the birefringent pattern of normal lamellar bone. Histomorphometry showed osteomalacia, with a prolonged mineralization lag time and a decreased mineral apposition rate. There was no biochemical evidence of abnormal calcium or phosphate metabolism, and type I collagen mutation analysis was negative. Munns et al. (2004) concluded that this was a bone fragility disorder of moderate severity, with fractures mainly of the lower limbs and accumulation of osteoid due to an intrinsic mineralization defect.

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) studied 2 sibs with severe autosomal recessive OI born to Egyptian parents who were first cousins. The proband, a 15-year-old girl, had a left tibial fracture noted after a normal full-term vaginal delivery. Thereafter she had frequent spontaneous fractures of the upper and lower limbs, averaging 10 to 15 per year. She had a triangular face, broad forehead, wide palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, faint blue sclerae, long philtrum, thin lips and prominent ears. Gross motor development was delayed, and she could not stand unsupported. Mental development was normal, and she had no dentinogenesis imperfecta. Her thorax was relatively large and compressed the abdomen. She had a large umbilical hernia (5 cm wide and 7 cm long) containing abdominal contents. Other findings were generalized hypotonia, muscle wasting, nocturnal enuresis and generalized hirsutism. Her skin had normal stretchability, with no bruising or scars. She had severe generalized bone deformities with pain on touch, arachnodactyly, hyperextensible elbows, wrists and interphalangeal joints, limited knee movement, kyphoscoliosis and pectus carinatum. Radiographs showed deformed long bones with multiple fractures and callus formation and lack of bone modeling. The distal femoral metaphyses were wide, and the tibiae and fibulae were thin and serpentine. Radiographs also showed S-curve thoracolumbar scoliosis with platyspondyly, generalized decreased bone density, and wormian bones. At 13 years of age, her weight was -3.8 SD, length -11.5 SD and head circumference -3.0 SD. At 15 years, DEXA showed borderline osteoporosis (Z-scores -2.22 at the hip and -2.13 at the spine).

The proband's 5-year-old brother had bilateral femoral bowing on fetal ultrasound, and both femora were fractured at birth (Martínez-Glez et al., 2012). He had recurrent fractures at an average rate of 15 per year. His facial features resembled his sister's, and he had normal mentality and no dentinogenesis imperfecta. He could not stand unsupported. He had generalized bone deformities, hyperextensible joints, arachnodactyly, and a large umbilical hernia (4 cm wide and 5 cm long). Radiographs showed wormian bones, deformed long bones lacking modeling, fractures with callus formation, scoliosis, platyspondyly and a wide symphysis pubis. His weight was -3.7 SD and length -7.2 SD, with normal head circumference. DEXA showed borderline osteoporosis at the spine (Z-score -1.63) and osteoporosis at the left femur (Z-score -2.75). In both sibs, audiologic and cardiovascular examinations were unremarkable, serum calcium and phosphate were normal, and alkaline phosphatase was slightly high. Both were diagnosed with OI type III according to the Sillence classification (Sillence et al., 1979).

Asharani et al. (2012) studied a consanguineous family in which affected members had increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures.

Valencia et al. (2014) reported an additional patient with OI and normal bone density.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) studied 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder characterized mainly by lower extremity fractures and a defect of mineralization in bone tissue but not at the growth plate. They had previously published the clinical characteristics of this phenotype. Peripheral quantitative computed tomography showed elevated volumetric cortical bone mineral density in all 4 children. In iliac bone samples, histomorphometry and quantitative backscattered electron imaging showed delayed onset of mineralization at bone formation sites, but the mineralized matrix was hypermineralized. ⚠︎

Cho et al. (2015) reported a Korean patient with OI who had frequent fractures, an umbilical hernia and markedly short stature. Other features were delayed motor development, scoliosis, and dislocation of the radial head with bowing of the radius and ulna.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance of OI13 was shown by Martínez-Glez et al. (2012). In their family, affected sibs born to consanguineous parents were homozygous for a BMP1 mutation, and the parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) performed genomewide homozygosity mapping with SNP arrays in 2 affected Egyptian sibs and 2 unaffected sibs. The affected sibs were not homozygous at the CRTAP, PPIB, SERPINH1, SP7 or SERPINF1 loci, and only 1 of them was homozygous at the LEPRE1 and FKBP10 loci. The 2 affected sibs, but not the unaffected sibs, shared 2 large homozygous regions: a 35.4-Mb region around the centromere of chromosome 3 containing 151 genes, and a 10.27-Mb region on chromosome 8p containing 84 genes, including BMP1.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 Egyptian sibs with severe autosomal recessive OI born to first-cousin parents, Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) found a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in exon 6 of the BMP1 gene, predicting a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution. The variant was identified by sequencing BMP1, a candidate gene within a region of homozygosity on chromosome 8p. Phe249 lies in the protease domain and is conserved in all members of the astacin family of metalloproteases. The parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous. The variant was absent from 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin and 884 European control chromosomes, and COL1A1 and COL1A2 sequencing in 1 patient found no mutations. Patient fibroblasts showed reduced C-terminal processing of type I procollagen, with less pN-alpha-1(I) relative to pC-alpha-1(I) and less free C-terminal propeptide. Fibroblasts from a heterozygous sib showed slight impairment. In NIH3T3 cells and human primary fibroblasts, overexpression of wildtype mTLD (the longer BMP1 isoform) enhanced C-terminal processing of pro-alpha-1(I), whereas the mutant protein did not. Mutant protein levels in patient cells were not significantly reduced.

In affected members of a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures, Asharani et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing combined with filtering for homozygous stretches. It is located within the BMP1 signal peptide, and it impaired secretion and altered posttranslational modification of the protein.

In a patient with OI and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) identified the homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the BMP1 gene, which they described as recurrent. Patient dermal fibroblasts had decreased levels of both alternatively spliced BMP1 products and abnormal cleavage of the type I procollagen C-terminal propeptide.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing in 4 unrelated children with frequent fractures, mainly of the lower limbs, and a bone mineralization defect. Three were homozygous for a c.*241T-C variant in the BMP1 gene, which affects the polyadenylation signal of the transcript encoding the short isoform BMP1-1. Their skin fibroblasts had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein. The fourth child was compound heterozygous for the c.*241T-C variant and a c.2107G-C variant in exon 15 that affected splicing of both BMP1-1 and the long isoform BMP1-3. In fibroblasts with either the homozygous or the compound heterozygous genotype, the amount of free procollagen type I C-propeptide in the supernatant was lower than in controls, indicating impaired cleavage. Fahiminiya et al. (2015) concluded that isolated lack of BMP1-1 causes bone fragility in children.

In a Korean patient with OI, Cho et al. (2015) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous variants in the BMP1 gene. One was a c.808A-G transition predicting a met270-to-val (M270V) substitution; the other, c.1297G-T, caused skipping of exon 10. A zebrafish assay showed that both variants were hypofunctional.

### Pathogenesis

Valencia et al. (2014) studied cultured fibroblasts from 2 patients, one homozygous for the BMP1 c.34G-C mutation and one homozygous for the c.747C-G (F249L) mutation. Fluorescence and electron microscopy showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix. Valencia et al. (2014) concluded that BMP1 is essential for human type I collagen fibrillogenesis. ⚠︎

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) noted that their patients were more severely affected than patients with dominant mutations at the C-propeptide cleavage site of COL1A1 or COL1A2. They suggested that deficient BMP1/mTLD function would impair C-terminal processing of both procollagen I chains. Because the F249L mutation lies in the catalytic domain, they proposed that processing of other BMP1 substrates, such as type II and III procollagens and the lysyl oxidase zymogen, might also be compromised.

### Animal Model

Asharani et al. (2012) characterized a zebrafish bone mutant with lesions in bmp1a, showing that BMP1 function in osteogenesis is conserved across species. Genetic, biochemical and histologic analyses showed that Bmp1a is critically required in bone for the generation of mature collagen, acting downstream of osteoblast maturation.

Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) noted that Bmp1-null mice die perinatally and have no gross skeletal abnormalities at birth apart from reduced ossification of some skull bones. However, these mice consistently have herniation of the gut, which Martínez-Glez et al. (2012) compared with the large umbilical hernias in their patients.
