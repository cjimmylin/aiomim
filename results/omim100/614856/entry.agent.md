### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII (OI13) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the BMP1 gene (112264) on chromosome 8p21.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Osteogenesis imperfecta type XIII is an autosomal recessive bone fragility disorder characterized by recurrent fractures, which may begin at birth, and in severe cases by progressive deformity of the long bones and spine, short stature, and large umbilical hernia (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012; Cho et al., 2015). Bone mineral density has been reported as decreased (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012), normal (Valencia et al., 2014), or increased (Asharani et al., 2012; Fahiminiya et al., 2015). Impaired cleavage of the C-terminal propeptide of type I procollagen has been reported in patient fibroblasts (Martinez-Glez et al., 2012; Valencia et al., 2014; Fahiminiya et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Munns et al. (2004) described 3 unrelated children with an apparently novel bone fragility disorder associated with an idiopathic mineralization defect. Recurrent lower limb fractures started with weight bearing. The patients had none of the phenotypic, radiologic, or histomorphometric features classically associated with OI, idiopathic juvenile osteoporosis, or mild autosomal dominant osteopetrosis. Radiologically, there was increased metaphyseal trabeculation, normal to increased cortical thickness, and no evidence of rickets or osteomalacia. Areal and volumetric bone mineral density of the lumbar spine did not show any major alteration; peripheral quantitative CT of the radius in 1 patient showed elevated cortical thickness and elevated total and trabecular volumetric bone mineral density. Iliac bone biopsy specimens showed a paucity of the birefringent pattern of normal lamellar bone, and histomorphometry demonstrated osteomalacia with a prolonged mineralization lag time and decreased mineral apposition rate. There was no biochemical evidence of abnormal calcium or phosphate metabolism, and type I collagen mutation analysis was negative.

Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) studied 2 sibs, born of first-cousin Egyptian parents, with severe osteogenesis imperfecta and a large umbilical hernia. The proband, a 15-year-old girl, had a fracture of the left tibia noted after full-term vaginal delivery, followed by frequent spontaneous fractures at an average of 10 to 15 per year affecting the upper and lower limbs. She had triangular face, broad forehead, wide palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, faint blue sclerae, long philtrum, thin lips, and prominent ears. Gross motor development was delayed and she could not stand unsupported; mental development was normal and there was no dentinogenesis imperfecta. Her thorax was relatively large, compressing the abdomen, and she had a large umbilical hernia (5 cm wide and 7 cm long) with abdominal content, generalized hypotonia, muscle wasting, nocturnal enuresis, and generalized hirsutism. Skin had normal stretchability. Skeletal examination showed severe generalized deformities with pain on touch, deformed clavicles, bowed and angulated humerus, radius, and ulna, arachnodactyly, hyperextensibility of the elbow, wrist, and interphalangeal joints, bowed femora, severely angulated leg bones, limited knee movement, kyphoscoliosis, and pectus carinatum. Radiographs showed deformed long bones with multiple fractures and callus formation, lack of bone modeling with wide distal femoral metaphyses, serpentine thin tibiae and fibulae, S-curve thoracolumbar scoliosis with platyspondyly, generalized decreased bone density, and wormian bones. At 13 years of age, weight was -3.8 SD, length -11.5 SD, and head circumference -3.0 SD. DEXA at 15 years showed borderline osteoporosis at the hip and spine (Z-scores -2.22 and -2.13, respectively). Her 5-year-old brother had bilateral femoral bowing detected on fetal ultrasound and fractures of both femora at birth, with recurrent fractures at an average rate of 15 per year. He had similar facial features, no dentinogenesis imperfecta, normal mentality, inability to stand unsupported, generalized bone deformities, joint hyperextensibility, arachnodactyly, a relatively large thorax, and a large umbilical hernia (4 cm wide and 5 cm long). Radiographs showed wormian bones, deformed long bones with lack of modeling, fractures and callus formation, scoliosis, platyspondyly, and wide symphysis pubis. Weight was -3.7 SD and length -7.2 SD, with normal head circumference (-0.8 SD); DEXA at 5 years showed borderline osteoporosis at the spine (Z-score -1.63) and osteoporosis at the left femur (Z-score -2.75). Audiologic and cardiovascular examinations of both patients were unremarkable. Serum calcium and phosphate were normal, and alkaline phosphatase was slightly high. Both were diagnosed with OI Sillence type III and treated with cyclic intravenous bisphosphonate.

Asharani et al. (2012) studied a consanguineous family affected by increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures.

Valencia et al. (2014) described a patient with osteogenesis imperfecta with normal bone density.

Fahiminiya et al. (2015) studied 4 unrelated individuals with a bone fragility disorder in children, previously characterized clinically by the same group, that featured mainly lower extremity fractures and a mineralization defect in bone tissue but not at the growth plate level. Peripheral quantitative CT showed elevated volumetric cortical bone mineral density in all individuals. Histomorphometry and quantitative backscattered electron imaging of iliac bone samples indicated that the onset of mineralization at bone formation sites was delayed, but that the mineralized matrix was hypermineralized.

Cho et al. (2015) reported a Korean patient with OI who had an umbilical hernia, frequent fractures, markedly short stature, delayed motor development, scoliosis, and dislocation of the radial head with bowed radius and ulna.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of OI13 in the family reported by Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance: the affected sibs, born of consanguineous parents, were homozygous for a BMP1 mutation for which both parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous.

### Mapping

By genomewide homozygosity mapping using SNP arrays in a consanguineous Egyptian family with 2 sibs with severe OI, Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) excluded homozygosity at the CRTAP, PPIB, SERPINH1, SP7, and SERPINF1 loci in both patients and found that only 1 affected child was homozygous at LEPRE1 and FKBP10. They identified 2 large homozygous regions shared by the patients and absent in 2 unaffected sibs: a 35.4-Mb region around the centromere of chromosome 3 containing 151 genes, and a 10.27-Mb region on chromosome 8p containing 84 genes, including BMP1.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 sibs, born of consanguineous Egyptian parents, with severe OI and umbilical hernia, Martinez-Glez et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.747C-G transversion in exon 6 of the BMP1 gene, resulting in a phe249-to-leu (F249L) substitution at a residue within the protease domain that is conserved in all members of the astacin family of metalloproteases. The mutation was found by sequencing of BMP1, a candidate gene within a region of homozygosity on chromosome 8p, after sequencing of COL1A1 and COL1A2 in 1 patient revealed no mutations. Both parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous. The mutation was not found in 100 control chromosomes of the same ethnic origin or in 884 chromosomes from controls of European ethnicity. Patient fibroblasts showed decreased C-terminal processing of procollagen I, with a decrease of pN-alpha-1(I) relative to pC-alpha-1(I) and decreased free C-propeptide, although some residual activity remained; cells from a heterozygous sib showed slight impairment. Levels of BMP1 and mTLD protein in patient cell extracts were not significantly different from controls. Overexpression of wildtype mTLD in NIH3T3 cells resulted in C-terminal processing of most secreted pro-alpha-1(I), whereas overexpression of the mutant did not enhance processing; only wildtype mTLD rescued processing in patient fibroblasts.

By whole-exome sequencing with filtering for homozygous stretches in a consanguineous family with increased bone mineral density and multiple recurrent fractures, Asharani et al. (2012) identified a homozygous c.34G-C mutation in the signal peptide of BMP1. The mutation led to impaired secretion and altered posttranslational modification.

In a patient with OI and normal bone density, Valencia et al. (2014) identified homozygosity for the recurrent c.34G-C mutation in BMP1. Western blot analysis of the patient's dermal fibroblasts showed decreased protein levels of the 2 alternatively spliced BMP1 products and abnormal cleavage of the C-terminal propeptide of type I procollagen. Fluorescence and electron microscopy showed impaired assembly of type I collagen fibrils in the extracellular matrix of cultured fibroblasts from this patient and from a previously reported patient homozygous for the c.747C-G mutation.

By whole-exome sequencing in 4 unrelated children with a bone fragility disorder characterized mainly by lower extremity fractures and a mineralization defect, Fahiminiya et al. (2015) found that 3 were homozygous for a c.*241T-C variant in BMP1 affecting the polyadenylation signal of the transcript encoding the short isoform, BMP1-1. Skin fibroblasts from these individuals had low levels of BMP1-1 transcript and protein. The fourth individual was compound heterozygous for the c.*241T-C variant and a c.2107G-C variant in exon 15 that affected splicing of both BMP1-1 and the long isoform, BMP1-3. Both the homozygous and compound heterozygous genotypes were associated with impaired procollagen type I C-propeptide cleavage, with less free C-propeptide in fibroblast supernatants than in controls.

By whole-exome sequencing in a Korean patient with OI and umbilical hernia, Cho et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous variants in the BMP1 gene: c.808A-G, resulting in a met270-to-val (M270V) substitution, and c.1297G-T, which caused skipping of exon 10. A zebrafish assay demonstrated that both variants were hypofunctional.

### Animal Model

Asharani et al. (2012) characterized a zebrafish bone mutant harboring lesions in bmp1a, demonstrating conservation of BMP1 function in osteogenesis. Genetic, biochemical, and histologic analyses showed that Bmp1a is critically required for mature collagen generation in bone, downstream of osteoblast maturation.
