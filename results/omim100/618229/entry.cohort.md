### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 (MC1DN7) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NDUFV2 gene (600532) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 (MC1DN7) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutation in the NDUFV2 gene, which encodes the 24-kD subunit of complex I. Affected individuals have biochemical complex I deficiency. Reported clinical features include early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with trunk hypotonia and encephalopathy or brain atrophy, and Leigh syndrome without cardiac involvement. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Bénit et al. (2003) reported 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family who had complex I deficiency and early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with trunk hypotonia. The sibs also had encephalopathy.

Cameron et al. (2015) reported 2 sibs (family 1) with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and brain atrophy. They were diagnosed with complex I deficiency on the basis of low complex I activity in muscle and a high lactate/pyruvate ratio in fibroblasts.

Cameron et al. (2015) also reported a second, consanguineous family (family 2) in which 3 of 5 sibs were affected. These sibs had complex I deficiency and presented with Leigh syndrome but had no cardiac involvement.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MC1DN7 in the families reported by Bénit et al. (2003) and Cameron et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In the consanguineous families, affected sibs were homozygous for an NDUFV2 mutation. In a nonconsanguineous sibship, both affected sibs were compound heterozygous for NDUFV2 mutations. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family with MC1DN7, Bénit et al. (2003) identified a homozygous 4-bp deletion in intron 2 of the NDUFV2 gene (IVS2+5_+8delGTAA). The mutation altered the consensus splice-donor site of exon 2. It resulted in a 70% decrease in NDUFV2 protein and in complex I deficiency.

Cameron et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing on fibroblast DNA from 1 of 2 sibs with MC1DN7 (family 1). Filtering left 190 novel nonsynonymous variants in 185 candidate genes. Both sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 mutations in the NDUFV2 gene. One was the intronic c.IVS2+1delGTAA mutation. The other was a novel 1-bp insertion (c.669_670insG) predicted to cause a frameshift (Ser224ValfsTer3). The authors then screened 10 probands with complex I deficiency from different families. In a second, consanguineous family (family 2), they found that the affected sibs were homozygous for the c.IVS2+1delGTAA mutation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Bénit et al. (2003) noted that mutations in many complex I subunit genes cause mainly neurologic symptoms. In contrast, this first NDUFV2 mutation was strikingly associated with cardiomyopathy. Before this, such an association had been seen only in the unique case of NDUFS2 mutations.

Cameron et al. (2015) noted that the NDUFV2 c.IVS2+1delGTAA mutation had previously been reported in 2 families with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, trunk hypotonia, and encephalopathy. In their consanguineous family, homozygosity for this mutation caused Leigh syndrome without cardiac involvement, expanding the phenotypic spectrum associated with the mutation.

### Diagnosis

Pagniez-Mammeri et al. (2009) screened 8 patients with biochemically proven isolated complex I deficiency for mutations in 8 nuclear complex I genes, including NDUFV2. They used Surveyor nuclease digestion of 22 cDNA fragments and found molecular defects in 3 patients. They concluded that the method was a reliable way to genotype nuclear complex I deficiencies and would enhance the possibility of prenatal diagnosis.
