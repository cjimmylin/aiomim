### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 (MC1DN7) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NDUFV2 gene (600532) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 is an autosomal recessive disorder with decreased activity of mitochondrial complex I. Bénit et al. (2003) and Cameron et al. (2015) described early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with trunk hypotonia or brain atrophy. Cameron et al. (2015) also described a family with Leigh syndrome and no cardiac involvement. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Bénit et al. (2003) described 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family. They had complex I deficiency with early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and trunk hypotonia. The authors noted that the cardiomyopathy contrasted with the mainly neurologic symptoms seen with mutations in most other complex I subunit genes.

Cameron et al. (2015) reported 2 sibs with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and brain atrophy. Mitochondrial complex I deficiency was diagnosed from low enzyme activity in muscle and a high lactate/pyruvate ratio in fibroblasts.

Cameron et al. (2015) also reported a consanguineous family in which 3 of 5 sibs had complex I deficiency. They presented with Leigh syndrome but did not have cardiac involvement.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MC1DN7 is autosomal recessive. Bénit et al. (2003) found a homozygous NDUFV2 mutation in 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family. Cameron et al. (2015) found compound heterozygous NDUFV2 mutations in 2 sibs and a homozygous NDUFV2 mutation in a consanguineous family. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Loeffen et al. (2001) studied patients from 3 families with enzymatic complex I deficiency, cardiomyopathy, and encephalomyopathy. They screened NDUFS2 cDNA by RT-PCR and direct sequencing and found 3 missense mutations that changed conserved amino acids. Loeffen et al. (2001) noted a high rate of consanguinity and autosomal recessive inheritance in their group of patients with complex I deficiency. Together with the absence of common mtDNA mutations, this made a nuclear genetic cause likely. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Bénit et al. (2003) studied 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family with complex I deficiency and early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Using denaturing HPLC and sequencing, they identified a homozygous 4-bp deletion in intron 2 of the NDUFV2 gene (IVS2+5_+8delGTAA). The deletion altered the consensus splice-donor site of exon 2. It led to a 70% decrease in NDUFV2 protein and to complex I deficiency.

Cameron et al. (2015) studied 2 sibs with complex I deficiency, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and brain atrophy. They performed whole-exome sequencing on fibroblast DNA from one sib and filtered for novel nonsynonymous changes. This identified compound heterozygous mutations in the NDUFV2 gene. One was a previously reported intronic splice mutation (c.IVS2+1delGTAA), considered disease causing. The other was a novel 1-bp insertion (c.669_670insG) predicted to cause a frameshift (p.Ser224Valfs*3).

Cameron et al. (2015) also screened 10 probands with complex I deficiency from different families. They identified homozygosity for the NDUFV2 intronic c.IVS2+1delGTAA mutation in a consanguineous family with Leigh syndrome.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Cameron et al. (2015) noted that homozygosity for the c.IVS2+1delGTAA NDUFV2 mutation had previously been reported in 2 families with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, truncal hypotonia, and encephalopathy. In contrast, their family with the same genotype had Leigh syndrome without cardiac involvement. They concluded that this broadened the clinical phenotype associated with the mutation.

### Diagnosis

Pagniez-Mammeri et al. (2009) studied 8 patients with biochemically proven isolated mitochondrial complex I deficiency. They screened 8 nuclear complex I genes (NDUFS1, NDUFS2, NDUFS3, NDUFS4, NDUFS7, NDUFS8, NDUFV1, and NDUFV2) by Surveyor nuclease digestion of 22 cDNA fragments covering the coding sequences. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms and missense mutations were detected in 18.7% of the cDNA fragments, and molecular defects were identified in 3 patients. The authors concluded that Surveyor nuclease screening of cDNAs is a reliable and easy-to-interpret way to genotype nuclear complex I deficiencies and that it reduces the number of sequencing reactions needed. They suggested that the method would make prenatal diagnosis more feasible.
