### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 (MC1DN7) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NDUFV2 gene (600532) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Mitochondrial complex I deficiency nuclear type 7 (MC1DN7) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutation in the NDUFV2 gene, which encodes a subunit of mitochondrial complex I. Patients reported by Bénit et al. (2003) and Cameron et al. (2015) had early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with trunk hypotonia, encephalopathy, or brain atrophy. Cameron et al. (2015) also described a family with Leigh syndrome and no cardiac involvement. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Bénit et al. (2003) described 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family who had complex I deficiency with early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and trunk hypotonia. The authors noted that the cardiomyopathy contrasted with the mainly neurologic symptoms seen with mutations in most other complex I subunit genes.

Cameron et al. (2015) reported 2 sibs with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and brain atrophy. They were diagnosed with mitochondrial complex I deficiency on the basis of low enzyme activity in muscle and a high lactate/pyruvate ratio in fibroblasts.

Cameron et al. (2015) also reported a consanguineous family in which 3 of 5 sibs had complex I deficiency and presented with Leigh syndrome but no cardiac involvement.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MC1DN7 in the families reported by Bénit et al. (2003) and Cameron et al. (2015) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Affected sibs were born to consanguineous parents and carried homozygous NDUFV2 mutations, or were compound heterozygous for NDUFV2 mutations. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous family with complex I deficiency and early-onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Bénit et al. (2003) used denaturing HPLC and sequencing to identify a homozygous 4-bp deletion in intron 2 of the NDUFV2 gene (IVS2+5_+8delGTAA). The deletion altered the consensus splice-donor site of exon 2 and resulted in a 70% decrease in NDUFV2 protein and complex I deficiency.

Cameron et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing on fibroblast DNA from 1 of 2 sibs with complex I deficiency, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and brain atrophy, filtering for novel nonsynonymous changes. They found compound heterozygous mutations in the NDUFV2 gene. One was a previously reported intronic splice mutation, which they designated c.IVS2+1delGTAA and considered disease causing. The other was a novel 1-bp insertion (c.669_670insG) predicted to cause a frameshift (p.Ser224Valfs*3).

Cameron et al. (2015) then screened 10 probands with complex I deficiency from different families. In a consanguineous family with Leigh syndrome, they identified homozygosity for the NDUFV2 intronic c.IVS2+1delGTAA mutation.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Cameron et al. (2015) noted that homozygosity for the c.IVS2+1delGTAA NDUFV2 mutation had previously been reported in 2 families with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, truncal hypotonia, and encephalopathy. In contrast, their family with the same genotype had Leigh syndrome without cardiac involvement, which expanded the clinical phenotype associated with this mutation.

### Diagnosis

Pagniez-Mammeri et al. (2009) screened 8 patients with biochemically proven isolated mitochondrial complex I deficiency for mutations in 8 nuclear complex I genes, including NDUFV2. They used Surveyor nuclease digestion of 22 cDNA fragments covering the coding sequences. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms and missense mutations were detected in 18.7% of the cDNA fragments, and molecular defects were identified in 3 patients. The authors concluded that Surveyor nuclease screening of cDNAs is a reliable and easy-to-interpret way to genotype nuclear complex I deficiencies and that it reduces the number of sequencing reactions needed. They suggested that the method would make prenatal diagnosis more feasible.
