### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene on chromosome 10.

### Description

Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is a rare disorder of CoQ10 biosynthesis caused by defects in prenyldiphosphate synthase subunit 1 (PDSS1), the human ortholog of yeast COQ1. The clinical picture varies. Some patients have sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, with mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and cardiac valvulopathy. Others have a severe early-onset multisystem disease with encephalopathy, nephrotic syndrome or pulmonary hypertension, and death in early childhood (Mollet et al., 2007; Nardecchia et al., 2021; Bellusci et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) reported a family (family 1) with an inborn error of CoQ10 biosynthesis. As summarized by Nardecchia et al. (2021), the 2 affected sibs in this family were a 22-year-old male and a 14-year-old female. Both had neurosensorial deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscular weakness, and mild to moderate valvulopathy. Hearing loss developed before the optic atrophy. Both sibs also had lactic acidosis, macrocephaly, peripheral neuropathy, obesity, and livedo reticularis. Brain MRI was not performed. ⚠︎

Vasta et al. (2012) used targeted next-generation sequencing in patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders and found variants in PDSS1, among other genes. Nardecchia et al. (2021) summarized the patient with PDSS1 variants from that study. This patient had a severe phenotype: developmental delay, failure to thrive, leukoencephalopathy with a lactic acid peak on spectroscopy, and a severe nephrotic syndrome that led to kidney failure and death at age 16 months.

Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported 2 sisters born to consanguineous Egyptian parents. The older sister, aged 16 years, walked independently at 15 months but had severe language delay. At age 4 years she was found to have profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, and mild intellectual disability was diagnosed later. Her visual acuity was normal at 11 years but 1/10 bilaterally at 14 years, with red-green dyschromatopsia. Fundus examination showed optic atrophy that was most marked in the inferior temporal sector. Visual evoked potentials showed increased latency and reduced amplitude of the P100 waveform, and optical coherence tomography showed bilateral thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer. She also had diffuse mild muscle weakness and mild postural tremor of the upper limbs. Brain MRI was normal. Echocardiography showed a moderately dysplastic mitral valve with moderate insufficiency, medium-grade tricuspid insufficiency with an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 40 mm Hg, and a moderately dilated left atrium. Her 6-year-old sister had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. At age 6 years she developed decreased visual acuity, with optic atrophy (bilateral temporal pallor of the optic nerve head) and divergent strabismus. In both sisters, sensorineural deafness came before the optic atrophy.

Bellusci et al. (2021) reported a boy, born prematurely after an in vitro fertilization twin pregnancy complicated by twin-twin transfusion. At 5 months of age he had failure to thrive, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, bilateral hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, and chronic distal phalangeal erythema. Laboratory tests showed anemia, lactic acidosis, 3-methylglutaconic and tricarboxylic aciduria, and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes. He went on to develop a progressive multisystem disorder with severe developmental delay, pyramidal dysfunction, seizures, tremor, optic atrophy, nystagmus, severe visual and hearing impairment, and chronic heart failure. Brain MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy at 12 months. At 24 months it showed cystic change in the white matter, with small necrotic or porencephalic cysts around the lateral ventricles and in the commissural fibers. He died at age 3 years. The authors proposed that the combination of phalangeal erythema, livedo reticularis, and pulmonary hypertension pointed to microvascular dysfunction.

### Biochemical Features

In family 1, Mollet et al. (2007) found that quinone-dependent oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activities were in the range of the lowest control values, while OXPHOS enzyme activities were normal. Adding quinone restored normal OXPHOS activities, which confirmed CoQ10 deficiency.

### Inheritance

Mollet et al. (2007) and Nardecchia et al. (2021) found homozygous PDSS1 mutations in affected sibs; in the family of Nardecchia et al. (2021) the parents were consanguineous. Bellusci et al. (2021) found compound heterozygous mutations. These findings are consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Mollet et al. (2007) performed a genome-wide search for homozygosity in family 1. They identified a region of chromosome 10 that contains the PDSS1 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Mollet et al. (2007) sequenced PDSS1 in affected members of family 1 and identified a homozygous mutation (D308E) that changes a conserved amino acid. They transformed a yeast strain lacking coq1 with yeast COQ1 carrying the mutation, and the transformed strain grew poorly on respiratory medium. This indicated that the mutation causes the OXPHOS deficiency.

Vasta et al. (2012) performed targeted next-generation sequencing on 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders and identified variants in PDSS1, among other genes. According to Nardecchia et al. (2021), the affected patient carried a missense and a truncating PDSS1 variant in compound heterozygous state.

In 2 sisters with sensorineural deafness and optic atrophy born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, Nardecchia et al. (2021) used whole-exome sequencing and identified a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution at a highly conserved position. Sanger sequencing of OPA1, OPA3, and WFS1 had found no pathogenic variants. The PDSS1 variant was absent from gnomAD and lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity. Both parents were heterozygous. Among the unaffected sibs, 1 was homozygous wildtype and 1 was heterozygous. ⚠︎

In a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene: c.716T-G (V239G) and c.1183C-T (R395X).

### Clinical Management

The patient reported by Bellusci et al. (2021) began oral ubidecarenone at 15 mg/kg/day at 5 months of age, and the dose was increased to 30 mg/kg/day 3 months later. High-dose CoQ10 supplementation did not control disease progression, and he died at age 3 years.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, ASP308GLU [c.924T>G; p.Asp308Glu; D308E]

In affected members of a family with CoQ10 deficiency (family 1), Mollet et al. (2007) identified a homozygous asp308-to-glu (D308E) mutation in the PDSS1 gene, affecting a conserved residue. Nardecchia et al. (2021) gave the nucleotide change as c.924T-G. A yeast strain lacking coq1, transformed with yeast COQ1 carrying the mutation, grew poorly on respiratory medium.

**.0002 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, GLN245HIS [c.735G>T; p.Gln245His]

In 2 sisters with sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, Nardecchia et al. (2021) identified a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene by whole-exome sequencing, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution at a highly conserved position. The variant was absent from gnomAD and lay within a 12-Mb region of homozygosity. Both parents were heterozygous. Among the unaffected sibs, 1 was homozygous wildtype and 1 was heterozygous.

**.0003 COENZYME Q10 DEFICIENCY, PRIMARY, 2**  
PDSS1, VAL239GLY [NM_014317.3:c.716T>G; p.Val239Gly]

In a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency, Bellusci et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene: a c.716T-G transversion resulting in a val239-to-gly (V239G) substitution, and a c.1183C-T transition resulting in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution. His features included hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, distal phalangeal erythema, lactic acidosis, and 3-methylglutaconic aciduria. He died at age 3 years.
