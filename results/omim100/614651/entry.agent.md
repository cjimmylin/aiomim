### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 (COQ10D2) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PDSS1 gene on chromosome 10.

### Description

Primary coenzyme Q10 deficiency-2 is a rare disorder of CoQ10 biosynthesis caused by defects in PDSS1, which is involved in the first step of CoQ10 synthesis. The phenotype is clinically heterogeneous. Reported features include sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscle weakness, and cardiac valvulopathy, while more severely affected infants have had encephalopathy, nephrotic syndrome, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, and early death (Nardecchia et al., 2021; Bellusci et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Mollet et al. (2007) identified an inborn error of CoQ10 biosynthesis in a family (family 1). Enzymologic studies showed that quinone-dependent oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activities were in the range of the lowest control values, while OXPHOS enzyme activities were normal, and CoQ10 deficiency was confirmed by restoration of normal OXPHOS activities after addition of quinone. In a later review, Nardecchia et al. (2021) summarized the 2 affected sibs of this family (a 22-year-old male and a 14-year-old female) as having neurosensorial deafness, optic atrophy, mild intellectual disability, muscular weakness, and cardiac involvement consisting of mild to moderate valvulopathy; neurosensorial loss preceded optic atrophy. According to their table, both sibs also had lactic acidosis, macrocephaly, peripheral neuropathy, and obesity/livedo reticularis.

Vasta et al. (2012) performed targeted next-generation sequencing in 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders and identified variants in several known pathogenic genes, including PDSS1. Nardecchia et al. (2021) stated that the patient described by Vasta et al. (2012) had a much more severe phenotype, with leukoencephalopathy and a lactic acid peak on spectroscopy, associated with severe nephrotic syndrome that caused kidney failure and death at the age of 16 months.

Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported 2 sisters, born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, with sensorineural deafness followed by optic atrophy. The proband, a 16-year-old girl, had normal motor development but severe language delay; profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss was found at 4 years of age, and mild intellectual disability was later diagnosed. Her visual acuity was reported as normal at 11 years, but at 14 years it was 1/10 bilaterally, with red-green dyschromatopsia. Fundus examination showed optic atrophy prevalent in the inferior temporal sector, visual-evoked potentials showed increased latency and reduced amplitude of the P100 waveform, and optical coherence tomography showed bilateral reduction of retinal nerve fiber layer thickness. She had diffuse mild muscle weakness and mild postural tremor of the upper limbs; brain MRI was normal. Echocardiography showed a moderately dysplastic mitral valve with moderate insufficiency, medium-grade tricuspid insufficiency, and a moderately dilated left atrium. Her younger sister had bilateral sensorineural hearing loss and at 6 years of age developed decreased visual acuity, with optic atrophy and divergent strabismus. The authors noted that, in contrast to OPA1 and OPA3 defects, sensorineural deafness seemed to precede optic atrophy in PDSS1 deficiency.

Bellusci et al. (2021) gave a detailed clinical description of a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency who had been included in an earlier case series. He was born prematurely after an in vitro fertilization pregnancy complicated by twin-twin transfusion. At 5 months of age he had failure to thrive, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, bilateral hearing impairment, pulmonary hypertension, livedo reticularis, and distal phalangeal erythema. Laboratory tests showed anemia, lactic acidosis, 3-methylglutaconic and tricarboxylic aciduria, and decreased CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes. He developed a progressive multisystem disorder with severe developmental delay, pyramidal dysfunction, seizures, tremor, optic atrophy and nystagmus, severe visual and hearing impairment, white matter cystic change, and chronic heart failure, and died at 3 years of age. Brain MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy at 12 months and cystic change in the white matter at 24 months. The authors suggested that the coexistence of phalangeal erythema, livedo reticularis, and pulmonary hypertension pointed to microvascular dysfunction as a possible mechanism.

### Biochemical Features

In family 1 of Mollet et al. (2007), quinone-dependent OXPHOS activities were in the range of the lowest control values, while the activities of the OXPHOS enzymes themselves were normal. Normal OXPHOS activities were restored after addition of quinone. In the patient of Bellusci et al. (2021), CoQ10 levels in lymphocytes were decreased.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of COQ10D2 in the family reported by Nardecchia et al. (2021) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The 2 affected sisters, born to consanguineous parents, were homozygous for a PDSS1 mutation, the parents were carriers, and their unaffected sibs were either heterozygous or homozygous wildtype.

### Mapping

By a genome-wide search for homozygosity in family 1, Mollet et al. (2007) identified a region on chromosome 10 that included the PDSS1 gene.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a family with CoQ10 deficiency (family 1), Mollet et al. (2007) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the PDSS1 gene (D308E) that changed a conserved amino acid. The gene was identified by a genome-wide search for homozygosity followed by sequencing of PDSS1, the human ortholog of yeast COQ1. Mutant yeast COQ1 introduced into a yeast Delta-coq1 strain led to defective growth on respiratory medium, indicating that the mutation caused the OXPHOS deficiency.

Vasta et al. (2012) used targeted next-generation sequencing of the exons of 908 known and candidate nuclear genes in 26 patients with known or suspected mitochondrial disorders and identified variants in several known pathogenic genes, including PDSS1. Nardecchia et al. (2021) reported that this patient carried compound heterozygous PDSS1 mutations, one missense and one truncating.

In 2 sisters, born to consanguineous Egyptian parents, with sensorineural deafness and optic atrophy, Nardecchia et al. (2021) identified a homozygous c.735G-T transversion in the PDSS1 gene, resulting in a gln245-to-his (Q245H) substitution at a highly conserved position. The mutation, found by whole-exome sequencing, lay in a 12-Mb region of homozygosity and was not present in the gnomAD database. It segregated with the disorder in the family: the parents were carriers, an unaffected sister was homozygous wildtype, and an unaffected brother was heterozygous. Sanger sequencing of OPA1, OPA3, and WFS1 had shown no pathogenic variants.

In a boy with primary CoQ10 deficiency who died at 3 years of age, Bellusci et al. (2021) reported compound heterozygous mutations in the PDSS1 gene: c.716T-G, resulting in a val239-to-gly (V239G) substitution, and c.1183C-T, resulting in an arg395-to-ter (R395X) substitution.

### Clinical Management

The patient of Bellusci et al. (2021) was started on oral ubidecarenone at 15 mg/kg/day at 5 months of age, and the dose was increased to 30 mg/kg/day 3 months later when the PDSS1 mutations were identified. High-dose CoQ10 supplementation did not control disease progression, and the patient died at 3 years of age. Nardecchia et al. (2021) stated that CoQ10 synthesis defects should be considered treatable conditions and included in the differential diagnosis of optic atrophy with sensorineural deafness.
