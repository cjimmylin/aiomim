### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brachyolmia type 4 with mild epiphyseal and metaphyseal changes (BCYM4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the PAPSS2 gene (603005) on chromosome 10q23. ⚠︎

### Description

Brachyolmia type 4 with mild epiphyseal and metaphyseal changes is an autosomal recessive skeletal dysplasia. Its features include short-trunk short stature and normal intelligence. Radiographs show platyspondyly or rectangular vertebral bodies with irregular endplates and narrow intervertebral spaces, along with variable, generally mild epiphyseal and metaphyseal changes of the tubular bones (Ahmad et al., 1998; Miyake et al., 2012). Miyake et al. (2012) concluded that PAPSS2 mutations produce a skeletal dysplasia family with a gradation of phenotypes ranging from brachyolmia to spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ahmad et al. (1998) described a large, inbred, 8-generation kindred from a remote area of Pakistan with a distinct form of spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia (SEMD). They evaluated 16 affected individuals (11 males and 5 females). Clinical findings included short stature evident at birth, short bowed lower limbs, mild brachydactyly, kyphoscoliosis, an abnormal gait, enlarged knee joints, precocious osteoarthropathy, and normal intelligence. Radiographs showed delayed epiphyseal ossification at the hips and knees and platyspondyly with irregular end plates and narrowed joint spaces. There were diffuse early osteoarthritic changes, mainly in the spine and hands, and mild brachydactyly. Mild metaphyseal abnormalities were seen mostly at the hips and knees.

Faiyaz ul Haque et al. (1998) studied a large inbred Pakistani family with a distinct, recessively inherited form of SEMD, a dwarfing condition.

Noordam et al. (2009) described a girl with premature pubarche, hyperandrogenic anovulation, very low DHEAS levels, and increased androgen levels.

Miyake et al. (2012) described 6 patients with autosomal recessive brachyolmia: 3 affected members of a Turkish family, 2 Japanese patients, and 1 Korean patient. The patients had short-trunk short stature that became conspicuous during childhood, with normal intelligence and facies. Radiographic features included rectangular vertebral bodies with irregular endplates and narrow intervertebral discs, precocious calcification of rib cartilages, short femoral neck, and mildly shortened metacarpals. Spinal changes were very similar among the 6 patients, whereas epiphyseal and metaphyseal changes of the tubular bones were variable.

### Inheritance

Ahmad et al. (1998) concluded that pedigree analysis of the inbred Pakistani kindred strongly suggested autosomal recessive inheritance, and that consanguineous loops could explain all affected individuals being homozygous for the abnormal allele. Faiyaz ul Haque et al. (1998) also found that SEMD in a large inbred Pakistani family was inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern.

### Mapping

Faiyaz ul Haque et al. (1998) mapped the gene associated with SEMD in a large inbred Pakistani family to chromosome 10q23-q24. This region is syntenic with the locus for the brachymorphic mutation on mouse chromosome 19.

### Heterogeneity

Ahmad et al. (1998) concluded that the phenotype in their Pakistani kindred is distinct from other autosomal recessive forms of SEMD. They based this on the mild metaphyseal involvement, the type of brachydactyly, and the absence of loose joints or other clinical findings.

Miyake et al. (2012) noted that brachyolmia is clinically and genetically heterogeneous, with TRPV4 mutations causing an autosomal dominant form. They identified PAPSS2 as a disease gene for an autosomal recessive form.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a large inbred Pakistani family with SEMD, Faiyaz ul Haque et al. (1998) identified a nonsense mutation in the ATPSK2 gene (PAPSS2). This gene encodes a novel ATP sulfurylase/APS kinase that catalyzes the activation of inorganic sulfate to PAPS.

In a girl with premature pubarche and androgen excess, Noordam et al. (2009) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the PAPSS2 gene. In vitro coincubation of human SULT2A1 with wild-type or mutant PAPSS2 proteins confirmed that the mutations were inactivating.

In 3 affected members of a Turkish family with autosomal recessive brachyolmia, Miyake et al. (2012) used exon capture followed by next-generation sequencing to identify a homozygous loss-of-function mutation in the PAPSS2 gene (c.337_338insG; p.A113GfsX18). They then studied 3 additional patients (2 Japanese and 1 Korean) with a similar brachyolmia phenotype and found PAPSS2 mutations in all 3. One patient was homozygous for a splice site mutation (IVS3+2delT). The second was compound heterozygous for 2 loss-of-function mutations (c.616-634del19, p.V206SfsX9 and c.1309-1310delAG, p.R437GfsX19). The third was compound heterozygous for 2 other loss-of-function mutations (c.480_481insCGTA, p.K161RfsX6 and c.661delA, p.I221SfsX40).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Miyake et al. (2012) concluded that PAPSS2 mutations produce a skeletal dysplasia family with a gradation of phenotypes ranging from brachyolmia to spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia.

### Pathogenesis

Faiyaz ul Haque et al. (1998) noted that both the human and mouse phenotypes were specific to cartilage. They concluded that this provided evidence of the critical role of sulfate activation to PAPS, the universal donor for protein sulfation, in the maturation of cartilage extracellular matrix molecules and in skeletogenesis.

Noordam et al. (2009) noted that SULT2A1 converts DHEA to its inactive sulfate ester DHEAS, which prevents conversion of DHEA to active androgens, and that SULT2A1 requires PAPS for catalytic activity. They concluded that PAPSS2 deficiency is a monogenic adrenocortical cause of androgen excess.

### Animal Model

In the brachymorphic mouse, Faiyaz ul Haque et al. (1998) identified a missense mutation in the region of Atpsk2 encoding the APS kinase activity. Atpsk2 is the orthologous locus on mouse chromosome 19.
