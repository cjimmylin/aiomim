### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene (601906) on chromosome 12q13.12.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is an autosomal recessive form of split-hand/foot malformation. It is marked by central reductions of the feet, with or without hand involvement, and the malformation pattern can vary within a family. Sporadic SHFM may be caused by this recessive form, which has implications for recurrence risk.

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split-foot malformation in 2 sibs.

Verma et al. (1976) described an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships. Both sibships came from first-cousin marriages.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae.

Hennekam and Lommen (1988) reported ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split hand/foot malformation.

Gül and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 individuals with split-hand/split-foot malformation in an inbred kindred. The malformation pattern varied within the family.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive split-hand/foot malformation. Twelve affected members had central reductions of the feet, with or without hand involvement. One more affected member had the mildest phenotype, which was atypical SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with split-hand/foot malformation, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. Unlike some patients with WNT10B variants, the patient and all of her family members had normal bone density and were not obese. The authors suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated findings of SHFM6. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Freire-Maia (1971) described a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed what it means for genetic counseling.

Verma et al. (1976) concluded that their family, together with earlier reports including that of Freire-Maia (1971), further showed that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Their evidence was the occurrence of the deformity in 2 sibships born to first-cousin parents.

Mufti and Wood (1987) found ectrodactyly in sisters and half sisters of an extended family and concluded that autosomal recessive inheritance seemed likely.

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) questioned whether a rare autosomal recessive form of split hand/foot malformation exists. As an alternative, they proposed a two-locus model. In this model, a dominant split hand/foot mutation would be controlled by a gene at another locus, and a dominant mutation at that controlling locus would cause nonpenetrance of the split hand/foot mutation. ⚠︎

Gül and Oktenli (2002) noted that most familial SHFM is autosomal dominant. For their inbred kindred with 9 affected members, they discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a two-locus model.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) described autosomal recessive SHFM in a large consanguineous kindred with a homozygous WNT10B mutation. They suggested complex inheritance because of incomplete penetrance and a possible modifying variant in TP63 (see Heterogeneity and Molecular Genetics). ⚠︎

Blattner et al. (2010) concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait and may be inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. In that case, the recurrence risk is 25% for sibs and very low for the offspring of patients unless there is consanguinity.

### Mapping

Ugur and Tolun (2008) used homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM and identified a novel SHFM locus at 12q13.11-q13. The maximum multipoint lod score was 5.47. Linkage to 4 of the 5 known SHFM loci was excluded.

### Heterogeneity

Elliott and Evans (2006) noted that split-hand/foot malformation is genetically heterogeneous, with at least five loci identified. They used descriptive epidemiology, chi-square analysis, and discriminant function analysis to identify phenotypic patterns associated with the mapped SHFM loci. They suggested that these patterns could help with classification and with directing mapping efforts and targeted genetic testing.

In most affected members of their consanguineous SHFM kindred, Ugur and Tolun (2008) detected a rare insertion variant in the TP63 gene (rs34201045). The variant lies at the alternate promoter used to transcribe the N-terminal-truncated p63 isotype. The authors suggested complex inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Ugur and Tolun (2008) used a candidate gene approach in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM mapping to 12q13. They identified a homozygous missense mutation in the WNT10B gene (R332W). The mutation was present in all affected individuals except the one with atypical SHFM, and it was also present in an asymptomatic female. The authors proposed two explanations: either a second locus contributes to manifestation of the SHFM phenotype, or a suppressor locus prevented manifestation in the nonpenetrant female.

Blattner et al. (2010) sequenced WNT10B in a patient with sporadic split-hand/foot malformation and found a homozygous 4-bp duplication resulting in a premature termination codon. Nine relatives who were heterozygous for the duplication showed no sign of SHFM. The authors recommended testing patients with sporadic SHFM for WNT10B mutations to determine the recurrence risk.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a novel homozygous 3-bp deletion in the WNT10B gene (c.695_697delACA) in an Indian girl with SHFM6. The deletion was predicted to cause in-frame loss of asparagine-232 (N232del). Protein modeling suggested that this deletion at a critical position likely severely affects protein structure and function, likely decreasing protein lifetime and preventing binding to its receptors and other ligands.
