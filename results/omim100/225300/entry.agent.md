### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that split-hand/foot malformation-6 (SHFM6) is caused by homozygous mutation in the WNT10B gene on chromosome 12q13.

### Description

Split-hand/foot malformation (SHFM) is a limb malformation affecting the central rays of the hands and/or feet, characterized by absence of the central digital rays, a deep median cleft, and syndactyly of the remaining digits (Gul and Oktenli, 2002; Blattner et al., 2010). Most familial cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner (Gul and Oktenli, 2002); SHFM6 is the autosomal recessive form associated with mutation in WNT10B (Ugur and Tolun, 2008; Blattner et al., 2010).

### Clinical Features

Ray (1970) reported split-foot malformation in 2 sibs.

Freire-Maia (1971) reported a recessive form of ectrodactyly and discussed its implications for genetic counseling.

Verma et al. (1976) described an intermarried consanguineous family in which split-hand and split-foot deformity occurred in 2 sibships, both resulting from marriages between first cousins.

Mufti and Wood (1987) described an extended family in which 4 sisters and half sisters had ectrodactyly. Two of the sisters also had agenesis of the tibiae. Hennekam and Lommen (1988) published a further communication on ectrodactyly in these sisters and half sisters. ⚠︎

Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) described a family in which 4 individuals in 2 sibships had typical split hand/foot malformation.

Gul and Oktenli (2002) reported 9 cases of SHFM in an inbred kindred, with variable intrafamilial expression of the malformation pattern.

Ugur and Tolun (2008) studied a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM. Twelve affected members had central reductions of the feet with or without hand involvement, and 1 had the mildest phenotype, atypical SHFM.

Kantaputra et al. (2018) reported an Indian girl with SHFM, sparse hair, and interrupted eyebrows. The patient and all family members had normal bone density and were not obese. The authors suggested that sparse hair and interrupted eyebrows may be associated findings of SHFM6.

### Inheritance

Verma et al. (1976) concluded that their consanguineous family, together with the reports of Ray and Freire-Maia (1971), demonstrated that split-hand and split-foot deformity can be inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. Mufti and Wood (1987) considered an autosomal recessive gene the likely mode of inheritance in their family. Zlotogora and Nubani (1989) and Gul and Oktenli (2002) discussed both autosomal recessive inheritance and a 2-locus model; in the 2-locus model proposed by Zlotogora and Nubani (1989), a dominant mutation at a controlling locus leads to nonpenetrance of a dominant SHFM mutation.

Homozygous WNT10B mutations were identified in a consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM (Ugur and Tolun, 2008), in which the authors proposed either a contributing second locus or a suppressor locus, and in a patient with sporadic SHFM whose 9 heterozygous relatives showed no sign of SHFM (Blattner et al., 2010). Blattner et al. (2010) concluded that sporadic SHFM is not always a dominant trait and may carry a 25% recurrence risk for sibs.

### Mapping

By homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, Ugur and Tolun (2008) identified a novel SHFM locus at chromosome 12q13.11-q13 (maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47). Linkage to 4 of the 5 known SHFM loci was excluded; at TP63 (SHFM4), a rare insertion variant (rs34201045) at the alternate promoter used for transcription of the N-terminally truncated p63 isotype was detected in most affected subjects.

### Molecular Genetics

In a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM, Ugur and Tolun (2008) used a candidate gene approach after homozygosity mapping and identified a homozygous missense mutation in WNT10B (R332W) in all affected individuals except the one with atypical SHFM, as well as in an asymptomatic female. The authors proposed that a second locus contributes to manifestation of the phenotype or that a suppressor locus prevented manifestation in the nonpenetrant female.

In a patient with sporadic SHFM, Blattner et al. (2010) identified a homozygous 4-bp duplication in the WNT10B gene resulting in a premature termination codon. Nine heterozygous relatives showed no sign of SHFM.

In an Indian girl with SHFM6, Kantaputra et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delACA) in the WNT10B gene, predicted to cause an in-frame deletion of asn232 (Asn232del). Based on protein modeling, the deletion was predicted to severely affect protein structure and function.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SPLIT-HAND/FOOT MALFORMATION 6**  
WNT10B, ARG332TRP [p.R332W]

In affected members of a large consanguineous kindred with autosomal recessive SHFM6, Ugur and Tolun (2008) identified a homozygous arg332-to-trp (R332W) mutation in the WNT10B gene. The mutation was found in all affected individuals except 1 with atypical SHFM, and also in an asymptomatic female; the locus had been mapped to 12q13.11-q13 with a maximum multipoint lod score of 5.47.
