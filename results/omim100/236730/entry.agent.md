### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene on chromosome 10q24.

### Description

Urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome (UFS) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by congenital obstructive uropathy and an abnormal facial expression, in which patients grimace when attempting to smile (Wang et al., 1997; Daly et al., 2010). Patients present with urinary tract infection and voiding dysfunction from neurogenic bladder. The bladder fails to void completely even though there is no anatomic outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage. Reflux of infected urine to the upper renal tract puts patients at risk of kidney damage and renal failure (Daly et al., 2010). ✗

### Nomenclature

Elejalde (1979) referred to the condition as 'the Ochoa syndrome.' Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) suggested the term 'urofacial syndrome' for the disorder.

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families, 1 of which was consanguineous (first cousins). The patients had peculiar facies and gestures when smiling and crying. Urinary findings included hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal caliber of the urethra in the prostatic and membranous portions, and urethral valves. The bladder was abnormal, with trabeculation and diverticula associated with severe hypertrophy of the mucosa with sclerotic changes. Elejalde (1979) noted that the peculiar facies allows early recognition of the condition.

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) reported 36 children, seen between 1965 and 1986, who had enuresis and urinary tract infection together with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic work-up showed mild neuropathic bladder in all cases, and most patients had severe urinary tract damage. About two-thirds of the patients also had moderate to severe constipation.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European cases of urofacial syndrome. They noted that the condition had been described originally in Colombian patients and that cases may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing is not recognized.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported what they described as the first published case of urofacial syndrome in Turkey. The patient was diagnosed at 16 years of age after having been followed with a diagnosis of recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux. The authors noted that patients appear to be crying when they smile and that some severely affected patients become hypertensive and progress to end-stage renal disease.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 families from Turkey, with a median follow-up of 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months).

### Inheritance

From genetic analysis of 3 families, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance. In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that inheritance was autosomal recessive, based on occurrence in multiple sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio. Homozygous mutations in the HPSE2 gene in affected individuals (Daly et al., 2010) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) ran a genome screen in 20 selected patients using a combination of homozygosity mapping and DNA pooling. Marker D10S677 showed linkage to and association with UFS, with a significant excess of homozygosity in patients compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)). All 31 patients were homozygous for 2 closely linked markers, D10S1726 and D10S198, located approximately 5 cM telomeric to D10S677. Only 12% of unaffected relatives were homozygous for both markers (P less than 10(-19)). Recombinations placed the UFS gene within a 1-cM interval defined by D10S1433 and D10S603 on chromosome 10q23-q24.

The original mapping had used families from Colombia. By homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage, Wang et al. (1999) showed that the syndrome was genetically homogeneous. Haplotype analysis with new polymorphic markers placed the UFS gene within a single YAC clone of 1,410 kb.

Chauve et al. (2000) compared haplotypes in a French family, which they described as the first European cases of UFS, with published haplotypes of patients from Colombia and America. The results were compatible with the same critical region on 10q23-q24 and favored genetic homogeneity.

Wang et al. (2003) built a genomic sequence map covering the whole UFS interval, which earlier studies had placed in a region of about 360 kb on 10q23-q24. They narrowed the disease interval to a 220-kb region that contained the ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene. Their analyses indicated that the same 10q gene was responsible in all UFS patients from multiple ethnic groups.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (1999), Chauve et al. (2000), and Wang et al. (2003) concluded that UFS is genetically homogeneous, with all patients studied mapping to the same 10q region. However, Daly et al. (2010) found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 UFS patients and concluded that the disorder is genetically heterogeneous. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) mapped the GOT1 gene within the UFS critical region and determined its genomic structure, but mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) searched the coding region, the 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, the promoter region, and the exon/intron junctions of the ACDP1 gene in the UFS critical region. They found no pathogenic mutation in UFS patients. ⚠︎

Pang et al. (2010) identified loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all UFS patients originating from Colombia, the United States, and France. HPSE2 encodes a 592-amino acid protein with a domain homologous to the glycosyl hydrolase motif of heparanase (HPSE). The authors suggested that HPSE2 may be important for the synergic action of muscles involved in facial expression and urine voiding.

Whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual defined a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24. Within this region, Daly et al. (2010) identified a 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene. Homozygous exonic deletions, nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations in HPSE2 in 5 further unrelated families confirmed HPSE2 as the causative gene. HPSE2 was expressed in fetal and adult central nervous system and in bladder smooth muscle.

### Diagnosis

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) recommended that individuals with 'inversion' of facial expression undergo urologic evaluation, because the facial distortion is clearly associated with neuropathic bladder and resulting genitourinary damage. Derbent et al. (2009) advised that the syndrome be considered in any patient presenting with dysfunctional voiding, particularly in countries with high rates of consanguineous marriage.
