### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that urofacial syndrome-1 (UFS1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the HPSE2 gene (613469) on chromosome 10q24.2.

### Description

Urofacial (Ochoa) syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder marked by an abnormal facial expression and congenital obstructive uropathy (Wang et al., 1997; Wang et al., 1999). Affected individuals grimace when they try to smile, so that they appear to be crying when they smile (Derbent et al., 2009; Daly et al., 2010). The bladder fails to empty completely even though there is no anatomical outflow obstruction or overt neurologic damage. Patients have enuresis, urinary tract infection, hydronephrosis, and voiding dysfunction from neurogenic bladder (Wang et al., 1997). Reflux of infected urine to the upper renal tract puts the kidneys at risk of damage and renal failure (Daly et al., 2010). Some severely affected patients develop hypertension and progress to end-stage renal disease (Derbent et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Elejalde (1979) called the disorder 'the Ochoa syndrome.' He suggested that it represents a change in the facial and urinary developmental fields. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) proposed the name 'urofacial syndrome.'

### Clinical Features

Elejalde (1979) described 7 patients (4 females and 3 males) from 3 unrelated families. One family was consanguineous, with parents who were first cousins. The patients had peculiar facies and gestures when smiling and crying. Their urinary findings included hydronephrosis, hydroureter, intravesical stenosis of the ureter, abnormal caliber of the prostatic and membranous urethra, and urethral valves. They also had an abnormal trabeculated bladder with diverticula and severe mucosal hypertrophy with sclerotic changes.

Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) described 36 children, seen between 1965 and 1986, who had enuresis and urinary tract infection along with 'inversion' of facial expression when laughing. Urologic work-up showed mild neuropathic bladder in all of them, and most had severe urinary tract damage. About two-thirds also had moderate to severe constipation.

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) reported 3 European patients with urofacial syndrome, a disorder first described in Colombian patients. They suggested that the diagnosis may be missed because the urinary problems vary and the characteristic facial grimacing goes unrecognized.

Derbent et al. (2009) reported a patient from Turkey, the first published case of urofacial syndrome in that country. The patient had been followed for recurrent urinary tract infection and vesicoureteral reflux and was not diagnosed until age 16 years.

Aydogdu et al. (2010) reported 11 Turkish patients with urofacial syndrome from 5 families. They were followed for a median of 32 months (range, 2 to 44 months). The authors described the syndrome as combining uropathy with facial abnormalities.

### Inheritance

From genetic analysis of 3 families, Elejalde (1979) concluded that the condition was probably autosomal dominant, with variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance.

In contrast, Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) concluded that the disorder is autosomal recessive. Their reasons were its occurrence in multiple sibs, normal parents, increased parental consanguinity, and an equal sex ratio. Aydogdu et al. (2010) likewise described urofacial syndrome as an autosomal recessive condition affecting both sexes. Daly et al. (2010) found homozygous HPSE2 mutations in affected families, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Wang et al. (1997) carried out a genome screen that combined homozygosity mapping with DNA pooling. The study used 20 selected UFS patients, one patient pool, and 3 control pools of unaffected relatives. Of 36 random markers, D10S677 showed linkage and association with UFS. Patients had a significant excess of homozygosity at this marker compared with unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-6)), and allele frequencies differed between patient and control pools. The authors then fine-mapped the gene with 10 additional markers spanning 22 cM, using haplotype (linkage disequilibrium) analysis. All 31 patients were homozygous for D10S1726 and D10S198, about 5 cM telomeric to D10S677, compared with only 12% of unaffected relatives (P less than 10(-19)). Recombinations in patients who were heterozygous at the flanking markers D10S1433 and D10S603 placed the gene within a 1-cM interval on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (1999) built a physical map of the UFS critical region on 10q23-q24 and identified new polymorphic markers within it. Haplotype analysis with these markers placed the UFS gene within a single 1,410-kb YAC clone.

Chauve et al. (2000) reported a French family, the first European cases of urofacial syndrome. They compared the family's haplotypes with those previously reported in Colombian and American patients, and the results were compatible with a critical region on chromosome 10q23-q24.

Wang et al. (2003) built a genomic sequence map of the UFS interval on chromosome 10q23-q24, which homozygosity mapping had earlier placed in a region of about 360 kb. They narrowed the critical region to 220 kb. This region contains the newly identified ACDP1 gene and part of the GOT1 gene.

Using whole-genome SNP mapping in 1 affected individual, Daly et al. (2010) defined a 16-Mb autozygous region on chromosome 10q23-q24. Pang et al. (2010) noted that they had previously mapped the UFS gene to chromosome 10q24 by homozygosity mapping.

### Heterogeneity

Urofacial syndrome had previously been mapped to 10q23-q24 in Colombian families. Wang et al. (1999) used homozygosity mapping in American patients of Irish heritage and concluded that urofacial syndrome is genetically homogeneous. Chauve et al. (2000) concluded that haplotype results in a French family supported genetic homogeneity. Wang et al. (2003) concluded that the same chromosome 10q gene causes UFS in all patients across multiple ethnic groups.

In contrast, Daly et al. (2010) found no HPSE2 mutations in 4 additional patients with urofacial syndrome and concluded that the disorder is genetically heterogeneous.

### Molecular Genetics

Wang et al. (1999) located the GOT1 gene, which encodes glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase, within the UFS critical region and determined its genomic structure. Mutation analysis excluded GOT1 as the UFS gene.

Wang et al. (2003) searched ACDP1 extensively in UFS patients. They examined the coding region, the 5-prime and 3-prime untranslated regions, the promoter, and the exon/intron junctions, and found no pathogenic mutation. ⚠︎

Pang et al. (2010) found loss-of-function mutations in the HPSE2 gene in all of the urofacial syndrome patients they studied, who came from Colombia, the United States, and France. HPSE2 lies in the chromosome 10q24 region the authors had previously identified by homozygosity mapping.

Within a 10q23-q24 autozygous region, Daly et al. (2010) found a 10-kb deletion encompassing exons 8 and 9 of the HPSE2 gene in an individual with urofacial syndrome. In 5 further unrelated families with the syndrome, they found homozygous HPSE2 mutations: exonic deletions, nonsense mutations, and frameshift mutations. They concluded that these findings confirmed HPSE2 as the causative gene.

### Pathogenesis

Pang et al. (2010) noted that UFS patients completely lack HPSE2 function. On that basis, they suggested that HPSE2 may be important for coordinating the muscles used in facial expression and urine voiding.

Daly et al. (2010) showed that HPSE2 is expressed in the fetal and adult central nervous system, where they suggested it might help control facial expression and urinary voiding. HPSE2 is also expressed in bladder smooth muscle, which the authors considered consistent with a role in renal tract morphology and function.

### Diagnosis

Elejalde (1979) noted that the peculiar facies allows the condition to be recognized early, which could help with early assessment and treatment and perhaps improve prognosis. Ochoa and Gorlin (1987) stated that the clear link between distorted facial expression and neuropathic bladder, which damages the genitourinary tract, should prompt urologic evaluation of anyone with 'inversion' of facial expression. ⚠︎

Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001), Derbent et al. (2009), and Aydogdu et al. (2010) each stressed that early diagnosis, helped by recognizing the characteristic facial grimacing, is important for managing the urinary problems. Garcia-Minaur et al. (2001) added that early diagnosis also matters for prognosis. ⚠︎
