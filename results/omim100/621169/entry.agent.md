### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that monilethrix-2 (MNLIX2) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding the type II hair cortex keratin hHb1 (KRT81) on chromosome 12q13. ⚠︎

### Description

Monilethrix is a rare dominant hair disease characterized by beaded or moniliform hair, which results from periodic thinning of the hair shaft and shows a high propensity to excess weathering and fracturing (Winter et al., 1997). Monilethrix-2 is caused by mutation in the type II hair cortex keratin hHb1; the phenotype can be particularly variable within a family, ranging from dystrophic alopecia with follicular keratosis to moniliform hair detectable only by electron microscopy (Winter et al., 1998).

### Clinical Features

Winter et al. (1998) reported a French family with monilethrix in which affected members showed a particularly variable phenotype. The pedigree included 2 infants, one with pronounced dystrophic alopecia, follicular keratosis, and clear-cut moniliform hair, and the other with no hair loss at all and moniliform hair detectable only by electron microscopy. An adult family member had no clinically or electron microscopically detectable symptoms but had clear historical proof of the disease.

### Inheritance

Winter et al. (1998) described monilethrix as a rare hair disorder with autosomal dominant transmission.

### Mapping

Winter et al. (1997) noted that several cases of monilethrix had been linked to the type II keratin gene cluster on chromosome 12q13.

### Heterogeneity

Winter et al. (1997) found that monilethrix can result from mutation in either of 2 type II hair keratins, hHb6 or hHb1, at the corresponding glutamic acid residue in the helix termination motif.

### Molecular Genetics

Winter et al. (1997) studied 2 unrelated monilethrix families and a single patient. Affected members of one family and the single patient carried the prevalent glu410-to-lys (E410K) mutation in the type II hair keratin hHb6 (see MNLIX1). In affected individuals of the second family, they identified a lysine substitution of the corresponding glutamic acid residue, glu403 (E403K), in the type II hair keratin hHb1, suggesting that this site represents a mutational hotspot in these highly related type II hair keratins. Because hHb1 and hHb6 are largely coexpressed in cortical trichocytes of the hair shaft, the authors concluded that monilethrix is a disease of the hair cortex.

Winter et al. (1998) noted that pathogenic mutations in hHb6 and hHb1 had until then been restricted to a highly conserved glutamic acid residue, designated by them as glu413 (glu117 of the 2B subdomain), in the EIATYRRLLEGEE helix termination motif, substituted either by lysine or, less frequently, by aspartic acid. In a French family with a variable monilethrix phenotype, they identified a novel glu402-to-lys (E402K) mutation in hHb1, affecting another highly conserved glutamic acid residue (glu106 of the 2B subdomain) in the same motif.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MONILETHRIX 2**  
KRT81, GLU403LYS [Glu403Lys]

In affected individuals of a family with monilethrix, Winter et al. (1997) identified a glu403-to-lys (E403K) substitution in the helix termination motif of the type II hair keratin hHb1, at the glutamic acid residue corresponding to the hHb6 glu410 residue mutated in other monilethrix families, suggesting a mutational hotspot. Winter et al. (1998) later referred to the previously reported hHb1 and hHb6 mutations as affecting residue glu413 (glu117 of the 2B subdomain).

**.0002 MONILETHRIX 2**  
KRT81, GLU402LYS [Glu402Lys]

In members of a French family with a variable monilethrix phenotype, Winter et al. (1998) identified a glu402-to-lys (E402K) mutation, affecting a highly conserved glutamic acid residue (glu106 of the 2B subdomain) in the EIATYRRLLEGEE helix termination motif of hHb1. One infant carrier had pronounced dystrophic alopecia, follicular keratosis, and moniliform hair; another infant had no hair loss and moniliform hair detectable only by electron microscopy; and an adult had no clinically or electron microscopically detectable symptoms but clear historical proof of the disease.
