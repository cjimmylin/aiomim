### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN; 601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

Congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 (CMS11) is an autosomal recessive postsynaptic disorder of the neuromuscular junction. It results from a deficiency of acetylcholine receptors (AChR) at the endplate, caused by loss of function of rapsyn, the protein that clusters and anchors AChR at the postsynaptic membrane. Fatigable weakness usually begins at birth or in infancy, with ptosis, hypotonia, and feeding and breathing difficulties. Episodic crises with respiratory insufficiency are often brought on by intercurrent infections. Some patients have arthrogryposis or other congenital joint contractures. Onset in adolescence or adulthood also occurs. Patients usually respond to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, and some also respond to 3,4-diaminopyridine. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

This disorder was formerly designated CMS Ie, one of the subtypes of postsynaptic congenital myasthenic syndromes with AChR deficiency. ✗

### Clinical Features

Ohno et al. (2002) described patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and endplate AChR deficiency who did not have mutations in the AChR subunit genes. Symptoms began early in life and included ptosis and fatigable weakness. The patients responded to anticholinesterase medication. ✗

Burke et al. (2003) described 2 clinical subgroups among patients with rapsyn mutations. In the early-onset group, symptoms began at birth or in infancy. These patients had hypotonia, ptosis, bulbar weakness, and respiratory crises triggered by infections, and some had arthrogryposis multiplex congenita. In the late-onset group, symptoms began in adolescence or adulthood and could resemble seronegative myasthenia gravis. In both groups, ophthalmoparesis was mild or absent. ✗

Ohno et al. (2003) described Near Eastern Jewish patients with a myasthenic syndrome caused by a mutation in the RAPSN promoter. These patients had distinctive facial malformations, including a long face and prognathism with malocclusion. Their myasthenic symptoms included ptosis and bulbar weakness, with little or no involvement of the limbs. ✗

### Inheritance

CMS11 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Affected individuals carry homozygous or compound heterozygous RAPSN mutations, and their heterozygous parents are unaffected (Ohno et al., 2002). ✗

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) identified mutations in the RAPSN gene in patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and endplate AChR deficiency. All of these patients carried the asn88-to-lys (N88K) mutation, either in the homozygous state or in compound heterozygosity with a second RAPSN mutation. In expression studies, the mutant rapsyn was less able to cluster AChR.

Richard et al. (2003) and Muller et al. (2003) found that the N88K mutation was frequent among patients with CMS of European origin. Haplotype analysis indicated that N88K is an ancient founder mutation in Indo-European populations. Muller et al. (2003) also identified other RAPSN mutations. ✗

Ohno et al. (2003) identified a homozygous -38A-G mutation in Near Eastern Jewish patients with CMS and facial malformations. The mutation lies in an E-box element of the RAPSN promoter, and it impaired E-box-dependent transcriptional activation of RAPSN. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Burke et al. (2003) found that patients homozygous for N88K could have either the early-onset or the late-onset form of the disease. They therefore concluded that RAPSN genotype does not reliably predict disease severity. ✗

### Clinical Management

Patients generally improved with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. Some patients benefited further from the addition of 3,4-diaminopyridine.
