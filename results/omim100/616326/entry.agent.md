### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN).

### Description

CMS11 is an autosomal recessive congenital myasthenic syndrome caused by deficiency of the postsynaptic protein rapsyn. Rapsyn is needed to cluster acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the motor endplate, and its deficiency causes endplate AChR deficiency (Ohno et al., 2002; Engel et al., 2015). Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients presented in the first year of life and a few later in life. Arthrogryposis and other congenital malformations occurred in nearly one-third of patients, and intercurrent infections or fever could trigger respiratory crises. Most patients responded well to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin with congenital myasthenia and facial malformations. The malformations included an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was mainly limited to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive, and the patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. They had no antibodies to acetylcholine receptor. Repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz produced a decremental response, but a single nerve stimulus did not evoke a repetitive compound muscle action potential. The authors suggested that the facial abnormalities might be secondary to the neuromuscular defect, or might be primary and unrelated to it.

Burke et al. (2003) studied 16 unrelated patients with congenital or hereditary myasthenic syndrome due to rapsyn mutations and identified 2 distinct phenotypes, one with early onset and one with late onset. The early-onset form was frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The late-onset form developed in adolescence or adulthood and was initially mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency. At birth he had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity. Because of respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue, he spent a total of 71 days in the neonatal intensive care unit and 47 days in the pediatric intensive care unit. Imaging, metabolic tests, and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. A muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) noted that the clinical features of rapsyn deficiency can suggest autoimmune myasthenia gravis, but ocular ductions are intact in most patients. Apneic episodes occur in some patients with rapsyn mutations. Endplate studies show multiple synaptic contacts on single muscle fibers. The endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds are poorly differentiated. In the Mayo Clinic cohort of patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome investigated between 1988 and 2014, rapsyn deficiency accounted for 51 index cases (14.3%).

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that the congenital myasthenia with facial malformations seen in Iraqi and Iranian Jews was transmitted as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) later identified recessive rapsyn mutations in patients with endplate AChR deficiency.

### Mapping

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin with a phenotypically distinct form of congenital myasthenic syndrome. The families included 6 affected individuals, 4 of the families were consanguineous, and all came from the same geographic region. Linkage analysis excluded the regions on chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes. It also excluded the region on 17p to which familial infantile myasthenia had been mapped. The authors concluded that a defect in a different gene caused the disorder in these patients.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes. In all 4 they identified recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for L14P in the first tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR1) and N88K in TPR3. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. One patient was compound heterozygous for N88K and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5) that causes a frameshift in TPR5. Endplate studies in each patient showed reduced staining for rapsyn and AChR, as well as impaired postsynaptic morphologic development. Expression studies in HEK cells showed that none of the mutations impaired rapsyn self-association, but all 3 reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified 2 mutations in E-boxes in the RAPSN promoter region in 8 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation that changes the E-box at positions -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG, and the allele carrying it was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8 were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. They carried a -38A-G mutation that changes another E-box, at positions -40 to -35, from CAACTG to CAGCTG without altering the CANNTG consensus. Haplotype analysis showed that -38A-G arose from a common founder. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, -38A-G gained and -27C-G lost binding affinity for components of nuclear extracts from C2C12 myotubes. In luciferase reporter assays, both mutations reduced reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes.

Burke et al. (2003) identified rapsyn mutations in 16 unrelated patients with congenital or hereditary myasthenic syndrome. All 16 shared the N88K mutation.

Gaudon et al. (2010) described 3 patients with recessive congenital myasthenic syndrome and RAPSN mutations. In each, family studies showed that direct sequencing had failed to detect one of the mutant alleles. Allelic quantification of the patients' DNA identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN. These genomic rearrangements accounted for 15% of the authors' patients with RAPSN mutations.

In a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency, Das et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify 2 pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Engel et al. (2015) found no genotype-phenotype correlations in rapsyn deficiency, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A-G E-box mutation had a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe weakness of the masticatory and facial muscles, and hypernasal speech.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) noted that recognizing the late-onset phenotype, which had initially been mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis, should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy. Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended that SNP markers and a gene dosage method be used in addition to direct DNA sequencing, particularly when genetic counseling is at issue.

### Clinical Management

The boy reported by Das et al. (2014) was started on pyridostigmine at 16 months of age. His muscle tone and strength improved dramatically and his joint contractures steadily resolved. Four months after starting treatment he was beginning to bear weight, could sit unsupported, and could vocalize full words. Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. Some gain additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some benefit from adding ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) listed congenital myasthenia gravis among the disorders that were frequent in both the Iranian and the Iraqi Jewish communities.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) characterized the haplotype associated with the N88K mutation using 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb. They found a common haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes that was also present in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, which supported a founder effect. Two asymptomatic individuals homozygous for N88K carried a second haplotype that differed at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. The authors suggested that recombination within the rapsyn gene might have implications for the phenotypic expression of the disease.

Muller et al. (2004) reported that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder. Engel et al. (2015) likewise stated that Indo-Europeans harbor a common N88K mutation in RAPSN.

**.0001 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, ASN88LYS [N88K]

Ohno et al. (2002) identified an asn88-to-lys (N88K) mutation in the third tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR3) of rapsyn in all 4 patients they studied with endplate AChR deficiency. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. One was compound heterozygous for N88K and L14P, and one was compound heterozygous for N88K and a 5-bp insertion (553ins5). Expression studies in HEK cells showed that the mutation did not hinder rapsyn self-association but reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Burke et al. (2003) found the N88K mutation in each of 16 unrelated patients with rapsyn-related myasthenic syndrome. These patients had either an early-onset or a late-onset phenotype.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found a common intragenic SNP haplotype associated with N88K in 3 affected homozygotes and in all heterozygous carriers, which supported a founder effect. Two asymptomatic N88K homozygotes carried a discordant haplotype.

Muller et al. (2004) reported that RAPSN N88K derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

**.0002 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, LEU14PRO [L14P]

In a patient with endplate AChR deficiency, Ohno et al. (2002) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the RAPSN gene. One was a leu14-to-pro (L14P) substitution in the first tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR1) and the other was N88K in TPR3. **.0003 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, 5-BP INS, 553 [553ins5]

In a patient with endplate AChR deficiency, Ohno et al. (2002) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the RAPSN gene. One was N88K and the other was a 5-bp insertion (553ins5) that causes a frameshift in the fifth tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR5). Endplate studies showed reduced staining for rapsyn and AChR.

**.0004 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -38A-G [-38A>G; c.-38A>G]

Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -38A-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter in 7 patients (patients 2-8) with congenital myasthenic syndrome. The patients were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. The mutation changes an E-box at positions -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG, which does not affect the CANNTG consensus sequence. Haplotype analysis showed that the mutation arose from a common founder. The mutation gained binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts and reduced reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes.

Engel et al. (2015) noted that Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A-G E-box mutation had a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe weakness of the masticatory and facial muscles, and hypernasal speech.

**.0005 MYASTHENIC SYNDROME, CONGENITAL, 11, ASSOCIATED WITH ACETYLCHOLINE RECEPTOR DEFICIENCY**  
RAPSN, -27C-G [-27C>G]

In a patient with congenital myasthenic syndrome (patient 1), Ohno et al. (2003) identified a -27C-G mutation in the RAPSN promoter. The mutation changes an E-box at positions -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG, and the allele carrying it was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. In functional studies, the mutation lost binding affinity for components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. It reduced reporter gene expression in C2C12 myotubes and in HEK cells transfected with MyoD or myogenin, and it markedly reduced the enhancer activity of an E-box on an SV40 promoter.
