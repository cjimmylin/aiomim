### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that congenital myasthenic syndrome-11 associated with acetylcholine receptor deficiency (CMS11) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding rapsyn (RAPSN; 601592) on chromosome 11p11.2.

### Description

CMS11 is an autosomal recessive congenital myasthenic syndrome caused by rapsyn deficiency. It results in deficiency of acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the endplate and impaired postsynaptic development (Ohno et al., 2002). Burke et al. (2003) described 2 phenotypes: an early-onset form that is frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises, and a late-onset form that develops in adolescence or adulthood. Some patients, particularly Iraqi and Iranian Jews, have facial malformations including an elongated face, mandibular prognathism, and a high-arched palate, along with ptosis and facial and masticatory weakness (Goldhammer et al., 1990). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Goldhammer et al. (1990) described 14 Jewish patients from 10 families of Iraqi or Iranian origin who had congenital myasthenia with facial malformations. The malformations included an elongated face, mandibular prognathism with class III malocclusion, and a high-arched palate. Muscle weakness was restricted mainly to ptosis, weakness of the facial and masticatory muscles, and fatigable speech. The course was mild and nonprogressive, and patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors. None of the patients had antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor. Electrophysiologic studies showed a decremental response on repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz but no repetitive compound muscle action potential after a single nerve stimulus.

Menold et al. (1998) studied 5 families of Iranian Jewish origin, with 6 affected individuals, who had been diagnosed with a phenotypically unique form of CMS. Four of the families were consanguineous.

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with congenital myasthenic syndrome and endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes. Endplate studies in each patient showed decreased staining for rapsyn and AChR and impaired postsynaptic morphologic development.

Ohno et al. (2003) reported 8 patients with CMS who carried mutations in the RAPSN promoter. Seven of them (patients 2-8) were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin and had facial malformations.

Burke et al. (2003) studied 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome and rapsyn mutations and identified 2 distinct phenotypes. The early-onset form was frequently associated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita and life-threatening crises. The late-onset form developed in adolescence or adulthood.

Das et al. (2014) reported a 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency. At birth he had a weak cry, hypotonia, joint contractures, and facial deformity, and he had respiratory difficulty associated with muscle fatigue. Imaging and electrodiagnostic studies were normal. Muscle biopsy showed myofiber atrophy in a pattern consistent with early denervation.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency present in the first year of life, and a few present later. Nearly one-third have arthrogryposis or other congenital malformations at birth. Intercurrent infections or fever can trigger respiratory crises that lead to anoxic encephalopathy. Ocular ductions are intact in most patients.

### Inheritance

Goldhammer et al. (1990) concluded that congenital myasthenia with facial malformations in Iraqi and Iranian Jews is transmitted as an autosomal recessive disorder. Ohno et al. (2002) found recessive RAPSN mutations, homozygous or compound heterozygous, in patients with endplate AChR deficiency, which is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

In 5 Iranian Jewish families with a phenotypically unique form of CMS, 4 of them consanguineous, Menold et al. (1998) excluded linkage to the regions on chromosomes 2 and 17 that contain the AChR subunit genes. They also excluded linkage to the region on 17p where familial infantile myasthenia had been localized.

### Heterogeneity

Menold et al. (1998) suggested that the clinical differences among patients with CMS correlate with locus heterogeneity. They proposed that CMS in Iranian Jewish families, who probably carry the same ancestral mutation, is caused by a defect in a different gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Ohno et al. (2002) studied 4 patients with endplate AChR deficiency who had no mutations in the AChR subunit genes and identified 3 recessive mutations in the RAPSN gene. One patient was compound heterozygous for L14P, in tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) 1, and N88K, in TPR3. Two patients were homozygous for N88K. The fourth patient carried N88K and 553ins5, a frameshift mutation in TPR5. In expression studies in HEK cells, none of the 3 mutations impaired rapsyn self-association, but all 3 reduced coclustering of AChR with rapsyn.

Ohno et al. (2003) identified E-box mutations in the RAPSN promoter in 8 patients with CMS. They had determined that the major transcriptional start site of RAPSN lies 172 nucleotides upstream of the translational start site. Patient 1 carried a -27C-G mutation, which changes an E-box at -27 to -22 from CAGCTG to GAGCTG. The allele carrying this mutation was not transcribed in the patient's muscle. Patients 2 through 8 were Oriental Jews of Iraqi or Iranian origin with facial malformations. They carried a -38A-G mutation, which changes another E-box at -40 to -35 from CAACTG to CAGCTG without affecting the consensus CANNTG sequence. In electrophoretic mobility shift assays, -38A-G gained and -27C-G lost binding affinity for different components of C2C12 myotube nuclear extracts. In luciferase reporter assays, both mutations attenuated reporter expression in C2C12 myotubes. The -27C-G mutation also attenuated expression in MyoD- or myogenin-transfected HEK cells and markedly attenuated E-box enhancer activity on an SV40 promoter. These results predicted reduced rapsyn expression and endplate AChR deficiency.

Burke et al. (2003) identified rapsyn mutations in 16 unrelated patients with a congenital/hereditary myasthenic syndrome. All 16 shared the N88K mutation.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) studied 3 affected individuals who were homozygous for the RAPSN N88K mutation, as well as 2 asymptomatic N88K homozygotes and heterozygous carriers of N88K.

Gaudon et al. (2010) described 3 patients with a characteristic phenotype of recessive CMS and RAPSN mutations. Familial analysis showed that direct sequencing had missed 1 mutant allele in each patient. Allelic quantification of the patients' DNA then identified 3 novel multiexon deletions of RAPSN. Multiexon deletions accounted for 15% of their CMS patients with RAPSN mutations. Gaudon et al. (2010) recommended using SNP markers and a gene dosage method in addition to sequencing, particularly when genetic counseling is an issue. ⚠︎

In the 20-month-old boy with rapsyn deficiency reported by Das et al. (2014), whole-exome sequencing identified 2 confirmed pathogenic mutations in the RAPSN gene.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Dunne and Maselli (2004) found a second haplotype in 2 asymptomatic N88K homozygotes. It differed from the common N88K haplotype at 3 SNP sites downstream of the mutation. They suggested that recombination events within the rapsyn gene may affect expression of the disease.

In a review, Engel et al. (2015) stated that Indo-Europeans carry a common N88K mutation in RAPSN and that no genotype-phenotype correlations had been found, with one exception. Near-Eastern Jewish patients homozygous for the c.-38A-G E-box mutation have a mild phenotype with ptosis, prognathism, severe masticatory and facial weakness, and hypernasal speech.

### Pathogenesis

Goldhammer et al. (1990) suggested that the facial abnormalities in their patients might be secondary to the neuromuscular defect, or might be primary and unrelated to it.

Engel et al. (2015) reviewed that rapsyn concentrates and anchors AChR in the postsynaptic membrane and is required for the development of the junctional folds. In rapsyn deficiency, multiple synaptic contacts appear on single muscle fibers, the endplate AChR deficiency is milder than in primary AChR deficiency, and the junctional folds are poorly differentiated. Depending on the mutation, RAPSN mutations hinder rapsyn self-association or binding to AChR, impede agrin-MuSK-LRP4-mediated clustering of AChR, or decrease rapsyn expression.

### Diagnosis

Burke et al. (2003) found that the late-onset rapsyn phenotype was at first mistaken for seronegative myasthenia gravis. They stated that recognizing it should prevent inappropriate immunotherapy.

Das et al. (2014) diagnosed rapsyn deficiency by whole-exome sequencing after imaging, metabolic, electrodiagnostic, microarray, and muscle biopsy studies had not given a diagnosis. They highlighted next-generation sequencing as an important diagnostic tool that can lead to life-saving treatment.

Engel et al. (2015) listed rapsyn among the CMS proteins associated with congenital contractures and with sudden apneic episodes provoked by fever or stress. They also listed it as occasionally associated with limb-girdle and axial weakness. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Goldhammer et al. (1990) found that their patients responded to cholinesterase inhibitors.

Das et al. (2014) started pyridostigmine at 16 months of age in a boy with rapsyn deficiency. Treatment led to a dramatic improvement in muscle tone and strength and steady resolution of his joint contractures. Four months after treatment began, he was starting to bear weight, sit unsupported, and say full words. ⚠︎

Engel et al. (2015) stated that most patients with rapsyn deficiency respond well to AChE inhibitors. Some patients gain additional benefit from 3,4-diaminopyridine, and some benefit from the addition of ephedrine or albuterol.

### Population Genetics

Zlotogora (1995) noted that congenital myasthenia gravis was frequent in both the Iranian and the Iraqi Jewish communities. The Iranian Jewish community has a very high degree of inbreeding and has had strong ties with Babylonian Jewry in Iraq.

Ohno et al. (2003) found by haplotype analysis that the RAPSN -38A-G promoter mutation, carried by Oriental Jewish patients of Iraqi or Iranian origin, arose from a common founder.

Dunne and Maselli (2004) typed 7 intragenic SNPs spanning 8 kb of the rapsyn gene. They found a common haplotype in 3 affected N88K homozygotes and in all heterozygous carriers of N88K, which supported a founder effect for the mutation.

Muller et al. (2004) concluded that the RAPSN N88K mutation derives from an ancient Indo-European founder.

Engel et al. (2015) reported that rapsyn deficiency accounted for 51 of 356 index patients (14.3%) with congenital myasthenic syndrome investigated at the Mayo Clinic between 1988 and 2014.
