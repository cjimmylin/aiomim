### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that oblique facial clefting-1 (OBLFC1) is caused by loss-of-function mutation in the SPECC1L gene.

### Description

Oblique facial clefts are rare facial malformations. Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) stated that they include types 2 to 6 of the Tessier classification. Saadi et al. (2011) described oblique facial clefts as a unique class of facial malformations.

### Nomenclature

Tessier (1976) proposed an anatomical classification of facial, craniofacial, and laterofacial clefts. He based it on personal observations and used the orbit as the primary structure of reference. The classification distinguished 15 cleft locations and described the course of each through soft tissue and bone. Tessier (1976) also discussed combinations of several cleft types and associated malformations. Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) noted that oblique facial clefts include types 2 to 6 of this classification.

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (1988) reported an 8-month-old girl who was born with symmetric bilateral oblique facial clefts and a calcaneovarus foot deformity. Head CT showed severe bilateral ocular hypoplasia with normal brain parenchyma.

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) reported 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts who had a strikingly similar facial appearance. Three of the 4 patients had consanguineous parents. The authors suggested that the pattern of facial involvement and the overall clinical picture pointed to a unique dysmorphogenetic process. They also suggested that in some cases the condition could represent an oculomaxillofacial dysostosis.

### Inheritance

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) noted that 3 of their 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts had consanguineous parents. The title of their report cited evidence for clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity.

### Cytogenetics

In a girl with bilateral oblique facial clefts, Dasouki et al. (1988) found a de novo balanced translocation between chromosome 1 and chromosome 22 on peripheral blood karyotype. They noted that a submicroscopic imbalance resulting from the translocation could not be ruled out. They recommended chromosome studies in children with such rare malformations.

### Molecular Genetics

Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in the SPECC1L gene are pathogenic for oblique facial clefts. In functional studies, SPECC1L deficiency caused defective reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and abnormal cell adhesion and migration. Knockdown of SPECC1L homologs in zebrafish and Drosophila also produced abnormal phenotypes (see ANIMAL MODEL).

### Pathogenesis

Saadi et al. (2011) found that during mouse embryogenesis, Specc1l is expressed in the cells of the developing facial primordia, which proliferate and fuse to form the face. In mammalian cells, SPECC1L colocalized with both tubulin and actin. SPECC1L deficiency caused defective reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and abnormal cell adhesion and migration. The authors concluded that SPECC1L functions in reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton and is required for proper facial morphogenesis.

### Diagnosis

Dasouki et al. (1988) suggested that the pattern of anomalies seen in their patient would help distinguish oblique facial clefts from amniotic band disruption.

### Animal Model

Saadi et al. (2011) found that knockdown of a SPECC1L homolog in zebrafish produced a 'faceless' phenotype, with loss of jaw and facial structures. In Drosophila, knockdown of the homolog phenocopied mutants in the integrin signaling pathway, which have defects in cell migration and cell adhesion.
