### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that oblique facial clefting-1 (OBLFC1) is caused by loss-of-function mutation in the SPECC1L gene; the zygosity of the mutations and the chromosomal location of the gene are not given in the available evidence.

### Description

Oblique facial clefts are rare facial malformations that include types 2 to 6 of the Tessier classification of facial clefts (Richieri-Costa and Gorlin, 1994). Saadi et al. (2011) described oblique facial clefts as a unique class of facial malformations for which the responsible genetic mutations were largely unknown.

### Nomenclature

Tessier (1976) proposed an anatomical classification of facial, craniofacial, and laterofacial clefts based on personal observations, using the orbit as the primary structure of reference and differentiating 15 locations for clefts. The course of each cleft through soft tissue and bone was described, and combinations of several types of clefts and associated malformations were discussed. The classification was intended to eliminate the old confusing terminology and to facilitate recording of these malformations.

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (1988) reported an 8-month-old girl who was born with symmetric bilateral oblique facial clefts and calcaneovarus foot deformity. CT scan of the head showed severe bilateral ocular hypoplasia and normal brain parenchyma. The authors suggested that the pattern of anomalies would help distinguish oblique facial clefts from amniotic band disruption.

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) reported 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts and a strikingly similar facial appearance; consanguinity was present in 3 of them. The authors suggested that the pattern of facial involvement, the consanguinity, and the entire clinical picture indicated a unique dysmorphogenetic process that could represent, in some instances, an oculomaxillofacial dysostosis. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

In the girl with bilateral oblique facial clefts reported by Dasouki et al. (1988), peripheral blood karyotype showed a de novo balanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 22. The authors noted that a submicroscopic imbalance secondary to the translocation could not be ruled out, and recommended chromosomal studies in children with such rare malformations.

### Heterogeneity

On the basis of their 4 Brazilian patients, Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) presented evidence for clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity of oblique facial clefts.

### Molecular Genetics

Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in the SPECC1L gene are pathogenic for oblique facial clefting. In mammalian cells, SPECC1L colocalized with both tubulin and actin, and SPECC1L deficiency resulted in defective actin-cytoskeleton reorganization as well as abnormal cell adhesion and migration. The authors concluded that SPECC1L functions in actin-cytoskeleton reorganization and is required for proper facial morphogenesis.

### Animal Model

Saadi et al. (2011) found that during mouse embryogenesis, Specc1l is expressed in cell populations of the developing facial primordia, which proliferate and fuse to form the face. In zebrafish, knockdown of a SPECC1L homolog produced a faceless phenotype with loss of jaw and facial structures, and knockdown in Drosophila phenocopied mutants in the integrin signaling pathway that exhibit cell-migration and cell-adhesion defects.
