### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that oblique facial clefting-1 (OBLFC1) is caused by loss-of-function mutation in the SPECC1L gene.

### Description

Oblique facial clefts are a rare and distinctive class of facial malformations, corresponding to types 2 to 6 of the Tessier classification of facial clefts (Tessier, 1976; Richieri-Costa and Gorlin, 1994). Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) suggested that the pattern of facial involvement could represent, in some instances, an oculomaxillofacial dysostosis. Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in SPECC1L, a cytoskeletal protein, cause oblique facial clefting. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Tessier (1976) proposed an anatomic classification of facial, craniofacial, and laterofacial clefts based on personal observations, using the orbit as the primary structure of reference and differentiating 15 locations for clefts. Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) noted that oblique facial clefts include types 2 to 6 of the Tessier classification.

### Clinical Features

Dasouki et al. (1988) reported an 8-month-old girl who was born with symmetric bilateral oblique facial clefts and calcaneovarus foot deformity. CT scan of the head showed severe bilateral ocular hypoplasia and normal brain parenchyma.

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) reported 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts and a strikingly similar facial appearance; 3 of them had consanguineous parents. The authors suggested that the pattern of facial involvement and the entire clinical picture indicated a unique dysmorphogenetic process that could represent, in some instances, an oculomaxillofacial dysostosis. They considered the findings to be evidence for clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity.

### Inheritance

The presence of parental consanguinity in 3 of the 4 Brazilian patients with oblique facial clefts reported by Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) was taken as part of the evidence for a unique, genetically determined dysmorphogenetic process. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

In a girl with bilateral oblique facial clefts, Dasouki et al. (1988) found a de novo balanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 22 on peripheral blood karyotype. They noted that a submicroscopic imbalance secondary to the translocation could not be ruled out, and recommended that chromosome studies be performed in children with such rare malformations.

### Heterogeneity

Richieri-Costa and Gorlin (1994) interpreted their observations in 4 Brazilian patients as evidence for clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity among oblique facial clefts.

### Molecular Genetics

Saadi et al. (2011) showed that loss-of-function mutations in the SPECC1L gene are pathogenic for oblique facial clefting. In mammalian cells, SPECC1L colocalized with both tubulin and actin, and SPECC1L deficiency resulted in defective actin-cytoskeleton reorganization as well as abnormal cell adhesion and migration. The authors concluded that SPECC1L functions in actin-cytoskeleton reorganization and is required for proper facial morphogenesis.

### Diagnosis

Dasouki et al. (1988) stated that the pattern of anomalies observed in their patient would help distinguish oblique facial clefts from amniotic band disruption.

### Animal Model

Saadi et al. (2011) found that during murine embryogenesis, Specc1l is expressed in cell populations of the developing facial primordia, which proliferate and fuse to form the face. In zebrafish, knockdown of a SPECC1L homolog produced a faceless phenotype with loss of jaw and facial structures. In Drosophila, knockdown phenocopied mutants in the integrin signaling pathway that show cell migration and adhesion defects.
