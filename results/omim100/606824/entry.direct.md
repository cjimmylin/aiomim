### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding the Na+/glucose cotransporter (SGLT1, also called SLC5A1) on chromosome 22q13.1.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is an autosomal recessive disorder in which the intestine selectively fails to absorb dietary glucose and galactose. It begins within the first weeks of life. The resulting severe diarrhea and dehydration are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991). The disorder is caused by a defect in the Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1 in the intestinal brush border (Turk et al., 1991; Martín et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

Anderson et al. (1965) described an inborn defect in the intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied intestinal and renal glucose transport in a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption and in her family. Her jejunal mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. In contrast, mucosa from 6 normal controls and from her asymptomatic 5-year-old brother accumulated glucose to 16 times its concentration in the incubation medium. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption in the proband. Her brother's titration curve was similar to those of 7 normal volunteers.

Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported remission of glucose intolerance in familial glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported 8 Arab children from 8 different families with chronic diarrhea due to glucose-galactose malabsorption. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. Their mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. They presented with chronic watery diarrhea, present since birth, and failure to thrive. The diagnosis was confirmed by laboratory studies, including small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and a renal stone was found in another during follow-up. All of the children improved clinically on a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All affected individuals presented with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. Other initial findings included increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite illness, irritability, and apathy. When patients were correctly diagnosed and adequately managed, the diarrhea stopped immediately and they rehydrated quickly, with normal growth and development afterward. When the disease was not recognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) found that glucose transport by normal human jejunal mucosa in vitro was concentrative, saturable, and dependent on sodium and energy, and that it showed competitive inhibition. Both parents and a half sister of a proband with GGM had impaired glucose transport, with values between those of normal controls and those of the proband. Glucose influx was impaired in these 3 heterozygotes, but glucose efflux from their mucosa was the same as in 3 normal controls. Kinetic analysis showed reduced capacity (Vmax) but normal affinity (Km) for glucose transport in their intestinal mucosa. Fructose accumulation was similar in all subjects.

### Inheritance

From their family studies, Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait and that heterozygotes can be detected by their reduced capacity for intestinal glucose transport. Homozygous SLC5A1 mutations cosegregated with GGM in a large Old Order Amish pedigree (Xin and Wang, 2011), consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1991) noted that they had previously shown that the SGLT1 gene lies on the distal long arm of chromosome 22. Using fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe, Turk et al. (1993) refined the location of SGLT1 to chromosome 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) used PCR to amplify SGLT1 cDNA and genomic DNA from members of a family with GGM. Sequencing identified a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. Xenopus oocytes injected with the mutant cRNA showed complete loss of Na+-dependent glucose transport. Turk et al. (1993) stated that this mutation, asp28-to-asn (D28N), was found in 2 patients with GGM and abolished SGLT1 transport activity in vitro.

Martín et al. (1996) noted that they had previously found a missense mutation in the SGLT1 gene in 2 sisters with GGM. They then screened the gene in 30 new patients with GGM and used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype. They concluded that GGM results from defects in both the trafficking and the function of SGLT1.

Xin and Wang (2011) sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons and the exon-intron boundaries of SLC5A1 in 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. They found 4 homozygous missense mutations that cosegregated with GGM in all affected individuals: N51S, A411T, R558H, and H615Q.

### Pathogenesis

Turk et al. (1991) noted that intestinal biopsies from patients with GGM had shown a specific defect in Na+-dependent glucose absorption in the brush border. Normally, glucose is absorbed by the Na+/glucose cotransporter in the brush border membrane of the intestinal epithelium. Uptake into the cell is driven by the transmembrane Na+ electrochemical potential gradient, and the sugar then passes to the blood across the basolateral membrane via the facilitated glucose carrier. Martín et al. (1996) found that defects in both SGLT1 trafficking and SGLT1 function cause GGM.

### Diagnosis

Martín et al. (1996) carried out prenatal screening for GGM in 2 later pregnancies in a large consanguineous family in which 2 proband sibs carried the D28N mutation in SGLT1. They PCR-amplified the first exon of SGLT1 from genomic DNA and tested for D28N by EcoRV restriction digestion. The probands' sib was heterozygous for the mutation, and a cousin was not a carrier. Both children were healthy and free of diarrhea at 2 years of age.

Abdullah et al. (1996) confirmed the diagnosis using laboratory studies that included small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay. Xin and Wang (2011) concluded that a founder effect of SLC5A1 mutations underlies GGM in the Amish, and that population-specific genetic testing is needed to make the early diagnosis that is critical for a good outcome.

### Clinical Management

Turk et al. (1991) noted that the severe diarrhea and dehydration of GGM are usually fatal unless glucose and galactose are removed from the diet. All 8 Arab children reported by Abdullah et al. (1996) improved clinically on a fructose-based formula. In the Amish patients reported by Xin and Wang (2011), correct diagnosis and adequate management stopped the diarrhea immediately and led to quick rehydration, followed by normal growth and development.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) found 33 patients with GGM in a single large Old Order Amish pedigree, all homozygous for the same 4 SLC5A1 missense variants. They suggested that these mutations reflect a founder effect in the Amish.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 GLUCOSE/GALACTOSE MALABSORPTION**  
SLC5A1, ASP28ASN [D28N; Asp28Asn]

Turk et al. (1991) found a missense mutation in the SGLT1 gene in a family with GGM. The mutation cosegregated with the phenotype and caused complete loss of Na+-dependent glucose transport when expressed in Xenopus oocytes. Turk et al. (1993) identified this change as asp28-to-asn (D28N), found in 2 patients with GGM, and noted that it abolished SGLT1 transport activity in vitro.

Martín et al. (1996) reported that 2 proband sibs with GGM from a large consanguineous family carried the D28N mutation. In prenatal testing by EcoRV restriction digestion of PCR-amplified exon 1, a later sib was heterozygous and a cousin was not a carrier. Both remained healthy at 2 years of age.

**.0002 GLUCOSE/GALACTOSE MALABSORPTION**  
SLC5A1, ASN51SER [c.152A>G; p.N51S]

In 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree, Xin and Wang (2011) found a homozygous c.152A-G transition in the SLC5A1 gene, resulting in an asn51-to-ser (N51S) substitution. The variant was found by sequencing the coding exons and exon-intron boundaries. It cosegregated with GGM in all affected individuals, together with 3 other homozygous missense variants: A411T, R558H, and H615Q.
