### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the SLC5A1 gene (182380), which encodes the Na+/glucose cotransporter SGLT1, on chromosome 22q13.1.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disorder that appears in the first weeks of life. It is defined by a selective failure of the intestine to absorb dietary glucose and galactose. The resulting severe diarrhea and dehydration are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991). The disorder is caused by a defect in SGLT1, the Na+/glucose cotransporter of the intestinal brush border (Martín et al., 1996).

### Clinical Features

ANDERSON et al. (1965) reported an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption. In vitro, her jejunal mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption.

Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family.

Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported familial glucose-galactose malabsorption in which the glucose intolerance went into remission.

Abdullah et al. (1996) described 8 children with glucose-galactose malabsorption, each from a different family. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. Mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. The children had chronic watery diarrhea from birth and failure to thrive. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another was found to have one on follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with glucose-galactose malabsorption from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All affected individuals presented with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. At initial presentation the authors also noted increased bowel sounds, a distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite illness, irritability, and apathy.

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) found that normal human jejunal mucosa transported glucose against a concentration gradient. This transport was saturable, depended on sodium and energy, and showed competitive inhibition. Mucosa from 6 normal controls and from the proband's asymptomatic 5-year-old brother concentrated glucose to 16 times the medium concentration. All subjects accumulated fructose in a similar way. Intestinal glucose transport was impaired in both parents and in a half sister of the proband, with values falling between those of normal controls and the proband. In these heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired but efflux was the same as in controls. Kinetic analysis showed reduced capacity (Vmax) with normal affinity (Km). The authors concluded that heterozygotes can be detected.

Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. They also concluded that heterozygotes can be detected because their capacity for glucose transport is reduced. Xin and Wang (2011) found homozygous SLC5A1 mutations that cosegregated with the disorder in a large Old Order Amish pedigree. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1993) used fluorescence in situ hybridization with a cosmid probe to refine the location of the SGLT1 gene, which encodes the intestinal Na+/glucose cotransporter. The gene had previously been placed on the distal long arm of chromosome 22 (22q11.2-qter), and they narrowed this to 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

Turk et al. (1991) studied members of a family with GGM. Using PCR to amplify SGLT1 (SLC5A1) cDNA and genomic DNA, they found a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. Xenopus oocytes injected with cRNA carrying the mutation completely lost Na(+)-dependent glucose transport.

Turk et al. (1993) noted that the cause of autosomal recessive glucose/galactose malabsorption in 2 patients was a missense mutation in SGLT1, asp28-to-asn (D28N). In vitro, this mutation eliminated SGLT1 transport activity.

Martín et al. (1996) screened the SGLT1 gene in 30 new patients with GGM. They used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype and concluded that defects in both SGLT1 trafficking and SGLT1 function cause the disorder.

Xin and Wang (2011) studied 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. They sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons and the exon-intron boundaries of SLC5A1. All affected individuals were homozygous for 4 missense mutations, and all 4 cosegregated with the phenotype: c.152A-G (N51S), c.1231G-A (A411T), c.1673G-A (R558H), and c.1845C-G (H615Q).

### Pathogenesis

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that in glucose-galactose malabsorption, the absence of intestinal glucose transport is accompanied by partial impairment of renal glucose transport. The proband's unaffected brother had a renal titration curve similar to those of 7 normal volunteers.

Turk et al. (1991) noted that intestinal biopsies from patients with GGM had shown a specific defect in Na(+)-dependent glucose absorption in the brush border. Normal absorption depends on the Na+/glucose cotransporter in the brush border membrane, which is encoded by SGLT1 on distal chromosome 22q.

### Diagnosis

Martín et al. (1996) performed prenatal screening for GGM in 2 later pregnancies within a large consanguineous family. In this family, 2 proband sibs with GGM had the D28N missense mutation in SGLT1. The authors PCR-amplified exon 1 of SGLT1 from fetal genomic DNA and tested for D28N by EcoRV restriction digestion. The proband's sib was heterozygous, and a cousin was not a carrier. Both children were healthy and free of diarrhea at 2 years of age.

Abdullah et al. (1996) confirmed the diagnosis of glucose-galactose malabsorption with laboratory tests, including small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay.

### Clinical Management

Turk et al. (1991) noted that the severe diarrhea and dehydration of GGM are usually fatal unless glucose and galactose are removed from the diet.

Abdullah et al. (1996) reported that all 8 children with glucose-galactose malabsorption responded clinically to a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) observed that in patients who were correctly diagnosed and adequately managed, diarrhea stopped immediately and rehydration was rapid, followed by normal growth and development. When the disease was not recognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) suggested a founder effect for the SLC5A1 mutations associated with GGM in the Old Order Amish. They proposed population-specific genetic testing to allow early diagnosis, which they considered critical for a favorable outcome.
