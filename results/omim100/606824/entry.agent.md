### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that glucose/galactose malabsorption (GGM) is caused by homozygous mutation in the Na+/glucose cotransporter gene SGLT1 (SLC5A1) on chromosome 22q13.1.

### Description

Glucose/galactose malabsorption is an autosomal recessive disorder in which the intestine selectively fails to absorb dietary glucose and galactose. It begins within the first weeks of life with severe diarrhea and dehydration, which are usually fatal unless these sugars are removed from the diet (Turk et al., 1991). Absent intestinal glucose transport may be accompanied by partial impairment of renal glucose transport (Elsas et al., 1970).

### Clinical Features

Anderson et al. (1965) described an inborn defect of intestinal absorption of certain monosaccharides.

Elsas et al. (1970) studied a 3-year-old girl with congenital glucose-galactose malabsorption and her family. In vitro, her jejunal mucosa could not concentrate glucose at any point during a 60-minute incubation. In contrast, mucosa from 6 normal controls and from her asymptomatic 5-year-old brother accumulated glucose to 16 times its concentration in the incubation medium. Fructose accumulation was similar in all subjects. Renal glucose titration showed a partial defect in renal glucose reabsorption in the proband. Her brother's titration curve was similar to those of 7 normal volunteers.

Meeuwisse (1970) studied renal glucosuria in glucose-galactose malabsorption. Lebenthal et al. (1971) reported glucose-galactose malabsorption in an Oriental-Iraqi Jewish family. Elsas and Lambe (1973) reported remission of glucose intolerance in familial glucose-galactose malabsorption.

Abdullah et al. (1996) described 8 Arab children from 8 different families with glucose-galactose malabsorption. Six were Saudi Arabs and 2 were of other Arab nationalities. The mean age at presentation was 10.6 months. All had chronic watery diarrhea from birth and failure to thrive. Small-bowel biopsy, histology, and small-bowel enzyme assay confirmed the diagnosis. One child had a renal stone at the first visit, and another was found to have one at follow-up.

Xin and Wang (2011) reported 33 patients with GGM from a large Old Order Amish pedigree. All presented with classic watery diarrhea and dehydration. The initial presentation also included increased bowel sounds, distended abdomen, vigorous nursing despite illness, and irritability and apathy. When the disease was correctly diagnosed and adequately managed, the diarrhea stopped immediately, rehydration was quick, and growth and development were normal afterward. When the disease was not recognized, the clinical course was prolonged.

### Biochemical Features

Elsas et al. (1970) found that glucose transport by normal human jejunal mucosa was concentrative, saturable, sodium and energy dependent, and showed competitive inhibition. Both parents and a half sister of their proband had impaired mucosal glucose transport, with values between normal and those of the proband. In these 3 heterozygotes, glucose influx was impaired, but glucose efflux matched that in 3 normal controls. Kinetic analysis showed a reduced capacity (Vmax) but a normal affinity (Km) for glucose transport.

### Inheritance

Elsas et al. (1970) concluded that familial glucose-galactose malabsorption is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait and that heterozygotes can be detected by their reduced capacity for glucose transport. Xin and Wang (2011) found homozygous SLC5A1 mutations in affected members of an Old Order Amish pedigree.

### Mapping

Turk et al. (1991) stated that they had previously shown that SGLT1 lies on the distal q arm of chromosome 22. Using a cosmid probe for fluorescence in situ hybridization, Turk et al. (1993) refined the localization of SGLT1 to 22q13.1.

### Molecular Genetics

In members of a family with GGM, Turk et al. (1991) amplified SGLT1 cDNA and genomic DNA by PCR and sequenced the products. They found a single missense mutation in SGLT1 that cosegregated with the GGM phenotype. In Xenopus oocytes injected with the mutant cRNA, Na(+)-dependent glucose transport was completely lost. Turk et al. (1993) stated that the mutation in their 2 patients was an asp28-to-asn change, which eliminated SGLT1 transport activity in vitro.

Martin et al. (1996) screened the SGLT1 gene in 30 new patients with GGM. They used a heterologous expression system to link the mutations to the phenotype. According to the title of their report, the defects impair both SGLT1 trafficking and SGLT1 function.

Xin and Wang (2011) sequenced the 15 protein-coding exons and exon-intron boundaries of SLC5A1 in 33 Old Order Amish patients with GGM. They found 4 homozygous missense mutations: N51S, A411T, R558H, and H615Q. All 4 cosegregated with the GGM phenotype in every affected individual. The authors suggested a founder effect of the SLC5A1 mutations in the Amish.

### Diagnosis

Martin et al. (1996) performed prenatal screening in 2 pregnancies in a large consanguineous family in which 2 proband sibs carried the D28N mutation in SGLT1. They PCR-amplified exon 1 from genomic DNA and tested for D28N by EcoRV restriction digestion. A sib of the probands was heterozygous for D28N, and a cousin was not a carrier. Both children were healthy at 2 years of age and had had no diarrhea.

### Clinical Management

Turk et al. (1991) noted that the severe diarrhea and dehydration of GGM are usually fatal unless glucose and galactose are removed from the diet. All 8 Arab children described by Abdullah et al. (1996) improved clinically on a fructose-based formula and were thriving at follow-up.

### Population Genetics

Xin and Wang (2011) found the same 4 homozygous SLC5A1 missense mutations in all 33 affected members of a large Old Order Amish pedigree. They suggested a founder effect and recommended population-specific genetic testing for early diagnosis.

**.0001 GLUCOSE/GALACTOSE MALABSORPTION**  
SLC5A1, ASP28ASN [D28N; Asp28Asn]

In affected members of a family with glucose/galactose malabsorption, Turk et al. (1991) found a missense mutation in the SGLT1 gene that cosegregated with the disorder. The mutation completely abolished Na(+)-dependent glucose transport in Xenopus oocytes. Turk et al. (1993) identified the change as asp28-to-asn (D28N) and stated that it was present in 2 patients.

Martin et al. (1996) described the D28N mutation in 2 proband sibs from a large consanguineous family. In 2 later pregnancies in this family, prenatal testing by EcoRV restriction digestion showed that a sib of the probands was heterozygous for D28N and that a cousin was not a carrier. ⚠︎
