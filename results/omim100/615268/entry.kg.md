### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene (605870) on chromosome 13q12.

### Description

Cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder. Its features include cerebellar ataxia, hypotonia, intellectual disability, dysarthria and cerebellar atrophy, and some affected individuals have quadrupedal locomotion (Onat et al., 2013; Mohamadian et al., 2020). Severity varies widely. Some patients cannot sit or hold up their head and have involuntary movements and visual and hearing impairments (Narishige et al., 2022). Others learn to walk and have near-normal cognition (Damasio et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family in which 4 members had cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium syndrome with quadrupedal locomotion and cerebellar atrophy. ⚠︎

Mohamadian et al. (2020) described 3 sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family of Arab descent: a 9-year-old girl (the proband), her 15-year-old sister and her 17-year-old brother. Their parents were healthy first cousins. The sibs had nonprogressive cerebellar ataxia, severe hypotonia, intellectual disability, dysarthria and cerebellar atrophy. The proband had severe neonatal hypotonia, global psychomotor delay and feeding difficulties from infancy. She was wheelchair-bound and could not walk, sit or hold up her head. All 3 patients had an IQ of 20 to 40, delayed speech with dysarthria, absent deep tendon reflexes, and cerebellar atrophy on brain MRI. Hearing was normal, there were no seizures or optic atrophy, and metabolic investigations were normal.

Damasio et al. (2021) described 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who learned to walk on their own by age 4 years. After 16 years of follow-up, they had near-normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs and dystonia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4 who developed athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. They also had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia and severely delayed development. One sib had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. Areflexia and flattening of auditory brainstem responses developed later. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or control their head, although some nonverbal communication was preserved.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMRQ4 is autosomal recessive. Affected sibs in consanguineous families were homozygous for ATP8A2 mutations, and their unaffected parents were heterozygous carriers (Mohamadian et al., 2020; Damasio et al., 2021). Affected sibs born to nonconsanguineous parents were compound heterozygous (Narishige et al., 2022). ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Onat et al. (2013) noted an unrelated, previously identified individual with mental retardation and severe hypotonia who carried a de novo balanced t(10; 13) translocation that disrupted the ATP8A2 gene.

### Mapping

Onat et al. (2013) performed homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ. They found several homozygous regions shared by the affected members, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Ozcelik et al. (2008) studied 4 large consanguineous Turkish kindreds with quadrupedal gait (Unertan syndrome). In 2 of these families (B and C), the phenotype was not linked to chromosome 9p, where VLDLR mutations had been found in families A and D. The authors concluded that hereditary disorders with quadrupedal gait in humans are genetically heterogeneous. Ozcelik et al. (2008) returned to this genetic heterogeneity in a reply to Herz et al. and Humphrey et al.

Gulsuner et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous Turkish family with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation and cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia. Diffusion-weighted imaging and fiber tractography showed abnormalities of the cerebellum and corpus callosum, particularly atrophy of the superior, middle and inferior cerebellar peduncles. Structural MRI showed further morphometric abnormalities in cortical areas, including the corpus callosum, precentral gyrus and Brodmann areas BA6, BA44 and BA45. Homozygosity mapping linked the disorder to a 7.1-Mb region on chromosome 17p13.1-p13.3. Targeted sequencing of this region in 3 affected individuals and 2 obligate carriers identified a private homozygous missense mutation, P856L, in the WDR81 gene (614218). The mutation cosegregated with the disorder in the extended family. It lies in a highly conserved region of WDR81 between an N-terminal BEACH domain and C-terminal WD40 beta-propeller domains. WDR81 is predicted to be a transmembrane protein and is highly expressed in the cerebellum and corpus callosum, particularly in the Purkinje cell layer of the cerebellum. Gulsuner et al. (2011) noted that WDR81 was the third gene implicated in quadrupedal locomotion in humans, after VLDLR and CA8.

### Molecular Genetics

Onat et al. (2013) studied the affected members of a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ and identified a novel missense variant in the ATP8A2 gene, ile376-to-met (I376M). They first found it by targeted next-generation sequencing of one affected individual and then evaluated it by segregation analysis, population screening and prediction methods. The variant lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain.

Mohamadian et al. (2020) studied 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4. After a normal karyotype and chromosomal microarray, whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous c.1339G-A transition (NM_016529.6) in exon 14 of the ATP8A2 gene, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The mutation causes a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution at an extremely conserved residue in the ATP-binding (N) domain. It segregated with the disorder: the parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous, and 1 unaffected sib did not carry it. The mutation was absent from public databases and from 100 ethnically matched controls. Structural modeling predicted reduced protein stability and an altered ligand-binding pocket.

In 2 sibs with congenital ataxia, Damasio et al. (2021) used exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous variant in intron 17 of the ATP8A2 gene, c.1580-18C-G. Bioinformatics tools predicted that it creates a new acceptor splice site. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the variant construct used an alternative acceptor splice site and produced an aberrant transcript that retained 17 bp of intron 17. This inserted 4 amino acids and created a premature stop codon. The authors concluded that the variant was disease-causing.

In 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4, Narishige et al. (2022) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the ATP8A2 gene. One, c.1741C-T (R581X), had been reported before; the other, c.2158C-T (R720X), was novel.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Damasio et al. (2021) concluded that their findings extend the spectrum of ATP8A2-related disorders to milder forms of congenital ataxia.

### Pathogenesis

Onat et al. (2013) noted that ATP8A2 is expressed mainly in the brain and during development, particularly in the cerebellum. They suggested that ATP8A2 is involved in developing the cerebrocerebellar structures that humans need for posture and gait.

Using homology modeling, Mohamadian et al. (2020) predicted that the G447R substitution severely reduces the stability of ATP8A2 (FoldX ddG 7.94 kcal/mol). They also predicted that it alters the ligand-binding pocket next to the active site, which may lower ligand-binding affinity and catalytic activity. They suggested that the substitution could also disrupt protein folding and interaction with CDC50A (TMEM30A).

### Diagnosis

Narishige et al. (2022) noted that symptoms did not progress in early development. This makes CAMRQ4 difficult to distinguish from dyskinetic cerebral palsy, particularly in solitary cases. They suggested that visual and hearing impairments together with involuntary movements and severe developmental delay may be a clue to suspect CAMRQ4.
