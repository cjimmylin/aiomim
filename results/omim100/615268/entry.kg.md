### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene (605870) on chromosome 13q12. ⚠︎

### Description

Cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is an autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by cerebellar ataxia, intellectual disability, and cerebellar atrophy, with quadrupedal locomotion in some affected individuals (Onat et al., 2013; Mohamadian et al., 2020). Other reported features include severe hypotonia, dysarthria, areflexia, and, in some patients, athetosis, ophthalmoplegia, and visual and hearing impairment (Mohamadian et al., 2020; Narishige et al., 2022). The phenotypic spectrum also includes a less severe form of congenital ataxia with near-normal cognition (Damasio et al., 2021).

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family in which 4 members had cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium syndrome with quadrupedal locomotion and cerebellar atrophy. ⚠︎

Mohamadian et al. (2020) described 3 sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family of Arab descent born to healthy first-cousin parents: a 9-year-old girl (the proband), her 15-year-old sister, and her 17-year-old brother. They had non-progressive cerebellar ataxia, severe hypotonia, intellectual disability, dysarthria, and cerebellar atrophy. The proband had severe neonatal hypotonia, global psychomotor delay, and feeding difficulties since infancy. She used a wheelchair and was unable to walk, sit, or hold her head. All 3 patients had an IQ of 20 to 40, delayed speech with dysarthria, absent deep tendon reflexes, and cerebellar atrophy on brain MRI. Hearing was normal, there were no seizures or optic atrophy, and metabolic investigations were normal.

Damasio et al. (2021) described 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who learned to walk on their own by age 4 years. After 16 years of follow-up, they had near-normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4 who presented with athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. They also had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, and severely delayed development. One sib had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. Flattening of auditory brainstem responses and areflexia developed later. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or achieve head control, although some nonverbal communication was preserved.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMRQ4 in the families reported by Onat et al. (2013) and Mohamadian et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Both families were consanguineous. Mohamadian et al. (2020) and Damasio et al. (2021) found homozygous ATP8A2 mutations in affected sibs, and Narishige et al. (2022) found compound heterozygous mutations in affected sibs. In the family reported by Mohamadian et al. (2020), the parents were heterozygous carriers. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Onat et al. (2013) noted that an unrelated individual identified earlier, who had mental retardation and severe hypotonia, carried a de novo balanced t(10; 13) translocation that disrupted the ATP8A2 gene.

### Mapping

Onat et al. (2013) performed homozygosity mapping in a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ. They found several homozygous regions shared by the affected members, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Ozcelik et al. (2008) studied 4 large consanguineous Turkish kindreds with quadrupedal gait (Unertan syndrome), which they described as a rare phenotype associated with dysarthric speech, mental retardation, and varying degrees of cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia. VLDLR mutations had been found in families A and D. In families B and C, the phenotype was not linked to chromosome 9p. The authors concluded that hereditary disorders with quadrupedal gait in humans are genetically heterogeneous. In a reply to Herz et al. (2008) and Humphrey et al. (2008), Ozcelik et al. (2008) further discussed the genetic heterogeneity of cerebellar hypoplasia with quadrupedal locomotion.

Gulsuner et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous Turkish family with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation, and cerebro-cerebellar hypoplasia. Imaging showed abnormalities of the cerebellum and corpus callosum, including atrophy of the superior, middle, and inferior cerebellar peduncles. Additional morphometric abnormalities were seen in cortical areas, including the precentral gyrus and Brodmann areas BA6, BA44, and BA45. By homozygosity mapping, the authors linked the disorder to a 7.1-Mb region on chromosome 17p13.1-p13.3. Targeted sequencing of this region identified a homozygous P856L missense mutation in the WDR81 gene (614218) that cosegregated with the disorder. WDR81 is highly expressed in the cerebellum, particularly in the Purkinje cell layer. Gulsuner et al. (2011) noted that WDR81 was the third gene, after VLDLR and CA8, implicated in quadrupedal locomotion in humans. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

In the affected members of a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ, Onat et al. (2013) identified a novel missense variant in the ATP8A2 gene, ile376-to-met (I376M). They found it by targeted next-generation sequencing of 1 affected individual and then assessed it by segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction approaches. The variant lies in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1 E2 ATPase domain.

In 3 affected sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4, Mohamadian et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1339G-A transition (NM_016529.6) in exon 14 of the ATP8A2 gene. The mutation results in a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution at an extremely conserved residue in the ATP-binding (N) domain. Karyotype and chromosomal microarray analysis were normal, and the mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It segregated with the disease: the parents and 2 unaffected sibs were heterozygous, and 1 unaffected sib was wildtype. The mutation was absent from public databases and from 100 ethnically matched controls. Structural modeling predicted reduced protein stability and an altered ligand-binding pocket. ⚠︎

In 2 sibs with congenital ataxia, Damasio et al. (2021) used exome sequencing to identify a novel homozygous mutation in intron 17 of the ATP8A2 gene (c.1580-18C-G). Bioinformatics tools predicted that the mutation creates a new acceptor splice site. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the mutant construct used an alternative acceptor splice site and produced an aberrant transcript that retained 17 bp of intron 17. This resulted in the insertion of 4 amino acids and a premature stop codon. The authors concluded that the variant was disease-causing.

In 2 Japanese sibs with CAMRQ4, Narishige et al. (2022) used whole-exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the ATP8A2 gene. One was a previously reported c.1741C-T transition resulting in an arg581-to-ter (R581X) substitution. The other was a novel c.2158C-T transition resulting in an arg720-to-ter (R720X) substitution.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Damasio et al. (2021) reported sibs with a homozygous ATP8A2 splice site mutation who had near-normal cognition and learned to walk independently. They concluded that their findings expand ATP8A2-related disorders to include less severe forms of congenital ataxia.

### Pathogenesis

Onat et al. (2013) noted that ATP8A2 is expressed mainly in the brain and during development, particularly in the cerebellum. They suggested that ATP8A2 is involved in the development of the cerebro-cerebellar structures that humans need for posture and gait.

By homology modeling, Mohamadian et al. (2020) predicted that the G447R substitution in ATP8A2 severely reduces protein stability (FoldX ddG 7.94 kcal/mol). They also predicted that it alters the ligand-binding pocket next to the active site, which may decrease ligand-binding affinity and catalytic activity. They suggested that the mutation could also disrupt folding and interaction with CDC50A (TMEM30A).

### Diagnosis

Narishige et al. (2022) noted that symptoms did not progress in early development, which makes CAMRQ4 difficult to distinguish from dyskinetic cerebral palsy, particularly in solitary cases. They suggested that visual and hearing impairments together with involuntary movements and severe developmental delay may be a clue for suspecting CAMRQ4.
