### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome-4 (CAMRQ4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the ATP8A2 gene on chromosome 13q12.

### Description

Cerebellar ataxia, impaired intellectual development, and dysequilibrium syndrome (CAMRQ) is a genetically heterogeneous, early-onset neurodevelopmental condition characterized by nonprogressive cerebellar ataxia, intellectual disability of varying severity, and cerebellar atrophy; other possible features include severe hypotonia, delayed motor development, dysarthria, seizures, optic atrophy, and short stature, and quadrupedal locomotion has been seen in a few patients (summary by Mohamadian et al., 2020). The phenotypic spectrum of CAMRQ4, caused by ATP8A2 mutation, is broad, ranging from congenital ataxia with near-normal cognition (Damasio et al., 2021) to severe developmental delay with involuntary movements and visual and hearing impairment (Narishige et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Onat et al. (2013) studied a consanguineous Turkish family in which 4 individuals had cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium syndrome, associated with cerebellar atrophy and quadrupedal locomotion. Mohamadian et al. (2020) summarized the affected members of this family as having intellectual disability, mild cerebral and cerebellar atrophy, dysarthria, and truncal ataxia.

Mohamadian et al. (2020) reported 3 sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family of Arab descent with nonprogressive cerebellar ataxia, severe hypotonia, intellectual disability, dysarthria, and cerebellar atrophy. The proband, a 9-year-old girl born to healthy first-cousin parents, had normal weight, height, and head circumference at birth after an uncomplicated pregnancy. Her first sign was severe neonatal hypotonia, followed by delay of all psychomotor and developmental milestones. She was wheelchair-bound and could not walk, sit, or hold her head, and had had feeding difficulties since infancy. Her affected brother and sister, aged 17 and 15 years, had similar features. All 3 patients had IQ levels ranging from 20 to 40, delayed speech and language with dysarthria, and absent deep tendon reflexes; hearing examination showed no significant impairment. Metabolic investigations were normal, and brain MRI showed cerebellar atrophy in all patients. The authors' comparison table recorded onset at birth, no seizures, and no optic atrophy in these patients.

Damasio et al. (2021) described 2 sibs with congenital ataxia who acquired independent gait by 4 years of age. After 16 years of follow-up, they had near-normal cognition, cerebellar ataxia, mild pyramidal signs, and dystonia. The authors concluded that ATP8A2-related disorders include less severe forms of congenital ataxia.

Narishige et al. (2022) reported Japanese sibs who presented with athetotic movements at 1 and 2 months of age. They also had ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, feeding difficulty, hypotonia, and severely delayed development. One patient had retinal degeneration and optic atrophy. Flattening of the auditory brainstem responses and areflexia developed. At last follow-up, neither patient could sit or achieve head control, although some nonverbal communication was preserved. The symptoms did not progress in the early period of development, and the authors noted that the disorder may be difficult to distinguish from dyskinetic cerebral palsy, particularly in solitary cases.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of CAMRQ4 in the families reported by Onat et al. (2013) and Mohamadian et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance: in each consanguineous family, affected sibs carried a homozygous ATP8A2 variant, and in the family of Mohamadian et al. (2020) both parents were heterozygous carriers. Narishige et al. (2022) found compound heterozygous ATP8A2 variants in affected sibs. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Onat et al. (2013) noted that an unrelated individual with mental retardation and severe hypotonia had been found to carry a de novo balanced t(10; 13) translocation disrupting the ATP8A2 gene.

### Mapping

In a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ, Onat et al. (2013) used homozygosity mapping to identify several shared homozygous regions, including one on chromosome 13q12.

### Heterogeneity

Ozcelik et al. (2008) studied 4 large consanguineous Turkish kindreds with quadrupedal gait, dysarthric speech, mental retardation, and cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia. Mutations in the VLDLR gene were found in 2 families, whereas in the 2 other families the phenotype was not linked to chromosome 9p, indicating that hereditary disorders associated with quadrupedal gait in humans are genetically heterogeneous. Gulsuner et al. (2011) identified a mutation in the WDR81 gene in a consanguineous Turkish family with quadrupedal locomotion, mental retardation, and cerebrocerebellar hypoplasia, and noted that WDR81 was the third gene, after VLDLR and CA8, implicated in quadrupedal locomotion in humans. Onat et al. (2013) described cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and dysequilibrium syndrome as rare and heterogeneous.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a consanguineous Turkish family with CAMRQ4, Onat et al. (2013) identified a missense mutation (I376M) in the ATP8A2 gene. The mutation was found by targeted next-generation sequencing of an affected individual, followed by segregation analysis, population screening, and prediction approaches; it lay in a highly conserved C-terminal transmembrane region of the E1-E2 ATPase domain. The authors noted that ATP8A2 is expressed mainly in brain, particularly cerebellum. In a later comparison table, Mohamadian et al. (2020) listed this mutation as homozygous c.1128C-G.

In 3 sibs from a consanguineous Iranian family with CAMRQ4, Mohamadian et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1339G-A transition in exon 14 of the ATP8A2 gene, resulting in a gly447-to-arg (G447R) substitution. The mutation, which was found by whole-exome sequencing of the proband after normal karyotype and chromosomal microarray analysis, was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with the disorder: the parents, a healthy brother, and a healthy sister were heterozygous, and another healthy sister was wildtype. It was not found in public variant databases or in 100 healthy ethnically matched controls. The residue lies in a highly conserved region of the ATP-binding domain, and in silico analysis and structural modeling predicted reduced protein stability and an effect on the ligand-binding pocket.

In 2 sibs with a mild form of CAMRQ4, Damasio et al. (2021) identified a homozygous intronic variant (c.1580-18C-G) in intron 17 of the ATP8A2 gene by exome sequencing. In a minigene assay in HEK293T cells, the variant created an alternative acceptor splice site, producing an aberrant transcript that retained 17 bp of intron 17 and was predicted to result in a premature stop codon after insertion of 4 amino acids.

In Japanese sibs with severe CAMRQ4, Narishige et al. (2022) identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the ATP8A2 gene, c.1741C-T (R581X) and c.2158C-T (R720X), by whole-exome sequencing. The R581X variant had been reported previously, whereas R720X was novel.
