### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 is an autosomal recessive disorder in which seizures begin in the neonatal period or early infancy, often as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures, and are accompanied by severe developmental delay and hypotonia. Some patients have variable features outside the central nervous system, including cleft palate, joint contractures, pes equinovarus, omphalocele, skeletal abnormalities and dysmorphic features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish, Iranian and Brazilian origin with a syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. In all patients, seizures began in the first 2 months of life, mainly as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. Early EEG showed suppression-burst, burst attenuation or hypsarrhythmia. Eight patients had joint contractures and/or pes equinovarus, 7 had cleft palate and 2 had omphalocele. All living patients had profound psychomotor delay, and 4 patients died before 4 years of age. ⚠︎

In Family A, Chatron et al. (2020) described 2 sibs, a boy and a girl, born to Algerian first-cousin parents. Both had cleft soft palate. By 5 months of age the boy had developmental regression, axial hypotonia and hypsarrhythmia. At 6 years he had spastic tetraparesis and no speech. His sister had spasms from 2 days of age, and her EEG showed suppression-burst. Levetiracetam and vigabatrin controlled her seizures by 3 months of age, but she had severe developmental delay with axial hypotonia.

In Family B, Chatron et al. (2020) described a sister and brother born to Egyptian first-cousin parents. Both had omphalocele, talipes equinovarus and arthrogryposis. Both developed epileptic spasms and eye twitches at 2 weeks of age and became seizure-free on vigabatrin. The girl also had cleft soft palate, jejunal atresia, multiple dysmorphic features, a hypoplastic corpus callosum, cerebral atrophy and scoliosis. The girl died at 2 years of age and the boy at 4 years. ⚠︎

In Family C, Chatron et al. (2020) described a sister and brother born to Turkish first-cousin parents. Both had cleft palate and refractory epilepsy that began in the neonatal period with myoclonic seizures, and EEG showed suppression-burst. Brain MRI was normal in both. At 12 years of age, the sister had profound intellectual disability, tetraparesis, talipes, conductive hearing loss and photosensitivity. The brother died of refractory seizures at 2 years of age. ⚠︎

In Family D, an Iranian family in which the parents were second cousins, Chatron et al. (2020) described 2 sibs. Both had myoclonic seizures and epileptic spasms from the first week of life and profound developmental delay. Neither had cleft palate, joint contractures or pes equinovarus. In Family E, a Turkish first-cousin family, they described 2 brothers. The younger brother, the proband, had cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, flexion contractures and facial dysmorphism, with epileptic spasms from 1 month of age and hypsarrhythmia. His older brother was born at 29 weeks' gestation and had cleft palate, myoclonic seizures, congenital heart disease and pes equinovarus. He died on day 9 of life. ⚠︎

In Family F, Chatron et al. (2020) described a 7-year-old girl born to Brazilian first-cousin parents. Facial twitches began at 7 days of age, and EEG showed suppression-burst. Her seizures remained refractory until vigabatrin was started at 11 months of age. She had profound developmental delay, choreoathetotic movements, lower limb spasticity and severe scoliosis. Brain imaging showed cerebral atrophy with ventricular dilation. She had no cleft palate and no dysmorphic facial features.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients (3 male and 3 female) from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian and Turkish ancestry. Four of the families were consanguineous. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures. Some patients also had epileptic spasms, focal non-motor seizures or bilateral motor seizures. Medication controlled the seizures in 3 patients, and 3 patients had drug-resistant seizures. At seizure onset, EEG showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Brain MRI was normal except in 2 patients: 1 had slight ventricular enlargement and 1 had moderate global atrophy. All patients had severe developmental delay, and most never developed speech or nonverbal communication. Intellectual disability was severe in 5 patients and moderate in 1. Five of the 6 patients had reduced muscle strength or tone. Four could not hold their head up, sit or crawl, and 2 depended on a nasogastric tube because of dysphagia. Four patients had mild facial dysmorphism, and 1 had cleft palate. Other features occurred without a common pattern: hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, congenital hip dislocation, high myopia and hearing impairment. Metabolic workups were normal. Three of the 6 patients became seizure-free, yet all had severe intellectual disability, so cognitive development did not improve with seizure control. The authors hypothesized that GAD1 variants affect development independently of seizure control. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) described an Egyptian girl (Family E, patient III-3) born to first-cousin parents. She was the only patient in their series born with cleft palate. She also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with a shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus, hearing impairment, severe hypotonia and global brain atrophy. A similarly affected sib died without genetic testing.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 is autosomal recessive. In the 6 families reported by Chatron et al. (2020), the GAD1 variants segregated with the disease consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The authors also noted that GAD1 has a gnomAD pRec score of 0.934. In 6 further families, Neuray et al. (2020) confirmed by Sanger sequencing that the GAD1 variants segregated with disease according to autosomal recessive inheritance. The variants were homozygous in 5 families and compound heterozygous in 1.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 affected sibs from a consanguineous Algerian family (Family A) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous splice site mutation in the GAD1 gene (c.1414-1G-C). Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, which segregated with the disease. cDNA analysis in parental lymphoblastoid cells showed no normal mRNA from the mutant allele and 3 aberrant isoforms (see the allelic variant).

In the affected sibs of an Egyptian family (Family B), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA). The deletion removes a highly conserved lysine (Lys232del). The mutant protein was expressed at wildtype levels in a cell-free assay. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing and could not formally show loss of function.

In the affected sibs of a Turkish family (Family C), Chatron et al. (2020) used whole-genome sequencing to identify a homozygous 5-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene (c.812_816delTTAAG) that causes a frameshift. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutation segregated with the disease.

In affected members of a Persian family (Family D) and a Turkish family (Family E), Chatron et al. (2020) identified the same homozygous nonsense mutation in the GAD1 gene (R531X). Given that the 2 families lived relatively close geographically, the authors suggested that they might share a common haplotype.

In a Brazilian girl (Family F), Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous missense mutation in the GAD1 gene (E509K). The mutation affects an extremely well-conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain.

Chatron et al. (2020) noted that 4 of their 6 families carried truncating variants, which supports a loss-of-function mechanism.

In 6 patients from 6 unrelated families, Neuray et al. (2020) identified homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene. The approach included exome sequencing, and Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations segregated with disease. In 5 families the patients were homozygous: N564S in a Persian girl, F324C in a Pakistani boy, T347M in a Sudanese boy, Y29X in an Egyptian patient, and a 1-bp deletion (c.568delC) in a Turkish patient. An African American boy was compound heterozygous for R531X and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) observed phenotypic variability between Families D and E, which carried the same R531X mutation. Family D lacked pes equinovarus, joint contractures and cleft palate. The authors suggested that genetic background or environmental factors affecting fetal movement in utero might explain the difference.

Neuray et al. (2020) noted that the Persian girl homozygous for the N564S mutation had a relatively mild phenotype. She had moderate intellectual disability and simple speech, and her seizures became controlled. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) suggested that the non-neural features of DEE89 are secondary to reduced fetal movement caused by severe central nervous system involvement, as described in Gad1-null mice. These features include cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus and joint contractures. They noted that biallelic loss-of-function variants in humans fully reproduce the combination of epilepsy, cleft palate and omphalocele seen in animal models.

Neuray et al. (2020) concluded that the phenotype probably results from loss of function. Their evidence was 4 null variants, missense variants located in the PLP-binding and C-terminal catalytic domains, and the moderate intolerance of GAD1 to loss-of-function variation. They suggested that reduced GABA synthesis disrupts the balance of GABAergic signaling and so predisposes to seizures. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a remarkably good seizure response to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 treated patients. They cautioned that the response may reflect seizure type, because epileptic spasms respond well to vigabatrin whatever their cause. They called for vigabatrin to be validated as a targeted treatment.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported rational therapy with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet in a patient with GAD1 deficiency.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) used gene targeting to generate mice lacking Gad67. Gad67 -/- mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67 -/- mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%, but the brain showed no discernible defects. The authors concluded that this was the first demonstration that GAD67-derived GABA has a role in the development of nonneural tissue. By contrast, their earlier Gad65 -/- mice showed no change in brain GABA content or behavior apart from a slight increase in susceptibility to seizures.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 sibs with DEE89 from a consanguineous Algerian family (Family A), Chatron et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous G-to-C transversion in the GAD1 gene (c.1414-1G-C) at a splice site. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, which segregated with the disease. cDNA analysis in parental lymphoblastoid cells showed no normal mRNA from the mutant allele and 3 aberrant isoforms. One isoform skipped exon 15, one retained 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacked the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delaga; p.K232del]

In the affected sibs of an Egyptian family (Family B) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA). The deletion removes a highly conserved lysine (Lys232del; reported as Lys231del in the table). The mutation was absent from public databases, and in silico tools predicted that it disrupts splicing. In a cell-free assay the mutant protein was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing and could not formally show loss of function, but they considered the variant pathogenic given the phenotype.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, NT812 [c.812_816delttaag; p.V271D]

In the affected sibs of a Turkish family (Family C) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) used whole-genome sequencing to identify a homozygous 5-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene (c.812_816delTTAAG). The deletion causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutation segregated with the disease.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; g.171715383C>T; p.R531*]

In affected members of a Persian family (Family D) and a Turkish family (Family E) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene that results in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution. Given that the 2 families lived relatively close geographically, the authors suggested that they might share a common haplotype. Phenotypes differed between the families: affected members of Family D lacked pes equinovarus, joint contractures and cleft palate.

In an African American boy (Family C) with DEE89, Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene. One was the R531X mutation, which had a CADD score of 43 and had been reported only in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. The other was a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). His father was not available for testing.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.E509K]

In a Brazilian girl (Family F) with DEE89 who was born to first-cousin parents, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene that results in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. The mutation was absent from gnomAD and affects an extremely well-conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that the mutant residue disrupts salt bridges with arg316, lys318 and lys389 and no longer fits into the domain core.

**.0006 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ASN564SER [c.1691A>G; g.171716298A>G; p.N564S]

In a Persian girl (Family A) with DEE89 who was born to double-first-cousin parents, Neuray et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1691A-G transition in the GAD1 gene that results in an asn564-to-ser (N564S) substitution. Her phenotype was relatively mild, with moderate intellectual disability, simple speech, and seizures that became controlled. The variant had been seen once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. It had a CADD score of 26.1, and SIFT, MutationTaster and PolyPhen-2 predicted it to be pathogenic. It lies in the C-terminal catalytic domain, and in silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability. ⚠︎

GAD1, THR347MET [c.1040C>T; g.171704223C>T; p.T347M]

In a Sudanese boy (Family D) with DEE89 whose parents were second cousins, Neuray et al.
