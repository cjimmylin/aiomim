### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene (605363) on chromosome 2q31.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive disorder. Seizures begin in early infancy, and patients have hypotonia and severe developmental delay. Some patients also have variable non-CNS features, such as skeletal abnormalities, dysmorphic features and cleft palate (Neuray et al., 2020). Chatron et al. (2020) described the disorder as a neonatal-onset syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. In their patients, epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures began in the first 2 months of life and were accompanied by profound psychomotor delay. Some patients also had cleft palate, joint contractures, pes equinovarus or omphalocele.

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients with a syndromic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy from 6 unrelated consanguineous families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish, Iranian and Brazilian origin. Seizures began in the first 2 months of life in all patients, mainly as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. Early EEG showed suppression-burst, burst attenuation or hypsarrhythmia. Eight patients had joint contractures and/or pes equinovarus, 7 had cleft palate and 2 had omphalocele. All living patients had profound psychomotor delay, and 4 patients died before 4 years of age.

Family A of Chatron et al. (2020) had 2 affected sibs, a boy and a girl, born to Algerian first-cousin parents. Both had cleft soft palate. By 5 months of age the boy had developmental regression, axial hypotonia and hypsarrhythmia. At 6 years he had spastic tetraparesis and no speech. His sister had spasms from 2 days of age, and her EEG showed a suppression-burst pattern. Her seizures were controlled by 3 months of age with levetiracetam and vigabatrin, but she had severe developmental delay with axial hypotonia.

Family B of Chatron et al. (2020) had an affected sister and brother born to Egyptian first-cousin parents. Both sibs had omphalocele, talipes equinovarus and arthrogryposis. Both developed epileptic spasms and eye twitches at 2 weeks of age and became seizure-free on vigabatrin. The girl also had cleft soft palate, jejunal atresia, multiple dysmorphic features, a hypoplastic corpus callosum, cerebral atrophy and scoliosis. She died at 2 years of age, and her brother died at 4 years.

Family C of Chatron et al. (2020) had an affected sister and brother born to Turkish first-cousin parents. Both had cleft palate and refractory epilepsy that began in the neonatal period with myoclonic seizures, with a suppression-burst EEG. Brain MRI was normal in both sibs. At 12 years of age the sister had profound intellectual disability, tetraparesis, talipes, conductive hearing loss and photosensitivity. The brother died of refractory seizures at 2 years of age. ⚠︎

Family D of Chatron et al. (2020) was an Iranian second-cousin family with 2 affected sibs. Both had myoclonic seizures and epileptic spasms beginning in the first week of life, along with profound developmental delay. Neither sib had cleft palate, joint contractures or pes equinovarus. Family E was a Turkish first-cousin family with 2 affected brothers. The younger brother, the proband, had cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, flexion contractures, facial dysmorphism and hypsarrhythmia, and he had epileptic spasms from 1 month of age. His older brother was born at 29 weeks' gestation with cleft palate, myoclonic seizures, congenital heart disease and pes equinovarus, and he died on day 9. ⚠︎

Family F of Chatron et al. (2020) consisted of a 7-year-old girl born to Brazilian first-cousin parents. Facial twitches began at 7 days of age, and EEG showed suppression-burst. Her seizures were refractory until vigabatrin was started at 11 months of age. She had profound developmental delay, choreoathetotic movements, lower limb spasticity and severe scoliosis. Brain imaging showed cerebral atrophy with ventricular dilation. She had no cleft palate and no dysmorphic facial features.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients (3 male, 3 female) from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian and Turkish ancestry. Four of the families were consanguineous. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures. Some patients also had epileptic spasms, focal non-motor seizures or bilateral motor seizures. Seizures were controlled with medication in 3 patients and were drug-resistant in the other 3. At seizure onset, EEG showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Brain MRI was normal in 4 patients. Of the other 2, 1 had slight ventricular enlargement and 1 had moderate global atrophy.

All 6 patients of Neuray et al. (2020) had severe developmental delay. Most never developed speech or non-verbal communication. Intellectual disability was severe in 5 patients and moderate in 1. Five patients had reduced muscle strength or tone. Of these, 4 lacked head control or could not sit or crawl, and 2 depended on a nasogastric tube because of dysphagia. Four patients had mild facial dysmorphism, and 1 had cleft palate. Other features occurred without a common pattern: hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs, congenital hip dislocation, high myopia and hearing impairment. Metabolic workups were normal. Three of the 6 patients became seizure-free, yet all had severe intellectual disability, so cognitive development did not improve even when seizures were controlled. The authors hypothesized that GAD1 variants affect development independently of seizure control. ⚠︎

One patient of Neuray et al. (2020) was an Egyptian girl born to first-cousin parents (Family E, patient III-3). She was the only patient in their series born with cleft palate. She also had congenital bilateral hip dislocation with a shallow acetabulum, talipes equinovarus, hearing impairment, severe hypotonia and global brain atrophy. A similarly affected sib died without genetic testing.

### Inheritance

Autosomal recessive inheritance of DEE89 was established by 2 reports. In all 6 families studied by Chatron et al. (2020), the GAD1 variants segregated with the disease in a manner consistent with this mode of inheritance. Neuray et al. (2020) likewise found autosomal recessive segregation, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. In their series the variants were homozygous in 5 families and compound heterozygous in 1.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) found that the GAD1 variants segregated with the disease in all 6 of their consanguineous families. Four of the 6 families carried truncating variants, which supports a loss-of-function mechanism. They also noted that GAD1 has a gnomAD pRec score of 0.934.

In the 2 affected sibs from Algerian Family A, Chatron et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous splice site mutation in the GAD1 gene, c.1414-1G-C. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant, and it segregated with the disease. Analysis of cDNA from parental lymphoblastoid cells showed no normal mRNA from the mutant allele. Instead there were 3 aberrant isoforms: one skipped exon 15, one retained 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacked the first 7 bases of exon 15.

In the affected sibs of Egyptian Family B, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of GAD1, c.695_697delAGA. The deletion removes a highly conserved lysine (Lys232del). The variant was absent from public databases, and in silico tools predicted that it disrupts splicing. In a cell-free assay the mutant protein was expressed at levels comparable to wildtype. The authors noted that this assay bypasses splicing and so could not formally demonstrate loss of function, but they considered the variant pathogenic given the phenotype.

In the affected sibs of Turkish Family C, Chatron et al. (2020) used whole-genome sequencing to identify a homozygous 5-bp deletion in GAD1, c.812_816delTTAAG, which causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the variant segregated with the disease.

Chatron et al. (2020) identified the same homozygous nonsense mutation in GAD1, c.1591C-T (R531X), in Persian Family D and in Turkish Family E. Given the relative geographic proximity of the 2 families, they suggested that a common haplotype was possible.

In the Brazilian patient of Family F, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in GAD1, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. The variant was absent from gnomAD and affects an extremely well-conserved residue in the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. Structural modeling predicted that the change disrupts salt bridges with Arg316, Lys318 and Lys389 and that the mutant residue no longer fits into the core of the domain.

In a Persian girl (Family A) born to double-first-cousin parents, Neuray et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous c.1691A-G transition in GAD1, resulting in an asn564-to-ser (N564S) substitution. Her phenotype was relatively mild, with moderate intellectual disability, simple speech, and seizures that became controlled. The variant had been seen once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. It had a CADD score of 26.1 and was predicted to be pathogenic by SIFT, MutationTaster and PolyPhen-2. It lies in the C-terminal catalytic domain, and in silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability. ⚠︎

In a Pakistani boy from a consanguineous family (Family B), Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.971T-G transversion in GAD1, resulting in a phe324-to-cys (F324C) substitution. The variant had a gnomAD minor allele frequency of 0.00000796 (2 heterozygotes) and a CADD score of 28, and all prediction tools rated it damaging. It lies in the PLP-binding domain, and in silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability.

In an African American boy (Family C) whose parents were not reported to be consanguineous, Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in GAD1. The first was a c.1591C-T nonsense mutation (R531X), which had a CADD score of 43 and had been reported only in the heterozygous state in gnomAD. The second was a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC) causing a frameshift (Leu224SerfsTer5). His mother carried c.670delC, and his father was not available for testing.

In a Sudanese boy whose parents were second cousins (Family D), Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1040C-T transition in GAD1, resulting in a thr347-to-met (T347M) substitution. The variant had been reported twice in the heterozygous state in gnomAD and had a CADD score of 29.1. In silico analysis predicted that it breaks H-bonds in the PLP-binding domain. A heterozygous sister had a different neurodevelopmental condition without seizures.

In an Egyptian patient whose parents were first cousins (Family E), Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.87C-G transversion in GAD1, resulting in a tyr29-to-ter (Y29X) substitution. The variant was absent from all population databases queried and had a CADD score of 35. The authors predicted that it causes nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and complete loss of function.

In a Turkish patient whose parents were first cousins (Family F), Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion in GAD1 (c.568delC), causing a frameshift (Gln190SerfsTer11). Both parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from gnomAD and the other population databases queried, and it was classified as pathogenic under ACMG guidelines.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) noted phenotypic variability between Families D and E, which carry the same R531X mutation. Family D lacked the pes equinovarus, joint contractures and cleft palate seen in Family E. The authors suggested that genetic background, or environmental factors affecting fetal movement in utero, might explain this difference. ⚠︎

Neuray et al. (2020) noted that their patient homozygous for the N564S missense mutation had a relatively mild phenotype. She had moderate intellectual disability, simple speech, and seizures that became controlled. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) suggested that the non-neural features (cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus and joint contractures) are secondary to reduced fetal movement caused by severe CNS involvement, as described in Gad1-null mice. They noted that biallelic loss-of-function variants in humans fully reproduce the combination of epilepsy, cleft palate and omphalocele seen in animal models.

Neuray et al. (2020) concluded that the phenotype probably results from loss of function. Their evidence was the presence of 4 null variants, the location of the missense variants in the PLP-binding and C-terminal catalytic domains, and the moderate intolerance of GAD1 to loss-of-function variation. They suggested that reduced GABA synthesis disrupts the balance of GABAergic signaling and thereby predisposes to seizures.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a remarkably good seizure response to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 treated patients. They cautioned that the response might reflect seizure type, because epileptic spasms respond well to vigabatrin whatever their cause. They called for validation of vigabatrin as a targeted treatment.

von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported rational therapy with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet in a patient with GAD1 deficiency.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) used gene targeting to generate mice lacking GAD67. Gad67 -/- mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67 -/- mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7%, but the brain showed no discernible defects. The authors concluded that this was the first demonstration of a role for GAD67-derived GABA in the development of nonneural tissue. They noted that their earlier GAD65 -/- mice, in contrast, showed no change in brain GABA content or behavior apart from a slight increase in susceptibility to seizures.
