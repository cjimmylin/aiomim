### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the GAD1 gene, which encodes the glutamate decarboxylase isoform GAD67.

### Description

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy-89 (DEE89) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by loss of function of GAD67, the enzyme that converts glutamic acid to GABA. Seizures begin in the neonatal period or early infancy, often as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures, with suppression-burst or hypsarrhythmia on EEG. Affected children have severe to profound developmental delay and abnormal muscle tone. Some patients also have cleft palate, joint contractures or pes equinovarus, omphalocele, scoliosis or dysmorphic facial features (Chatron et al., 2020; Neuray et al., 2020).

### Clinical Features

Chatron et al. (2020) reported 11 patients from 6 unrelated families of Algerian, Egyptian, Turkish (2 families), Iranian and Brazilian origin. All patients were born to consanguineous parents who were first or second cousins. Seizures began in the first 2 months of life in all patients. In the 10 patients whose early disease history was available, onset was in the first month, mainly as epileptic spasms or myoclonic seizures. EEG at onset showed a suppression-burst pattern or a variant with interburst attenuation in 7 of 8 patients recorded in the neonatal period. The 2 patients first recorded later had hypsarrhythmia. Antiepileptic drugs eventually controlled seizures in 6 patients, and in 5 of these, seizures were controlled for long periods after vigabatrin was added. All living patients had severe psychomotor delay, and intellectual disability was profound in every patient who could be assessed. Head circumference was normal in all but 1 patient. Brain MRI was normal in 5 patients. Abnormal findings in other patients included a hypoplastic corpus callosum with dilated subarachnoid spaces in 1 patient and mild cerebral atrophy with ventricular enlargement at 6 years in another. Seven patients had cleft palate, 6 had joint contractures, 5 had pes equinovarus and 4 had scoliosis. Three patients had dysmorphic facial features with no clear common elements. Chatron et al. (2020) considered markedly increased muscle tone to be a core feature seen in all patients, although axial hypotonia was also frequent. Four patients had died before 4 years of age.

Within the cohort of Chatron et al. (2020), the 2 affected sibs of Egyptian Family B both had omphalocele, pes equinovarus, arthrogryposis and multiple dysmorphic features, and both had seizures from 2 weeks of age. The sister also had cleft soft palate, jejunal atresia, a branchial fistula, multiple hemangiomas, camptodactyly, scoliosis and abnormal external genitalia. She died at 2 years of age of failure to thrive and respiratory infection. Her brother died at 4 years of age. In Turkish Family E, the proband had facial dysmorphism, cleft palate, Hirschsprung disease, pes equinovarus, flexion contractures, cervical and axillary webbing, and epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia. His older brother was born at 29 weeks' gestation with cleft palate, myoclonic seizures, congenital heart disease and pes equinovarus, and died of sepsis on postnatal day 9. In Turkish Family C, both sibs had refractory epilepsy from the neonatal period and cleft palate. The brother died of refractory seizures at 2 years of age, after his seizures worsened on a ketogenic diet.

Neuray et al. (2020) reported 6 patients from 6 unrelated families of Persian, Pakistani, African American, Sudanese, Egyptian and Turkish ancestry. The text stated that 4 patients were born to consanguineous parents, but the clinical table listed parental consanguinity in 5 families. All patients were born at term after a normal pregnancy. Seizures began between 2 and 6 months of age and were mainly focal motor seizures with or without impaired awareness. Two patients also had epileptic spasms, 4 had focal non-motor seizures and 5 had bilateral motor seizures. Seizures were controlled with medication in 3 patients and were drug-resistant in the other 3. EEG at onset showed burst suppression in 2 patients, diffuse slowing with multifocal and generalized sharp waves in 2, and hypsarrhythmia in 2. Cranial MRI was normal in all but 2 patients, 1 with slight ventricular enlargement and 1 with moderate global atrophy. All patients had severe developmental delay. Most achieved no speech, and only 1 developed simple speech. Five of the 6 had reduced muscle strength, 4 had slight dysmorphic facial features, and only 1 had cleft palate. Other features without clear common elements included hirsutism, kidney stones, urogenital malformations, diastasis recti, reduced head circumference, clinodactyly, short arms, arthrogryposis of the lower limbs and congenital hip dislocation. Metabolic workups were normal. Neuray et al. (2020) noted that all patients still had severe intellectual disability despite seizure freedom in 3 of them.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DEE89 in the families reported by Chatron et al. (2020) and Neuray et al. (2020) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. In both studies, Sanger sequencing confirmed that the GAD1 variants segregated with the disease.

### Molecular Genetics

Chatron et al. (2020) studied 11 patients with DEE89 from 6 consanguineous families, ascertained through GeneMatcher. Using whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing, they identified homozygous GAD1 mutations in all affected individuals. Four families carried mutations predicted to truncate the protein: a splice site mutation (c.1414-1G-C) in Family A, a 5-bp deletion (c.812_816delTTAAG) in Family C, and a nonsense mutation (R531X) shared by Families D and E. Family B carried an in-frame 3-bp deletion (c.695_697delAGA; K232del), and Family F carried a missense mutation (E509K). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation in all families. In Family A, cDNA analysis found no normal mRNA product and showed 3 aberrant transcripts. In a cell-free expression system, the K232del protein was expressed at levels comparable to wild type, so loss of function could not be formally demonstrated for this variant.

Neuray et al. (2020) used whole-exome sequencing to identify biallelic GAD1 mutations in 6 patients with DEE89 from 6 unrelated families. Five patients carried homozygous variants: 3 missense mutations (F324C, T347M, N564S), a nonsense mutation (Y29X) and a 1-bp deletion (c.568delC). The sixth patient, from an African American family, was compound heterozygous for R531X and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). Sanger sequencing confirmed segregation with a recessive pattern. All of the variants were ultrarare. In silico analysis predicted that the missense variants reduce protein stability or disrupt binding of pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate. Neuray et al. (2020) concluded that the phenotype is likely caused by loss of function. No functional studies of the variants were reported.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Chatron et al. (2020) noted phenotypic variability between Families D and E, which carried the same homozygous R531X mutation. In particular, pes equinovarus and joint contractures were absent in Family D. The authors suggested that genetic background or environmental factors affecting movement in utero might explain the difference.

Neuray et al. (2020) discussed an earlier report of a homozygous GAD1 missense variant (S12C), located in the N-terminal domain, in a family with spastic cerebral palsy. They noted conflicting pathogenicity predictions for this variant and suggested that variants in the pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate-binding and C-terminal domains cause a more severe deficiency of GAD67 activity.

### Pathogenesis

Chatron et al. (2020) proposed that the non-neural features of DEE89, namely cleft palate, omphalocele, pes equinovarus and joint contractures, are probably secondary to reduced fetal movements caused by severe CNS involvement. Neuray et al. (2020), in contrast, cited Gad1 expression in non-neural embryonic tissues as support for a possible primary role of impaired GAD1 function in non-neural defects, and stated that further studies were needed to confirm this in humans.

### Clinical Management

Chatron et al. (2020) observed a remarkably good seizure response to vigabatrin in 5 of 7 patients treated with it. Vigabatrin inhibits GABA transaminase. The authors cautioned that this response seemed to depend on seizure type, since patients with epileptic spasms appeared to benefit most, and spasms respond well to vigabatrin whatever the cause. Von Hardenberg et al. (2020) reported treatment with vigabatrin and a ketogenic diet in a patient with GAD1 deficiency; only the title of this report was available.

### Animal Model

Asada et al. (1997) generated mice lacking GAD67. These mice were born at the expected frequency but died of severe cleft palate during the first morning after birth. In the cerebral cortex of newborn Gad67 -/- mice, GAD activity was reduced to 20% and GABA content to 7% of normal, but the brain showed no discernible defects.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, IVS14AS, G-C, -1 [c.1414-1G>C]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Algerian family (Family A) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous G-to-C transversion at the acceptor splice site of exon 15 of the GAD1 gene (c.1414-1G-C). Both sibs had cleft soft palate. cDNA analysis of lymphoblastoid cells from both parents found no normal mRNA product and showed 3 aberrant isoforms: one with exon 15 skipped, one retaining 76 bases of intron 14, and one lacking the first 7 bases of exon 15.

**.0002 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 3-BP DEL, 695AGA [c.695_697delAGA; p.(Lys232del); p.(Lys231del)]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Egyptian family (Family B) with DEE89, omphalocele, pes equinovarus and arthrogryposis, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 3-bp deletion in exon 7 of the GAD1 gene (c.695_697delAGA). The text described this as deleting a highly conserved lysine at position 232 (Lys232del), whereas the clinical table gave the protein change as Lys231del. The variant was absent from public databases. In a cell-free expression system, the mutant protein was expressed at a level comparable to wild type. The authors noted that this assay could not exclude an effect on splicing, which in silico tools predicted.

**.0003 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, 5-BP DEL, 812TTAAG [c.812_816delTTAAG; p.(Val271Aspfs*9)]

In 2 sibs from a consanguineous Turkish family (Family C) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous 5-bp deletion in the GAD1 gene (c.812_816delTTAAG). The deletion causes a frameshift (Val271AspfsTer9). Both sibs had refractory seizures from the neonatal period and cleft palate.

**.0004 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, ARG531TER [c.1591C>T; p.(Arg531*)]

In affected members of 2 consanguineous families with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1591C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in an arg531-to-ter (R531X) substitution. One family was Iranian (Family D) and the other Turkish (Family E). The authors suggested that the 2 families might share a common haplotype.

In an African American boy with DEE89 (Family C), Neuray et al. (2020) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the GAD1 gene: R531X and a 1-bp deletion (c.670delC). His mother carried the c.670delC variant, and his father was not available for testing.

**.0005 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**  
GAD1, GLU509LYS [c.1525G>A; p.(Glu509Lys)]

In a girl from a consanguineous Brazilian family (Family F) with DEE89, Chatron et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1525G-A transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a glu509-to-lys (E509K) substitution. The affected residue is highly conserved and lies within the pyridoxal-dependent decarboxylase domain. The variant was absent from gnomAD. Structural modeling predicted that it disrupts salt bridges and no longer fits into the domain core.

**.0006 DEVELOPMENTAL AND EPILEPTIC ENCEPHALOPATHY 89**

In a Persian girl born to double first-cousin parents (Family A), Neuray et al. The variant was reported once in the heterozygous state in gnomAD, and in silico analysis predicted reduced protein stability. She had seizure onset at 2 months, moderate intellectual disability and simple speech at 4 years.

In a Sudanese boy born to second-cousin parents (Family D), Neuray et al. (2020) identified a homozygous c.1040C-T transition in the GAD1 gene, resulting in a thr347-to-met (T347M) substitution.
