### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN9A gene (603415) on chromosome 2q24.3.

### Description

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEPD), formerly called familial rectal pain, is an inherited condition characterized by paroxysms of rectal, ocular, or submandibular pain with flushing (Fertleman et al., 2006). Fertleman et al. (2007) described it as an autosomal dominant sodium channelopathy involving SCN9A. Onset is in the neonatal period or infancy and the disorder persists throughout life. Autonomic features such as flushing, harlequin color change, tonic attacks, and syncope predominate early, while attacks of excruciating deep burning pain characterize later life (Fertleman et al., 2007).

### Nomenclature

Fertleman et al. (2006) proposed the name paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEPD) for the condition previously known as familial rectal pain (FRP). Fertleman and Ferrie (2006) likewise noted that the disorder formerly called familial rectal pain syndrome had been renamed paroxysmal extreme pain disorder, and Fertleman et al. (2007) used this name for the condition previously called familial rectal pain syndrome. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hayden and Grossman (1959) reported a family with rectal, ocular, and submaxillary pain. They described the condition as a familial autonomic disorder related to proctalgia fugax.

Familial rectal pain was reported by Dugan (1972) and by Mann and Cree (1972).

Fertleman et al. (2007) described the clinical phenotype of 77 individuals from 15 families with paroxysmal extreme pain disorder. Clinical details were collected through an international consortium, and 14 affected individuals completed a validated pain questionnaire. Onset was in the neonatal period or infancy, and the disorder persisted throughout life. Early on, autonomic features predominated: all patients had skin flushing, and most had harlequin color change and tonic attacks. Dramatic syncopes with bradycardia, sometimes with asystole, were common. Later in life, the disorder was marked by attacks of excruciating deep burning pain. The pain was often in the rectal, ocular, or jaw areas but could also be diffuse. Attacks were triggered by defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion.

Meglič et al. (2014) described a 3-year-old girl with an unusual presentation of PEPD: extremely painful voiding (micturition) that had been present since birth. Her father had occasionally had some pain in his jaw while yawning since childhood.

### Inheritance

Fertleman et al. (2007) described paroxysmal extreme pain disorder as an autosomal dominant disorder. In the family reported by Meglič et al. (2014), the heterozygous SCN9A mutation in the affected child was inherited from her mildly affected father, consistent with autosomal dominant transmission.

### Molecular Genetics

Fertleman et al. (2006) carried out a genome-wide linkage search followed by mutation analysis of the candidate gene SCN9A, which encodes the sodium channel Nav1.7. They identified 8 missense mutations in 11 families with PEPD and in 2 sporadic cases. In vitro, 3 of the mutant Nav1.7 channels showed reduced fast inactivation, leading to a persistent sodium current.

In a 3-year-old girl with PEPD presenting with painful micturition, Meglič et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.554G-A transition in the SCN9A gene, resulting in an arg185-to-his (R185H) substitution. The mutation was inherited from her mildly affected father, who also carried it. The authors noted that the same mutation had been found in patients with small fiber sensory neuropathy, and suggested that intra- and interfamilial phenotypic diversity associated with gain-of-function Nav1.7 variants may explain their case.

### Pathogenesis

Fertleman et al. (2006) tested 3 of the PEPD mutant Nav1.7 channels in vitro and found reduced fast inactivation, which led to a persistent sodium current. By contrast, SCN9A mutations that cause primary erythermalgia are associated with more negative activation thresholds. The authors concluded that PEPD and primary erythermalgia are allelic disorders with distinct biophysical mechanisms and represent a separate class of peripheral neuronal sodium channelopathy.

### Diagnosis

Fertleman et al. (2007) noted that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder is most likely to be misdiagnosed as epilepsy. In infancy in particular, it may be misdiagnosed as hyperekplexia or as reflex anoxic seizures.

### Clinical Management

Fertleman et al. (2006) noted that carbamazepine is effective in PEPD but not in primary erythermalgia. In vitro, carbamazepine selectively blocked the persistent current of the PEPD mutant channels but did not affect the negative activation threshold of a primary erythermalgia mutant.

Fertleman et al. (2007) found that carbamazepine was effective in almost all patients who tried it, but the response was often incomplete.

Meglič et al. (2014) reported that carbamazepine significantly reduced pain in a 3-year-old girl with PEPD and painful voiding.
