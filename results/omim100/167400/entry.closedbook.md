### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN9A gene (603415) on chromosome 2q24.

### Description

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder is an autosomal dominant neuropathic pain disorder that starts in the neonatal period or infancy and lasts throughout life. It is marked by attacks of severe burning pain in the rectal, ocular, and submandibular areas. The attacks come with autonomic signs such as skin flushing, often in a harlequin pattern. Attacks can be set off by defecation, cold wind, eating, or emotion. In infants, attacks may include tonic nonepileptic episodes with bradycardia and sometimes asystole. The disorder was formerly called familial rectal pain syndrome. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Fertleman et al. (2006) proposed the name 'paroxysmal extreme pain disorder' in place of 'familial rectal pain syndrome.' Their reason was that the pain is not limited to the rectum and also affects the eye and jaw areas. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Hayden and Grossman (1959) described a family in which affected members, across several generations, had attacks of pain in the rectum, eyes, and submandibular region together with flushing. ✗

Fertleman et al. (2007) gave a detailed clinical description of affected members from a series of families. In the neonatal period and infancy, the main features were autonomic: skin flushing, harlequin color change, and tonic attacks. Some infants also had syncope with bradycardia and occasional asystole. Excruciating rectal pain followed, typically triggered by defecation. Later in life, ocular and submandibular pain became more prominent, triggered by cold or eating. Carbamazepine helped some patients, at least in part. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, as seen in the multigenerational families reported by Hayden and Grossman (1959) and Fertleman et al. (2006). ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Fertleman et al. (2006) identified heterozygous missense mutations in the SCN9A gene, which encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.7, in families with paroxysmal extreme pain disorder. The mutations included I1461T and T1464I, which lie in the inactivation gate motif of the domain III-IV linker, and M1627K. Expressed in cells, the mutant channels showed impaired fast inactivation and a persistent sodium current. This differs from the hyperpolarizing shift in activation seen with SCN9A mutations that cause primary erythromelalgia. ✗

Jarecki et al. (2010) reported that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder mutations in Nav1.7 increased resurgent sodium currents. They suggested that this may add to neuronal hyperexcitability. ✗

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Some SCN9A mutations produce a mixed phenotype with features of both paroxysmal extreme pain disorder and erythromelalgia. Estacion et al. (2008) reported such an overlap with the A1632E mutation, which affected both activation and fast inactivation of the channel. ✗

SCN9A, ILE1461THR [p.Ile1461Thr]
