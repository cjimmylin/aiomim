### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that paroxysmal extreme pain disorder (PEXPD) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the SCN9A gene, which encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.7.

### Description

Paroxysmal extreme pain disorder, previously known as familial rectal pain, is an inherited condition characterized by paroxysms of rectal, ocular, or submandibular pain with flushing (Fertleman et al., 2006). Onset is in the neonatal period or infancy and the disorder persists throughout life; autonomic manifestations predominate early, and attacks of excruciating deep burning pain characterize the later course (Fertleman et al., 2007). The disorder is a sodium channelopathy caused by mutation in SCN9A (Fertleman et al., 2006).

### Nomenclature

Fertleman and Ferrie (2006) proposed that the condition previously called familial rectal pain syndrome be renamed paroxysmal extreme pain disorder.

### Clinical Features

Hayden and Grossman (1959) reported a family with rectal, ocular, and submaxillary pain, which they regarded as a familial autonomic disorder related to proctalgia fugax.

Dugan (1972) and Mann and Cree (1972) published further reports on familial rectal pain.

Fertleman et al. (2007) described the clinical phenotype in 77 individuals from 15 families identified through an international consortium; a validated pain questionnaire was completed by 14 affected individuals. Onset was in the neonatal period or infancy, and the disorder persisted throughout life. Autonomic manifestations predominated initially, with skin flushing in all and harlequin color change and tonic attacks in most. Dramatic syncopes with bradycardia and sometimes asystole were common. Later, the disorder was characterized by attacks of excruciating deep burning pain, often in the rectal, ocular, or jaw areas, but also diffuse. Attacks were triggered by factors such as defecation, cold wind, eating, and emotion.

Meglič et al. (2014) described a 3-year-old girl with an unusual presentation of PEPD: extremely painful voiding had been present since birth. Her father, who carried the same SCN9A mutation, had occasionally had some pain in his jaw while yawning since childhood.

### Inheritance

Fertleman et al. (2007) described PEPD as an autosomal dominant condition. In the family reported by Meglič et al. (2014), the heterozygous SCN9A mutation was inherited from the proband's mildly affected father. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Fertleman et al. (2006) performed a genome-wide linkage search in families with PEPD, which led to mutational analysis of the candidate gene SCN9A.

### Molecular Genetics

Following a genome-wide linkage search, Fertleman et al. (2006) identified 8 missense mutations in the SCN9A gene in 11 families and 2 sporadic cases with PEPD. Functional analysis in vitro of 3 of the mutant Nav1.7 channels showed a reduction in fast inactivation, leading to persistent sodium current.

In a 3-year-old girl with painful micturition since birth, Meglič et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.554G-A mutation in the SCN9A gene, resulting in an arg185-to-his (R185H) substitution. The mutation was inherited from her father, who had occasional jaw pain while yawning.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fertleman et al. (2006) noted that other SCN9A mutations, associated with more negative activation thresholds, cause primary erythermalgia. Carbamazepine, which is effective in PEPD but not in primary erythermalgia, selectively blocked the persistent current associated with PEPD mutants but did not affect the negative activation threshold of a primary erythermalgia mutant. The authors concluded that PEPD and primary erythermalgia are allelic disorders with distinct underlying biophysical mechanisms.

Meglič et al. (2014) noted that the R185H mutation found in their patient and her father had also been found in patients with small fiber sensory neuropathy, which did not correlate with the clinical picture in their family; they suggested that intra- and interfamilial phenotypic diversity associated with gain-of-function Nav1.7 variants may explain this.

### Diagnosis

Fertleman et al. (2007) stated that PEPD is most likely to be misdiagnosed as epilepsy and, particularly in infancy, as hyperekplexia and reflex anoxic seizures.

### Clinical Management

Fertleman et al. (2007) found that carbamazepine was effective in almost all patients who tried it, although the response was often incomplete.

In the 3-year-old girl reported by Meglič et al. (2014), significant reduction of pain was achieved with carbamazepine.
