### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant form of primary torsion dystonia. It usually begins in adulthood, most often as cervical dystonia. It may stay focal or spread to nearby regions, especially the cranial and laryngeal muscles and the upper limbs, so that it becomes segmental. Penetrance is incomplete. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported several families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. Affected members mostly had onset in adulthood, with neck involvement (cervical dystonia) as the most common first sign. In many patients the dystonia later spread to the face, jaw, larynx, or arms. Some affected individuals had focal disease, and others had segmental or more widespread dystonia.

Vemula et al. (2013) described further families with GNAL-related dystonia. The phenotype was similar: mainly cervical onset, with spread to cranial regions in some patients. ✗

### Inheritance

In the families reported by Fuchs et al. (2013), dystonia-25 followed an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance with reduced penetrance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia and found heterozygous mutations in the GNAL gene that segregated with the disorder. When they screened additional families with dystonia, they found further heterozygous GNAL mutations, including missense and truncating changes. GNAL encodes the stimulatory G protein alpha subunit G-alpha-olf. In the striatum, G-alpha-olf couples dopamine D1 receptors and adenosine A2A receptors to adenylyl cyclase. Fuchs et al. (2013) found that some of the mutations impaired the protein's coupling function.

Working independently, Vemula et al. (2013) identified heterozygous GNAL mutations in families with adult-onset cervical or segmental dystonia. ✗

### Pathogenesis

G-alpha-olf mediates dopamine D1 receptor and adenosine A2A receptor signaling in striatal medium spiny neurons. Fuchs et al. (2013) proposed that loss of G-alpha-olf function disrupts this striatal signaling and that this causes the dystonia.
