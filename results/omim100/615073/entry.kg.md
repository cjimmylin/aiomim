### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. It mainly affects the neck and usually begins in adulthood, although age at onset varies widely. The dystonia often spreads to other sites, especially the face, and can affect speech. Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that brachial onset was not seen and that arm involvement was less common than in DYT6. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 individuals from 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected. Their mean age at onset was 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7 affected members, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) described 28 individuals with definite dystonia from 8 multiplex families with GNAL mutations. All were Caucasian of mixed European ancestry. Mean age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82%, and 93% had neck involvement at final examination. However, most patients progressed to other sites, and only 46% had focal dystonia at last examination. Cranial involvement was present in 57% and speech involvement in 44%. Brachial onset was not observed, and only 32% had arm involvement, which distinguished the disorder from DYT6. The authors summarized the phenotype as mainly cervical dystonia with a fairly broad range of age at onset. In over half of subjects the dystonia spread to other muscles, especially facial muscles. Some individuals had generalized dystonia with onset in the legs, and other onset sites included the larynx, jaw, and tongue.

### Inheritance

Fuchs et al. (2013) found that dystonia was inherited in an autosomal dominant manner in the GNAL families. Because GNAL may be imprinted, they examined the parent of origin. Dystonia was inherited equally from the maternal and paternal sides, and maternally and paternally inherited cases showed no apparent phenotypic differences. ⚠︎

### Cytogenetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that GNAL lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci. Because dystonia occurs in patients with 18p deletions, they suggested that loss of GNAL may contribute to dystonia in these cases.

### Heterogeneity

In a non-Jewish family with adult cervical-onset dystonia, Bressman et al. (1994) used linkage data to exclude the DYT1 region on chromosome 9q34. They concluded that a gene other than DYT1 causes some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia and found heterozygous mutations in the GNAL gene in both. In family P they found a nonsense mutation (S293X), which lay within 1 of 3 regions of potential linkage (lod scores 1.5 to 2.1). In family D1 they found a missense mutation (V137M). The authors then Sanger sequenced the GNAL coding region in 39 additional multiplex primary torsion dystonia families of mixed European origin who did not have DYT1 or DYT6 mutations. This identified 6 additional novel mutations in 6 families (19%). The mutations segregated with disease in every family with DNA from multiple affected individuals. None of the variants was found in dbSNP135, in about 3,500 European exomes, or in 572 control chromosomes. ⚠︎

Family P had been reported previously and was of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage. Fuchs et al. (2013) found a heterozygous S293X mutation in affected members of this family. The mutation co-segregated with dystonia. In a BRET assay, the mutant protein failed to support any D1 dopamine receptor (D1R)-driven responses. In family D1, the heterozygous V137M mutation segregated with disease and affected a residue that is completely conserved in GNAL orthologs. In BRET assays, the V137M mutant showed an intermediate phenotype, which was largely consistent with impaired association with G-beta-gamma subunits.

In the remaining families, Fuchs et al. (2013) found the following heterozygous GNAL mutations: - E155K missense mutation in 2 affected members of family S - 1-bp insertion (c.283_284insT; S95fsX110) in 4 affected members of family D2 - 1-bp insertion (c.591_592insA; R198fsX210) in 3 affected members of family H - R21X nonsense mutation in 3 affected members of family T - 9-bp in-frame deletion (P102_V104del) in the proband of family N - possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C) in 2 affected members of family B

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Fuchs et al. (2013) found no correlation between mutation type and phenotype. Phenotypes also varied among members of the same family carrying the same GNAL mutation.

### Pathogenesis

Fuchs et al. (2013) used a cell-based BRET assay in HEK293 cells. The assay measured G-alpha-olf function through its interaction with G-beta-gamma subunits during the cycle initiated by the D1 dopamine receptor. The S293X mutant failed to support any D1R-driven responses, and the E155K and V137M mutants showed intermediate impairment. A control variant, V16F, behaved like wildtype. The authors concluded that the assay results suggested loss of function. They proposed that GNAL mutations point to primary abnormalities of D1R and/or A2AR transmission in striatal neurons as a possible cause of dystonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) studied affected members of a previously reported family (family P) with primary torsion dystonia. The family was of mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage. Exome sequencing identified a heterozygous c.878C-A transversion in the GNAL gene, resulting in a ser293-to-ter (S293X) substitution. **.0008 DYSTONIA 25**  
GNAL, IVS3AS, T-C, -5 [c.274-5T>C]

Fuchs et al. (2013) studied 2 affected members of a family (family B) with primary torsion dystonia. They identified a heterozygous possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C; chr18:11,753,820, hg19) upstream of exon 4 of the GNAL gene. Bioinformatic analysis suggested that the wildtype sequence was 10 times more likely than the mutant sequence to be found near an acceptor splice site. However, the effect could not be proven because GNAL expression was undetectable in patient lymphoblasts.
