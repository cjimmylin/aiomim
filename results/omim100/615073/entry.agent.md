### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene on chromosome 18p.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant form of primary torsion dystonia with reduced penetrance. It mostly begins in adulthood, usually in the neck, and is mainly cervical dystonia. The age at onset varies widely, and in more than half of patients the dystonia spreads to other sites, especially the face and other cranial muscles. Speech can be affected. Arm involvement is uncommon (Fuchs et al., 2013). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Bressman et al. (1994) examined 53 people from 4 generations of a non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset idiopathic torsion dystonia. Seven family members were affected. Their mean age at onset was 28.4 years (range, 7 to 50 years). In 6 of the 7, the neck was affected first. All 7 developed cervical dystonia, and 5 had dysarthria or dysphonia.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 8 multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia and GNAL mutations. Complete clinical information was available for 28 people with definite dystonia, all of them Caucasian of mixed European ancestry. The average age at onset was 31.3 years (range, 7 to 54 years). Onset was in the neck in 82% of carriers, and 93% had neck involvement at the final examination. Most carriers developed dystonia at other sites, and only 46% still had focal dystonia at the last examination. Cranial involvement was present in 57% and speech involvement in 44%. No carrier had brachial onset, and only 32% eventually had arm involvement, which the authors noted distinguished this disorder from DYT6. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation. In 2 families, the proband had generalized dystonia that began in the legs, at ages 7 and 25 years. Other sites of onset in individual patients included the jaw, tongue, and larynx.

### Inheritance

Fuchs et al. (2013) found that dystonia was inherited in an autosomal dominant manner in the families they studied. They noted that dominant inheritance in primary torsion dystonia comes with reduced penetrance. In their families, dystonia was transmitted equally from the maternal and paternal sides, and they saw no obvious phenotypic difference between maternally and paternally inherited cases.

### Mapping

In the non-Jewish North American family with adult-onset, cervical-onset dystonia that they studied, Bressman et al. (1994) used linkage analysis to exclude the region containing the DYT1 locus on chromosome 9q34. This indicated that a gene other than DYT1 caused the disorder in this family.

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported that a previous whole-genome scan of family P had found 3 regions of possible linkage, with lod scores of 1.5 to 2.1. They noted that GNAL lies on chromosome 18p, centromeric to the DYT7 and DYT15 loci.

### Heterogeneity

Bressman et al. (1994) excluded linkage to the DYT1 locus in a family with adult cervical-onset dystonia. They took this as evidence that a gene other than DYT1 causes some cases of adult cervical-onset dystonia.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in 2 families with primary torsion dystonia. In family P, which had mixed Russian and Swiss Mennonite heritage, they found a heterozygous nonsense mutation in the GNAL gene (S293X) within a linkage region. The mutation was shared by the 3 affected family members who were sequenced and segregated with dystonia in the rest of the family. In family D1, they found a heterozygous missense mutation in GNAL (V137M) that segregated with the disease. V137M was not found in 572 control chromosomes, and neither mutation was found in about 3,500 European exomes in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project database.

Fuchs et al. (2013) then Sanger sequenced the GNAL coding region in 39 more multiplex families of mixed European origin with primary torsion dystonia who did not have DYT1 or DYT6 mutations. They found GNAL mutations in 6 of these families (19%). These were a nonsense mutation (R21X), 2 frameshift mutations (S95fsX110 and R198fsX210), a missense mutation (E155K), an in-frame deletion of 3 amino acids (P102_V104del), and a possible splice site mutation (c.274-5T-C). None of these variants was found in dbSNP135, in about 3,500 European exomes, or in 572 control chromosomes. In every family where DNA was available from more than one affected person, the mutation segregated with the disease. In a BRET assay of Galpha(olf) coupling to the D1 dopamine receptor, the S293X mutant did not support any D1R-driven response. The E155K and V137M mutants gave intermediate results, largely consistent with impaired binding to G-beta-gamma subunits. The authors concluded that the assay suggested loss of function.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Among the 8 families with GNAL mutations, Fuchs et al. (2013) found no correlation between mutation type and phenotype. Phenotypes varied within families carrying the same mutation.

GNAL, IVS3AS, T-C, -5 [c.274-5T>C]

In family B with primary torsion dystonia, Fuchs et al. (2013) found a heterozygous c.274-5T-C transition upstream of exon 4 of the GNAL gene, which they described as a possible splice site mutation. The authors could not confirm an effect on splicing because GNAL expression was undetectable in patient lymphoblasts.
