### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dystonia-25 (DYT25) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the GNAL gene (139312) on chromosome 18p11.

### Description

Dystonia-25 is an autosomal dominant form of primary torsion dystonia. It usually begins in adulthood, most often as cervical dystonia. It may stay focal, but in many patients it spreads to other sites, including cranial regions, with speech involvement in some; arm involvement is less common (Fuchs et al., 2013). Fuchs et al. (2013) noted reduced penetrance for primary torsion dystonia in general.

### Clinical Features

Fuchs et al. (2013) reported 8 multiplex families with autosomal dominant primary torsion dystonia. Affected members mostly had onset in adulthood, and neck involvement (cervical dystonia) was the most common first sign. In many patients the dystonia later spread to cranial regions, speech, or the arms. Some affected individuals had focal dystonia, and others had segmental or more widespread dystonia. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

In the families reported by Fuchs et al. (2013), dystonia-25 followed an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance. Fuchs et al. (2013) noted that primary torsion dystonia in general shows reduced penetrance.

### Molecular Genetics

Fuchs et al. (2013) performed exome sequencing in individuals from 2 families with primary torsion dystonia and found heterozygous mutations in the GNAL gene that segregated with the disorder. Sanger sequencing of the coding region of GNAL in 39 additional multiplex families with primary torsion dystonia identified further heterozygous GNAL mutations in 6 families, including missense and truncating changes. GNAL encodes the stimulatory G protein alpha subunit G-alpha-olf, which in striatal medium spiny neurons couples dopamine D1 receptors and adenosine A2A receptors to adenylyl cyclase. In functional assays, Fuchs et al. (2013) found that some of the mutations impaired the function of the protein.

### Pathogenesis

G-alpha-olf mediates dopamine D1 receptor and adenosine A2A receptor signaling in striatal medium spiny neurons. Fuchs et al. (2013) proposed that loss of G-alpha-olf function disrupts this striatal signaling, and that this may lead to dystonia.
