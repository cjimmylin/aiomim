### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (PMGEDSV) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL3A1 gene on chromosome 2q32.2.

### Description

Polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic COL3A1 mutations. Affected individuals have structural brain anomalies, including bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone type, cerebellar cysts, and white matter abnormalities. Some also have developmental delay and seizures. Some patients have severe features of vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, such as arterial dissection or rupture, thin translucent skin, and gastrointestinal rupture (Jorgensen et al., 2015; Vandervore et al., 2017; Horn et al., 2017). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Plancke et al. (2009) studied a young girl with recessive Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS). She was the child of consanguineous, healthy parents and met the criteria for laboratory testing for vascular EDS. Jorgensen et al. (2015) summarized her course. She developed acute abdominal pain, and during surgery to repair a necrotic and perforated jejunum, the surgeons encountered extreme fragility of intestinal arteries and tissue. She died at 11 years of age, 6 days after the intervention, from multisystem failure. Besides features characteristic of EDS type IV, she had delayed motor development, pulmonary valve stenosis, and absence epilepsy. Brain MRI showed diffuse cortical dysplasia that was mainly frontal, as well as ventricular dilatation.

Jorgensen et al. (2015) reported sibs in a family with marked intrafamilial clinical variability. The proband was a boy born with right-sided clubfoot. He also had unilateral hydrocele of the testis, crowded teeth, retrognathia, and long, slender fingers. He was otherwise healthy until he died unexpectedly at 15 years of age after a week of backaches. At autopsy he had 3 dissections: a transverse dissection involving nearly the whole circumference of the thoracic aorta, a dissection of the right renal artery, and a dissection of the left femoral artery. There was extensive bleeding into the thoracic and abdominal cavities, and the aortic wall showed cystic medial necrosis. The brain was macroscopically normal, but no brain imaging or histologic examination was done. His sister had long, slender fingers and small-joint hypermobility. By age 14 she had crowded teeth in a small lower jaw and translucent skin with visible subcutaneous vessels, and she later had several episodes of spontaneous rupture of small superficial vessels. Both sibs had pale, translucent skin and facial features characteristic of EDS type IV, including a small lower jaw, prominent eyes, a pinched nose, and a thin upper lip. At 19 years of age the sister had dissection of the right internal carotid artery, and a dissection flap was later found in the ectatic left internal carotid artery. She also had a stenotic process in the right external iliac artery. Her other features were intermittent abdominal pain with occasional blood in the stool, migraine with aura, and surgery for rupture of the medial meniscus and the anterior cruciate ligament. Brain MRI showed cerebral cortical dysplasia with thickened frontoparietal cortices bilaterally, small gyri, and findings consistent with pachy-micropolygyria. Her cognitive function was normal, she had no seizures, and her motor and language development had been normal. Their heterozygous parents and other heterozygous relatives had no complications. The father was clinically normal at 63 years of age. The mother had thin skin with visible subcutaneous vessels and reduced elasticity of the ascending aorta but did not show the full picture of vascular EDS. ⚠︎

Vandervore et al. (2017) reported a family with 2 affected and 2 unaffected sibs. They also reviewed brain imaging from the 2 previously reported individuals with biallelic COL3A1 mutations (Plancke et al., 2009; Jorgensen et al., 2015). In the affected sibs and in both previously reported individuals, brain MRI showed a phenotype similar to that associated with GPR56 mutations: cobblestone-like cortical malformation, white matter changes, and cerebellar cysts. ⚠︎

Horn et al. (2017) reported 2 unrelated families. The first patient was a 3-year-old girl with developmental delay who was born with bilateral clubfoot, joint laxity, and dysmorphic facial features. At 2 years of age she had an aneurysmal brain hemorrhage. Cerebral MRI showed profound abnormalities, including bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone type. In the second family, 2 affected sibs had cobblestone-like cortical malformation, cerebellar cysts, white matter abnormalities, developmental delay, and seizures. Horn et al. (2017) concluded that the brain phenotype in all families reported with biallelic COL3A1 variants consisted of bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone type, cerebellar microcysts, and white matter abnormalities, and that some of these families also had a severe EDS phenotype.

### Inheritance

Plancke et al. (2009) established autosomal recessive inheritance in a girl from a consanguineous family. She was homozygous for a COL3A1 mutation inherited from each of her healthy parents. Jorgensen et al. (2015) found compound heterozygous mutations in affected sibs, with each parent carrying 1 of the mutations. Homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations were also reported by Vandervore et al. (2017) and Horn et al. (2017).

### Molecular Genetics

Plancke et al. (2009) sequenced the entire COL3A1 cDNA of a girl with recessive EDS from a consanguineous family. They identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.479dupT) that resulted in a premature termination codon (Lys161GlnfsTer45). Genomic DNA studies showed that each parent had passed on the mutation. Expression studies, including RT-PCR, quantitative PCR, immunohistochemistry, and Western blot, showed strong mRNA decay and no type III collagen in the proband. The parents were expected to be haploinsufficient for COL3A1 but did not have manifestations of vascular EDS.

Jorgensen et al. (2015) sequenced COL3A1 in 2 sibs with vascular EDS, one of whom had bilateral frontal and parietal polymicrogyria. Both sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 mutations: a c.1786C-T transition in exon 26 resulting in an arg596-to-ter (R596X) substitution, and a c.3851G-A transition in exon 50 resulting in a gly1284-to-glu (G1284E) substitution in the carboxyl-terminal propeptide. The father was heterozygous for the nonsense mutation and the mother for the missense mutation, which confirmed that the mutations were in trans. No variants were found in FBN1, TGFBR1, or TGFBR2 in the proband. Dermal fibroblasts from the compound heterozygous sister produced a reduced amount of type III procollagen, and all of its chains had abnormal electrophoretic mobility consistent with overmodification. Paternal relatives heterozygous for the nonsense mutation had no complications or clinical features consistent with vascular EDS.

Vandervore et al. (2017) performed exome analysis in a family with 2 affected and 2 unaffected sibs. They identified a homozygous c.145C-G transversion in exon 2 of the COL3A1 gene, resulting in a pro49-to-ala (P49A) substitution. Functional assays were performed on dermal fibroblasts.

Horn et al. (2017) identified biallelic COL3A1 variants in 2 unrelated families. The 3-year-old girl in the first family was compound heterozygous for 2 novel variants: a nonsense variant (c.1282C-T, R428X) and a 1-bp deletion (c.2057delC) causing a frameshift (Pro686LeufsTer105). The 2 affected sibs in the second family were homozygous for the P49A missense variant (c.145C-G).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jorgensen et al. (2015) concluded that biallelic COL3A1 variants lead to a much worse outcome than heterozygous null or missense variants, and that frontoparietal polymicrogyria may be an additional feature. They noted, however, that they could not rule out that the cerebrocortical findings were coincidental or caused by variants in a second gene. Vandervore et al. (2017) suggested that the variable phenotype among patients reflects alterations in different domains of type III procollagen leading to different outcomes.

### Pathogenesis

Type III collagen is a major constituent of the pial basement membrane and serves as the ligand for GPR56. Mutations in GPR56 cause bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria (Jeong et al., 2012). Vandervore et al. (2017) and Horn et al. (2017) noted that the brain phenotype of patients with biallelic COL3A1 mutations resembles that of individuals with recessive GPR56 mutations. Vandervore et al. (2017) suggested that these findings emphasize the critical role of the type III collagen-GPR56 axis and the pial membrane in brain development and cortical lamination.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (1997) inactivated the Col3a1 gene in mouse embryonic stem cells. About 10% of homozygous mutant mice survived to adulthood, but their life span was much shorter than that of wildtype mice. The main cause of death was rupture of the major blood vessels, similar to type IV EDS in humans. Ultrastructural analysis showed that type III collagen is essential for normal collagen I fibrillogenesis in the cardiovascular system and other organs.

Jeong et al. (2012) found a cobblestone-like cortical malformation in Col3a1-null mice. The malformation involved breakdown of the pial basement membrane, neuronal overmigration, abnormal attachment of radial glial endfeet, and marginal zone heterotopias. The pial basement membrane formed normally at embryonic day (E) 10.5, but regional breakdown with neuronal overmigration was seen in about half of E11.5 embryos and in all embryos older than E12.5. Ectopias were found in 23 of 28 null animals and in none of the wildtype or heterozygous animals. The ectopias contained neurons from both deep and superficial cortical layers, as well as misplaced Cajal-Retzius cells and interneurons. Meningeal fibroblasts developed normally, and the expression and glycosylation of alpha-dystroglycan were unchanged. Jeong et al. (2012) suggested that mutations in both COL3A1 alleles might be associated with a lethal form of cobblestone lissencephaly.
