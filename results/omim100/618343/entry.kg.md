### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (PMGEDSV) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL3A1 gene (120180) on chromosome 2q32.2.

### Description

Polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic COL3A1 mutations. Affected individuals have specific structural brain anomalies, and some also have a severe Ehlers-Danlos syndrome phenotype. The brain phenotype is characterized by bilateral frontoparietal cobblestone polymicrogyria, cerebellar microcysts, and white matter abnormalities. It resembles the neurologic features of patients with autosomal recessive GPR56 mutations (Horn et al., 2017). The phenotype varies across patients (Vandervore et al., 2017).

### Clinical Features

Plancke et al. (2009) studied a young girl with recessive Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) who was born to healthy consanguineous parents. She met the criteria for laboratory testing for vascular EDS.

Jørgensen et al. (2015) described a boy (III-2) who was born with right-sided clubfoot. He had a unilateral testicular hydrocele, crowded teeth, retrognathia, long slender fingers, and pale translucent skin. He died unexpectedly at age 15 years after a week of backache. At autopsy there were dissections of the thoracic aorta, the right renal artery, and the left femoral artery, with extensive bleeding and cystic medial necrosis of the aortic wall. The brain looked normal macroscopically, but no brain imaging or histologic cerebral examination was performed. His sister (III-3) had long slender fingers, small-joint hypermobility, and translucent skin with visible veins. She also had the facial features of vascular EDS, spontaneous rupture of small superficial vessels, intermittent abdominal pain with blood in the stool, and migraine with aura. By age 19 to 20 years she had dissections of both internal carotid arteries and stenosis of the right external iliac artery, but her aortic dimensions were normal. Brain MRI showed bilateral thickened frontoparietal cortices with small gyri, consistent with pachy-micropolygyria. Despite this, her cognition and neurologic examination were normal, she had never had seizures, and her motor and language development had been normal.

Vandervore et al. (2017) reported a family with 2 affected and 2 unaffected sibs. Brain MRI of the 2 affected sibs showed a brain phenotype similar to that associated with GPR56 mutations: cobblestone-like cortical malformation, white matter changes, and cerebellar cysts. MRI of 2 previously reported individuals with biallelic COL3A1 mutations showed the same pattern. The authors noted that the phenotype varied among patients. ⚠︎

Horn et al. (2017) described a 3-year-old girl with developmental delay who was born with bilateral clubfoot, joint laxity, and dysmorphic facial features. At age 2 years she had an aneurysmal brain hemorrhage. Cerebral MRI showed a distinctive pattern of profound cerebral abnormalities, including bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone type. Horn et al. (2017) also described a second family in which 2 affected sibs had cobblestone-like cortical malformation, cerebellar cysts, white matter abnormalities, developmental delay, and seizures. The authors noted that 3 more families with biallelic COL3A1 variants had been reported previously. All of them had specific structural brain anomalies, and some had a severe EDS phenotype.

### Biochemical Features

Jørgensen et al. (2015) studied cultured dermal fibroblasts from members of their family. Cells from the compound heterozygous affected sister made a reduced amount of type III procollagen, and all of its chains had abnormal electrophoretic mobility, consistent with overmodification. Cells from the mother, who was heterozygous for the missense variant, made mostly normal type III procollagen plus a small amount of slightly overmodified procollagen. Cells from the father, who was heterozygous for the nonsense variant, made a reduced amount of apparently normal type III procollagen.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of PMGEDSV in the reported families is autosomal recessive. Plancke et al. (2009) found a homozygous COL3A1 mutation in a girl born to healthy consanguineous parents, each of whom carried the mutation. Jørgensen et al. (2015) found compound heterozygous COL3A1 variants in affected sibs, and each parent carried one of the variants. Homozygous or compound heterozygous COL3A1 mutations were also found in affected individuals by Vandervore et al. (2017) and Horn et al. (2017).

### Molecular Genetics

Plancke et al. (2009) sequenced the entire COL3A1 cDNA of a girl with recessive EDS and found a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.479dupT) in the COL3A1 gene. The duplication causes a frameshift and a premature termination codon (Lys161GlnfsTer45). Testing of genomic DNA showed that she had inherited the mutation from each of her parents. Using RT-PCR, quantitative PCR, immunohistochemistry, and Western blot analysis, the authors found strong mRNA decay and no type III collagen in the proband. ⚠︎

Jørgensen et al. (2015) found that 2 affected sibs were compound heterozygous for 2 COL3A1 variants. The first was a nonsense variant, c.1786C-T (R596X), in exon 26, which encodes part of the triple helical domain. The sibs had inherited it from their father, who was clinically unaffected at age 63. The second was a missense variant, c.3851G-A (G1284E), in exon 50, which encodes part of the C-terminal propeptide. The sibs had inherited it from their mother, who had only partial features of the disorder. The substituted glycine is highly conserved and lies next to a cysteine involved in interchain disulfide bonding, within a calcium-binding region. The authors predicted that the change would slow assembly of the trimer. An unaffected brother carried neither variant. The proband had no variants in the FBN1, TGFBR1, or TGFBR2 genes. Fibroblast studies showed reduced and overmodified type III procollagen in cells from the affected sister.

Vandervore et al. (2017) studied 2 sibs with cobblestone-like cortical malformation, white matter changes, and cerebellar cysts. Exome analysis identified a novel homozygous variant in exon 2 of the COL3A1 gene, c.145C-G (P49A). Functional assays were performed on dermal fibroblasts. ⚠︎

In a 3-year-old girl with polymicrogyria and connective tissue features, Horn et al. (2017) identified compound heterozygous COL3A1 variants. One was a nonsense variant, c.1282C-T (R428X), and the other was a frameshift variant, c.2057delC (Pro686LeufsTer105). Both variants were novel. In 2 sibs from a second family who had cobblestone-like cortical malformation, cerebellar cysts, and seizures, Horn et al. (2017) identified a homozygous missense variant in COL3A1, c.145C-G (P49A).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Schwarze et al. (2001) identified heterozygous COL3A1 null mutations in 4 probands. In all 4, the presenting feature was vascular aneurysm or rupture, and the resulting phenotype overlapped with vascular EDS. Three of the mutations were frameshifts (1832delAA, 413delC, and 555delT) whose mRNA was transcribed efficiently but rapidly degraded. The fourth was a nonsense mutation in the final exon (4294C-T; R1432X), which produced a stable mRNA. The truncated protein it encoded was not incorporated into mature type III procollagen molecules.

In contrast, Plancke et al. (2009) found that the heterozygous parents of their homozygous proband, and other healthy relatives in earlier generations who were expected to have COL3A1 haploinsufficiency, did not show features of vascular EDS. The authors suggested that COL3A1 haploinsufficiency has a stochastic effect in humans. They proposed that this might depend on how efficient nonsense-mediated mRNA decay is, and on the resulting dominant-negative effect, according to where the mutation lies and/or modifying factors. ⚠︎

Jørgensen et al. (2015) reported marked clinical variation within their family. The father and other paternal relatives who were heterozygous for the nonsense variant had no complications or clinical features of vascular EDS, and the paternal grandmother was healthy at 85 years. The mother, who was heterozygous for the missense variant, did not have the full vascular EDS phenotype by age 60. However, she had thin skin with visible vessels, reduced elasticity of the ascending aorta, and emphysema. The authors concluded that biallelic variants lead to a significantly worse outcome than heterozygous variants. ⚠︎

Vandervore et al. (2017) concluded that homozygous or compound heterozygous COL3A1 mutations were associated with cobblestone-like malformation in all 3 families reported to date. They suggested that the phenotypic variability indicates that alterations in distinct domains of type III procollagen can lead to different outcomes.

### Pathogenesis

Jørgensen et al. (2015) noted that type III collagen is the ligand for GPR56, and that GPR56 variants cause recessive bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria. They suggested that the interaction between GPR56 and type III collagen forms part of a pathway in brain formation. They proposed a novel syndromic recessive form of vascular EDS that combines tissue fragility with cerebrocortical anomalies. They also acknowledged that the brain findings in the affected sister could be coincidental or caused by variants in a second gene.

Vandervore et al. (2017) noted that type III procollagen is the ligand of GPR56 (ADGRG1). They stated that cobblestone-like malformation in patients with biallelic COL3A1 mutations emphasized the critical role of the type III collagen-GPR56 axis and the pial membrane in brain development and cortical lamination. Consistent with findings in knockout mice, Horn et al. (2017) concluded that collagen III helps regulate cortical development as well as connective tissue formation.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (1997) inactivated the Col3a1 gene in mouse embryonic stem cells by homologous recombination and derived homozygous mutant mice from heterozygous intercrosses. About 10% of homozygous mutant mice survived to adulthood, but their life span was much shorter than that of wildtype mice. The major cause of death was rupture of the major blood vessels, similar to patients with type IV EDS. Ultrastructural analysis of mutant tissues showed that type III collagen is essential for normal collagen I fibrillogenesis in the cardiovascular system and other organs.

Jeong et al. (2012) found that Col3a1-null mice had a cobblestone-like cortical malformation. Its features were breakdown of the pial basement membrane (BM), neuronal overmigration, detachment of radial glial endfeet, and marginal zone heterotopias. All Col3a1-null mice examined at embryonic day (E) 18.5 had neuronal ectopias on the brain surface. The ectopias contained neurons from both deep and superficial cortical layers (Cux1+, Tbr1+, and CTIP2+), mirroring Gpr56-null mice. Ectopias were present in 23 of 28 null embryos, 0 of 12 heterozygotes, and 0 of 20 wildtype embryos. The pial BM formed normally at E10.5 despite the absence of collagen III. Regional breakdown of the BM with neuronal overmigration appeared in about half of E11.5 embryos and in all embryos older than E12.5. In the mutants, radial glial endfeet protruded through the breached BM, and Cajal-Retzius cells and calbindin-positive interneurons were misplaced into the ectopias.

Jeong et al. (2012) found no obvious defects in meningeal fibroblasts from Col3a1-null mice, and the level of glycosylated alpha-dystroglycan in mutant brains was unchanged. They suggested that collagen III, the ligand of GPR56, regulates cortical development independently of the dystroglycan pathway. They also suggested that the dyslamination results from the absence of collagen III rather than from a defect in meningeal fibroblasts. The authors noted that homozygous Col3a1 deletion mostly caused perinatal lethality, with only about 5% of mice surviving to adulthood. The survivors had features resembling vascular-type (type IV) EDS, including rupture of large blood vessels, whereas loss of 1 allele caused no obvious defects. The authors postulated that biallelic COL3A1 mutations in humans may be associated with a lethal form of cobblestone lissencephaly similar to Walker-Warburg syndrome.
