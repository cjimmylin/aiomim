### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (PMGEDSV) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL3A1 gene on chromosome 2q32.2.

### Description

Polymicrogyria with or without vascular-type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (PMGEDSV) is a disorder caused by biallelic COL3A1 mutation in which affected individuals have structural brain anomalies, characterized by bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone type, cerebellar cysts, and white matter abnormalities, together, in some, with a severe Ehlers-Danlos syndrome phenotype including arterial dissection or rupture and tissue fragility (Jorgensen et al., 2015; Vandervore et al., 2017; Horn et al., 2017). Developmental delay and seizures were reported in some patients, whereas one affected woman had normal cognitive functioning (Jorgensen et al., 2015; Horn et al., 2017).

### Clinical Features

Plancke et al. (2009) studied a young girl, born of consanguineous healthy parents, with recessive Ehlers-Danlos syndrome; she fulfilled the vascular EDS criteria for laboratory testing. In a later review of this patient, Jorgensen et al. (2015) stated that she was 11 years old when she developed acute abdominal pain; during surgery to repair a necrotic and perforated jejunum, extreme intestinal arterial and tissue fragility were encountered, and she died 6 days after the intervention from multisystem failure. In addition to features of EDS type IV, she had delayed motor development, pulmonary valve stenosis, and absence epilepsy, and brain MRI showed diffuse cortical dysplasia with a predominantly frontal location and ventricular dilatation.

Jorgensen et al. (2015) reported a brother and sister with vascular-type EDS. The proband (III-2) was born with right-sided clubfoot and developed unilateral hydrocele of the testis; he had crowded teeth and retrognathia, and long, slender fingers. He was otherwise healthy until he died unexpectedly at 15 years of age after a week of backaches. Autopsy revealed 3 dissections, of the thoracic aorta, the right renal artery, and the left femoral artery, with extensive bleeding into the thoracic and abdominal cavities and cystic medial necrosis of the aortic wall. The brain had a normal macroscopic appearance, but neither brain imaging nor histologic cerebral examination was performed. His sister (III-3) had long, slender fingers with small joint hypermobility at age 6 years, and by 14 years had crowded teeth in a small lower jaw and translucent skin with visible subcutaneous vessels. At 19 years, both sibs were noted to have pale, translucent skin and facial features characteristic of EDS type IV, with a small lower jaw, prominent eyes, pinched nose, and thin upper lip. The sister had normal motor and language development, several episodes of spontaneous superficial vessel rupture, surgery at 16 and 19 years for traumatic rupture of the medial meniscus and anterior cruciate ligament, respectively, intermittent abdominal pain with occasional blood in the stool, and migraine with aura. Imaging showed a stenotic process in the right external iliac artery, dissection of the right internal carotid artery, and an ectatic left internal carotid artery with a dissection flap; aortic dimensions were normal. Brain MRI showed cerebral cortical dysplasia with thickened frontoparietal cortices bilaterally, small gyri, and findings consistent with pachy micropolygyria. She had normal cognitive functioning and a normal neurologic examination, and had not had seizures. The maternal grandfather had died from cerebral hemorrhage at age 43 years. ⚠︎

Vandervore et al. (2017) studied a family in which 2 of 4 sibs were affected, and reviewed brain imaging of these sibs and of 2 previously reported individuals with biallelic COL3A1 mutations. Brain MRI in all showed a phenotype similar to that associated with mutations in GPR56, with cobblestone-like cortical malformation, white matter changes, and cerebellar cysts. The authors noted variability of the phenotype across patients. ⚠︎

Horn et al. (2017) reported 2 unrelated families. The first patient was a 3-year-old girl with developmental delay who was born with bilateral clubfoot, joint laxity, and dysmorphic facial features. At 2 years of age she developed an aneurysmal brain hemorrhage. Cerebral MRI showed profound cerebral abnormalities including bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone variant. In the second family, 2 affected sibs showed cobblestone-like cortical malformation, cerebellar cysts, white matter abnormalities, developmental delay, and seizures.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of PMGEDSV is autosomal recessive. Plancke et al. (2009) found a homozygous COL3A1 mutation in a girl with recessive EDS whose healthy consanguineous parents each carried it, and Jorgensen et al. (2015) found affected sibs to be compound heterozygous for COL3A1 variants inherited from each parent. Biallelic COL3A1 mutations were also found in affected individuals by Vandervore et al. (2017) and Horn et al. (2017).

### Molecular Genetics

In a girl, born of consanguineous parents, with recessive EDS, Plancke et al. (2009) sequenced COL3A1 cDNA and identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.479dupT) resulting in a premature termination codon (Lys161GlnfsTer45). Analysis of genomic DNA showed that the mutation was inherited from each parent. Expression studies (RT-PCR, quantitative PCR, immunohistochemistry, and Western blot) showed strong mRNA decay and an absence of type III collagen in the proband. The expected COL3A1 haploinsufficiency in her healthy relatives did not lead to manifestations of vascular EDS.

In 2 sibs with vascular-type EDS, 1 of whom also had bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria, Jorgensen et al. (2015) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL3A1 gene: a c.1786C-T transition in exon 26, resulting in an arg596-to-ter (R596X) substitution in the triple helical domain, and a c.3851G-A transition in exon 50, resulting in a gly1284-to-glu (G1284E) substitution in the carboxyl-terminal propeptide. The mutations were found by sequencing of COL3A1 in the proband; no variants were found in FBN1, TGFBR1, or TGFBR2. The father was heterozygous for R596X and the mother for G1284E, confirming that the variants were in trans, and an unaffected brother carried neither. Cultured dermal fibroblasts from the affected sister produced a reduced amount of type III procollagen, all the chains of which had abnormal electrophoretic mobility, consistent with overmodification. Fibroblasts from the father produced a reduced amount of apparently normal type III procollagen, and fibroblasts from the mother produced mostly normal and a small amount of abnormal type III procollagen.

By exome analysis in a family with 2 affected sibs with cobblestone-like cortical malformation, white matter changes, and cerebellar cysts, Vandervore et al. (2017) identified a homozygous c.145C-G transversion in exon 2 of the COL3A1 gene, resulting in a pro49-to-ala (P49A) substitution. Functional assays were performed on dermal fibroblasts.

In a 3-year-old girl with developmental delay, aneurysmal brain hemorrhage, and bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria of the cobblestone variant, Horn et al. (2017) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL3A1 gene: a c.1282C-T transition resulting in an arg428-to-ter (R428X) substitution, and a 1-bp deletion (c.2057delC) resulting in a frameshift (Pro686LeufsTer105). In a second family, 2 affected sibs were homozygous for the P49A missense variant in COL3A1.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Schwarze et al. (2001) identified heterozygous COL3A1 mutations leading to premature termination codons (3 frameshift mutations, 1832delAA, 413delC, and 555delT, and a nonsense mutation in the final exon, arg1432-to-ter) in 4 probands whose presenting feature was vascular aneurysm or rupture. The frameshift alleles were transcribed efficiently but the mRNA was rapidly degraded, whereas the final-exon nonsense allele produced stable mRNA and a truncated protein that was not incorporated into mature type III procollagen.

In contrast, Plancke et al. (2009) found that the heterozygous relatives of their patient with a homozygous null COL3A1 mutation were healthy, and suggested a stochastic effect of COL3A1 haploinsufficiency in humans. In the family reported by Jorgensen et al. (2015), the father, heterozygous for R596X, had no abnormal clinical findings at age 63, and none of the paternal relatives carrying this variant had complications or features consistent with vascular EDS; the mother, heterozygous for G1284E, had thin skin with visible subcutaneous vessels and reduced elasticity of the ascending aorta but did not express a complete picture of vascular EDS by age 60. Jorgensen et al. (2015) concluded that biallelic variants have a significantly worse outcome than heterozygous variants. Vandervore et al. (2017) suggested that the variability of the phenotype across patients with biallelic mutations reflects alterations in distinct domains of type III procollagen.

### Pathogenesis

Jeong et al. (2012) noted that collagen III is a major constituent of the pial basement membrane and serves as the ligand for GPR56, mutations in which cause bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria. Vandervore et al. (2017) stated that the presence of cobblestone-like malformation in patients with biallelic COL3A1 mutations emphasizes the role of the type III collagen-GPR56 axis and the pial membrane in brain development and cortical lamination.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (1997) inactivated the Col3a1 gene in mouse embryonic stem cells by homologous recombination. About 10% of homozygous mutant mice survived to adulthood but had a much shorter life span than wildtype mice; the major cause of death was rupture of the major blood vessels, similar to patients with type IV EDS. Ultrastructural analysis showed that type III collagen is essential for normal collagen I fibrillogenesis in the cardiovascular system and other organs.

Jeong et al. (2012) found that Col3a1 -/- mice had a cobblestone-like cortical malformation, with pial basement membrane breakdown, neuronal overmigration, radial glial endfeet detachment, and marginal zone heterotopias. The pial basement membrane was initially properly formed at embryonic day (E) 10.5, but regional breakdown with concurrent neuronal overmigration was observed in about half of E11.5 embryos and all embryos older than E12.5; ectopias were found in 23 of 28 null animals and in none of 12 heterozygous or 20 wildtype animals. Ectopias contained neurons from both deep and superficial cortical layers, Cajal-Retzius cells, and interneurons. Meningeal fibroblasts developed normally, and the expression and glycosylation of alpha-dystroglycan were unaffected.
