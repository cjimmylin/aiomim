### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome (CLPED1) is caused by homozygous mutation in the PVRL1 gene.

### Description

Cleft lip/palate-ectodermal dysplasia syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. According to Yoshida et al. (2015), its clinical manifestations comprise a unique facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, ectodermal dysplasia, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers and/or toes, and in some cases, mental retardation. Suzuki et al. (1998) described the disorder as Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia, characterized by unusual facies, dental anomalies, hypotrichosis, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis and onychodysplasia, syndactyly, and cleft lip/cleft palate.

### Nomenclature

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) referred to the association of ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, and syndactyly as the Zlotogora-Ogur syndrome. The disorder on Margarita Island was designated Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia (ED4) by Suzuki et al. (1998); Suzuki et al. (2000) used the designation CLPED1, noting that it was previously termed ED4.

### Clinical Features

Zlotogora et al. (1987) reported 2 sibs with cleft lip and palate, sparse scalp hair, malformed protruding ears, and partial syndactyly of the fingers and toes. The older child also had mental retardation and pili torti.

Oğur and Yüksel (1988) reported 2 Turkish brothers, the offspring of a second-cousin marriage, with tetramelic syndactyly, ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip and palate, renal anomalies, and mental retardation.

Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990) reported 3 Brazilian brothers born to normal consanguineous parents (F = 1/16) who had ectodermal dysplasia, cleft lip/palate, mental retardation, syndactyly of fingers 2-3, accessory nipples, and ear anomalies.

Bustos et al. (1991) described 27 individuals from 7 families, related to each other with high probability, from a relatively isolated and inbred population on Margarita Island in northeastern Venezuela (Nueva Esparta State). The patients showed manifestations of ectodermal dysplasia and other anomalies, with females affected as severely as males and with variable expressivity. All parents were normal. The authors considered the condition to be a newly recognized autosomal recessive dysplasia/malformation syndrome that could not be classified within the known ectodermal dysplasias.

Yoshida et al. (2015) reported a 7-year-old Japanese boy, the first child of a consanguineous marriage, who had hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia with sparse, brittle, fine, dry hair and hypodontia, a unique facial appearance with cleft lip/palate, cutaneous syndactyly of the fingers, and mild mental retardation. Scanning electron microscopy of the hair demonstrated pili torti and pili trianguli et canaliculi. The authors stated that this was the first report of an Asian individual with the disorder.

### Inheritance

Zlotogora et al. (1987) suggested autosomal recessive inheritance on the basis of affected sibs. Parental consanguinity was reported by Oğur and Yüksel (1988), Rodini and Richieri-Costa (1990), and Yoshida et al. (2015), and Bustos et al. (1991) considered the Margarita Island disorder, in which all parents were normal, to be autosomal recessive. Homozygous PVRL1 mutations in affected individuals (Sözen et al., 2001; Yoshida et al., 2015) confirmed autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Using an affected-only DNA-pooling strategy, Suzuki et al. (1998) carried out linkage disequilibrium mapping of the gene for Margarita Island ectodermal dysplasia to 11q23. Haplotype analysis of 4 complex Margarita Island families localized the gene to an interval of approximately 1 to 2 Mb spanned by 2 YACs.

### Molecular Genetics

By positional cloning, Suzuki et al. (2000) identified PVRL1, encoding nectin-1, an immunoglobulin-related transmembrane cell-cell adhesion molecule, as the gene responsible for autosomal recessive CLPED1. Nectin-1 is also the principal cell surface receptor for alpha-herpesviruses, and the authors suggested that the high frequency of CLPED1 on Margarita Island might result from resistance of heterozygotes to infection by these viruses.

Sözen et al. (2001) stated that homozygosity for a nonsense mutation in PVRL1, W185X, results in CLPED1 on Margarita Island. They demonstrated a highly significant association between heterozygosity for this mutation and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip/palate in northern Venezuela.

In a 7-year-old Japanese boy with CLPED1, born to consanguineous parents, Yoshida et al. (2015) identified a homozygous nonsense mutation (c.400C-T; R134X) in exon 2 of the PVRL1 gene. His parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The authors noted that all 4 PVRL1 mutations identified in the disorder to date resulted in truncated proteins lacking the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CLEFT LIP/PALATE-ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA SYNDROME**  
PVRL1, TRP185TER [W185X]

Sözen et al. (2001) stated that homozygosity for a nonsense mutation in the PVRL1 gene, trp185-to-ter (W185X), results in autosomal recessive CLPED1 on Margarita Island. They found a highly significant association between heterozygosity for this mutation and sporadic, nonsyndromic cleft lip/palate in northern Venezuela.

**.0002 CLEFT LIP/PALATE-ECTODERMAL DYSPLASIA SYNDROME**  
PVRL1, ARG134TER [c.400C>T; p.Arg134*]

In a 7-year-old Japanese boy with CLPED1, the first child of a consanguineous marriage, Yoshida et al. (2015) identified a homozygous c.400C-T transition in exon 2 of the PVRL1 gene, resulting in an arg134-to-ter (R134X) substitution. His parents were heterozygous for the mutation. The predicted truncated protein lacks the transmembrane and intracellular domains of nectin-1.
