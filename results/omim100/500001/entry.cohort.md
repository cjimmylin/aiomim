### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is caused by homoplasmic or heteroplasmic mutation in the mitochondrially encoded genes MT-ND6 (516006), MT-ND4 (516003), MT-ND1 (516000), or MT-ND3 (516002). ⚠︎

### Description

Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is a maternally inherited mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation disorder. Affected people have vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia in different combinations (Wang et al., 2009). Within a family, some members have Leber optic neuropathy, some have dystonia, and some have both. The dystonia is associated with bilateral lesions of the striatum, especially the putamen (Marsden et al., 1986; Novotny et al., 1986). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Miyoshi et al. (1969) reported a familial form of holotopistic striatal necrosis.

Marsden et al. (1986) described 7 members of 2 families who had dystonia. In some, it occurred with subacute visual loss and in others with asymptomatic optic atrophy. All had striking bilateral symmetrical lucencies on CT scan, especially in the putamen.

Novotny et al. (1986) studied a kindred in which 8 members had the neuroretinopathy of Leber disease and 14 had a progressive, generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration. One member had both disorders.

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family with maternally transmitted Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) and/or early-onset dystonia. The dystonia was associated with bilateral basal ganglia lesions.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family with a rare form of LHON associated with hereditary spastic dystonia. Some family members had both the neurologic and the ophthalmologic lesions, while others had only one of them.

Watanabe et al. (2006) studied a Japanese family with 2 features: generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration, which began in childhood, and late-onset optic neuropathy.

Spruijt et al. (2007) reported a family in which LHON with central visual loss and spastic dystonia both occurred.

Wang et al. (2009) described a Chinese family with 18 LDYT patients, who had vision loss and/or progressive generalized dystonia. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

De Vries et al. (1996) found severe complex I deficiency in a muscle biopsy from a Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia. They suggested that this deficiency linked the family's mtDNA mutations to its complex phenotype, including Leber optic neuropathy.

Spruijt et al. (2007) found that the 3697G-A mutation in the ND1 gene seemed to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency.

### Inheritance

Novotny et al. (1986) found that the mode of inheritance in a kindred with Leber optic neuropathy and dystonia was compatible with maternal transmission. They suggested that the disorder may be a mitochondrial disease. Jun et al. (1994) reported maternal transmission in a 5-generation Hispanic family and found an mtDNA mutation in maternal relatives. This finding is consistent with mitochondrial inheritance.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (2009) stated that LDYT was already known to be caused by mutations in the mitochondrial ND6 or ND4 genes. They showed that it can also be caused by mutation in the ND3 gene. Spruijt et al. (2007) showed that a mutation in the ND1 gene can cause LDYT.

### Molecular Genetics

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family with LHON and/or early-onset dystonia. They identified a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 14459 of the MT-ND6 gene, which changed a moderately conserved alanine to valine at residue 72 (A72V). All 6 maternal relatives tested, from 3 generations, carried the mutation. One woman with LHON was heteroplasmic. The authors gave 4 reasons for concluding that this was likely the disease-causing mutation: it was specific to the family, it altered a moderately conserved amino acid in a complex I gene, it was unique among haplogroup D mtDNAs, and it was heteroplasmic.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia. The family had none of the mtDNA mutations previously reported in LHON. Sequencing of the protein-coding mitochondrial genes found 2 changes: - a heteroplasmic A-to-G transition at nucleotide 11696 in the MT-ND4 gene, causing a val312-to-ile (V312I) substitution - a homoplasmic T-to-A change at nucleotide 14596 in the MT-ND6 gene, causing an ile26-to-met (I26M) substitution A muscle biopsy showed severe complex I deficiency. The authors suggested that this deficiency linked the mtDNA mutations to the phenotype.

Watanabe et al. (2006) sequenced the entire mtDNA of the proband from a Japanese family with childhood-onset generalized dystonia and late-onset optic neuropathy. They found 42 nucleotide changes compared with the revised Cambridge reference sequence. Two of these, A3203G and G14459A (in MT-ND6), were absent from 200 healthy Japanese controls. The significance of A3203G remained unknown.

Spruijt et al. (2007) studied a patient with LHON who lacked the 3 primary LHON mutations. Screening of the complete mtDNA found a pathogenic 3697G-A mutation in the MT-ND1 gene, which seemed to be associated with isolated complex I deficiency. The authors concluded that this mutation caused both LHON and spastic dystonia in the same family.

Wang et al. (2009) studied a Chinese family with 18 LDYT patients. Resequencing microarray analysis of the entire mitochondrial genome found a m.10197G-A mutation in the MT-ND3 gene, causing an ala47-to-thr (A47T) substitution at a highly conserved residue. The mutation was on a haplogroup D4b background. It was homoplasmic in the leukocytes of all affected individuals on the maternal side. This was the first description of an ND3 mutation causing LDYT.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Spruijt et al. (2007) noted that the 3697G-A mutation in MT-ND1 can also cause MELAS syndrome. They suggested that other genetic factors may contribute to its clinical expression. They also observed that their family's clinical features resembled those of previously described families with LHON and dystonia due to an ND6 mutation.

Wang et al. (2009) noted that the m.10197G-A mutation in MT-ND3 had also been reported in French and Korean patients with Leigh syndrome. They suggested that this mutation causes a much wider range of presentations, including both LDYT and Leigh syndrome.

### Diagnosis

Marsden et al. (1986) suggested that the disorder might be a variant of Leigh disease, but noted considerable differences from pathologically proven Leigh disease. They stated that the disorder must be excluded in all patients thought to have idiopathic dystonia, subacute visual failure similar to Leber optic neuropathy, or both.

### Population Genetics

Jun et al. (1994) found that the family's mtDNA belonged to Native American haplogroup D. The np 14459 variant was absent from 38 Native American haplogroup D mtDNAs. It was also absent from 108 Asian, 103 Caucasian, and 99 African mtDNAs. Watanabe et al. (2006) found that the G14459A change was absent from 200 healthy Japanese individuals.
