### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is caused by mutation in one of several mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-encoded complex I genes, including MTND6, MTND1, and MTND3; mutations in MTND4 have also been implicated. ⚠︎

### Description

Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder associated with variable combinations of vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia (Wang et al., 2009). Within affected families, some members have optic neuropathy, others have dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration, and some have both (Novotny et al., 1986; De Vries et al., 1996). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Miyoshi et al. (1969) reported familial holotopistic striatal necrosis.

Marsden et al. (1986) described a disorder in 7 members of 2 families in whom dystonia was variably associated with subacute visual loss or asymptomatic optic atrophy, and with striking bilateral symmetric lucencies on CT scan, especially involving the putamen. They noted that the disorder might be a variant of Leigh disease, but that there were considerable differences between these patients and those with pathologically proven Leigh disease.

Novotny et al. (1986) studied a kindred in which 8 members had the neuroretinopathy of Leber disease, 14 had a progressive, generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration, and 1 had both disorders.

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family with maternally transmitted Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and/or early-onset dystonia associated with bilateral basal ganglia lesions.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family with Leber hereditary optic neuropathy associated with hereditary spastic dystonia. Neuropathy and ophthalmologic lesions were present together in some family members, whereas only 1 type of abnormality was found in others.

Watanabe et al. (2006) investigated a Japanese family with generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration, which occurred in childhood, and late-onset optic neuropathy.

Spruijt et al. (2007) reported a family with Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and spastic dystonia, with clinical characteristics similar to those of previously described families with LHON and dystonia due to an ND6 mutation.

Wang et al. (2009) described a Chinese family with 18 patients with LDYT.

### Biochemical Features

De Vries et al. (1996) found severe complex I deficiency in a muscle biopsy from a member of a Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia. Spruijt et al. (2007) found that the 3697G-A mutation in the MTND1 gene seemed to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency.

### Inheritance

Novotny et al. (1986) found that the mode of inheritance in their kindred was compatible with maternal transmission, and suggested that the disorder may be a mitochondrial disease. Jun et al. (1994) described maternal transmission in a 5-generation Hispanic family and identified a mitochondrial DNA mutation, consistent with mitochondrial inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-generation Hispanic family with LHON and/or early-onset dystonia, Jun et al. (1994) amplified buffy coat mtDNA from a severely affected child by PCR and sequenced more than 90% of it. The mtDNA was of the Native American haplogroup D genotype and differed from the Cambridge sequence at 40 nucleotide positions. One variant, a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 14459 in the MTND6 gene, changed a moderately conserved alanine to valine at residue 72 (A72V). The variant was not found in 38 Native American haplogroup D mtDNAs or in 108 Asian, 103 Caucasian, or 99 African mtDNAs. Six maternal relatives in 3 generations harbored the mutation, and 1 female with LHON was heteroplasmic.

In a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia in whom previously reported LHON mtDNA mutations were absent, De Vries et al. (1996) sequenced the protein-coding mitochondrial genes and identified 2 previously unreported mutations: a heteroplasmic A-to-G transition at nucleotide 11696 in the MTND4 gene, resulting in a val312-to-ile (V312I) substitution, and a homoplasmic T-to-A change at nucleotide 14596 in the MTND6 gene, resulting in an ile26-to-met (I26M) substitution. Severe complex I deficiency in muscle provided a link between these mtDNA mutations and the phenotype.

In the proband of a Japanese family with childhood-onset generalized dystonia and late-onset optic neuropathy, Watanabe et al. (2006) sequenced the entire mtDNA and found 42 nucleotide changes relative to the revised Cambridge reference sequence. Two changes, A3203G and G14459A (in MTND6), were absent in 200 healthy Japanese individuals. The significance of the A3203G change remained unknown.

Spruijt et al. (2007) screened the complete mtDNA of a patient with LHON who did not carry 1 of the 3 primary LHON mutations and identified a 3697G-A mutation in the MTND1 gene, which caused LHON and spastic dystonia in the same family. They noted that this mutation can also cause MELAS syndrome and that other genetic factors may contribute to clinical expression.

In a Chinese family with 18 LDYT patients, Wang et al. (2009) used resequencing microarray analysis of the entire mitochondrial genome and identified a 10197G-A mutation in the MTND3 gene, resulting in an ala47-to-thr (A47T) substitution at a highly conserved residue, on a haplogroup D4b background. The mutation was homoplasmic in leukocytes of all affected individuals on the maternal side. The authors noted that the same mutation had been described in French and Korean patients with Leigh syndrome.

### Diagnosis

Marsden et al. (1986) stated that this condition must be excluded in all patients thought to have idiopathic dystonia, subacute visual failure similar to Leber optic neuropathy, or a combination of these disorders.
