### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is caused by mutation in one of several mitochondrial genes encoding subunits of complex I: MTND6 (516006), MTND4 (516003), MTND1 (516000), and MTND3 (516002). ⚠︎

### Description

Leber optic atrophy and dystonia (LDYT) is a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder in which affected family members show variable combinations of vision loss, resembling Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), and progressive generalized dystonia (Wang et al., 2009). Some members of a family have both features and others have only one (Novotny et al., 1986; De Vries et al., 1996). CT scans can show bilateral symmetrical lucencies in the striatum, especially in the putamen (Marsden et al., 1986). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Miyoshi et al. (1969) reported familial holotopistic striatal necrosis.

Marsden et al. (1986) described 7 members of 2 families with dystonia. In some cases the dystonia occurred with subacute visual loss and in others with asymptomatic optic atrophy. CT scans showed striking bilateral symmetrical lucencies, especially in the putamen.

Novotny et al. (1986) studied a kindred in which 8 members had the neuroretinopathy of Leber disease, 14 had progressive generalized dystonia attributed to striatal degeneration, and 1 had both disorders.

Jun et al. (1994) studied a 5-generation Hispanic family whose members had maternally transmitted LHON, early-onset dystonia associated with bilateral basal ganglia lesions, or both.

De Vries et al. (1996) studied a large Dutch family with a rare form of LHON associated with hereditary spastic dystonia. Some family members had both the neurologic and the ophthalmologic abnormalities, and others had only one of the two.

Watanabe et al. (2006) described a Japanese family with childhood-onset generalized dystonia, attributed to striatal degeneration, together with late-onset optic neuropathy.

Spruijt et al. (2007) reported a family in which LHON and spastic dystonia occurred together. They noted that the clinical features of this family resembled those of previously described families with LHON and dystonia who carried an ND6 mutation.

Wang et al. (2009) described a Chinese family with 18 patients with LDYT. Affected members had variable combinations of vision loss and progressive generalized dystonia. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

De Vries et al. (1996) found a severe complex I deficiency in a muscle biopsy from a Dutch family with LHON and spastic dystonia. They concluded that this deficiency linked the family's mtDNA mutations to its complex phenotype, which included Leber optic neuropathy.

In a family with LHON and spastic dystonia, Spruijt et al. (2007) found that the 3697G-A mutation in the ND1 gene seemed to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency.

### Inheritance

In the kindred studied by Novotny et al. (1986), the pattern of inheritance was compatible with maternal transmission, and the authors suggested that the disorder might be a mitochondrial disease. Jun et al. (1994) found that LHON, dystonia, or both were transmitted maternally in a 5-generation Hispanic family.

### Heterogeneity

Wang et al. (2009) noted that LDYT had previously been attributed to mutations in the mitochondrial ND6 and ND4 genes. They identified MTND3 as an additional causative gene.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-generation Hispanic family with LHON and dystonia, Jun et al. (1994) used PCR to amplify buffy coat mtDNA from a severely affected child and sequenced more than 90% of it. The mtDNA belonged to Native American haplogroup D and differed from the Cambridge sequence at 40 positions. One of these differences was a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 14459 in the ND6 gene (MTND6), which changed a moderately conserved alanine to valine at residue 72 (A72V). The variant was absent from 38 Native American haplogroup D mtDNAs and from 108 Asian, 103 Caucasian, and 99 African mtDNAs. All 6 maternal relatives tested, spanning 3 generations, carried the mutation, and 1 female relative with LHON was heteroplasmic. The authors suggested that this was the disease-causing mutation.

In a large Dutch family with LHON and hereditary spastic dystonia, De Vries et al. (1996) found none of the mtDNA mutations previously reported in LHON. Sequencing of the protein-coding mitochondrial genes identified 2 changes. The first was a heteroplasmic A-to-G transition at nucleotide 11696 in the ND4 gene (MTND4), which resulted in a val312-to-ile (V312I) substitution. The second was a homoplasmic T-to-A transition at nucleotide 14596 in the ND6 gene, which resulted in an ile26-to-met (I26M) substitution. A muscle biopsy from the family showed a severe complex I deficiency.

Watanabe et al. (2006) sequenced the whole mitochondrial genome of the proband from a Japanese family with LHON and dystonia. They found 42 nucleotide changes relative to the revised Cambridge Reference Sequence. One of these was the 14459G-A mutation in the MTND6 gene, which was absent in 200 healthy Japanese controls. The authors noted that this mutation had previously been suggested to cause LHON and/or dystonia in Hispanic, African American, and Caucasian families. They stated that this was the first case of LHON with dystonia in which an mtDNA mutation had been found in a Japanese family. The proband also carried an A3203G mtDNA change, which was likewise absent in the 200 controls but was of unknown significance.

Spruijt et al. (2007) studied a family with LHON and spastic dystonia. A patient with LHON from this family did not carry any of the 3 primary LHON mutations. Screening of the patient's complete mitochondrial DNA identified a 3697G-A mutation in the ND1 gene (MTND1). The authors measured the heteroplasmy level, and the mutation seemed to be associated with an isolated complex I deficiency. They concluded that the mutation caused the LHON and spastic dystonia phenotype in this family. They noted that the same mutation can also cause MELAS syndrome and suggested that other genetic factors may contribute to clinical expression.

Wang et al. (2009) resequenced the entire mitochondrial genome by microarray in a Chinese family with 18 patients with LDYT. In affected members they identified a homoplasmic m.10197G-A mutation in the MTND3 gene, which resulted in an ala47-to-thr (A47T) substitution at a highly conserved residue. The mutation was on a haplogroup D4b background. Quantitative analysis showed that the mutation was homoplasmic in leukocytes of all affected individuals on the maternal side. This was the first description of an ND3 mutation causing LDYT.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wang et al. (2009) noted that the m.10197G-A mutation in MTND3 had also been described in French and Korean patients with Leigh syndrome. They suggested that the clinical spectrum of this mutation is broad and includes both LDYT and Leigh syndrome.

### Diagnosis

Marsden et al. (1986) suggested that the disorder could be a variant of Leigh disease but noted considerable differences from pathologically proven Leigh disease. They stated that the condition must be excluded in every patient thought to have idiopathic dystonia, subacute visual failure resembling Leber optic neuropathy, or both.
