### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome 2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene (611507) on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome 2 is an autosomal recessive disorder with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy. In the families in which it was first described, diabetes insipidus was absent, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding (El-Shanti et al., 2000).

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients from 4 families who had the Wolfram syndrome phenotype, including insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus. Several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding. The authors stated that these features had not been reported before in Wolfram syndrome.

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with Wolfram syndrome.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) reported a 22-year-old Italian woman (FS) who had had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers since age 5 years. She also had intestinal malrotation, partial duodenal intussusception, slow intestinal transit, and entero-gastric bile reflux. Her eye findings included reduced visual acuity, dyschromatopsia, optic disc pallor, and visual field defects, which were consistent with optic neuropathy. She did not have optic atrophy, and her pattern visual evoked potentials were normal. She had non-autoimmune, insulin-treated diabetes mellitus. She also had bilateral high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss with tinnitus triggered by loud environments. Her platelets showed defective aggregation to ADP and no secretion in response to ADP, but aggregation to collagen was normal. At age 22 years, she had diabetes mellitus, optic neuropathy with preserved visual acuity and normal electrophysiologic tests, no optic atrophy, tinnitus, bile reflux, slow intestinal transit, a reduced and reversible ADP platelet aggregation defect, and bleeding upper intestinal ulcers. Mozzillo et al. (2014) noted that their patient had optic neuropathy rather than optic atrophy, and that platelet aggregation was impaired to ADP rather than to collagen. They proposed that the eye disease in the Jordanian patients might also have been optic neuropathy. They suggested that so-called WFS2 might not be a subtype of Wolfram syndrome.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) reported Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome 2.

### Other Features

Mozzillo et al. (2014) found that the proband's heterozygous parents and brother had no eye abnormalities, diabetes, or deafness. However, they did have a reversible defect of ADP-induced platelet aggregation.

### Inheritance

Wolfram syndrome 2 is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. El-Shanti et al. (2000) mapped the locus by homozygosity mapping. Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene in consanguineous families. Mozzillo et al. (2014) found a homozygous CISD2 deletion in a patient whose parents and brother were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 4 families with Wolfram syndrome. In 3 of them, they significantly excluded linkage to the Wolfram syndrome locus on chromosome 4p16.1 using markers D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366. These 3 families showed linkage to chromosome 4q22-24.

Amr et al. (2007) noted that the 3 Jordanian families they studied did not have WFS1 mutations and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus on 4q22-q25.

### Heterogeneity

El-Shanti et al. (2000) found that the 2 affected members of 1 of their 4 families were homozygous for all 3 markers in the 4p16.1 region, whereas the other 3 families were linked to 4q22-24. The authors considered this further evidence for genetic heterogeneity in Wolfram syndrome.

### Molecular Genetics

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with WFS2. They found a homozygous G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 in a novel, highly conserved zinc-finger gene, ZCD2 (CISD2). The change predicts a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution. Although the change is nonsynonymous at a conserved residue, the authors found that it was pathogenic because it disrupted mRNA splicing, removing exon 2 and introducing a premature stop codon. The encoded protein, which they named ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein), localized to the endoplasmic reticulum but did not interact directly with wolframin. Amr et al. (2007) found no mutations in the WFS2 gene in 377 probands with hearing loss.

In an Italian woman with WFS2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) sequenced CISD2 cDNA and found a novel homozygous deletion of the whole of exon 2. Using SNP array and PCR, they mapped the deletion to a maximum size of 2,050 bp. The deleted region included the codons for amino acids 102 to 106 within the 2Fe-2S domain, and the authors suggested that this could result in a nonfunctional protein. Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers. The parents denied consanguinity, and runs of homozygosity did not show a relationship. However, microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor, and the grandparents had been born on the same small Italian island.

Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a novel CISD2 mutation in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome 2.

### Pathogenesis

Amr et al. (2007) found that lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals had a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium after thapsigargin stimulation than control cells. Resting intracellular calcium did not differ.

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 was located mainly in mitochondria. Based on their findings in knockout mice, they proposed that WFS2 is a mitochondria-mediated disorder caused by CISD2 mutation. They considered that this supported the earlier clinical hypothesis that Wolfram syndrome is partly mitochondria-mediated.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused breakdown and dysfunction of mitochondria, together with autophagic cell death. These changes came before the earliest signs of nerve and muscle degeneration and led to features suggestive of premature aging. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased in parallel with the premature aging phenotype.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, GLU37GLN [c.109G>C; p.E37Q]

In 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome 2, Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous c.109G-C transversion in the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene, which predicts a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution. The authors showed that the mutation disrupted mRNA splicing, causing exon 2 to be skipped and introducing a premature stop codon. Lymphoblastoid cells from affected individuals had a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium after thapsigargin stimulation than control cells.

**.0002 WOLFRAM SYNDROME 2**  
CISD2, EX2 DEL

In an Italian woman with Wolfram syndrome 2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) found a homozygous deletion of the whole of exon 2 of the CISD2 gene. Her features included diabetes mellitus, optic neuropathy without optic atrophy, recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers, high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, and defective platelet aggregation to ADP. The deletion was at most 2,050 bp in size and removed the codons for amino acids 102 to 106 in the 2Fe-2S domain. Her parents and brother were heterozygous carriers. They had no diabetes, eye abnormalities, or deafness, but they did have a reversible ADP-induced platelet aggregation defect. Microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor.
