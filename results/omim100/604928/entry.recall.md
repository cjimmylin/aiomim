### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome-2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene (611507) on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

El-Shanti et al. (2000) described Wolfram syndrome as an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder for which only insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and optic atrophy are necessary to make the diagnosis. In the families with the 4q-linked form (WFS2), El-Shanti et al. (2000) reported that diabetes insipidus was absent in all affected family members and that several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding. Mozzillo et al. (2014) listed sensorineural deafness as a feature of WFS2 and noted that WFS2 differs from WFS1 by the presence of bleeding upper intestinal ulcers and defective platelet aggregation. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients from 4 families with the Wolfram syndrome phenotype. Diabetes insipidus was absent in all affected family members. Several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding.

### Inheritance

El-Shanti et al. (2000) described Wolfram syndrome as an autosomal recessive disorder. Amr et al. (2007) found a homozygous mutation in patients with WFS2, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Mapping

El-Shanti et al. (2000) performed linkage analysis in 4 families with Wolfram syndrome. They significantly excluded linkage to the WFS1 region on chromosome 4p16 in 3 of the 4 families; the 2 affected individuals in the remaining family showed homozygosity for all 3 markers from the region of linkage on chromosome 4p16.1. In the other 3 families, El-Shanti et al. (2000) demonstrated linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24.

### Heterogeneity

El-Shanti et al. (2000) verified genetic heterogeneity for Wolfram syndrome by demonstrating linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24 in 3 families in which linkage to the WFS1 region on 4p16 had been excluded.

### Molecular Genetics

Amr et al. (2007) studied 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with Wolfram syndrome that had been shown not to have mutations in the WFS1 gene and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus at 4q22-q25. They identified a homozygous mutation (E37Q) in the CISD2 gene (also called ZCD2), which encodes the protein ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein). The mutation predicted a single amino acid substitution, but it was also found to disrupt mRNA splicing by eliminating exon 2, resulting in the introduction of a premature stop codon.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice drove premature aging and caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction, preceding nerve and muscle degeneration. They concluded that CISD2 is involved in mammalian life-span control. Cisd2 was primarily localized in the mitochondria, and mitochondrial degeneration appeared to have a direct phenotypic consequence that triggered the accelerated aging process in Cisd2 knockout mice.

In affected members of 3 consanguineous families of Jordanian descent with WFS2, Amr et al. The mutation caused aberrant splicing with elimination of exon 2, resulting in the introduction of a premature stop codon.
