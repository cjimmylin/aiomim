### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Wolfram syndrome 2 (WFS2) is caused by homozygous mutation in the CISD2 gene (611507) on chromosome 4q24.

### Description

Wolfram syndrome 2 is an autosomal recessive disorder that includes insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and visual impairment due to optic nerve involvement. Unlike the classic Wolfram syndrome, patients do not have diabetes insipidus. Several patients have had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding (El-Shanti et al., 2000), and a defect in platelet aggregation has been reported (Mozzillo et al., 2014).

### Clinical Features

El-Shanti et al. (2000) studied 16 patients from 4 families with the Wolfram syndrome phenotype who also had features not previously reported. None of the affected family members had diabetes insipidus, and several patients had profound upper gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding.

Mozzillo et al. (2014) reported a 17-year-old Italian girl whose parents said they were not consanguineous. She had recurrent bleeding upper intestinal ulcers from age 5 years. At age 14 years she was diagnosed with malrotation and partial duodenal intussusception, which required duodenal resection. At age 16 she developed reduced visual acuity and dyschromatopsia with optic disc pallor, and at age 17 she developed non-autoimmune diabetes requiring insulin. She also had bilateral high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss and slow intestinal transit with entero-gastric bile reflux. Eye examination showed slight optic disc pallor with preserved pupillary light reflexes. Pattern VEPs were normal, and brain MRI showed normal optic nerve caliber. Visual field, ERG, microperimetry, and OCT testing showed moderate bilateral optic nerve involvement compatible with optic neuropathy rather than optic atrophy, and the findings were unchanged after 5 years of follow-up. At age 22 years she had optic neuropathy with preserved visual acuity and no optic atrophy. Mozzillo et al. (2014) suggested that the ocular disease in the Jordanian WFS2 patients was probably also optic neuropathy, and that WFS2 might not be a true WFS subtype. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Mozzillo et al. (2014) found that their patient had reduced, reversible platelet aggregation in response to ADP at concentrations up to 10 micromolar, with no platelet secretion in response to ADP. Aggregation in response to collagen, epinephrine, and ristocetin was normal, and the other main coagulation tests were normal. Her heterozygous parents and brother also showed reversible ADP-induced aggregation defects without clinical signs. The authors suggested that differences in experimental conditions or between patients could explain why the Jordanian patients had a collagen aggregation defect instead.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of Wolfram syndrome 2 in the families reported by El-Shanti et al. (2000) and Amr et al. (2007), in which affected individuals from consanguineous families were homozygous at the disease locus, is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Mozzillo et al. (2014) found a homozygous CISD2 deletion in an affected girl whose parents and brother were heterozygous carriers. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Using 3 microsatellite markers (D4S432, D4S3023, and D4S2366) linked to the Wolfram syndrome locus on chromosome 4p16.1, El-Shanti et al. (2000) significantly excluded linkage to that locus in 3 of 4 families. In the remaining family, the 2 affected individuals were homozygous for all 3 markers from the 4p16.1 region. Homozygosity mapping in the 3 unlinked families showed linkage to chromosome 4q22-q24.

Amr et al. (2007) noted that 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome lacked WFS1 mutations and had been mapped to the WFS2 locus on chromosome 4q22-q25.

### Heterogeneity

El-Shanti et al. (2000) reported further evidence of genetic heterogeneity in Wolfram syndrome: 1 family was consistent with the 4p16.1 locus and 3 families were linked to 4q22-q24. The authors stated that they had identified a new locus that plays a role in the etiology of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus.

Because WFS1 mutations also cause nonsyndromic low-frequency and progressive hearing loss and optic atrophy with hearing loss, Amr et al. (2007) screened 377 probands with hearing loss for mutations in the WFS2 gene and found none.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of 3 consanguineous Jordanian families with Wolfram syndrome who had previously been shown not to have WFS1 mutations and whose disease mapped to the WFS2 locus on 4q22-q25, Amr et al. (2007) identified a homozygous G-to-C transversion at nucleotide 109 of the ZCD2 (CISD2) gene, predicting a glu37-to-gln (E37Q) substitution at a conserved residue. The mutation disrupted mRNA splicing by eliminating exon 2, resulting in a premature stop codon; the authors concluded that the aberrant splicing, rather than the amino acid change, was the basis of pathogenesis.

In an Italian girl with WFS2, Mozzillo et al. (2014) found a novel homozygous deletion of the entire CISD2 exon 2, detected because exon 2 failed to amplify by PCR and confirmed by cDNA sequencing. SNP array analysis restricted the deletion to at most 2,050 bp, extending from intron 2 to intron 3. Her parents and brother were heterozygous for the deletion. The deleted region includes the codons for amino acids 102 to 106 within the 2Fe-2S domain, and the authors suggested that the deletion would result in a nonfunctional protein. Runs-of-homozygosity analysis showed no parental relationship, but microsatellite analysis supported a common ancestor; the grandparents came from the same small Italian island. ⚠︎

Rondinelli et al. (2015) identified a novel CISD2 mutation in Italian sibs with Wolfram syndrome 2.

### Pathogenesis

Amr et al. (2007) showed that the ZCD2-encoded protein, ERIS (endoplasmic reticulum intermembrane small protein), localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum, like wolframin, but does not interact directly with wolframin. Lymphoblastoid cells from individuals with Wolfram syndrome 2 showed a significantly greater rise in intracellular calcium on thapsigargin stimulation compared with controls, although resting intracellular calcium concentrations did not differ.

Chen et al. (2009) showed that Cisd2 is located mainly in the mitochondria and is involved in controlling mammalian life span. They concluded that their Cisd2 knockout mouse findings strongly support the hypothesis that Wolfram syndrome is partly a mitochondria-mediated disorder, and proposed that CISD2 mutation causes the mitochondria-mediated disorder WFS2 in humans.

### Animal Model

Chen et al. (2009) found that Cisd2 deficiency in mice caused mitochondrial breakdown and dysfunction accompanied by autophagic cell death. These events preceded the 2 earliest signs, nerve and muscle degeneration, and together led to a set of features suggestive of premature aging. Mitochondrial degeneration worsened with age, and autophagy increased alongside the premature aging phenotype.
