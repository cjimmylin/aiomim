### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene on chromosome 20q13.3.

### Description

Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive collagenopathy caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in the COL9A3 gene. Rad et al. (2022) found high myopia and moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss to be consistent features in all reported cases. Skeletal findings, such as epiphyseal or spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, were more variable. Vitreoretinal degeneration, retinal detachment, and cataract occurred in some patients. ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Rad et al. (2022) designated Stickler syndrome caused by homozygous loss-of-function variants in COL9A3 as STL type VI.

### Clinical Features

Faletra et al. (2014) described a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. According to the summary by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), the family included 3 affected sibs with moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss, high myopia, normal vitreous, midface hypoplasia, tibial rotation, mild irregularities of the capital femoral and metacarpal epiphyses, and pes planus. The sibs also had moderate to severe intellectual disability. Rad et al. (2022) tabulated these patients as Moroccan offspring of first-cousin parents.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy whose parents were third cousins from India. He failed his newborn hearing screen. Audiology at 5 months identified moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe sensorineural hearing loss on the left, and the hearing loss remained stable. A head CT at 7 months showed mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months, but by 2 years his myopia was -6.75 OD and -8 OS, progressing to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS by 12 years. No vitreous abnormalities were noted. Femoral and tibial bowing were present at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, normal knee epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly. Growth parameters and intelligence were normal. He also had autoimmune hypothyroidism, identified at 10 years. His parents and sister were not reported to have hearing loss, myopia, or skeletal anomalies.

Nixon et al. (2019) assessed 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome due to type IX collagen variants. One family, with 2 patients, had a COL9A3 variant. All 6 patients were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and all had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy. Markova et al. (2021) noted that the eldest COL9A3 patient reported by Nixon et al. (2019) had refraction of up to -23 D in each eye at 20 years of age. That patient also had severe arthropathy and operated scoliosis and used a wheelchair.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a boy from a nonconsanguineous family. At 1 month he had adductor spasm, hip dysplasia, and bilateral absence of ossification centers. Congenital high bilateral myopia was found at 3 months. His speech development was delayed, and bilateral grade 3-4 sensorineural hearing loss was diagnosed at 11 months. Spinal radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed sacral spina bifida occulta, mild platyspondyly, and kyphosis with the apex at T11. Radiographs of the lower limbs at 3 years showed flattened and irregular femoral heads, broadened and shortened femoral necks, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. Ophthalmic assessment showed peripheral pigment rearrangement, multiple vitreous floaters, and a preretinal membrane in the right eye, with a normal electroretinogram. At 2 years 5 months he had valgus knee deformity, waddling gait, rapid fatigability, and pain in the lower limb joints. Facial features included a slightly flattened nasal bridge, small nose, mild midface hypoplasia, and high palate. He also had genu valgum and planovalgus foot deformity. The authors considered the phenotype more severe than in earlier COL9A3 cases because of the early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration.

Rad et al. (2022) reported 5 affected individuals from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families. In family 1, the only affected individual was a 28-year-old woman born to first-cousin parents. She had high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, and retinal pigmentary changes. She also had severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss. Her joint examination and x-rays were normal, although she complained of knee pain. In family 2, 2 brothers aged 65 and 57 years had a history of multiple vitreoretinal surgeries for recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachment caused by advanced vitreoretinal degeneration. The older brother was blind without light perception. Both brothers had severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss and a herniated cervical disc. In family 3, a boy aged 11.8 years and his sister aged 3.1 years had myopia and congenital moderate to severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss. Both had spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, pes planus, depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. None of the patients had intellectual disability. The authors observed that the older patients in family 2 had only myopia, hearing loss, and a herniated cervical disc, whereas the much younger patients in family 3 had more prominent skeletal and craniofacial findings.

### Inheritance

Faletra et al. (2014) described the first family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to COL9A3 mutation. In the family reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), the affected boy was homozygous for the mutation, and his parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. Markova et al. (2021) showed that the 2 mutations in their patient were in trans. Rad et al. (2022) noted that Nixon et al. (2019) had observed mild Stickler phenotypes in carrier parents, but that neither their own study nor others had found mild phenotypes in heterozygous individuals.

### Mapping

Markova et al. (2021) stated that the COL9A3 gene maps to chromosome 20q13.3.

### Molecular Genetics

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function mutation (c.1176_1198del, p.Gln393CysfsTer25) in the COL9A3 gene. Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) and Rad et al. (2022) reported that the affected sibs were homozygous for this mutation.

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) studied a boy with Stickler syndrome born to consanguineous parents. Earlier sequence and deletion/duplication analysis of COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and of specific exons of COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3, had been negative. Exome sequencing then identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene, predicted to cause premature termination and absence of the gene product. The variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and was not found in public SNP databases. Both parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous.

Nixon et al. (2019) found homozygous type IX collagen variants in 6 patients with recessive Stickler syndrome from 4 families. One family, with 2 patients, carried a COL9A3 variant. Rad et al. (2022) listed this variant as a homozygous nonsense mutation, c.1411C-T (p.Arg471Ter).

In a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) used targeted next-generation sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes. They identified compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (p.Arg90Ter) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (p.Arg577Ter) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans. The proband's healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants. This was the first reported patient with COL9A3-related Stickler syndrome who was not homozygous.

In probands from 3 unrelated consanguineous Iranian families with Stickler syndrome, Rad et al. (2022) used exome sequencing and identified 3 different homozygous loss-of-function variants in the COL9A3 gene. These were a 10-bp deletion (c.107_116del, p.Pro36ArgfsTer49) in exon 2 in family 1, a nonsense variant (c.1204C-T, p.Arg402Ter) in exon 23 in family 2, and a 1-bp deletion (c.355delC) in exon 7 in family 3. For the family 3 variant, the protein change was given as p.Leu119SerfsTer9 in the text and as p.Leu119SerfsTer10 in the table. Segregation was tested by Sanger sequencing in available family members.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 23-BP DEL, NT1176 [c.1176_1198del; p.Gln393Cysfs*25]

In a family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Faletra et al. (2014) identified a loss-of-function deletion (c.1176_1198del) in the COL9A3 gene, resulting in a frameshift (Gln393CysfsTer25). Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported that the 3 affected sibs were homozygous for this mutation. ⚠︎

**.0002 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 1-BP DUP, 650C [c.650dupC]

In a boy with Stickler syndrome born to consanguineous parents from India, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) identified a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene, predicted to cause premature termination of translation. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It was not present in public SNP databases. The parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous.

**.0003 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG471TER [c.1411C>T; p.Arg471Ter]

Nixon et al. (2019) reported a family with 2 patients with recessive Stickler syndrome due to a homozygous COL9A3 variant. Rad et al. (2022) listed this variant as a homozygous c.1411C-T transition in exon 28, resulting in an arg471-to-ter (R471X) substitution.

**.0004 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG90TER [c.268C>T; p.Arg90Ter]

In a boy from a nonconsanguineous family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the COL9A3 gene. One was a c.268C-T transition in exon 5, resulting in an arg90-to-ter (R90X) substitution. The other was R577X. The mutations were found by targeted next-generation sequencing, and Sanger sequencing showed they were in trans.

**.0005 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG577TER [c.1729C>T; p.Arg577Ter]

In a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Markova et al. (2021) identified a c.1729C-T transition in exon 30 of the COL9A3 gene, resulting in an arg577-to-ter (R577X) substitution. It was in compound heterozygosity with R90X.

**.0006 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 10-BP DEL, NT107 [c.107_116del; p.Pro36Argfs*49]

In a 28-year-old Iranian woman with Stickler syndrome, born to first-cousin parents (family 1), Rad et al. (2022) identified a homozygous 10-bp deletion (c.107_116del) in exon 2 of the COL9A3 gene, resulting in a frameshift (Pro36ArgfsTer49). The mutation was found by exome sequencing. Its allele frequency in gnomAD was 0.00001390.

**.0007 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, ARG402TER [c.1204C>T; p.Arg402*]

In 2 Iranian brothers with Stickler syndrome, born to first-cousin parents (family 2), Rad et al. (2022) identified a homozygous c.1204C-T transition in exon 23 of the COL9A3 gene, resulting in an arg402-to-ter (R402X) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and was not reported in public databases.

**.0008 STICKLER SYNDROME, TYPE VI**  
COL9A3, 1-BP DEL, 355C [c.355delC; p.Leu119Serfs*9; p.Leu119Serfs*10]

In 2 Iranian sibs with Stickler syndrome, born to first-cousin parents (family 3), Rad et al. The protein change was given as Leu119SerfsTer9 in the text and as Leu119SerfsTer10 in the table. The variant was found by exome sequencing and was not reported in public databases.
