### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Stickler syndrome type VI (STL6) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the COL9A3 gene (120270) on chromosome 20q13.33.

### Description

Stickler syndrome type VI is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function variants in COL9A3. In a review of 7 families, Rad et al. (2022) found that consistent features were moderate-to-profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred occasionally, and skeletal involvement was more variable. Markova et al. (2021) noted that none of the patients they reviewed had cleft palate.

### Clinical Features

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reported a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, born to consanguineous parents (third cousins) from India. He failed newborn hearing screening. At 5 months he had moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right and severe loss on the left, which remained stable. Temporal bone CT showed mild enlargement of the vestibular aqueducts. He had no myopia at 5 months. By 2 years he had myopia of -6.75 OD and -8 OS, which progressed to -10.75 OD and -11.25 OS by 12 years. He had no vitreous abnormalities. Femoral and tibial bowing were present at birth. Radiographs at 6 years showed slightly flattened proximal femoral epiphyses, flattened acetabula, irregularity of the medial distal femoral epiphyses, and mild platyspondyly. He also had midface hypoplasia and did not have cleft palate. Growth and intelligence were normal. Autoimmune hypothyroidism was diagnosed at 10 years.

Nixon et al. (2019) described 6 patients from 4 families with recessive Stickler syndrome due to homozygous variants in type IX collagen genes. One family (1 patient) had a COL9A1 variant, 2 families (3 patients) had COL9A2 variants, and 1 family (2 patients) had a COL9A3 variant. All patients were highly myopic, with congenital megalophthalmos and abnormal, hypoplastic vitreous gel, and all had sensorineural hearing loss. One patient had severe arthropathy.

Markova et al. (2021) reported a boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, born to healthy nonconsanguineous Russian parents. At 1 month he had hip dysplasia with bilateral absence of ossification centers. At 3 months he had congenital high bilateral myopia. At 11 months he was diagnosed with bilateral grade 3-4 sensorineural hearing loss, and he had delayed speech. Radiographs at 1 and 3 years showed mild spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with spina bifida occulta of the sacral vertebrae, mild platyspondyly, kyphosis, flattened and irregular femoral heads, coxa valga, and flattened distal femoral epiphyses. Eye examination showed peripheral pigment changes, multiple vitreous floaters, and a preretinal membrane, and the ERG was normal. At age 2 years 5 months he had genu valgum, waddling gait, lower limb joint pain, a flat nasal bridge, mild midface hypoplasia, and a high palate, without cleft palate. The authors considered his joint pain from age 3 years to be early-onset arthropathy. ⚠︎

Rad et al. (2022) described a 28-year-old Iranian woman (family 1) who was the only affected child of first-cousin parents. She had high myopia, vitreoretinal degeneration with empty vitreous, multiple lattice degenerations, retinal pigmentary changes, and severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss. One skull x-ray showed unilateral absence of the frontal sinus. Her joint and bone examination and x-rays were normal, although she had knee pain. She did not have the typical craniofacial features of Stickler syndrome.

Rad et al. (2022) described 2 affected brothers (family 2), aged 65 and 57 years, born to Iranian first-cousin parents. In both, advanced vitreoretinal degeneration caused recurrent rhegmatogenous retinal detachments that required multiple surgeries. The older brother had no light perception in either eye. The younger brother could count fingers with one eye and had no light perception in the other. Both had severe progressive sensorineural hearing loss and a herniated cervical disc. The older brother also had muscular atrophy.

Rad et al. (2022) described 2 sibs (family 3), a boy aged 11.8 years and a girl aged 3.1 years, born to Iranian first-cousin parents. Both had myopia and congenital moderate to severe progressive sensorineural hearing impairment. Both also had spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, pes planus, a depressed nasal bridge, and anteverted nares. Midface hypoplasia and downslanting palpebral fissures were more pronounced in the girl. The boy had knee pain. Neither sib had intellectual disability.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of Stickler syndrome type VI is autosomal recessive. Faletra et al. (2014), Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), Markova et al. (2021), and Rad et al. (2022) each reported recessive disease with biallelic COL9A3 mutations. In the family reported by Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018), the unaffected parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous for the mutation.

Rad et al. (2022) observed no mild phenotypes in heterozygous carriers in their 3 families. They noted that this differed from the report of Nixon et al., who stated that carrier parents can show mild features of Stickler syndrome.

### Heterogeneity

Faletra et al. (2014) noted that Stickler syndrome is genetically heterogeneous. Dominant forms are caused by mutations in COL2A1, COL11A1, and COL11A2, and recessive forms had been associated with COL9A1 and COL9A2. They also noted that, before their report, only heterozygous COL9A3 mutations had been described in humans. These had been associated with multiple epiphyseal dysplasia type 3, susceptibility to intervertebral disc disease, and hearing loss.

### Molecular Genetics

Faletra et al. (2014) described the first family with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome due to a loss-of-function mutation in the COL9A3 gene. The mutation was a 23-bp deletion (c.1176_1198del) predicted to cause a frameshift and premature termination (Gln393CysfsTer25). ⚠︎

In a boy from a consanguineous Indian family (R08-308) with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome, Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) used exome sequencing to identify a homozygous 1-bp duplication (c.650dupC, NM_001853.3) in exon 13 of the COL9A3 gene. The duplication was predicted to cause a frameshift, premature termination, and absence of the gene product. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, which was not found in public SNP databases. The unaffected parents and an unaffected sib were heterozygous. Earlier targeted analysis of COL2A1, COL11A1, COL11A2, and specific exons of COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 had been negative.

In 2 patients from 1 family with recessive Stickler syndrome, Nixon et al. (2019) identified a homozygous variant in the COL9A3 gene. Of the variants they found in their 4 families (in COL9A1, COL9A2, or COL9A3), 3 were novel.

Markova et al. (2021) performed targeted sequencing of 166 skeletal disorder genes in a Russian boy with autosomal recessive Stickler syndrome. They found compound heterozygous nonsense mutations in the COL9A3 gene: c.268C-T (R90X) in exon 5 and c.1729C-T (R577X) in exon 30. Sanger sequencing showed that the 2 mutations were in trans. His healthy sibs did not carry pathogenic COL9A3 variants. The authors stated that this was the first reported case of COL9A3-related recessive Stickler syndrome that was not caused by a homozygous mutation.

Rad et al. (2022) used exome sequencing to study 3 consanguineous Iranian families with Stickler syndrome. In the proband of family 1, they identified a homozygous 10-bp deletion in exon 2 of COL9A3 (c.107_116del, NM_001853.3), predicted to cause a frameshift (Pro36ArgfsTer49). In gnomAD this deletion had an allele frequency of 0.0000139 (2 carriers), and it was not reported in Iranome, GME, or 1000 Genomes. Segregation was checked by Sanger sequencing in available family members. In 2 affected brothers from family 2, they identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in exon 23 (c.1204C-T, R402X). In 2 affected sibs from family 3, they identified a homozygous 1-bp deletion in exon 7 (c.355delC). The variants in families 2 and 3 were not reported in public databases.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) reviewed 12 patients from 6 families with Stickler syndrome due to COL9A1, COL9A2, or COL9A3 mutations. All of these patients had high myopia, midface hypoplasia, and mild to severe hearing loss. Vitreous and retinal degeneration had been reported with COL9A1 and COL9A2 mutations but not with COL9A3 mutations. The authors suggested that loss of the alpha-3 chain of type IX collagen results in a phenotype similar to that caused by loss of the alpha-1 and alpha-2 chains. They considered this phenotype distinct from recessive COL11A1-related Stickler syndrome, in which hearing loss is severe to profound and cleft palate is present.

Nixon et al. (2019) noted that congenital megalophthalmos and myopia occur in both the dominant and recessive forms of Stickler syndrome. In contrast, sensorineural hearing loss is more common and more severe in the recessive forms caused by type IX collagen variants.

Markova et al. (2021) compared their patient with 6 previously reported patients from 3 families with recessive COL9A3-related Stickler syndrome. All of the patients had high myopia and sensorineural hearing loss, and none had cleft palate. Their patient also had early-onset arthropathy and vitreoretinal degeneration, which the authors described as a more severe presentation. This contrasted with the earlier observation of Hanson-Kahn et al. (2018) that vitreoretinal degeneration had not been reported with COL9A3 mutations.

Rad et al. (2022) compared their 5 patients with previously reported patients carrying biallelic COL9A3 loss-of-function variants, for a total of 7 families. Consistent features were moderate-to-profound progressive sensorineural hearing loss and moderate to high myopia with vitreoretinal degeneration. Retinal detachment and cataract occurred occasionally, and skeletal involvement was more variable. The older patients in their family 2 had only myopia, hearing loss, and herniated cervical discs. In contrast, the younger patients in family 3 had spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia and craniofacial abnormalities.

### Diagnosis

Nixon et al. (2019) suggested adding COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 to genetic screening panels for patients with congenital hearing loss. They reasoned that early diagnosis of recessive Stickler syndrome would have a high impact for children at risk of dual sensory impairment.
