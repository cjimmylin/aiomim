### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PRPH2 gene (179605) on chromosome 6p21.1. Digenic RP7 is caused by double heterozygous mutation in the PRPH2 and ROM1 genes. Homozygous mutation in PRPH2 has also been found in patients with juvenile RP. ⚠︎

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-7 is a form of retinitis pigmentosa caused by mutation in the gene encoding peripherin/RDS, a photoreceptor cell-specific glycoprotein and the human homolog of the murine retinal degeneration slow (rds) gene (Kajiwara et al., 1991; Farrar et al., 1991). The disorder is usually transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait, but a digenic form occurs in individuals who are double heterozygotes for mutations in PRPH2 and ROM1 (Kajiwara et al., 1994). Manes et al. (2015) found that PRPH2 mutations cause highly variable phenotypes and moderate forms of autosomal dominant RP, with some affected members of a family having pericentral RP or macular dystrophy and others having widespread RP. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Weleber et al. (1993) described a family in which a 75-year-old woman, her 2 daughters (aged 44 and 50 years), and her 49-year-old son showed 3 different phenotypes. The mother presented at age 63 years with adult-onset retinitis pigmentosa and a profoundly abnormal ERG; over 12 years her disease progressed dramatically, with marked loss of peripheral visual field. One daughter developed pattern macular dystrophy at age 31 years; at age 44 her ERG was moderately abnormal, but her disease was limited to the macula. The other daughter presented with macular degeneration at age 42 years and over 10 years developed the picture of fundus flavimaculatus, with preserved peripheral field and a moderately abnormal ERG. The son had onset of macular degeneration at age 44 years, with pericentral scotomas, a markedly abnormal ERG, and punctate pigment epithelial transmission defects on fluorescein angiography. ⚠︎

Wang et al. (2013) described 3 unrelated patients (1318, 3256, and 741) who were homozygous for PRPH2 missense mutations; 2 had Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) and 1 had juvenile RP. All had visual impairment within the first year of life, nondetectable or reduced ERGs, and a very similar maculopathy in the fundus. The 2 LCA patients also had nystagmus. Fundus findings included a multilobulated central atrophic maculopathy, vessel narrowing, peripheral pigment deposits or diffuse retinal dystrophy, and optic disc pallor in 1 patient.

Manes et al. (2015) found that disease severity was moderate in French patients with autosomal dominant RP and PRPH2 mutations: visual acuity was 1 to 0.5 in 78% of eyes, and 52% of eyes retained more than 50% of the visual field. Some patients characteristically had vitelliform deposits or macular involvement. Within some families, certain members had pericentral RP or macular dystrophy, whereas others had widespread RP. The authors concluded that PRPH2 mutations cause highly variable phenotypes and moderate forms of adRP, including mild cases that could be underdiagnosed.

### Inheritance

RP7 is usually transmitted in an autosomal dominant pattern, as shown in the families reported by Kajiwara et al. (1991), Farrar et al. (1991), Jordan et al. (1992), Wells et al. (1993), Weleber et al. (1993), Kikawa et al. (1994), and Manes et al. (2015).

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified digenic inheritance of RP in 3 families in which only individuals who were double heterozygotes for mutations in the unlinked ROM1 and peripherin/RDS genes developed retinitis pigmentosa. They concluded that the allelic and nonallelic heterogeneity of monogenic RP is further complicated by interactions between unlinked mutations causing digenic RP, and suggested that recognition of this inheritance pattern might help identify other examples of digenic inheritance in human disease.

Wang et al. (2013) found homozygous PRPH2 mutations in patients with severe early-onset disease (LCA or juvenile RP), whereas heterozygous relatives had no symptoms but showed detectable macular pattern dystrophy; the authors suggested a dose-dependent effect.

### Mapping

Farrar et al. (1991) performed linkage analysis with chromosome 6p markers in an Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. They obtained positive 2-point lod scores with 5 markers, including D6S105 (Z = 6.081 at theta = 0.00), HLA-DRA (Z = 4.364 at theta = 0.00), and RDS (Z = 5.376 at theta = 0.00). Multipoint analysis gave a maximum lod score of 6.6 at HLA-DRA. The authors concluded that the adRP gene in this pedigree was localized to 6p.

In the large Irish family with late-onset autosomal dominant RP previously linked to 6p, Jordan et al. (1992) found that an intragenic marker from the peripherin-RDS gene cosegregated with the disease locus with no recombination (lod score 5.46 at theta = 0.00). Using the CEPH reference panel to map peripherin-RDS relative to other 6p markers, they obtained a multipoint lod score for adRP of 8.21, peaking at the peripherin-RDS locus, which they considered strong evidence suggesting a role for peripherin-RDS in one form of adRP.

### Heterogeneity

Farrar et al. (1991) concluded that the linkage of autosomal dominant RP to 6p in an Irish pedigree provided direct evidence for an additional autosomal dominant RP locus, and they proposed peripherin-RDS, the human equivalent of the mouse retinal degeneration slow (rds) gene, as a strong candidate gene.

### Molecular Genetics

Kajiwara et al. (1991) identified 3 mutations in RDS, the human homolog of the murine retinal degeneration slow (rds) gene, in separate families with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. In each family the mutation cosegregated with the disease. The authors concluded that some cases of autosomal dominant RP are due to mutations at the RDS locus.

In affected members of a large Irish pedigree with autosomal dominant RP linked to 6p, in which the disease gene was tightly linked to the gene encoding peripherin, Farrar et al. (1991) identified a 3-bp deletion in the peripherin/RDS gene that removed one of a pair of highly conserved cysteine residues in the predicted third transmembrane domain of peripherin. The deletion segregated with the disease and was not found in unaffected controls.

Wells et al. (1993) screened the RDS gene in families with autosomal dominant retinal dystrophies and found deletion of a cysteine at codon 118/119 in 1 family with retinitis pigmentosa.

In a family whose members had retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus, Weleber et al. (1993) found a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene. They concluded that this single mutation can produce clinically disparate phenotypes even within the same family.

Kikawa et al. (1994) identified an asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene, detected by nonradioisotopic SSCP analysis, as the cause of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa associated with bull's-eye maculopathy.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with mutations in the unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes ROM1 and peripherin/RDS. In these families, only individuals who were double heterozygotes, carrying a mutation in both genes, developed retinitis pigmentosa.

Using targeted next-generation sequencing of 163 retinal disease genes in patients with LCA or juvenile RP, Wang et al. (2013) found a homozygous c.554T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a leu185-to-pro (L185P) substitution, in LCA patient 3256; they noted that this mutation had previously been reported to cause digenic RP together with a ROM1 null mutation. Sequencing of PRPH2 in 135 additional unsolved patients identified the same homozygous L185P mutation in juvenile RP patient 741, whose parents were both heterozygous. Wang et al. (2013) also found a homozygous c.637T-C transition, resulting in a cys213-to-arg (C213R) substitution, in LCA patient 1318; this mutation had previously been associated with autosomal dominant pattern dystrophy. The patient's mother and son were heterozygous carriers, and an unaffected brother was homozygous wildtype. The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Manes et al. (2015) identified a novel recurrent leu254-to-gln (L254Q) mutation in PRPH2 in French families with autosomal dominant RP. Peripherin-2 carrying the mutation, produced in yeast and analyzed by Western blot, aggregated, which the authors suggested indicated abnormal folding.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Weleber et al. (1993) observed intrafamilial phenotypic variability, with the same peripherin/RDS codon 153/154 deletion causing retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus within a single family.

Wells et al. (1993) found that, in contrast to the RP-associated cysteine deletion at codon 118/119, 3 families with similar macular dystrophy had RDS mutations at codon 172 (arg172-to-trp in 2 families and arg172-to-gln in 1), and a family with adult vitelliform macular dystrophy had a stop codon at codon 258. They concluded that both RP and macular dystrophies are caused by RDS mutations and that the functional significance of certain amino acids in peripherin-RDS may differ in cones and rods.

Wang et al. (2013) found that relatives heterozygous for PRPH2 mutations had no symptoms but showed detectable macular pattern dystrophy. The 56-year-old heterozygous father of patient 741 (L185P) had macular pattern dystrophy with essentially normal visual acuity, and the 57-year-old mother of patient 1318 (C213R) had a butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy with 20/20 acuity. The authors suggested that the severe early-onset disease in homozygotes reflects a dose-dependent effect, consistent with rds mouse models, and that heterozygous L185P carriers previously reported as asymptomatic in digenic RP families may have a subclinical phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Manes et al. (2015) screened PRPH2 by Sanger sequencing in 310 French families with autosomal dominant RP and identified 15 mutations (6 novel and 9 previously reported) in 32 families, a prevalence of 10.3%. This was higher than previously reported (0 to 8%) and made PRPH2 the second most frequent adRP gene after RHO in their series.

### Animal Model

Kajiwara et al. (1991) noted that the murine retinal degeneration slow (rds) mutation is semidominant. In rds mice, rod and cone photoreceptors develop abnormally and then slowly degenerate, making it a possible model for retinitis pigmentosa.
