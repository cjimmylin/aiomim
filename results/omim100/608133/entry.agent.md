### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene (PRPH2) on chromosome 6p. A digenic form of RP7 is caused by double heterozygous mutations in the PRPH2 and ROM1 genes. Homozygous mutation in PRPH2 has also been reported in patients with an early-onset retinal dystrophy with Leber congenital amaurosis/juvenile RP phenotypes. ⚠︎

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-7 is a form of retinitis pigmentosa, a group of retinopathies characterized by photoreceptor degeneration, caused by mutation in the gene encoding peripherin/RDS, a photoreceptor-specific glycoprotein. It is usually inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, but a digenic form requiring heterozygous mutations in both PRPH2 and ROM1 has been described (Kajiwara et al., 1994). PRPH2 mutations produce highly variable phenotypes, and retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus can occur within a single family (Weleber et al., 1993; Manes et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

In the title of their linkage report on an Irish pedigree, Farrar et al. (1991) designated the chromosome 6p autosomal dominant RP locus RP6, although the abstract of the same report referred to the gene segregating in the pedigree as RP5. Jordan et al. (1992) also referred to the locus in this late-onset Irish family as RP6.

### Clinical Features

Farrar et al. (1991) studied an Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa.

Weleber et al. (1993) reported 3 separate phenotypes within a single family carrying a 3-bp deletion in the peripherin/RDS gene. The mother presented at age 63 years with a profoundly abnormal electroretinogram (ERG) and adult-onset retinitis pigmentosa that progressed dramatically over 12 years, with marked loss of peripheral visual field. One daughter developed pattern macular dystrophy at age 31 years; at age 44 years her ERG was moderately abnormal but her clinical disease was limited to the macula. Another daughter presented at age 42 years with macular degeneration and over 10 years developed the clinical picture of fundus flavimaculatus, with preserved peripheral visual field and a moderately abnormal ERG. The son had onset of macular degeneration at age 44 years, with pericentral scotomas, a markedly abnormal ERG, and punctate pigment epithelial transmission defects on fluorescein angiography.

Kikawa et al. (1994) reported autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa associated with bull's-eye maculopathy in association with a mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene.

Wang et al. (2013) described 3 patients homozygous for PRPH2 mutations who had Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) or juvenile RP phenotypes, including visual impairment within the first year of life, nystagmus in the 2 LCA patients, nondetectable or reduced ERGs, and a very similar form of maculopathy. Fundus findings included multilobulated central atrophic maculopathy, vessel narrowing, pigment deposits, choroidal sclerosis, and optic disc pallor. Heterozygous family members were asymptomatic but showed maculopathy; for example, the 56-year-old father of 1 patient had macular pattern dystrophy with essentially normal visual acuity, and the asymptomatic 57-year-old mother of another patient had a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy and other retinal flecks.

Manes et al. (2015) found that the clinical severity of PRPH2-associated adRP in the patients they examined was moderate, with 78% of eyes having visual acuity of 1-0.5 and 52% of eyes retaining more than 50% of the visual field. Some patients showed vitelliform deposits or macular involvement, and in some families pericentral RP or macular dystrophy was found in some members while widespread RP was present in others.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of RP7 was shown by Kajiwara et al. (1991), who found peripherin/RDS mutations cosegregating with autosomal dominant RP in separate families, and by Farrar et al. (1991), who mapped the disorder in a large Irish autosomal dominant pedigree. Kajiwara et al. (1994) demonstrated digenic inheritance in 3 families in which only double heterozygotes for mutations in the unlinked ROM1 and peripherin/RDS genes developed RP. ⚠︎

### Mapping

By linkage analysis in an Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant RP, Farrar et al. (1991) obtained positive 2-point lod scores with 5 chromosome 6p markers, including D6S105 (Z = 6.081 at theta = 0.00), HLA-DRA (Z = 4.364 at theta = 0.00), and RDS (Z = 5.376 at theta = 0.00). Multipoint analysis gave a lod score of 6.6, maximizing at HLA-DRA, localizing the disease gene to 6p and implicating peripherin-rds as a candidate gene.

In the same late-onset Irish family, Jordan et al. (1992) found that an intragenic marker from the peripherin-RDS gene cosegregated with the disease locus with zero recombination (lod score 5.46). After mapping peripherin-RDS relative to other 6p markers using the CEPH reference panel, multipoint analysis produced a lod score of 8.21, maximizing at the peripherin-RDS locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Kajiwara et al. (1991) reported 3 mutations in the human homolog of the mouse rds gene (RDS) that cosegregated with autosomal dominant RP in separate families.

In the large Irish pedigree with 6p-linked adRP, Farrar et al. (1991) identified a 3-bp deletion in the peripherin-RDS gene resulting in loss of one of a pair of highly conserved cysteine residues in the predicted third transmembrane domain of peripherin. The deletion segregated with the disease and was not present in unaffected controls.

Wells et al. (1993) found a cysteine deletion at codon 118/119 of the RDS gene associated with RP in 1 family with autosomal dominant retinal dystrophy. Other RDS mutations in their study were associated with macular dystrophy (see Genotype/Phenotype Correlations).

In a family with RP, pattern dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus, Weleber et al. (1993) identified a novel 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene.

Kikawa et al. (1994) identified an asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene, detected by nonradioisotopic SSCP, in autosomal dominant RP associated with bull's-eye maculopathy.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with mutations in the unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes ROM1 and peripherin/RDS in which only double heterozygotes developed RP, indicating digenic inheritance.

By targeted next-generation sequencing of 163 retinal disease genes in 179 unrelated, prescreened patients with LCA or juvenile RP, Wang et al. (2013) identified homozygous PRPH2 missense mutations in 2 patients with LCA: C213R and L185P. Sequencing of PRPH2 in another 135 unsolved LCA or juvenile RP patients identified homozygosity for L185P in a third patient, who had juvenile RP. The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregation was examined; in 1 family the parents and son of the proband were heterozygous for C213R and an unaffected brother was homozygous wildtype, and in another family both parents of the proband were heterozygous for L185P. The authors noted that the L185P mutation, together with a null mutation in ROM1, had been reported to cause digenic RP.

Manes et al. (2015) screened PRPH2 by Sanger sequencing in 310 French families with adRP and identified 15 mutations, 6 novel and 9 previously reported, in 32 families. A new recurrent mutation, leu254-to-gln (L254Q), led to protein aggregation when peripherin-2 was produced in yeast, suggesting abnormal folding.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Wells et al. (1993) found that, whereas a cysteine deletion at codon 118/119 of RDS was associated with RP, 3 families with similar macular dystrophy had mutations at codon 172 (arg172 to trp in 2 and arg172 to gln in 1), and a stop codon at position 258 was found in a family with adult vitelliform macular dystrophy. They concluded that the functional significance of certain amino acids in peripherin-RDS may differ in cones and rods.

Wang et al. (2013) observed that patients homozygous for PRPH2 mutations had severe early-onset retinal dystrophy, whereas heterozygous family members had milder phenotypes, being asymptomatic but having detectable macular flecks; they suggested a dose-dependent effect.

### Population Genetics

Manes et al. (2015) found that PRPH2 mutations accounted for 10.3% of adRP in a French cohort of 310 families, higher than previously reported (0 to 8%), making PRPH2 the second most frequent adRP gene after RHO in their series.

### Animal Model

Kajiwara et al. (1991) noted that the murine retinal degeneration slow (rds) mutation is semidominant, with rod and cone photoreceptors that develop abnormally and then slowly degenerate, and that it is a possible model for retinitis pigmentosa.

**.0002 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**

**.0004 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7 (EARLY-ONSET, LCA PHENOTYPE; HOMOZYGOUS)**  
PRPH2, CYS213ARG [c.637T>C; p.C213R]

In a patient (1318) with Leber congenital amaurosis, Wang et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.637T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a cys213-to-arg (C213R) substitution, by targeted next-generation sequencing. Both parents and the patient's son were heterozygous, and an unaffected brother was homozygous wildtype. The heterozygous mother was asymptomatic at age 57 years but had a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy. The authors noted that C213R had been associated with autosomal dominant pattern dystrophy.

**.0005 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7 (DIGENIC WITH ROM1; EARLY-ONSET LCA/JUVENILE RP WHEN HOMOZYGOUS)**  
PRPH2, LEU185PRO [c.554T>C; p.L185P]

Wang et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.554T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a leu185-to-pro (L185P) substitution, in a patient (3256) with LCA and in a patient (741) with juvenile RP; both parents of patient 741 were heterozygous, and his 56-year-old heterozygous father had macular pattern dystrophy with essentially normal visual acuity. The authors noted that L185P, together with a null mutation in ROM1, had been reported to cause digenic RP.

**.0006 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**  
PRPH2, LEU254GLN [p.Leu254Gln]

In a study of 310 French families with adRP, Manes et al. (2015) identified a new recurrent leu254-to-gln (L254Q) mutation in PRPH2. Peripherin-2 carrying the mutation, produced in yeast, showed protein aggregation, suggesting abnormal folding. ⚠︎
