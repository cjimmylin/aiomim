### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PRPH2 gene (also known as peripherin/RDS) on chromosome 6p. Digenic RP7 is caused by double heterozygosity for mutations in the PRPH2 gene and the ROM1 gene. Homozygous mutation in PRPH2 has been found in patients with Leber congenital amaurosis or juvenile retinitis pigmentosa. ⚠︎

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is a form of retinitis pigmentosa, a hereditary retinal degeneration characterized by degeneration of the photoreceptors, that is caused by mutation in the gene encoding the photoreceptor-specific glycoprotein peripherin/RDS (Kajiwara et al., 1991; Farrar et al., 1991). The disorder is usually transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait, and onset may be late (Farrar et al., 1991; Jordan et al., 1992). A digenic form occurs in individuals who are double heterozygotes for mutations in PRPH2 and ROM1 (Kajiwara et al., 1994). PRPH2 mutations cause highly variable phenotypes, even within a single family, including retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus (Weleber et al., 1993; Manes et al., 2015). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

In reports localizing the adRP gene segregating in a large Irish pedigree to chromosome 6p, the locus was designated RP6 by Farrar et al. (1991) (in the title of their report, although the abstract refers to the gene as RP5) and by Jordan et al. (1992).

### Clinical Features

Farrar et al. (1991) and Jordan et al. (1992) studied a large Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP). ⚠︎

Weleber et al. (1993) reported 3 separate phenotypes within a single family carrying a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the PRPH2 gene. The mother presented at age 63 years with a profoundly abnormal electroretinogram (ERG) and adult-onset retinitis pigmentosa that progressed dramatically over 12 years, with marked loss of peripheral visual field. One daughter developed pattern macular dystrophy at age 31 years; at age 44 years her ERG was moderately abnormal but her clinical disease was limited to the macula. Another daughter presented at age 42 years with macular degeneration and over 10 years developed the clinical picture of fundus flavimaculatus; her peripheral visual field was preserved but her ERG was moderately abnormal. The son had onset of macular degeneration at age 44 years, with pericentral scotomas and a markedly abnormal ERG; fluorescein angiography revealed punctate pigment epithelial transmission defects.

Kikawa et al. (1994) reported autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa associated with bull's-eye maculopathy in patients with a mutation in the PRPH2 gene.

Wang et al. (2013) described 3 patients homozygous for PRPH2 mutations who had a clinical diagnosis of Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) (2 patients) or juvenile RP (1 patient). All had visual impairment within the first year of life, nondetectable or reduced ERGs, and a very similar form of maculopathy in the fundus; the 2 LCA patients had nystagmus. Fundus photography of one patient showed a prominent multilobulated central atrophic maculopathy surrounded by concentric rings of yellow deposits, with vessel narrowing and fine diffuse peripheral retinal changes; another showed pigment deposits peripherally and in the macular region, extensive disease with choroidal sclerosis, vessel narrowing, optic disc pallor, and a central extensive atrophic maculopathy, with unusual globular lesions in the foveal region on optical coherence tomography (OCT). The third patient had diffuse retinal dystrophy with retinal vessel narrowing, retinal pigment epithelium mottling and loss, and a multilobulated maculopathy, with fairly good visual acuity (20/40). In contrast, heterozygous family members were asymptomatic but showed detectable maculopathy: the 56-year-old father of one patient had macular pattern dystrophy with essentially normal visual acuity, and the 57-year-old mother of another patient had 20/20 visual acuity but a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy and retinal flecks.

Manes et al. (2015) studied the clinical features of adRP patients with PRPH2 mutations from French families. The clinical severity was moderate, with 78% of eyes having 1-0.5 visual acuity and 52% of eyes retaining more than 50% of the visual field. Some patients characteristically showed vitelliform deposits or macular involvement. In some families, pericentral RP or macular dystrophy was found in some family members while widespread RP was present in other members of the same family. Manes et al. (2015) concluded that PRPH2 mutations cause highly variable phenotypes and moderate forms of adRP, including mild cases that could be underdiagnosed.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of RP7 was demonstrated by cosegregation of PRPH2 mutations with adRP in families (Kajiwara et al., 1991; Farrar et al., 1991).

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with mutations in the unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes ROM1 and PRPH2 in which only double heterozygotes developed RP, demonstrating digenic inheritance.

Wang et al. (2013) found that homozygous mutations in PRPH2 caused LCA or juvenile RP, whereas heterozygous carriers in the families were asymptomatic but showed maculopathy.

### Mapping

In a large Irish pedigree with late-onset adRP, Farrar et al. (1991) obtained positive 2-point lod scores with 5 markers on chromosome 6p, including D6S105 (theta = 0.00, Z = 6.081), HLA-DRA (theta = 0.00, Z = 4.364), and RDS (theta = 0.00, Z = 5.376). Multipoint analysis yielded a lod score of 6.6, maximizing at HLA-DRA, localizing the adRP gene to 6p and implicating the human equivalent of the mouse retinal degeneration slow (rds) gene, peripherin-RDS, as a candidate. ⚠︎

In the same Irish family, Jordan et al. (1992) found that an intragenic marker from the peripherin-RDS gene cosegregated with the disease with zero recombination (lod score 5.46 at theta = 0.00). After mapping the peripherin-RDS gene relative to other 6p markers using the CEPH reference panel, multipoint analysis produced a lod score of 8.21, maximizing at the peripherin-RDS locus.

### Molecular Genetics

Kajiwara et al. (1991) identified 3 mutations in the human homolog of the rds gene (RDS, PRPH2) that cosegregated with autosomal dominant RP in separate families.

In the large Irish pedigree with adRP linked to 6p, Farrar et al. (1991) identified a 3-bp deletion in the PRPH2 gene resulting in loss of one of a pair of highly conserved cysteine residues in the predicted third transmembrane domain of peripherin. The deletion segregated with the disease phenotype and was not present in unaffected controls.

In a family with RP, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus, Weleber et al. (1993) identified a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the PRPH2 gene.

In families with autosomal dominant retinal dystrophies, Wells et al. (1993) found a cysteine deletion at codon 118/119 of the PRPH2 gene associated with RP in 1 family, whereas mutations at codon 172 and a stop codon at codon 258 were found in families with macular dystrophy, demonstrating that both RP and macular dystrophies are caused by PRPH2 mutations.

Kikawa et al. (1994) identified an asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the PRPH2 gene, detected by nonradioisotopic SSCP, causing autosomal dominant RP associated with bull's-eye maculopathy.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with mutations in both the ROM1 and PRPH2 genes, in which only double heterozygotes developed RP. Wang et al. (2013) noted that the PRPH2 leu185-to-pro (L185P) mutation, together with a null mutation in ROM1, had been reported to cause digenic RP.

By targeted next-generation sequencing of 163 retinal disease genes in 179 unrelated prescreened patients with LCA or juvenile RP, Wang et al. (2013) identified homozygous missense mutations in the PRPH2 gene in 2 patients with LCA: C213R (c.637T-C) and L185P (c.554T-C). Sequencing of PRPH2 in another 135 unsolved LCA or juvenile RP patients identified homozygosity for L185P in a third patient, with juvenile RP. The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and segregated with the disease in the families; heterozygous parents and offspring were asymptomatic but had maculopathy. ⚠︎

Manes et al. (2015) screened the PRPH2 gene by Sanger sequencing in 310 French families with adRP and identified 15 mutations, including 6 novel and 9 previously reported changes, in 32 families. A novel recurrent leu254-to-gln (L254Q) mutation led to protein aggregation when peripherin-2 was produced in yeast, suggesting abnormal folding.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Weleber et al. (1993) concluded that a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the PRPH2 gene can produce clinically disparate phenotypes even within the same family. Wells et al. (1993) suggested that the functional significance of certain amino acids in peripherin-RDS may differ in cones and rods.

Wang et al. (2013) observed that patients homozygous for PRPH2 mutations had severe, early-onset retinal disease with consistent maculopathy, whereas heterozygous family members had milder phenotypes, which they attributed to a likely dose-dependent effect.

### Population Genetics

Manes et al. (2015) found that PRPH2 mutations accounted for 10.3% of adRP in a cohort of 310 French families, higher than previously reported (0 to 8%), making PRPH2 the second most frequent adRP gene after RHO in their series.

### Animal Model

Kajiwara et al. (1991) noted that the murine retinal degeneration slow (rds) mutation is semidominant, with a phenotype in which rod and cone photoreceptors develop abnormally and then slowly degenerate, a possible model for RP.

Wang et al. (2013) noted that rds/rds mice homozygous for a null mutation fail to develop photoreceptor outer segments and show early-onset severe retinal degeneration, whereas rds/+ heterozygous mice display milder degeneration, and that rds/+ mice overexpressing the L185P peripherin mutant exhibited a mild phenotype, while rds/rds mice overexpressing L185P had dramatically reduced peripherin levels and a much more severe phenotype.

**.0004 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7, DIGENIC; LEBER CONGENITAL AMAUROSIS/JUVENILE RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA**  
PRPH2, LEU185PRO [c.554T>C; p.Leu185Pro]

Wang et al. (2013) noted that the leu185-to-pro (L185P) mutation in the PRPH2 gene, together with a null mutation in ROM1, had been reported to cause digenic RP.

Wang et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.554T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a leu185-to-pro (L185P) substitution, in a patient with LCA (patient 3256), whose parents' DNA was unavailable, and in a patient with juvenile RP (patient 741), whose parents were heterozygous. The patients had visual impairment within the first year of life, reduced or nondetectable ERGs, and maculopathy; the heterozygous 56-year-old father of patient 741 had macular pattern dystrophy with essentially normal visual acuity. The affected residue is conserved across species.

**.0005 LEBER CONGENITAL AMAUROSIS/JUVENILE RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA**  
PRPH2, CYS213ARG [c.637T>C; p.Cys213Arg]

In a patient with LCA (patient 1318), Wang et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.637T-C transition in the PRPH2 gene, resulting in a cys213-to-arg (C213R) substitution, a mutation previously associated with autosomal dominant pattern dystrophy. Both parents and the son of the patient were heterozygous, and an unaffected brother was homozygous wildtype. The heterozygous mother was asymptomatic at 57 years of age with 20/20 visual acuity but had a florid butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy, and the heterozygous 7-year-old son had a miniature form of foveal butterfly-shaped macular pattern dystrophy.

**.0006 RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA 7**  
PRPH2, LEU254GLN [p.Leu254Gln]

In French families with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa, Manes et al. (2015) identified a novel recurrent leu254-to-gln (L254Q) mutation in the PRPH2 gene. Peripherin-2 carrying the mutation, produced in yeast and analyzed by Western blot, showed protein aggregation, suggesting abnormal folding.
