### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that retinitis pigmentosa-7 (RP7) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the PRPH2 gene (179605) on chromosome 6p21.1. Digenic retinitis pigmentosa is caused by double heterozygosity for mutations in the PRPH2 gene and the ROM1 gene (180721). ⚠︎

### Description

Retinitis pigmentosa-7 is a form of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa caused by mutation in the gene encoding peripherin-RDS, a photoreceptor-specific glycoprotein. The phenotype is variable: the same mutation can cause retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, or fundus flavimaculatus within one family, and pericentral RP, macular dystrophy, and widespread RP have been seen in members of the same family. A digenic form occurs in individuals who are double heterozygotes for mutations in peripherin/RDS and ROM1.

### Nomenclature

Jordan et al. (1992) referred to the late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa locus on chromosome 6p as RP6.

### Clinical Features

Farrar et al. (1991) studied a large Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa. ⚠︎

Weleber et al. (1993) described a family in which a 75-year-old woman, her 2 daughters (aged 44 and 50 years), and her 49-year-old son carried the same peripherin/RDS deletion but had different phenotypes. The mother presented at age 63 years with a profoundly abnormal electroretinogram (ERG) and adult-onset retinitis pigmentosa that progressed dramatically over 12 years, with marked loss of peripheral visual field. One daughter developed pattern macular dystrophy at age 31 years; at age 44 years her ERG was moderately abnormal, but her clinical disease was limited to the macula. The other daughter presented at age 42 years with macular degeneration and over 10 years developed the clinical picture of fundus flavimaculatus; her peripheral visual field was preserved, but her ERG was moderately abnormal. The son had onset of macular degeneration at age 44 years, with pericentral scotomas, a markedly abnormal ERG, and punctate pigment epithelial transmission defects on fluorescein angiography. ⚠︎

Kikawa et al. (1994) reported autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa associated with bull's-eye maculopathy in patients with a mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene.

Manes et al. (2015) characterized French families with PRPH2-associated autosomal dominant RP. The disease was of moderate severity: 78% of eyes had visual acuity of 1-0.5, and 52% retained more than 50% of the visual field. Some patients characteristically showed vitelliform deposits or macular involvement. Phenotypes were highly variable; in some families, pericentral RP or macular dystrophy was found in some members while widespread RP was present in others. The authors suggested that mild cases could be underdiagnosed.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of RP7 is autosomal dominant, as shown by cosegregation of heterozygous RDS mutations with the disease in families reported by Kajiwara et al. (1991) and Farrar et al. (1991). ⚠︎

Kajiwara et al. (1994) found 3 families in which retinitis pigmentosa developed only in individuals who were double heterozygotes for mutations in the unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes ROM1 and peripherin/RDS, establishing digenic inheritance of RP.

### Mapping

Farrar et al. (1991) typed members of an Irish pedigree with late-onset autosomal dominant RP with a series of chromosome 6p markers and mapped the disease locus to 6p. Two-point lod scores were positive with several markers, including D6S105 (Z = 6.081 at theta = 0.00) and RDS (Z = 5.376 at theta = 0.00). Multipoint analysis gave a maximum lod score of 6.6 at HLA-DRA. The authors concluded that the data provided direct evidence for an additional autosomal dominant RP locus and strongly implicated peripherin-rds, the human equivalent of the mouse retinal degeneration slow (rds) gene, as a candidate gene.

In the large Irish family with late-onset autosomal dominant RP linked to 6p, Jordan et al. (1992) found that an intragenic marker from the peripherin-RDS gene cosegregated with the disease with zero recombination (lod score 5.46 at theta = 0.00). They mapped the peripherin-RDS gene against other 6p markers using the CEPH reference panel; in multipoint analysis, the lod score reached 8.21 and peaked at the peripherin-RDS locus.

### Heterogeneity

Farrar et al. (1991) noted that autosomal dominant RP is genetically heterogeneous, with loci mapped to chromosome 3q near the rhodopsin gene, to chromosome 8p, and to chromosome 6p.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) concluded that the allelic and nonallelic heterogeneity of monogenic RP is further complicated by interactions between unlinked mutations causing digenic RP.

### Molecular Genetics

Kajiwara et al. (1991) identified 3 mutations in the human homolog of the mouse rds gene (RDS; PRPH2), each cosegregating with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa in a separate family. They concluded that some cases of autosomal dominant RP are due to mutations at the RDS locus.

In a large Irish pedigree with autosomal dominant RP linked to chromosome 6p, Farrar et al. (1991) identified a heterozygous 3-bp deletion in the peripherin-RDS gene resulting in the loss of one of a pair of highly conserved cysteine residues in the predicted third transmembrane domain of peripherin. The deletion segregated with the disease phenotype and was not present in unaffected controls, suggesting that mutant peripherin gives rise to RP. ⚠︎

Jordan et al. (1992) concluded that their linkage results in the Irish family gave strong evidence for a role of peripherin-RDS, a photoreceptor-specific protein, in the cause of one form of autosomal dominant RP.

In a family with phenotypic variation including RP, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus, Weleber et al. (1993) found a 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene in the affected mother and her 3 affected children. ⚠︎

Wells et al. (1993) screened families with autosomal dominant retinal dystrophies for mutations in the RDS gene and found that a heterozygous cysteine deletion at codon 118/119 was associated with retinitis pigmentosa in one family. ⚠︎

Kikawa et al. (1994) identified a novel asn244-to-lys (N244K) mutation in the peripherin/RDS gene in autosomal dominant RP associated with bull's-eye maculopathy, using nonradioactive single-strand conformation polymorphism (SSCP) analysis.

Kajiwara et al. (1994) identified 3 families with mutations in the unlinked photoreceptor-specific genes ROM1 and peripherin/RDS in which only double heterozygotes developed RP.

Using targeted next-generation sequencing in patients with Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) or juvenile RP, Wang et al. (2013) identified homozygous PRPH2 missense mutations in 3 patients: cys213-to-arg (C213R) in 1 patient and leu185-to-pro (L185P), previously reported in digenic RP with a ROM1 null mutation, in 2 patients. The authors concluded that homozygous mutations in PRPH2 cause early-onset retinal dystrophy with LCA/juvenile RP phenotypes.

Manes et al. (2015) identified 15 PRPH2 mutations, including 6 novel and 9 previously reported changes, in 32 French families with autosomal dominant RP. A recurrent novel mutation, leu254-to-gln (L254Q), expressed in yeast led to protein aggregation, suggesting abnormal folding.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Weleber et al. (1993) concluded that the 3-bp deletion of codon 153 or 154 of the peripherin/RDS gene can produce clinically disparate phenotypes, namely retinitis pigmentosa, pattern macular dystrophy, and fundus flavimaculatus, even within one family.

Wells et al. (1993) found that RDS mutations could cause either RP or macular dystrophy. Three families with similar macular dystrophy had mutations at codon 172: arg172 to trp in 2 families and arg172 to gln in 1. A stop codon at codon 258 was found in a family with adult vitelliform macular dystrophy. The authors suggested that certain amino acids in peripherin-RDS may have different functional significance in cones and rods.

Wang et al. (2013) found that heterozygous carriers of PRPH2 mutations in the families of their homozygous patients had no symptoms but showed macular pattern dystrophy; for example, the 56-year-old father of a patient homozygous for L185P. The authors suggested a dose-dependent effect and proposed that heterozygous L185P carriers in digenic RP families may have a subclinical phenotype.

### Population Genetics

Manes et al. (2015) screened PRPH2 in 310 French families with autosomal dominant RP and identified mutations in 32 families, a prevalence of 10.3%, making PRPH2 the second most frequent autosomal dominant RP gene after RHO in their series.

### Animal Model

Kajiwara et al. (1991) noted that the mouse retinal degeneration slow (rds) mutation is semidominant; in rds mice, rod and cone photoreceptors develop abnormally and then slowly degenerate, making the mouse a possible model for retinitis pigmentosa.
