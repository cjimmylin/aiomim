### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the NEFL gene (162280) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F is a peripheral neuropathy caused by mutation in the NEFL gene. It often begins early and can be severe. Motor nerve conduction velocities are slowed, from mildly to severely. Nerve biopsy has shown axonal pathology with regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations (Jordanova et al., 2003; Abe et al., 2009). Both dominant and recessive inheritance have been reported (Abe et al., 2009; Yum et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) proposed the term 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) for the disorder in their patients, who were homozygous for a NEFL nonsense mutation. They noted that it resembled Dejerine-Sottas neuropathy but had faster conduction and distinct biopsy findings. Under the OMIM scheme for recessive axonal neuropathies, they suggested that these patients would be designated CMT2B5.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) found that patients with NEFL mutations had early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. On electrophysiologic examination, nerve conduction velocities were moderately to severely slowed. They also reported the first nerve biopsy from a CMT patient with a de novo missense mutation in NEFL. It showed axonal pathology with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) reported that all 6 Japanese patients with NEFL mutations had mildly to moderately slowed nerve conduction velocities. They suggested that the slowing might be caused by a loss of large-diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) described 4 of 5 children of consanguineous Palestinian parents, who were first cousins, with a severe, progressive neuropathy that began at about 1.5 years of age. All 4 had hypotonia in infancy and early childhood, mildly delayed motor milestones, and bilateral pes cavus. Distal atrophy and weakness of the feet and hands progressed slowly. They also had moderate glove-and-stocking sensory impairment and absent ankle jerks. The oldest sib, aged 23 years, used a walker and had proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait; the others walked with ankle-foot orthoses. CMT neuropathy scores ranged from 16 to 27. None had enlarged nerves, pathologic reflexes, cranial neuropathies, or clinically evident hearing loss. In electrophysiologic studies of these sibs, sensory responses were absent or nearly so. Median and ulnar motor conduction velocities were severely slowed (12 to 25 m/s), and amplitudes became progressively smaller with age. Peroneal and tibial responses eventually became unobtainable, and EMG showed severe distal denervation. Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, indicating subclinical CNS axonal involvement. Brain MRI was normal in the 3 children tested. Yum et al. (2009) also examined a sural nerve biopsy taken from the index patient at age 16 years. Myelinated axon density was markedly reduced (1,300/mm vs about 6,000/mm in controls). All myelinated axons were smaller than 3 microns and often irregular in shape. There were occasional regenerating clusters but no actively degenerating axons. The myelinated axons contained microtubules but no intermediate filaments. They were immunoreactive with an N-terminal NFL antiserum but not with a C-terminal one, consistent with the presence of the truncated protein. NFM, NFH, peripherin, and alpha-internexin were also detected.

### Inheritance

Both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance have been reported. Jordanova et al. (2003) identified NEFL mutations in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT, including a de novo missense mutation. Abe et al. (2009) found heterozygous NEFL missense mutations in 5 patients and a homozygous nonsense mutation in 1 patient. Yum et al. (2009) described autosomal recessive inheritance in a consanguineous Palestinian family: affected sibs were homozygous for a NEFL nonsense mutation, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies for mutations in the NEFL gene. They identified 6 disease-associated missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. The authors suggested that NEFL mutations should be considered in the molecular evaluation of patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT.

Abe et al. (2009) screened the NEFL gene in 223 Japanese patients with CMT. All were negative for mutations in other known CMT genes: PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX in the demyelinating form, and MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS in the axonal form. In 5 unrelated patients, they found 4 heterozygous NEFL missense mutations: P8L, E90K, N98S, and E396K. In 1 further patient, they identified a homozygous nonsense mutation (E140X), which they described as the first report of a homozygous nonsense NEFL mutation.

In 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family, Yum et al. (2009) found a homozygous c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, predicting a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous carriers, and the unaffected sib did not carry the mutation. The mutation was absent from more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at Athena Diagnostics. Sequencing of PMP22, GJB1, MPZ, EGR2, PRX, LMNA, GDAP1, and MFN2 found no other mutations. The authors stated that this was the first reported recessive NEFL mutation. In SW-13 vim- cells, the E210X mutant protein did not form filaments, and the authors concluded that it causes a simple loss of function. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Abe et al. (2009) concluded that nonsense NEFL mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype, whereas missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) expressed E210X mutant NFL in SW-13 vim- cells. The mutant protein did not form filaments, appeared diffuse and mostly nuclear, and accumulated poorly, as a band of about 30 kD. Unlike the dominant Q333P mutant, it did not interfere with filament formation by coexpressed wildtype NFL or with the vimentin network. The authors concluded that E210X causes a simple loss of function.

Yum et al. (2009) noted that conduction velocities in their patients, about 20 m/s, fell in the demyelinating range. However, NEFL is not prominently expressed by myelinating Schwann cells. They therefore concluded that the slowed conduction is likely explained by the absence of large myelinated axons, which lack neurofilaments and fail to reach normal caliber. They suggested that neurofilaments are required to maintain myelinated PNS axons.
