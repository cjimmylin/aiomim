### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1F (CMT1F) is caused by heterozygous or homozygous mutation in the gene encoding the neurofilament light chain polypeptide (NEFL) on chromosome 8p21. ⚠︎

### Description

CMT1F is a hereditary peripheral neuropathy caused by mutation in the NEFL gene, which encodes the neurofilament light chain. It is characterized by early onset, often with a severe phenotype, and by moderately to severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. Jordanova et al. (2003) found that patients with NEFL missense mutations or an in-frame deletion had early onset and often severe disease. Both dominantly inherited forms, caused by missense mutations, and recessively inherited forms, caused by homozygous nonsense mutations, have been reported (Abe et al., 2009; Yum et al., 2009). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Yum et al. (2009) noted that, by the conventional 38 m/s criterion, the neuropathy in their family with a homozygous NEFL mutation would be classified as demyelinating. However, they considered the absence of large myelinated axons the likely cause of the slowed conduction. They proposed the term 'severe, early-onset axonal neuropathy' (SEOAN) for the disorder. They also suggested that, under the OMIM convention, their patients should be designated CMT2B5.

### Clinical Features

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies and identified patients with NEFL mutations. These patients had early onset and often a severe clinical phenotype. Electrophysiologic examination showed moderately to severely slowed nerve conduction velocities. A nerve biopsy from a patient with a de novo NEFL missense mutation showed axonal pathology with axonal regeneration clusters and onion bulb formations.

Abe et al. (2009) reported 6 Japanese CMT patients with NEFL mutations. All had mildly to moderately delayed nerve conduction velocities, which the authors suggested were possibly caused by loss of large-diameter fibers.

Yum et al. (2009) reported a 2-generation Palestinian family in which 4 of 5 children of consanguineous parents (first cousins) had CMT. The index patient presented at age 2 years with toe walking, and onset in all affected children was at about 1.5 years. All 4 affected individuals had hypotonia in infancy and early childhood, mildly delayed motor milestones, slowly progressive distal atrophy and weakness of the feet and hands, and bilateral pes cavus. At the time of the report, one patient used a walker and the others were ambulatory with molded ankle-foot orthoses. One patient had moderate proximal leg weakness and a waddling gait, and another had mild to moderate proximal arm and shoulder girdle weakness. All had moderate sensory impairment in a glove-and-stocking distribution and absent ankle jerks. CMT neuropathy scores ranged from 16 to 27. None had pathologic reflexes, palpably enlarged nerves, ulcerated feet, cranial neuropathies, or clinically evident hearing loss. One patient had learning difficulties, and the 3 patients who had brain MRI had normal scans.

In the affected children, sensory responses were absent except for 2 responses with low amplitudes and slowed conduction velocities (Yum et al., 2009). Median and ulnar motor conduction velocities were severely slowed, ranging from 12 to 25 m/s, and amplitudes decreased progressively with age. Peroneal and tibial responses eventually became unobtainable, and EMG confirmed severe distal denervation. Visual evoked responses were prolonged in 3 of the 4 affected children, indicating subclinical involvement of central nervous system axons.

Yum et al. (2009) examined a sural nerve biopsy taken from 1 patient at age 16 years. It showed a myelinated axon density of 1,300/mm, compared with about 6,000/mm in controls. All myelinated axons were less than 3 microns in diameter and were often irregular in shape. There were occasional clusters of regenerated axons but no evidence of actively degenerating axons. Myelinated axons contained microtubules but no intermediate filaments. Immunostaining with an antiserum against the N terminus of NFL labeled axons, whereas an antiserum against the C terminus did not.

### Inheritance

Both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive inheritance have been reported. Jordanova et al. (2003) found NEFL mutations in patients with sporadic or dominantly inherited CMT, including a de novo missense mutation. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that NEFL missense mutations cause a dominant phenotype, whereas nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype. In the family reported by Yum et al. (2009), CMT segregated as an autosomal recessive trait. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Jordanova et al. (2003) screened the NEFL gene in 323 patients with CMT or related peripheral neuropathies. They detected 6 disease-associated missense mutations and one 3-bp in-frame deletion, clustered in functionally defined domains of the NEFL protein. One of the missense mutations had occurred de novo.

Abe et al. (2009) analyzed the NEFL gene in 223 Japanese CMT patients. Those with the demyelinating form had tested negative for mutations in PMP22, MPZ, GJB1, LITAF, EGR2, GDAP1, MTMR2, and PRX. Those with the axonal form had tested negative for mutations in MFN2, MPZ, GJB1, HSP27, HSP22, and GARS. They identified 4 heterozygous missense mutations in 5 unrelated patients: pro8-to-leu (P8L), glu90-to-lys (E90K), asn98-to-ser (N98S), and glu396-to-lys (E396K). In 1 other patient, they found a homozygous nonsense mutation, glu140-to-ter (E140X; -style numbering not given), which was the first reported homozygous nonsense mutation of NEFL.

In 4 affected sibs from a consanguineous Palestinian family with CMT, Yum et al. (2009) identified a homozygous c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene by sequencing, predicted to result in a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous for the mutation, and the unaffected sib did not carry it. Sequencing of PMP22, GJB1, MPZ, EGR2, PRX, LMNA, GDAP1, and MFN2 in 1 or 2 patients showed no additional mutations, and PMP22 was neither duplicated nor deleted. The mutation was not found in more than 11,000 individuals analyzed at a diagnostic laboratory. In SW-13 vim- cells, the E210X mutant did not form an intermediate filament network, and it did not interfere with filament formation by wildtype NFL. In SW-13 vim+ cells, which express vimentin, it did not interfere with the endogenous vimentin network. By contrast, the dominant Q333P mutant formed aggregates. The authors concluded that E210X causes a simple loss of function.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Jordanova et al. (2003) concluded that the clinical variation in CMT depends on the gene mutated and on the specific type of mutation. Abe et al. (2009) concluded that NEFL nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype, in contrast to missense mutations, which cause a dominant phenotype.

### Pathogenesis

Yum et al. (2009) concluded that the absence of large myelinated axons in their patients with homozygous E210X was the likely explanation for the slowed nerve conduction. They noted that NEFL is not prominently expressed by myelinating Schwann cells. They suggested that their findings showed neurofilaments to be the main determinant of axonal caliber and conduction velocity. They also concluded that neurofilaments are required for maintenance of myelinated peripheral nervous system axons.

### Animal Model

Yum et al. (2009) noted that Nefl-null mice show no demonstrable axonal loss and that quiver quail, which are homozygous for a Q114X nonsense mutation in Nefl, show minimal pathology. This contrasted with the progressive axonal loss in their patients. The authors suggested the discrepancy may relate to the much greater length of human nerves and/or the longer human lifespan.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CHARCOT-MARIE-TOOTH DISEASE, TYPE 1F**  
NEFL, GLU140TER [p.Glu140Ter; Glu140Stop]

Abe et al. (2009) found a homozygous glu140-to-ter (E140X) nonsense mutation in the NEFL gene in 1 Japanese patient with CMT, the first reported homozygous nonsense mutation of NEFL. The patient was identified by NEFL screening of 223 Japanese CMT patients. The authors concluded that NEFL nonsense mutations probably cause a recessive phenotype.

**.0002 CHARCOT-MARIE-TOOTH DISEASE, TYPE 1F**  
NEFL, GLU210TER [c.628G>T; p.Glu210Ter; E210X]

In 4 affected children of consanguineous Palestinian parents with severe early-onset CMT and slowed nerve conduction, Yum et al. (2009) identified a homozygous c.628G-T transversion in the NEFL gene, resulting in a glu210-to-ter (E210X) substitution. Both parents were heterozygous, and the unaffected sib did not carry the mutation. The mutation was not found in more than 11,000 individuals tested at a diagnostic laboratory. In transfected SW-13 vim- cells, the mutant protein did not form filaments and did not interfere with filament assembly by wildtype NFL, consistent with a simple loss of function. A sural nerve biopsy from an affected patient showed reduced numbers of small myelinated axons that lacked intermediate filaments.
