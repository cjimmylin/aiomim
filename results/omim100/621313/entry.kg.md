### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that brain small vessel disease-4 (BSVD4) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the NIT1 gene (604618) on chromosome 1q23.3.

### Description

Rutten et al. (2024) described NIT1-small vessel disease as a new autosomal recessive cerebral small vessel disease caused by loss-of-function variants in NIT1. It is characterized by a triad of movement disorders, massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia, and intracerebral hemorrhage.

### Clinical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) described 3 brothers with bilateral small cystic lesions of the basal ganglia (a deep cavitating state) and positive urine sulfite dipstick tests. The index patient (II.4) had nonsevere mental retardation and an acute psychotic episode followed by generalized seizures. At age 34 years he developed a sudden pure motor left hemiparesis. Arteriography, EEG, EMG, Doppler ultrasound, and echocardiography were normal. Over 20 years he progressed to severe neuropsychological deterioration and spastic tetraplegia, and he died at age 57.

A brother of the index patient reported by Rendu et al. (2019) (II.5) had diabetes mellitus and had led an active professional life. At age 58 years he had a sudden coma with cardiovascular collapse. CT and 3T MRI showed the same basal ganglia cystic lesions, and his urine sulfite test was positive. He recovered but had neuropsychological deterioration (BREF 14/18, MMSE 22/30). A third brother (II.7), diagnosed with schizophrenia 40 years earlier, had a normal clinical examination but the same brain lesions and a positive urine sulfite test.

Rutten et al. (2024) reported 7 patients with NIT1-small vessel disease from 5 unrelated pedigrees, comprising 3 sibs and 4 single cases. The patients presented in mid-adulthood with movement disorders. All had striking abnormalities on brain MRI, with numerous and massively dilated perivascular spaces in the basal ganglia. Three patients had a non-lobar intracerebral hemorrhage between ages 45 and 60 years, which was fatal in 2 of them. Brain autopsy showed large electron-dense deposits in the walls of small and medium-sized cerebral arteries.

### Biochemical Features

Rendu et al. (2019) found that the 3 affected brothers had positive urine sulfite dipstick tests, whereas the mother and the asymptomatic sibs who were tested did not. GC/MS organic acid analysis detected a urinary compound with a mass spectrum identical to deaminated glutathione (dGSH), matching the compound excreted by Nit1-knockout mice. This compound was present in the 3 affected brothers but undetectable in the other sibs tested and the mother. The authors proposed that dGSH accumulation was probably responsible for the positive sulfite test.

Rutten et al. (2024) found by Western blotting that NIT1 protein was absent from patient fibroblasts, and metabolic analysis of patient urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzyme function.

### Inheritance

Rendu et al. (2019) concluded that this is a novel autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by NIT1 mutations, combining neurological signs, basal ganglia lesions, and a positive urine sulfite test. The authors excluded sulfite oxidase deficiency and molybdenum cofactor deficiency. Rutten et al. (2024) also described NIT1-small vessel disease as autosomal recessive.

### Molecular Genetics

In 2 affected brothers (II.5 and II.7) from the family with a deep cavitating state reported by Rendu et al. (2019), exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: a c.457G-A transition (gly153-to-arg; G153R) at a highly conserved residue on the last nucleotide of exon 4, and a 1-bp duplication (c.670dupA) causing a frameshift (Thr224AsnfsTer41) predicted to cause loss of function. Sequencing of the NIT1 transcript from a skin biopsy of patient II.5 showed that the c.457G-A variant affected splicing. Cloning showed that the 2 variants were on different alleles. The duplication was inherited from the asymptomatic mother; the father's DNA was not available. Asymptomatic sibs whose DNA was available carried neither variant. NIT1 protein was undetectable on Western blot of the patient's fibroblasts. ⚠︎

By exome sequencing in 3 sibs with NIT1-small vessel disease, Rutten et al. (2024) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the NIT1 gene: c.727C-T (arg243-to-trp; R243W) and c.198_199del (ala68-to-ter; A68X). Four additional patients, each a single case from 1 of 4 unrelated pedigrees, were homozygous for the R243W mutation. NIT1 protein was absent from patient fibroblasts on Western blotting, and metabolic analysis of patient urine confirmed loss of NIT1 enzyme function.

### Pathogenesis

In fibroblasts from patient II.5, Rendu et al. (2019) found unchanged cytoplasmic Cu/Zn SOD activity but a 2-fold increase in mitochondrial MnSOD activity. They suggested that dGSH accumulation causes oxidative stress and redox damage, and noted that other effects of NIT1 deficiency, such as altered Wnt/beta-catenin signaling, could contribute.

### Diagnosis

Rendu et al. (2019) suggested that the urine sulfite test could easily be used to screen cohorts of psychiatric patients for the disorder.
