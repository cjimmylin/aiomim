### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is caused by homozygous mutation in the KIRREL1 gene on chromosome 1.

### Description

Nephrotic syndrome type 23 (NPHS23) is an autosomal recessive form of steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) without extrarenal manifestations. Solanki et al. (2019) described onset in childhood or adolescence, resistance to steroids at initial presentation, and variable response to other treatment and renal outcome. Renal biopsy findings included minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and podocyte foot process effacement.

### Clinical Features

Solanki et al. (2019) reported 2 patients with SRNS from 2 unrelated consanguineous families (A666 and B742). The patient from family B742 was a child of Italian descent. Neither patient had extrarenal manifestations or syndromic features. Patient A666_21, a boy, was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at 5 years of age. He was steroid-resistant at initial presentation and did not respond to various immunosuppressive drugs. He never achieved complete remission and continued to have undulating proteinuria with a partial response to treatment. ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers did not help. His renal function stayed normal, and he stopped attending clinical visits after 12.5 years of follow-up. A younger sister was reported to have periorbital edema and proteinuria. Extended relatives included 3 children with end-stage renal disease and 1 child with nephrotic syndrome, but their genotypes could not be determined. Renal biopsy in patient A666_21 showed only minor mesangial hypercellularity, consistent with minimal change disease.

Patient B742_21, also a boy, was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome at 14 years of age and was steroid-resistant at initial presentation (Solanki et al., 2019). He was treated with tacrolimus while corticosteroids were weaned. He also received a calcium channel blocker and an ACE inhibitor for hypertension and proteinuria, and he achieved complete remission. Over 4 years of follow-up, however, he developed chronic kidney disease, which had progressed to stage 2 at the time of the report. Light microscopy of his renal biopsy showed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and electron microscopy showed podocyte foot process effacement. Across both patients, Solanki et al. (2019) described low-grade mesangial hypercellularity on histology. Ultrastructural findings were extensive foot process effacement, increased mesangial matrix, and mesangial proliferation.

### Inheritance

Solanki et al. (2019) found homozygous KIRREL1 mutations in affected individuals from 2 unrelated consanguineous families, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Molecular Genetics

Solanki et al. (2019) performed whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping in 596 individuals from 406 families with SRNS. In 2 patients from unrelated consanguineous families with SRNS, they identified homozygous missense mutations in the KIRREL1 gene: R440C in patient A666_21 and S573L in patient B742_21. Both mutations lay within the maximum homozygosity peak on chromosome 1, and both altered residues were conserved to D. rerio. In gnomAD, R440C had a minor allele frequency of 3.97 x 10(-6) and S573L had a minor allele frequency of 7.87 x 10(-4). S573L had an allele frequency of 0.0011 in European populations and was found in the homozygous state in 1 gnomAD control subject. The authors therefore raised the possibility of variable expressivity or incomplete penetrance for this variant. KIRREL1 mutations accounted for 2 of the 406 families (0.4%).

In functional studies, Solanki et al. (2019) expressed both Flag-tagged mutant proteins stably in cultured human podocytes. Unlike wildtype KIRREL1, the mutant proteins were mislocalized, and less of them was found in the membrane fraction. Both mutants accumulated in the Golgi apparatus, early endosomes, and lysosomes, indicating a trafficking defect. The mutations did not affect membrane localization of NPHS1. Podocyte monolayers expressing the mutants had greater transepithelial permeability to albumin than those expressing wildtype protein.

### Animal Model

Liu et al. (2003) noted that Neph1 (Kirrel)-deficient mice develop nephrotic syndrome at birth. Using immunogold electron microscopy in rodents, they localized Neph1 to the glomerular slit diaphragm. They found that Neph1 forms dimers and multimers and binds nephrin through its extracellular segment and ZO-1 through its cytoplasmic tail. In vivo, they disrupted the Neph1-nephrin interaction by injecting combinations of subnephritogenic doses of anti-Neph1 and anti-nephrin antibodies. This caused complement- and leukocyte-independent proteinuria with preserved foot processes, along with a dramatic reduction in ZO-1 protein expression.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NEPHROTIC SYNDROME, TYPE 23**  
KIRREL1, ARG440CYS [p.Arg440Cys]

Solanki et al. (2019) used whole-exome sequencing and homozygosity mapping to identify a homozygous arg440-to-cys (R440C) mutation in the KIRREL1 gene in patient A666_21, who was from a consanguineous family. He had steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (NPHS23) diagnosed at 5 years of age, and his biopsy findings were consistent with minimal change disease. The mutation lay within the maximum homozygosity peak on chromosome 1 and affected a residue conserved to D. rerio. Its minor allele frequency in gnomAD was 3.97 x 10(-6). In cultured human podocytes, the mutant protein was mislocalized and was retained in intracellular compartments.

**.0002 NEPHROTIC SYNDROME, TYPE 23**  
KIRREL1, SER573LEU [p.Ser573Leu]

Solanki et al. (2019) identified a homozygous ser573-to-leu (S573L) mutation in the KIRREL1 gene in patient B742_21, a child of Italian descent from a consanguineous family. He had steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (NPHS23) diagnosed at 14 years of age, and his biopsy showed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. He achieved complete remission with tacrolimus, a calcium channel blocker, and an ACE inhibitor, but later progressed to stage 2 chronic kidney disease. The mutation was found by whole-exome sequencing within a homozygosity peak on chromosome 1. In gnomAD it had a minor allele frequency of 7.87 x 10(-4) overall and 0.0011 in European populations, and it was present in the homozygous state in 1 control subject. In cultured human podocytes, the mutant protein was mislocalized and increased permeability to albumin.
