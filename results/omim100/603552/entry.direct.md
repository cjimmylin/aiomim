### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-4 (FHL4) is caused by homozygous mutation in the gene encoding syntaxin-11 (STX11) on chromosome 6q24.

### Description

Familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (FHL) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of immune dysregulation. It is characterized by hyperactive phagocytes and defects in natural killer (NK) cell function (zur Stadt et al., 2005; Rudd et al., 2006). FHL usually affects young children and presents as a severe hyperinflammatory syndrome with activated macrophages and T lymphocytes (Zur Stadt et al., 2006). FHL4 is caused by mutation in the STX11 gene, which encodes a t-SNARE involved in intracellular trafficking (Zur Stadt et al., 2006). STX11 mutations account for only a small fraction of FHL patients (Muller et al., 2014).

### Clinical Features

zur Stadt et al. (2005) studied a large consanguineous Kurdish kindred in which 5 children were affected with FHL. They also studied 5 further consanguineous Turkish/Kurdish FHL kindreds.

Rudd et al. (2006) investigated the clinical features of 6 children with STX11 mutations from 4 unrelated families. Three patients had long periods (1 year or more) in remission without specific treatment, which is very uncommon in this disease. Although the phenotype was milder, some children with STX11 mutations developed severe psychomotor retardation. Two of the 6 patients developed myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or acute myelogenous leukemia (AML). Rudd et al. (2006) suggested that STX11 mutations may be associated with secondary malignancies (MDS/AML). ⚠︎

Muller et al. (2014) reported 3 children with HLH from unrelated consanguineous Pakistani families. Patient A was a boy diagnosed at 2 months of age. Patient B was a boy diagnosed at 5 years of age, and his family had a history of the disease. Patient C was a girl diagnosed at 48 months of age. Patients A and B both had fever, splenomegaly, and hepatomegaly. They also had anemia (hemoglobin 55 and 71 g/L), neutropenia (0.3 and 0.4 x 10(9)/L), thrombocytopenia (platelets 13 and 8 x 10(9)/L), hypertriglyceridemia (5.1 and 5.5 mmol/L), hypofibrinogenemia (0.28 and 0.16 g/L), and hyperferritinemia (8,636 and 1,929 microg/L). Hemophagocytosis was not found in either patient, and neither had neurologic manifestations. Both patients were treated with dexamethasone, cyclosporine A, and etoposide and were in remission at 2 months. Patient A underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation at 15 months of age. Both patients A and B died. Laboratory data from initial presentation were not available for patient C, who was lost to follow-up. All 3 patients had deficient NK cell activity, with defective lysis of K562 target cells, and deficient NK cell degranulation. Stimulation with IL-2 partially restored cytotoxicity and degranulation. Expression of perforin, granzyme B, and CD107a in patient NK cells was normal, which suggested that granule integrity was not impaired.

### Inheritance

FHL is an autosomal recessive disorder (zur Stadt et al., 2005). zur Stadt et al. (2005) found homozygous STX11 mutations in affected members of consanguineous kindreds. In the 3 consanguineous Pakistani families reported by Muller et al. (2014), the patients were homozygous for an STX11 mutation and the parents were heterozygous for it, with no recorded history of inflammatory disease.

### Mapping

zur Stadt et al. (2005) performed genome-wide homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous Kurdish kindred with 5 children affected with FHL. They found linkage to a 10-cM region on chromosome 6q24 between D6S1569 and D6S960, which defined a novel FHL locus.

### Heterogeneity

FHL is genetically heterogeneous. Mutations in the perforin-1 gene (PRF1) cause FHL2, and mutations in UNC13D cause FHL3 (zur Stadt et al., 2005). Muller et al. (2014) noted that FHL is associated with autosomal recessive mutations in PRF1, UNC13D, STX11, and STXBP2.

### Molecular Genetics

zur Stadt et al. (2005) screened positional candidate genes in a large consanguineous Kurdish kindred with FHL linked to chromosome 6q24 and identified a homozygous 5-bp deletion in the STX11 gene. Syntaxin-11 protein was absent from the mononuclear cell fraction of patients homozygous for the deletion. The authors also found homozygous STX11 mutations in 5 further consanguineous Turkish/Kurdish FHL kindreds. Two of these families had the 5-bp deletion, and 1 family had a 19.2-kb genomic deletion spanning the entire coding region of STX11 (exon 2). The remaining 2 families had a nonsense mutation predicted to cause a premature stop codon near the C-terminal end of the protein.

Zur Stadt et al. (2006) analyzed STX11, PRF1, and UNC13D in 63 unrelated FHL patients of different geographic origins (32 from Turkey, 23 from Germany, and 8 from elsewhere). They identified STX11 mutations in 6 patients, all of whom were from Turkey. None of the patients from Germany or from other origins had STX11 mutations.

Rudd et al. (2006) used direct sequencing to analyze STX11 in 28 FHL families that did not have PRF1 mutations. They identified 2 STX11 mutations, a nonsense mutation and a deletion, in 6 of 34 children from 4 of the 28 unrelated families. Both mutations had been reported previously.

Muller et al. (2014) sequenced the coding regions and splice sites of UNC13D, STX11, and STXBP2 in 3 children with HLH from unrelated consanguineous Pakistani families. All 3 patients were homozygous for a novel missense mutation in STX11, c.173T-C, which results in a leu58-to-pro (L58P) substitution in the predicted Habc domain. The parents were heterozygous for the mutation. Patient A also carried UNC13D variants: he was heterozygous for c.811C-T (P271S) and homozygous for c.2782C-T (R928C). Muller et al. (2014) suggested that these variants may have contributed to his earlier presentation and to the smaller recovery of NK cell degranulation after IL-2 stimulation. Syntaxin-11 expression was greatly reduced in PBMCs and in lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) cells from patient C, whereas Munc18-2 expression was normal. When expressed in HEK293T cells, syntaxin-11 L58P was present at levels comparable to wildtype but did not bind Munc18-2. An engineered N-terminal R4A mutant also failed to bind Munc18-2. By contrast, the C-terminal Q268X mutation, previously associated with FHL4, did not impair Munc18-2 binding. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

zur Stadt et al. (2005) noted that both STX11 and UNC13D are involved in vesicle trafficking and membrane fusion. They concluded that, in addition to mutations in perforin-1, defects in the endocytotic or exocytotic pathway may be a common mechanism in FHL.

Muller et al. (2014) found that the N-peptide and the Habc domain of syntaxin-11 are both required for binding to Munc18-2. They proposed that loss of the syntaxin-11/Munc18-2 interaction in patients homozygous for L58P may destabilize syntaxin-11, which would explain the low expression of the mutant protein in patient cells.

### Clinical Management

Locatelli et al. (2020) conducted an open-label, single-group, phase 2-3 study of emapalumab, a human anti-interferon-gamma antibody, given with dexamethasone. The study enrolled patients aged 18 years or younger with primary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Of the 34 patients treated, 27 had received conventional therapy before enrollment and 7 had not. A response occurred in 63% of the previously treated patients and in 65% of all patients who received emapalumab. Seventy percent of the previously treated patients were able to proceed to transplantation. Emapalumab was not associated with any organ toxicity. Severe infections developed in 10 patients during treatment.

### Population Genetics

Zur Stadt et al. (2006) found STX11 mutations in 6 of 32 FHL patients from Turkey but in none of 23 patients from Germany or 8 patients from other origins. Rudd et al. (2006) found STX11 mutations in 14% (4 of 28) of PRF1-negative FHL families in their cohort.
