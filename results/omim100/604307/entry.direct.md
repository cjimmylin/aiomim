### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that multiple types of cataract (CTRCT2), with or without microcornea, are caused by heterozygous mutation in the gamma-C-crystallin gene (CRYGC) on chromosome 2q33-q35. ⚠︎

### Description

CTRCT2 is an autosomal dominant congenital cataract that maps to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster on chromosome 2q33-q35. The lens opacities vary in type. Reported forms include Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus, zonular pulverulent cataract, and nuclear cataract. In some families, the cataract occurs together with microcornea, nystagmus, and amblyopia.

### Clinical Features

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family with a hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus that occurred in heterozygotes.

Scott et al. (1994) described a 7-generation family in which 48 of 138 individuals were known to have autosomal dominant congenital cataracts of the pulverulent type. Fifty-three family members were examined, and 28 of them had congenital cataracts. Of these 28 patients, 19 had unilateral cataract (8 right eyes and 11 left eyes) and 9 had bilateral cataract. In patients with unilateral cataract, the clinically unaffected eye showed no lenticular opacity on detailed slit-lamp examination. Severity ranged from a subtle unilateral zonular cataract with 20/20 visual acuity, and bilateral inner fetal nuclear pulverulent opacities with 20/16 visual acuity in both eyes, to dense unilateral and bilateral nuclear cataracts that needed early surgical removal. When historical data on unexamined members were included, there were 48 affected members: 28 unilateral, 17 bilateral, and 3 obligate carriers who were not examined. The authors stated that, to their knowledge, this was the first family described with apparently random expression of an autosomal dominant cataract gene as either unilateral or bilateral cataract. ⚠︎

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) described a family with several members affected by primary nuclear congenital cataract.

Zhang et al. (2009) studied a 4-generation Chinese family from a relatively isolated region of northern China. Six members had congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, with no other ocular or systemic abnormalities. The affected individuals had bilateral central nuclear opacities involving the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nucleus of the lens. They also had congenitally small eyeballs and corneas with a diameter of approximately 9 to 10 mm. All affected individuals had the same poor visual acuity of hand movements in front of the eye, and all had nystagmus and amblyopia.

Li et al. (2012) studied a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract.

Guo et al. (2012) reported a 4-generation Chinese family in which 6 members had isolated bilateral nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The nuclear opacities involved the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nuclei, and the corneal diameter was approximately 9 mm in all affected individuals. The proband was diagnosed at 8 years of age and underwent iridectomy on both eyes at that age. At age 38 he had phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation in the left eye. His best corrected visual acuity was 0.08 in the right eye and counting fingers at 30 cm in the left eye. Two other affected individuals were first diagnosed at 6 months and 1 year of age. Intraocular pressure was normal. All patients had nystagmus and amblyopia, and there was no history of related systemic abnormalities.

### Inheritance

Several families have shown autosomal dominant transmission of CTRCT2 (Lubsen et al., 1987; Scott et al., 1994; Ren et al., 2000; Gonzalez-Huerta et al., 2007). In the family reported by Zhang et al. (2009), affected individuals appeared in each of 4 generations and there was male-to-male transmission. Penetrance appeared to be virtually complete in that family.

### Mapping

In a family with Coppock-like cataract, Lubsen et al. (1987) found that a gamma-crystallin cluster haplotype defined by TaqI RFLPs (haplotype P) was present only in affected members, and that all affected members carried it. This established close linkage between the cataract locus and the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, with a maximum lod score of 7.58 at theta = 0.

In the 7-generation family with variable zonular pulverulent cataract originally described by Scott et al. (1994), Ren et al. (2000) localized the disorder to a 19-cM interval on chromosome 2q33-q35 that includes the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. Maximum lod scores were 4.56 (theta = 0.02) with D2S157, 3.66 (theta = 0.12) with D2S72, and 3.57 (theta = 0.052) with CRYG. ⚠︎

In a Chinese family with nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) obtained a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.29 at marker D2S325 (theta = 0.0). Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 19.04-cM region on chromosome 2q32.3-q35, bounded by D2S117 and D2S2382.

### Molecular Genetics

In the original Coppock-like cataract family reported by Lubsen et al. (1987), Héon et al. (1999) identified a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The mutation was not found in a large control population. Zhang et al. (2009) and Guo et al. (2012) listed this Coppock-like cataract mutation as thr5-to-pro (T5P; c.13A-C). ⚠︎

In the family with variable zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. (2000) found no abnormalities in the CRYGD gene. They identified a 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of the CRYGC gene in 1 allele of each affected family member. The duplication was absent from unaffected family members and from unaffected controls. It disrupts the reading frame and is predicted to produce an unstable gamma-C-crystallin containing 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids.

Santhiya et al. (2002) reported novel mutations in the gamma-crystallin genes that cause autosomal dominant congenital cataracts.

In a family with nuclear congenital cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) sequenced the CRYGA-D gene cluster and identified a heterozygous missense mutation in the CRYGC gene (R168W). The mutation segregated with the disease and was absent from healthy family members and 170 unrelated controls. The authors noted that this mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract. It was also listed as a SNP in the NCBI database, but the authors concluded that it was disease-causing.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene (W157X; c.470G-A). They found it by linkage analysis followed by sequencing of the CRYGA-CRYGD gene cluster. The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was not found in 100 normal controls.

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) used candidate gene sequencing to identify a heterozygous missense mutation in the CRYGC gene (G129C). In zebrafish, the mutant protein caused vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens, reproducing the cataract phenotype. Molecular modeling and spectroscopic studies indicated that the mutation impaired the tertiary structure of the protein by modifying the H-bonding network in the C-terminal domain. The mutation also markedly reduced thermal stability and increased the tendency to aggregate under stress from storage at high concentration, heat, and UV irradiation.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) screened 9 candidate genes and identified a heterozygous c.471G-A transition in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene. This change also results in W157X but differs at the nucleotide level from the c.470G-A change reported by Zhang et al. (2009). The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was not found in 100 controls. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) noted that the R168W mutation in CRYGC had previously been associated with lamellar cataract, whereas their family had nuclear cataract. They considered this to be phenotypic heterogeneity associated with this mutation.

Guo et al. (2012) noted that 2 unrelated Chinese families with different nucleotide changes (c.470G-A and c.471G-A), both causing W157X in CRYGC, had similar phenotypes of nuclear cataract and microcornea.

### History

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) studied the Coppock-like cataract family. They found that the CRYGD gene cloned from the disease chromosome was functionally equivalent to its wildtype allele. The CRYGE pseudogene on the disease chromosome, however, contained a cluster of sequence changes within and around its TATA box. Together these changes increased promoter activity 10-fold, raising expression of the pseudogene to 30% of that of CRYGD. The authors proposed that reactivation of the pseudogene, with overexpression of a predicted 6-kD N-terminal gamma-crystallin fragment, could cause the cataract. Héon et al. (1999) later found that this CRYGE change was a polymorphism present in 23% of their control population. They then identified a CRYGC missense mutation in the original family. In the family with zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. (2000) also concluded that activation of the CRYGE pseudogene was unlikely to cause the disease. They found base changes in the TATA box, but not in the Sp1-binding site, in unaffected controls.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CATARACT 2, COPPOCK-LIKE**  
CRYGC, THR5PRO [c.13A>C; p.T5P]

In the original family with Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus reported by Lubsen et al. (1987), Héon et al. **.0002 CATARACT 2, ZONULAR PULVERULENT**  
CRYGC, 5-BP DUP [c.123_128insGCGGC]

In a 7-generation family with highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataract (Scott et al., 1994), Ren et al. (2000) identified a 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of the CRYGC gene in 1 allele of every affected family member. The duplication was absent from unaffected family members and from controls. It disrupts the reading frame and is predicted to produce an unstable gamma-C-crystallin with 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids. Guo et al. (2012) listed this mutation as c.123-128insGCGGC. ⚠︎

**.0003 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR**  
CRYGC, ARG168TRP [c.502C>T; p.R168W]

In a family with primary congenital nuclear cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disease and was absent from healthy family members and 170 unrelated controls. The authors noted that the mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract and was listed as a SNP in the NCBI database. They concluded that it is a disease-causing mutation.

**.0004 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR, WITH MICROCORNEA**  
CRYGC, TRP157TER, 470G-A [c.470G>A; p.W157X]

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.470G-A change (TGG-to-TAG) in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was not found in 100 normal controls. It is predicted to truncate 17 amino acids from the C terminus and to affect the fourth Greek key motif.

**.0005 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR**  
CRYGC, GLY129CYS [c.385G>T; p.Gly129Cys]

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.385G-T transversion in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. In zebrafish, the mutant protein caused lens vacuoles and incomplete denucleation. Spectroscopic studies showed that the mutation impaired tertiary structure, markedly reduced thermal stability, and increased the tendency to aggregate under stress. ⚠︎

**.0006 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR, WITH MICROCORNEA**  
CRYGC, TRP157TER, 471G-A [c.471G>A; p.W157X]

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.471G-A transition (TGG-to-TGA) in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was not found in unaffected family members or in 100 controls. It differs at the nucleotide level from the c.470G-A change that also causes W157X (Zhang et al., 2009). ⚠︎
