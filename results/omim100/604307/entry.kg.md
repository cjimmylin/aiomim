## CATARACT 2, MULTIPLE TYPES; CTRCT2

### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cataract-2 (CTRCT2), which comprises multiple types of cataract with or without microcornea, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gamma-C-crystallin gene (CRYGC; 123680) on chromosome 2q33.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Cataract-2 is an autosomal dominant congenital cataract caused by mutation in the CRYGC gene. The lens opacities vary in type and include Coppock-like, zonular pulverulent, lamellar, and nuclear cataracts. In some families the nuclear cataract occurs with microcornea (Zhang et al., 2009; Guo et al., 2012).

### Clinical Features

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family with a hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus that occurred in heterozygotes.

Scott et al. (1994) described a 7-generation family in which 48 of 138 individuals were known to have autosomal dominant congenital cataracts of the pulverulent type. Of 53 family members examined, 28 had congenital cataracts: 19 were unilateral (8 right eyes and 11 left eyes) and 9 were bilateral. On detailed slit-lamp examination, the clinically unaffected eye in unilateral cases had no lens opacity. When historical data were included, there were 48 affected members: 28 unilateral, 17 bilateral, and 3 obligate carriers who were not examined. Cataract severity varied within the family. At one extreme was a subtle unilateral zonular cataract with 20/20 vision. Other members had bilateral inner fetal nuclear pulverulent opacities with 20/16 vision in both eyes, and some had dense unilateral or bilateral nuclear cataracts that required early surgery. The authors reported that the autosomal dominant gene was expressed in an apparently random way as either unilateral or bilateral cataract. To their knowledge, this was the first such family described in the literature. ⚠︎

Ren et al. (2000) studied a previously described 7-generation family in which 30 members had highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) described a family in which several members had autosomal dominant primary congenital cataract of the nuclear type.

Zhang et al. (2009) studied a 4-generation Chinese family from a relatively isolated region of northern China in which 6 members had autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea. They had no other ocular or systemic abnormalities. The affected individuals had bilateral congenital nuclear cataracts, with a central nuclear opacity involving the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nucleus of the lens. They also had small eyeballs, and their corneas were about 9 to 10 mm in diameter. Every affected individual had visual acuity of hand movements in front of the eye, together with nystagmus and amblyopia.

Li et al. (2012) described a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract.

Guo et al. (2012) described a 4-generation Chinese family in which 6 members had isolated bilateral autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The nuclear opacities involved the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nuclei. Corneal diameter was about 9 mm in all affected members. All patients had nystagmus and amblyopia, and their intraocular pressure was normal. There was no family history of related systemic abnormalities. The proband was diagnosed at age 8 years, and the other affected members were diagnosed between 6 months and 13 years of age. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Cataract-2 is transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait. Lubsen et al. (1987) observed Coppock-like cataract in heterozygotes. Scott et al. (1994) described autosomal dominant pulverulent cataract in a 7-generation family. In the Chinese family studied by Zhang et al. (2009), autosomal dominant inheritance was supported by affected individuals in each of 4 generations and by male-to-male transmission, and penetrance appeared to be virtually complete.

### Mapping

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family with hereditary Coppock-like cataract using TaqI RFLPs within the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. A haplotype (P) was present only in family members with cataract, and all affected members carried it. This haplotype was not among those found in the Dutch and English populations. The finding showed close linkage between the gamma-crystallin gene cluster and the cataract locus, with a maximum lod score of 7.58 at theta = 0.

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) found that the Coppock-like cataract locus was closely linked to a particular combination of polymorphic TaqI sites within the human gamma-crystallin gene cluster. Mapping of these sites defined a 15-kb region that includes the gamma-D and psi-gamma-E genes.

Ren et al. (2000) mapped the cataracts in a 7-generation family with variable zonular pulverulent cataract to a 19-cM interval on chromosome 2q33-q35 that includes the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. The maximum lod scores were 4.56 (theta = 0.02) with D2S157, 3.66 (theta = 0.12) with D2S72, and 3.57 (theta = 0.052) with CRYG.

Zhang et al. (2009) carried out a genomewide linkage scan in a Chinese family with congenital nuclear cataract and microcornea. The maximum 2-point lod score was 2.29 at marker D2S325 (theta = 0.0). Haplotype analysis placed the disease locus in a 19.04-cM region at 2q32.3-q35, between D2S117 and D2S2382, which contains the gamma-crystallin gene cluster.

### Molecular Genetics

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) cloned the gamma-D and psi-gamma-E genes from the Coppock-like cataract (CCL) chromosome. The gamma-D gene functioned the same as its allele cloned from a wild-type chromosome. In contrast, the psi-gamma-E pseudogene (CRYGEP) from the CCL chromosome had a cluster of sequence changes within and around its TATA box. Together, these changes increased psi-gamma-E promoter activity tenfold, raising its expression to 30% of that of the gamma-D gene.

Heon et al. (1999) found that the Coppock-like cataract mutation previously reported in the CRYGE pseudogene was a polymorphism, present in 23% of their control population. Further analysis of the original Coppock-like cataract family identified a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. This mutation was not found in a large control population. The authors considered this the first evidence that CRYGC is involved in human cataract.

In a family with variable zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The duplication was present on one allele in every affected family member and was absent from unaffected family members and unaffected controls. The CRYGD gene showed no abnormalities. Ren et al. (2000) also sequenced the CRYGEP promoter region and carried out allele-specific oligonucleotide analysis in members of the pedigree. They concluded that activation of the gamma-E pseudogene was unlikely to cause the cataracts. Because base changes in the TATA box were also found in unaffected controls, these changes were excluded as a sole cause of cataract.

Santhiya et al. (2002) reported novel mutations in the gamma-crystallin genes that cause autosomal dominant congenital cataracts.

In affected members of a family with autosomal dominant nuclear congenital cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) sequenced the CRYGA-D gene cluster. They identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disease and was not found in healthy family members or in 170 unrelated controls. The variant was listed as a SNP in the NCBI database, but the authors concluded that R168W is the actual disease-causing mutation.

In a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) sequenced the candidate genes CRYGA through CRYGD. They identified a heterozygous c.470G-A change in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene that converts the conserved tryptophan at codon 157 to a stop codon (W157X). The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was absent from 100 normal controls. ⚠︎

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) used candidate gene sequencing to identify a heterozygous c.385G-T mutation in the CRYGC gene. The mutation results in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. Molecular modeling and spectroscopic studies indicated that the mutation impaired the tertiary structure of the protein and dramatically decreased its thermal stability.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) screened 9 candidate genes. They identified a heterozygous c.471G-A transition in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene that converts a conserved tryptophan codon to a stop codon (TGG-to-TGA; W157X). The mutation segregated with the disease and was absent from unaffected family members and from 100 ethnically matched controls. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) noted that the CRYGC R168W mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract. They concluded that their family with nuclear cataract was the first case of phenotypic heterogeneity specifically associated with this mutation.

Guo et al. (2012) noted that 2 Chinese families carried the CRYGC W157X change, one caused by c.471G-A and the other by c.470G-A, and that both had nuclear cataract with microcornea. Other CRYGC mutations had been associated with Coppock-like, zonular pulverulent, lamellar, and nuclear cataracts. The authors concluded that W157X is disease-causing and should no longer be considered a polymorphism.

### Pathogenesis

Lubsen et al. (1987) concluded that the linkage of Coppock-like cataract to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster provided genetic evidence that a lesion in a crystallin gene could be the primary cause of a cataract in humans.

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) predicted that the psi-gamma-E gene encodes a 6-kD N-terminal gamma-crystallin fragment. They suggested that reactivation of the psi-gamma-E gene, with resulting overexpression of this fragment, could cause Coppock-like cataract. Later studies by Heon et al. (1999) and Ren et al. (2000) did not support this mechanism (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

Ren et al. (2000) suggested that their 5-bp duplication in CRYGC showed that mutant beta-gamma-crystallins do not need to associate appropriately into oligomers to cause cataracts.

Zhang et al. (2009) proposed that CRYGC mutations that disrupt lens biochemistry early in development may cause microphakia, with microcornea arising secondarily from damage to the lens.

Guo et al. (2012) suggested that mutant CRYGC W157X transcripts may escape nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and be translated into a truncated protein that could have dominant-negative effects. They proposed that loss of the fourth Greek key motif may disrupt the symmetric structure and folding of gamma-C-crystallin and change how it interacts with other crystallins.

### Animal Model

Li et al. (2012) showed that expression of the CRYGC G129C mutant in zebrafish caused vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens, recapitulating the human cataract phenotype.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CATARACT 2, COPPOCK-LIKE**  
CRYGC, EXON 2 MISSENSE MUTATION

In the original family with Coppock-like cataract, Heon et al. (1999) identified a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The mutation was not found in a large control population.

**.0002 CATARACT 2, ZONULAR PULVERULENT**  
CRYGC, 5-BP DUP, EXON 2

In a 7-generation family in which 30 members had highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts, Ren et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The duplication was present on one allele in every affected family member and was absent from unaffected family members and controls. It disrupts the reading frame and was predicted to produce an unstable gamma-C-crystallin made up of 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids.

**.0003 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR**  
CRYGC, ARG168TRP [c.502C>T; p.R168W]

In affected members of a family with autosomal dominant primary congenital nuclear cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disease and was not found in healthy family members or in 170 unrelated controls. The variant was listed as a SNP in the NCBI database, but the authors concluded that it was the disease-causing mutation. They noted that this mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract, and that their family was the first case of phenotypic heterogeneity specifically associated with it.

**.0004 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR, WITH MICROCORNEA**  
CRYGC, TRP157TER [c.470G>A; c.471G>A; p.W157*]

In a 4-generation Chinese family in which 6 members had autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.470G-A change (TGG-to-TAG) in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution at a conserved residue. The mutation cosegregated with the disease and was absent from 100 normal controls. It was predicted to truncate the last 17 amino acids and disrupt the fourth Greek key motif. ⚠︎

In a 4-generation Chinese family in which 6 members had autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.471G-A transition (TGG-to-TGA) in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene. This is a different nucleotide change that causes the same W157X protein change. The mutation segregated with the disease and was absent from unaffected family members and from 100 ethnically matched controls. Bioinformatic modeling predicted that it truncates 18 C-terminal residues and partially removes the fourth Greek key motif. The authors concluded that W157X is disease-causing and should no longer be considered a polymorphism. ⚠︎

**.0005 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR**  
CRYGC, GLY129CYS [c.385G>T; p.G129C]

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.385G-T mutation in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. Molecular modeling and spectroscopic studies indicated that the mutation modified the H-bonding network in the C-terminal domain, impairing the protein's tertiary structure. It dramatically decreased thermal stability and increased aggregation propensity under high concentration, heat, and UV irradiation stresses. Expression of the mutant in zebrafish caused vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens, recapitulating the human cataract phenotype.
