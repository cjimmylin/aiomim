### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cataract-2 of multiple types (CTRCT2), with or without microcornea, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding gamma-C-crystallin (CRYGC) on chromosome 2q33-q35. ⚠︎

### Description

CTRCT2 is an autosomal dominant congenital cataract caused by mutation in the CRYGC gene. The cataracts reported in families with CRYGC mutations have been of several morphologic types, including Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus, variable zonular pulverulent cataract, lamellar cataract, and nuclear cataract (Lubsen et al., 1987; Ren et al., 2000; Gonzalez-Huerta et al., 2007). In some families the nuclear cataracts have been accompanied by microcornea (Zhang et al., 2009; Guo et al., 2012). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Lubsen et al. (1987) and Brakenhoff et al. (1994) referred to the cataract in the family they studied as 'Coppock-like cataract' (CCL), affecting the embryonic lens nucleus. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family in which a hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus occurred in heterozygotes.

Scott et al. (1994) described a 7-generation family with 48 of 138 individuals known to be affected with autosomal dominant congenital cataracts of the pulverulent type. Of 53 family members examined, 28 had congenital cataracts: in 19 the cataract was unilateral (8 right eyes and 11 left eyes) and in 9 it was bilateral. The clinically unaffected eye of patients with unilateral cataract showed no lenticular opacity on detailed slit-lamp examination. Severity ranged from a subtle unilateral zonular cataract with 20/20 visual acuity and bilateral inner fetal nuclear pulverulent opacities with 20/16 visual acuity in both eyes to dense unilateral and bilateral nuclear cataracts requiring early surgical removal. Including historical data on unexamined members, there were 48 affected members (28 unilateral, 17 bilateral, and 3 unexamined obligate carriers). Scott et al. (1994) noted that hereditary cataracts are typically symmetric, and that this family showed apparently random expression of the gene as unilateral or bilateral cataract.

Ren et al. (2000) studied a previously described 7-generation family with 30 members affected by highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) described a family with several members affected by primary congenital cataract of the nuclear type.

Zhang et al. (2009) studied a 4-generation Chinese family, residing in a relatively isolated region of northern China, with 6 members affected by nuclear cataracts and microcornea in the absence of other ocular or systemic abnormalities. Affected individuals had bilateral congenital nuclear cataracts consisting of a central nuclear opacity affecting the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nucleus of the lens, with congenitally small eyeballs and corneas approximately 9 to 10 mm in diameter. Every affected individual had visual acuity of hand movement in front of the eye, as well as nystagmus and amblyopia.

Li et al. (2012) studied a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract.

Guo et al. (2012) reported a 4-generation Chinese family in which 6 members had isolated bilateral nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The nuclear opacities involved the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nuclei, and corneal diameter was approximately 9 mm in all affected individuals. The proband was diagnosed with bilateral nuclear cataract and microcornea at age 8 years, when he underwent iridectomy on both eyes; his left eye underwent phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation at age 38, and his best corrected visual acuity was 0.08 in the right eye and FC/30cm in the left. Other affected members were diagnosed at 6 months, 1 year, and 13 years of age, and 1 had cataract extraction at 6 years of age. Intraocular pressure was normal, nystagmus and amblyopia were observed in all patients, and there was no history of related systemic abnormalities.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of congenital cataract was reported in the families described by Scott et al. (1994), Ren et al. (2000), Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007), Li et al. (2012), and Guo et al. (2012). In the family reported by Zhang et al. (2009), autosomal dominant inheritance was supported by affected individuals in each of 4 generations and by male-to-male transmission. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Using polymorphic TaqI sites within the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, Lubsen et al. (1987) identified a haplotype (P) that was present in all family members with Coppock-like cataract and only in affected members, demonstrating close linkage of the cataract locus to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster (maximum lod score of 7.58 at theta = 0).

Ren et al. (2000) localized the zonular pulverulent cataract in a 7-generation family to a 19-cM interval on chromosome 2q33-q35 including the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, with maximum lod scores of 4.56 (theta = 0.02) with D2S157, 3.66 (theta = 0.12) with D2S72, and 3.57 (theta = 0.052) with CRYG.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) obtained a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.29 at marker D2S325 (theta = 0.0); linkage and haplotype analyses placed the disease gene in a 19.04-cM region between D2S117 and D2S2382 on chromosome 2q32.3-q35.

### Molecular Genetics

In the family with Coppock-like cataract, Brakenhoff et al. (1994) mapped the linked polymorphic TaqI sites to a 15-kb region encompassing the gamma-D (CRYGD) gene and the gamma-E pseudogene (CRYGE). The gamma-D gene cloned from the cataract chromosome was functionally equivalent to the wildtype allele, whereas the pseudogene contained a cluster of sequence changes within and around its TATA box that caused a 10-fold increase in promoter activity, raising its expression to 30% of that of the gamma-D gene. Brakenhoff et al. (1994) proposed that reactivation of the pseudogene, with overexpression of a predicted 6-kD N-terminal gamma-crystallin fragment, could be the cause of the cataract.

Heon et al. (1999) found that the variant in the CRYGE pseudogene previously reported in the Coppock-like cataract family represented a polymorphism present in 23% of their control population. Further analysis of the original Coppock-like cataract family identified a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene that was not found in a large control population. Heon et al. (1999) noted that there was no direct evidence that upregulation of a pseudogene causes cataract.

In a 7-generation family with variable zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. (2000) found that sequencing and allele-specific oligonucleotide analysis of the CRYGE pseudogene promoter made activation of the pseudogene an unlikely cause, and that base changes in the TATA box were also present in unaffected controls. Sequencing of the CRYGC and CRYGD genes showed no abnormality in CRYGD, but identified a 5-bp duplication within exon 2 of CRYGC in one allele of each affected family member; it was absent from unaffected family members and unaffected controls.

In affected members of a family with primary nuclear congenital cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The healthy family members and 170 unrelated controls had normal sequence of the CRYGA-D gene cluster. The authors noted that this mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract but was listed as a SNP in the NCBI database, and concluded that R168W is disease-causing.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a c.470G-A change in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, predicted to introduce a stop codon at tryptophan-157 (W157X). The mutation, found by sequencing the CRYGA-CRYGD gene cluster after linkage to 2q, cosegregated with cataract in the family and was not observed in 100 normal controls.

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.385G-T mutation in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue, by sequencing candidate genes. Molecular modeling and spectroscopic studies indicated that the mutation impaired the tertiary structure of the protein by modifying the H-bonding network in the C-terminal domain. The mutation markedly decreased the thermal stability of gamma-C-crystallin and increased its propensity to aggregate under storage at high concentration, heat, and UV irradiation.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) identified a c.471G-A transition in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. The mutation was found by sequencing 9 candidate genes, cosegregated with disease in the family, and was not found in 100 ethnically matched controls.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) noted that the CRYGC R168W mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract, whereas the family they studied had nuclear congenital cataract, and considered this the first case of phenotypic heterogeneity associated with R168W.

Guo et al. (2012) noted that the c.471G-A mutation in their family and the c.470G-A mutation reported by Zhang et al. (2009) both produce W157X in different Chinese pedigrees, and that both were associated with similar phenotypes of nuclear cataracts and microcornea.

### Animal Model

Li et al. (2012) found that expression of the human CRYGC G129C mutant in zebrafish caused vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens, recapitulating the human cataract phenotype. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 CATARACT 2, COPPOCK-LIKE**  
CRYGC, THR5PRO [c.13A>C; T5P]

In affected members of the original family with Coppock-like cataract (Lubsen et al., 1987), Heon et al. (1999) identified a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene that was not present in a large control population. In reviews of CRYGC mutations, Zhang et al. (2009) and Guo et al. (2012) listed the Coppock-like cataract mutation as T5P (c.13A-C); Zhang et al. (2009) noted that the T5P mutation had been reported to cause partial unfolding, reduced protein solubility and stability, and decreased interactions with wildtype gamma-C-crystallin and with alpha-A-, alpha-B-, and beta-B2-crystallins. ⚠︎

**.0002 CATARACT 2, ZONULAR PULVERULENT**  
CRYGC, 5-BP DUP, NT123 [c.123_128insGCGGC]

In affected members of a 7-generation family with variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous 5-bp duplication within exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The duplication was absent from unaffected family members and unaffected controls. It disrupts the reading frame and is predicted to produce an unstable protein containing 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids. Guo et al. (2012) listed this mutation as c.123-128insGCGGC.

**.0003 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR OR LAMELLAR**  
CRYGC, ARG168TRP [c.502C>T; R168W]

In affected members of a family with primary nuclear congenital cataract, Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The mutation segregated with the disorder and was not found in healthy family members or 170 unrelated controls. The authors noted that the same mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract.

**.0004 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR, WITH MICROCORNEA**  
CRYGC, TRP157TER, 470G-A [c.470G>A; W157X]

In 6 affected members of a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a c.470G-A change in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, changing a phylogenetically conserved tryptophan codon (TGG) to a stop codon (TAG) (W157X). The mutation cosegregated with cataract and was absent from 100 normal controls. It is predicted to truncate 17 amino acids from the C terminus and to affect the fourth Greek key motif.

**.0005 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR**  
CRYGC, GLY129CYS [c.385G>T; p.Gly129Cys]

In a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract, Li et al. (2012) identified a heterozygous c.385G-T mutation in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. The mutant protein showed impaired tertiary structure, markedly decreased thermal stability, and increased propensity to aggregate, and in zebrafish the mutant caused lens vacuoles and incomplete lens denucleation.

**.0006 CATARACT 2, NUCLEAR, WITH MICROCORNEA**  
CRYGC, TRP157TER, 471G-A [c.471G>A; p.W157X]

In affected members of a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) identified a c.471G-A transition in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, changing the tryptophan codon TGG to a TGA stop codon (W157X). The mutation was not found in unaffected family members or in 100 ethnically matched controls. Modeling predicted truncation of 18 amino acids and partial loss of the fourth Greek key motif. Guo et al. (2012) noted that this family differed from that reported by Zhang et al. (2009), in which W157X resulted from a c.470G-A change.
