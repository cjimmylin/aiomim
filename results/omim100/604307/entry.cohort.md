### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cataract-2 of multiple types (CTRCT2), with or without microcornea, is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gamma-C-crystallin gene (CRYGC; 123680) on chromosome 2q33. ⚠︎

### Description

CTRCT2 is an autosomal dominant congenital cataract caused by mutation in the CRYGC gene. Families with CRYGC mutations have had Coppock-like cataract, highly variable zonular pulverulent cataract, or nuclear congenital cataract. In some families the nuclear cataract occurred with microcornea, nystagmus, and amblyopia.

### Clinical Features

Lubsen et al. (1987) studied a family in which a hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus occurred in heterozygotes.

Scott et al. (1994) described a 7-generation family in which 48 of 138 individuals were known to have autosomal dominant congenital cataracts of the pulverulent type. Twenty-eight of the 53 individuals examined had congenital cataracts: in 19 the cataract was unilateral (8 right eyes and 11 left eyes) and in 9 it was bilateral. When historic data were included, the 48 affected members comprised 28 with unilateral cataract, 17 with bilateral cataract, and 3 obligate carriers who were not examined. Whether the cataract was unilateral or bilateral appeared to be random. In patients with unilateral cataract, detailed slit-lamp examination of the clinically unaffected eye showed no lens opacity. Severity ranged from a subtle unilateral zonular cataract with 20/20 visual acuity, through bilateral inner fetal nuclear pulverulent opacities with 20/16 visual acuity in both eyes, to dense unilateral and bilateral nuclear cataracts that required early surgical removal. ⚠︎

Ren et al. (2000) studied a previously described 7-generation family with 30 members who had highly variable autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataracts.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) described a family with autosomal dominant primary nuclear congenital cataract.

Zhang et al. (2009) studied a 4-generation family from northern China with 6 members who had nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The affected individuals had bilateral congenital nuclear cataracts, with a central nuclear opacity affecting the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nucleus of the lens. They also had congenitally small eyeballs and corneas with a diameter of approximately 9 to 10 mm. Every affected individual had the same poor visual acuity of hand movement in front of the eye, and all had nystagmus and amblyopia.

Li et al. (2012) studied a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract.

Guo et al. (2012) described a 4-generation Chinese family with isolated bilateral autosomal dominant nuclear cataracts and microcornea. The opacities involved the embryonic, fetal, and infantile nuclei, and the corneal diameter was approximately 9 mm. All patients had nystagmus and amblyopia, intraocular pressure was normal, and there were no systemic abnormalities. The male proband (II:7) was diagnosed with bilateral nuclear cataract and microcornea at age 8 years and underwent iridectomy on both eyes at that age. At age 38, his left eye underwent phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation. Individual I:2 was examined at age 68 years, and individual II:2 was diagnosed at age 13 years. Individual III:3 had cataract extraction at 6 years of age. Individuals III:6 and IV:1 were first diagnosed at 6 months and 1 year of age, respectively.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of CTRCT2 was reported by Lubsen et al. (1987), who noted that the cataract in their family occurred in heterozygotes. It was also reported in multigenerational families by Scott et al. (1994), Ren et al. (2000), Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007), Zhang et al. (2009), Li et al. (2012), and Guo et al. (2012). ⚠︎

### Mapping

In a family with a hereditary Coppock-like cataract of the embryonic lens nucleus, Lubsen et al. (1987) found that the cataract locus was closely linked to the gamma-crystallin gene cluster, with a maximum lod score of 7.58 at theta = 0. A gamma-crystallin haplotype (P) was found only in family members with cataract, and all family members with cataract carried it. Lubsen et al. (1987) concluded that the primary cause of a human cataract could possibly be a lesion in a crystallin gene.

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) mapped the polymorphic TaqI sites that were closely linked to the Coppock-like cataract locus. These sites defined a 15-kb region that encompassed the gamma-D (CRYGD) and pseudo-gamma-E (CRYGEP) crystallin genes. ⚠︎

Scott et al. (1994) reported that work was under way to determine whether the causative gene in their family was linked to previously defined cataract loci on chromosomes 1, 2, or 16.

Ren et al. (2000) mapped the cataract in a 7-generation family to a 19-cM interval on chromosome 2q33-q35 that included the gamma-crystallin gene cluster. The maximum lod score was 4.56 (theta = 0.02) with D2S157, and the lod score with CRYG was 3.57 (theta = 0.052).

In a Chinese family with autosomal dominant nuclear cataract and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) found linkage to chromosome 2q32.3-q35, with a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.29 at marker D2S325 (theta = 0.0). Haplotype analysis placed the locus in a 19.04-cM region between D2S117 and D2S2382 that contained the gamma-crystallin gene cluster.

### Molecular Genetics

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) cloned the gamma-D and pseudo-gamma-E genes from the Coppock-like cataract chromosome of the family studied by Lubsen et al. (1987). The gamma-D gene was functionally equivalent to the wildtype allele. The pseudo-gamma-E gene, however, had a cluster of sequence changes in and around its TATA box. Together these changes increased promoter activity 10-fold, raising expression of the pseudogene to 30% of that of gamma-D. ⚠︎

Héon et al. (1999) re-examined the original Coppock-like cataract family and identified a missense mutation in a highly conserved segment of exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The mutation was not seen in a large control population. They found that the CRYGE pseudogene change reported earlier as the Coppock-like cataract mutation was a polymorphism present in 23% of their controls. They noted that, to date, there was no direct evidence that upregulation of a pseudogene causes cataracts. Héon et al. (1999) concluded that their findings were the first evidence that CRYGC is involved in human cataract formation.

In a 7-generation family with autosomal dominant zonular pulverulent cataract, Ren et al. (2000) identified a heterozygous 5-bp duplication in exon 2 of the CRYGC gene. The duplication was present in each affected family member and absent from unaffected family members and from unaffected controls. Ren et al. (2000) found that activation of the pseudo-gamma-E-crystallin gene was unlikely to cause the cataracts in this family: base changes in its TATA box were also found in unaffected controls, which excluded them as a sole cause. The CRYGD gene showed no abnormalities.

Santhiya et al. (2002) reported novel mutations in the gamma-crystallin genes that caused autosomal dominant congenital cataracts.

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) sequenced the CRYGA-D gene cluster in a family with nuclear congenital cataract. In affected members they identified a heterozygous c.502C-T transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in an arg168-to-trp (R168W) substitution. The mutation segregated with cataract. Healthy family members and 170 unrelated controls had a normal sequence. Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) concluded that R168W, which was listed as a SNP in the NCBI database, is a disease-causing mutation and should no longer be considered a SNP.

In a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataracts and microcornea, Zhang et al. (2009) identified a heterozygous c.470G-A transition in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution at a highly conserved tryptophan. The mutation cosegregated with cataract in all affected members and was absent in unaffected members and in 100 controls. Zhang et al. (2009) predicted that the mutation truncates 17 amino acids from the C terminus of gamma-C-crystallin, disrupting the fourth Greek key motif and possibly altering protein folding, stability, and/or solubility. ⚠︎

Li et al. (2012) sequenced candidate genes in a 3-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract. They identified a heterozygous c.385G-T transversion in the CRYGC gene, resulting in a gly129-to-cys (G129C) substitution at a highly conserved residue. Molecular modeling and spectroscopy showed that the mutation impaired the tertiary structure of gamma-C-crystallin by modifying the hydrogen-bonding network in the C-terminal domain. The mutation also dramatically decreased the thermal stability of the protein and increased its propensity to aggregate under high-concentration, heat, and UV irradiation stresses.

In affected members of a 4-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital nuclear cataract and microcornea, Guo et al. (2012) identified a c.471G-A transition (TGG-TGA) in exon 3 of the CRYGC gene, resulting in a trp157-to-ter (W157X) substitution. They predicted that the mutation truncates 18 C-terminal amino acids and disrupts the fourth Greek key motif. The mutation segregated with the disease and was not found in unaffected family members or in 100 controls.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Gonzalez-Huerta et al. (2007) noted that the CRYGC R168W mutation had previously been associated with lamellar cataract, whereas the family they studied had nuclear congenital cataract. They described this as the first case of phenotypic heterogeneity in primary congenital cataract specifically associated with R168W.

Guo et al. (2012) noted that the same W157X protein change, arising from a different nucleotide substitution (c.470G-A), had previously been reported in an unrelated Chinese family with a similar phenotype of nuclear cataract and microcornea. They concluded that W157X is disease-causing rather than a polymorphism.

### Pathogenesis

Brakenhoff et al. (1994) noted that the predicted protein product of the pseudo-gamma-E gene was a 6-kD N-terminal gamma-crystallin fragment. They suggested that reactivation of the pseudogene, with the resulting overexpression of this fragment, could be the cause of Coppock-like cataract.

Ren et al. (2000) predicted that the CRYGC 5-bp duplication disrupts the reading frame and produces an unstable gamma-C-crystallin. The predicted protein would contain 38 amino acids of the first Greek key motif followed by 52 random amino acids. Ren et al. (2000) suggested that mutant beta-gamma-crystallins may not need to associate properly into oligomers to cause cataracts.

Zhang et al. (2009) suggested that mutations disrupting lens biochemistry early in development may cause microphakia and secondary microcornea.

### Population Genetics

Lubsen et al. (1987) found that polymorphic TaqI sites in the gamma-crystallin gene cluster could define 8 possible haplotypes. Only 3 of these (Q, R, and S) were found in the Dutch and English population. A fourth haplotype (P) was found in the family with cataract.

### Animal Model

Li et al. (2012) found that expressing the gamma-C-crystallin G129C mutant in zebrafish caused vacuoles and incomplete denucleation of the lens, recapitulating the human cataract phenotype.
