### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the WASHC4 gene (also known as KIAA1033 or SWIP). ⚠︎

### Description

MRT43 is an autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder caused by mutation in WASHC4. WASHC4 encodes a subunit of the WASH complex, which takes part in endosomal trafficking. The first family was reported with nonsyndromic intellectual disability (Ropers et al., 2011). Later patients had a more variable, syndromic phenotype that included short stature, macrocephaly or microcephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies (Assoum et al., 2020), and skeletal muscle involvement (Gangfuss et al., 2022). Courtland et al. (2021) re-examined the original patients and found that they also had motor symptoms.

### Clinical Features

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. In a later summary, Assoum et al. (2020) described this family as including 7 affected individuals with moderate intellectual disability and short stature.

Assoum et al. (2020) reported 3 patients from 2 unrelated families who had syndromic intellectual disability. Two sisters, aged 4 and 5.5 years, both had learning disabilities, macrocephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, and subependymal heterotopic nodules. The younger sister also had congenital absence of the right internal carotid artery and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. The third patient, aged 34 years, had mild intellectual disability, short stature, and microcephaly.

Courtland et al. (2021) retrospectively re-evaluated the patients reported by Ropers et al. (2011), all of whom were homozygous for the P1019R mutation. Developmental delay or learning difficulties had been the main reason for medical evaluation. However, all of the patients also had motor symptoms (mean age, 10.4 years). Their movements were described as 'clumsy', and clinical examination showed notable fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. According to recent communication with the parents, the patients were now an average of 21 years old, and their symptoms had not notably worsened.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) reported 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype defined by dysmorphisms, intellectual disability, profound developmental disorder, and skeletal muscle involvement. A muscle biopsy from 1 of the patients was studied by immunostaining, which showed dysregulation of proteins relevant to proper muscle function. Gangfuss et al. (2022) stated that this was the first time skeletal muscle involvement had been shown in patients with this disorder.

### Inheritance

Ropers et al. (2011) established autosomal recessive inheritance of this disorder in a large consanguineous family. Assoum et al. (2020) found compound heterozygous variants in 2 further families, with 1 variant inherited from each parent, and Gangfuss et al. (2022) reported 2 sisters who were homozygous for a WASHC4 variant.

### Mapping

Ropers et al. (2011) used homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability.

### Molecular Genetics

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. They combined homozygosity mapping, targeted exon enrichment, and high-throughput sequencing, and after SNP filtering only 2 changes remained. One was a nonsynonymous change in the SWIP gene (WASHC4), which they considered causative because of high pathogenicity and evolutionary conservation scores and functional considerations. The mutation led to significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex. Courtland et al. (2021) gave this mutation as c.3056C-G, causing a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution.

Assoum et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to study 3 patients from 2 unrelated families with syndromic intellectual disability, and found compound heterozygous variants in KIAA1033 (WASHC4). Two sisters had a stop-gain variant (Q442*) and a missense variant (D1048G), each inherited from 1 parent. The third patient had 2 missense variants (K1079R and H503R), also inherited 1 from each parent.

Gangfuss et al. (2022) found a homozygous WASHC4 variant in 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype. Proteomic profiling of fibroblasts from 1 of the patients showed dysregulation of proteins relevant to maintaining the neuromuscular axis.

### Pathogenesis

Courtland et al. (2021) found that the mutant SWIP P1019R protein co-immunoprecipitated significantly less strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype SWIP in HEK cells (54.8% and 41.4% of wildtype, respectively). These results suggested that the mutation hinders formation of the WASH complex. Gangfuss et al. (2022) reported histologic and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering microscopy studies in their patient. They concluded that the findings supported a functional role of WASHC4 in humans in protein processing and clearance.

### Animal Model

Courtland et al. (2021) used CRISPR to create mice carrying the human WASHC4 P1019R mutation. In brains of homozygous mutant mice, strumpellin and WASH1 levels were markedly decreased. Spatial proteomics showed that the WASH complex was destabilized, with a decrease in an endosomal module and an increase in a lysosomal module. Cultured mutant neurons had enlarged EEA1-positive endosomes and fewer but larger cathepsin D-positive puncta. In the motor cortex of 7-month-old mutant mice, electron microscopy showed lipofuscin accumulation, and at 10 months, cleaved caspase-3 staining was increased. Mutant mice had persistent deficits in cued fear memory and markedly worse rotarod performance at both adolescence and adulthood. Gait abnormalities appeared by 5.5 months of age, indicating progressive motor decline.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE 43**  
WASHC4, PRO1019ARG [c.3056C>G; p.Pro1019Arg]

Ropers et al. (2011) identified a homozygous nonsynonymous change in the SWIP (WASHC4) gene in affected members of a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. The mutation led to significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the WASH complex. Courtland et al. (2021) gave this mutation as c.3056C-G, causing a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution. They found that it reduced binding of SWIP to strumpellin and WASH1. Mice homozygous for the mutation had markedly reduced WASH complex proteins in the brain, along with cognitive and progressive motor deficits. On re-evaluation, all of the patients homozygous for this mutation had motor symptoms. ⚠︎
