### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the WASHC4 gene (615748) on chromosome 12q23.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Autosomal recessive intellectual developmental disorder-43 (MRT43) is an intellectual disability disorder that was first described as nonsyndromic (Ropers et al., 2011). Later reports described syndromic forms with dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, macrocephaly or microcephaly, and skeletal muscle involvement (Assoum et al., 2020; Gangfuß et al., 2022). Motor symptoms have also been reported, including clumsiness, fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria (Courtland et al., 2021). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability, which they described as nonsyndromic.

Assoum et al. (2020) described 2 sisters, aged 4 and 5.5 years, with syndromic intellectual disability. Both sisters had learning disabilities, macrocephaly, dysmorphic features, skeletal anomalies, and subependymal heterotopic nodules. The younger sister also had congenital absence of the right internal carotid artery and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss.

Assoum et al. (2020) also described a 34-year-old patient with mild intellectual disability, short stature, and microcephaly. Assoum et al. (2020) concluded that KIAA1033 (WASHC4) is responsible for a heterogeneous autosomal recessive intellectual disability phenotype. They stated that further descriptions would be needed to refine the clinical phenotype.

Courtland et al. (2021) retrospectively analyzed clinical findings from 7 children who had been evaluated at the Royal Hospital in Muscat, Oman. The children were homozygous for the WASHC4 c.3056C-G mutation, and Courtland et al. (2021) stated that they had originally been reported by Ropers et al. (2011). Developmental delay or learning difficulties were the primary reason for medical evaluation. However, all patients also had motor symptoms at a mean age of 10.4 years. Their movements were described as 'clumsy', and clinical examination showed fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. By the time of recent communication with the parents, the patients were an average of 21 years old, and their symptoms had not notably worsened. The authors suggested that their mouse model either differs from the human patients or may predict future disease progression in these individuals. The mice worsened significantly at 5 to 6 months of age, which was thought to be equivalent to about 30 to 35 human years.

Gangfuß et al. (2022) reported 2 sisters with a syndromic phenotype that included dysmorphic features, intellectual disability, profound developmental disorder, and skeletal muscle involvement. The authors stated that these features expanded the clinical spectrum of WASHC4-related disease, and they compared the phenotype with previously reported cases.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of MRT43 is autosomal recessive. Ropers et al. (2011) established this in a large consanguineous family. Assoum et al. (2020) found compound heterozygous variants in which each variant was inherited from one parent. Courtland et al. (2021) and Gangfuß et al. (2022) reported affected individuals with homozygous mutations.

### Mapping

Ropers et al. (2011) studied a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. They identified the underlying gene defect using homozygosity mapping combined with targeted exon enrichment and high-throughput sequencing.

### Heterogeneity

Courtland et al. (2021) noted that autosomal recessive point mutations in WASHC5 (Strumpellin), another WASH complex member, have also been associated with syndromic and nonsyndromic intellectual disabilities. They also noted that dominant missense mutations in WASHC5 are associated with hereditary spastic paraplegia (SPG8).

### Molecular Genetics

Ropers et al. (2011) studied affected members of a large consanguineous family with autosomal recessive intellectual disability. In them, the authors found a nonsynonymous sequence change in the SWIP (WASHC4) gene, which encodes a member of the WASH complex. After SNP filtering, this was one of only 2 remaining changes. The authors considered it causative based on high pathogenicity and evolutionary conservation scores and on functional considerations. In functional studies, the mutation significantly reduced SWIP levels and destabilized the entire WASH complex.

In 2 sisters with syndromic intellectual disability, Assoum et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous variants in the KIAA1033 (WASHC4) gene. One was a stop-gain variant (Q442X) and the other a missense variant (D1048G). Each variant was inherited from one parent.

In a 34-year-old patient with mild intellectual disability, short stature, and microcephaly, Assoum et al. (2020) used exome sequencing to identify compound heterozygous missense variants in the KIAA1033 gene, K1079R and H503R. Each variant was inherited from one parent.

Courtland et al. (2021) studied the autosomal recessive WASHC4 mutation c.3056C-G (P1019R). They stated that Ropers et al. (2011) had previously identified this mutation in a cohort of children with nonsyndromic intellectual disability. They noted that the affected residue lies in a region of SWIP thought to be critical for binding the WASH component Strumpellin. In coimmunoprecipitation studies in HEK cells, mutant SWIP pulled down significantly less Strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype SWIP (54.8% and 41.4% of wildtype, respectively). This indicated that the mutation hinders WASH complex formation.

In 2 sisters with intellectual disability, dysmorphic features, and skeletal muscle involvement, Gangfuß et al. (2022) identified a homozygous variant in the WASHC4 gene.

### Pathogenesis

Ropers et al. (2011) noted that SWIP belongs to the WASH complex, which is involved in actin polymerization and in multiple endosomal transport processes. Because the mutation destabilized the WASH complex, they suggested that SWIP is a novel gene for autosomal recessive intellectual disability.

Courtland et al. (2021) defined the neuronal WASH complex proteome using in vivo BioID in mouse brain, with a WASH1-BioID2 probe expressed under the human synapsin-1 promoter. They identified 175 significantly enriched proteins (fold-change of 4 or more, FDR less than 0.05). These included all 5 WASH complex subunits and 13 previously reported WASH interactors, as well as endosomal, endocytic, cytoskeletal, and excitatory postsynaptic proteins. These results supported a major endosomal trafficking role for WASH in mouse brain. Courtland et al. (2021) proposed that WASH complex destabilization caused by the P1019R mutation drives cognitive and motor impairments through endolysosomal dysfunction in the brain. Over time, this dysfunction led to accumulation of neurodegenerative markers, including upregulation of unfolded protein response modulators and lysosomal enzymes, lipofuscin buildup, and cleaved caspase-3.

Gangfuß et al. (2022) performed proteomic profiling of fibroblasts from a WASHC4-deficient patient and found dysregulation of proteins relevant for maintaining the neuromuscular axis. Immunostaining of a muscle biopsy from the same patient confirmed dysregulation of proteins needed for proper muscle function. The authors stated that histologic studies and coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering microscopy supported a role for WASHC4 in protein processing and clearance in humans.

### Animal Model

Courtland et al. (2021) used CRISPR to generate a knockin mouse carrying a CCT-to-CGT point mutation in exon 29 of the mouse Washc4 gene. This mutation mimics the human WASHC4 c.3056C-G (P1019R) mutation. On Western blot of adult whole-brain lysate from homozygous mutant mice, the WASH complex members Strumpellin (3.8% of wildtype) and WASH1 (1.1% of wildtype) were almost completely lost. This phenocopied data from the human patients and indicated that the WASH complex is unstable in vivo.

Courtland et al. (2021) performed tandem-mass-tag spatial proteomics of brain from 10-month-old mutant mice. Four WASH proteins (WASHC1, WASHC2, WASHC4, and WASHC5) and RAB21A were differentially abundant in all 7 subcellular fractions, and 728 proteins differed overall between genotypes. An endosomal module (M38) containing WASH, CCC, Retriever, and CORVET-HOPS proteins was significantly decreased in mutant brain. A lysosomal module (M36) containing cathepsins, hydrolases, and progranulin was significantly increased. Cultured cortical neurons from homozygous mutant mice had enlarged somatic EEA1-positive early endosomes, with no change in puncta number. They also had fewer but larger cathepsin D-positive lysosomal puncta than wildtype neurons. These findings were consistent with a loss-of-function mutation causing morphologic abnormalities of the endolysosomal pathway.

Courtland et al. (2021) found significantly increased cleaved caspase-3 staining in the motor cortex of 10-month-old mutant mice (44.0% vs 25.4% of DAPI-positive nuclei), but not in adolescent mice. Transmission electron microscopy of motor cortex from 7-month-old mutant mice showed large electron-dense lipofuscin deposits. Dopaminergic cell number in the substantia nigra pars compacta and striatal dopaminergic innervation were unchanged. This suggested that the motor cortex was the main movement-related region affected.

In behavioral testing, Courtland et al. (2021) found that homozygous mutant mice performed normally in novel object recognition and Y-maze alternation. However, the mice showed a significant deficit in cued fear memory recall 48 hours after tone-footshock pairing, both at adolescence (P42) and in adulthood (6.5 months), while their responses to footshock and tones were intact. These results were consistent with the hypothesis that the P1019R mutation causes cognitive impairment in humans. The mutant mice also had an almost 50% reduction in rotarod retention time at both P45 and 5.5 months. TreadScan gait analysis was largely normal at adolescence. By 5.5 months, however, it showed significantly longer hindlimb swing times, longer stride lengths, and decreased homologous coupling, demonstrating progressive motor decline.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER, AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE 43**  
WASHC4, PRO1019ARG [c.3056C>G; p.P1019R]

Courtland et al. (2021) retrospectively analyzed 7 Omani children who were homozygous for a c.3056C-G transversion in the WASHC4 gene, resulting in a pro1019-to-arg (P1019R) substitution. Courtland et al. (2021) stated that these patients with MRT43 had originally been reported by Ropers et al. (2011). In addition to developmental delay or learning difficulties, all patients had motor symptoms, including clumsiness, fine motor difficulties, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and mild dysarthria. The affected residue lies in a region of SWIP thought to be critical for binding Strumpellin. In coimmunoprecipitation studies in HEK cells, mutant SWIP pulled down significantly less Strumpellin and WASH1 than wildtype SWIP (54.8% and 41.4% of wildtype, respectively). In homozygous knockin mice carrying the equivalent mutation, Strumpellin and WASH1 were almost completely lost in brain lysate (3.8% and 1.1% of wildtype, respectively).
