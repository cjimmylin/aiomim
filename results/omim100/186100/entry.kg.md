## SYNDACTYLY, TYPE III; SDTY3

*Alternative titles; symbols*

SYNDACTYLY OF FINGERS IV AND V  
RING AND LITTLE FINGER SYNDACTYLY

### TEXT

This entry describes type III syndactyly (SDTY3), syndactyly of the ring and little fingers, which has been reported both as an isolated autosomal dominant malformation and as part of oculodentodigital dysplasia (ODD); isolated type III syndactyly appears to map to the same region as ODD. ⚠︎

### Description

Type III syndactyly is syndactyly of the ring and little fingers (Johnston and Kirby, 1955). It can occur as an isolated malformation, often with brachymesophalangia of the fifth finger (De Smet et al., 1994), and is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait (McKiernan and McCann, 1993; De Smet et al., 1994). Type III syndactyly also occurs as part of oculodentodigital syndrome (Gladwin et al., 1997). ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Johnston and Kirby (1955) reported syndactyly of the ring and little fingers.

Opjordsmoen and Nyberg-Hansen (1980) reported a family from northern Norway in which 9 of 22 members across 3 generations had spastic paraplegia together with neurogenic bladder disturbance and syndactyly. The main complaint was the bladder disturbance, an incomplete supranuclear bladder paresis (uninhibited neurogenic bladder), which appeared early in the disease. The authors suggested that the family probably represented an unusual form of hereditary spastic paraplegia.

Brueton et al. (1990) described a family with type III syndactyly and a facial appearance resembling the facial phenotype of oculodentodigital dysplasia. The family had none of the other characteristic findings of oculodentodigital dysplasia.

Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) described a newborn boy with complete cutaneous syndactyly of fingers 3 to 5 on the left hand and fingers 4 and 5 on the right hand. His mother and maternal grandmother had been born with the same hand malformation. The mother had a thin nose and small teeth, and the grandmother's teeth had been very small, with enamel hypoplasia. Both women had normal eyes.

McKiernan and McCann (1993) reported a large family with 14 members who had syndactyly of the ring and little fingers (type III syndactyly). The clinical features varied between affected members. Three affected members had surgery in the authors' unit, and the senior author had previously operated on a fourth elsewhere.

De Smet et al. (1994) described 5 new cases of isolated syndactyly of the ring and small fingers. Brachymesophalangia of the fifth finger was a frequent association.

Paznekas et al. (2003) noted that oculodentodigital dysplasia is an autosomal dominant syndrome with craniofacial (ocular, nasal, and dental) and limb dysmorphisms, spastic paraplegia, and neurodegeneration, in which syndactyly type III and conductive deafness can occur in some cases and cardiac abnormalities are rarely seen.

### Inheritance

Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) concluded that inheritance was autosomal dominant in a family in which syndactyly was transmitted through 3 generations from maternal grandmother to mother to son. Pedigree analysis of a large family with 14 affected members by McKiernan and McCann (1993) confirmed autosomal dominant inheritance of type III syndactyly. De Smet et al. (1994) concluded that autosomal dominant inheritance was obvious in their cases of isolated ring-small finger syndactyly.

Opjordsmoen and Nyberg-Hansen (1980) concluded that the disorder of spastic paraplegia, neurogenic bladder, and syndactyly in their 3-generation Norwegian family was inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern.

### Mapping

Gladwin et al. (1997) studied a large family with type III syndactyly and atypical facial features. Their analysis suggested that isolated type III syndactyly maps to the same region of chromosome 6q22-q24 as the oculodentodigital syndrome (ODD) locus, to which they found linkage of ODD with polymorphic markers (pairwise maximum lod score = 9.37 at theta = 0.001).

### Heterogeneity

Brueton et al. (1990) raised the question of whether type III syndactyly and oculodentodigital dysplasia are separate genetic entities or part of a single disease spectrum, on the basis of a family with type III syndactyly and an ODD-like facial appearance but no other characteristic ODD findings. Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) concluded that their 3-generation family showed variable expression of oculodentodigital dysplasia and proposed that type III syndactyly and oculodentodigital dysplasia form a clinical spectrum. Gladwin et al. (1997) noted that type III syndactyly occurs as part of oculodentodigital syndrome but has also been reported as an isolated entity.

### Molecular Genetics

Paznekas et al. (2003) found mutations in the GJA1 (connexin-43) gene in all 17 families with oculodentodigital dysplasia that they screened, comprising 16 different missense mutations and 1 codon duplication. They suggested that the mutations may cause misassembly of channels or alter channel conduction properties.

### Pathogenesis

Richardson et al. (2004) reported that expression of Gja1 correlated with the phenotype observed in oculodentodigital syndrome/type III syndactyly.

### Clinical Management

De Smet et al. (1994) stated that surgery is required at a young age for syndactyly of the ring and small fingers.
