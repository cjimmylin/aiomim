## SYNDACTYLY, TYPE III; SDTY3

*Alternative titles; symbols*

SYNDACTYLY OF FINGERS IV AND V  
RING AND LITTLE FINGER SYNDACTYLY

### TEXT

This entry describes type III syndactyly (SDTY3). Isolated type III syndactyly has been suggested to map to the same region of chromosome 6q as oculodentodigital dysplasia, which is caused by mutation in the GJA1 gene and of which type III syndactyly is a feature.

### Description

Type III syndactyly is a hand malformation in which the ring and little fingers are joined. It shows autosomal dominant inheritance with variable expression (McKiernan and McCann, 1993), and brachymesophalangy of the fifth finger is a frequent association (De Smet et al., 1994). Type III syndactyly also occurs as part of oculodentodigital dysplasia, and the relationship between the isolated and syndromic forms has been debated (Brueton et al., 1990; Gladwin et al., 1997).

### Clinical Features

Johnston and Kirby (1955) reported syndactyly of the ring and little finger.

Opjordsmoen and Nyberg-Hansen (1980) reported a family from northern Norway with a hereditary neurologic disorder consisting of spastic paraplegia associated with neurogenic bladder disturbances and syndactylia. Nine of 22 members in 3 generations had these features. The bladder disturbance, an incomplete supranuclear bladder paresis (uninhibited neurogenic bladder), was the main complaint and occurred at an early stage of the disease. The authors considered that the family probably represented an unusual form of hereditary spastic paraplegia with autosomal dominant inheritance.

Brueton et al. (1990) described a family with type III syndactyly and facies resembling the facial phenotype of oculodentodigital dysplasia, in the absence of any of the other characteristic findings of that condition, and discussed whether the 2 disorders are separate genetic entities or a disease spectrum.

Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) reported a newborn boy with complete cutaneous syndactyly of fingers 3 to 5 of the left hand and fingers 4 and 5 of the right hand. His mother and maternal grandmother were born with the same hand malformation. The mother had a thin nose and small teeth, and the grandmother had had very small teeth with enamel hypoplasia; eyes were normal in both. The authors considered that the family provided evidence of variable expression in oculodentodigital dysplasia.

McKiernan and McCann (1993) reported a pedigree containing 14 cases of familial syndactyly of the ring and little finger, 3 of whom were treated surgically in their unit and a fourth previously elsewhere. Clinical study revealed variable phenotypic expression. The authors noted that this was one of the largest pedigrees reported for this type of syndactyly.

De Smet et al. (1994) described 5 new cases of isolated syndactyly of the small and ring fingers. Brachymesophalangy of the fifth finger was a frequent association. The authors stated that surgery is required at a young age.

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of type III syndactyly was confirmed by pedigree analysis in the large family reported by McKiernan and McCann (1993) and was evident in the families reported by Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) and De Smet et al. (1994).

### Mapping

In a genomewide search, Gladwin et al. (1997) established linkage of oculodentodigital syndrome to polymorphic markers on chromosome 6q22-q24 (maximum pairwise lod = 9.37 at theta = 0.001). Analysis of a large family with type III syndactyly but atypical facial features further suggested that isolated type III syndactyly is located in the same region of the genome.

### Heterogeneity

Type III syndactyly occurs as part of oculodentodigital dysplasia (ODD) and also as an isolated entity (Gladwin et al., 1997). Brueton et al. (1990) questioned whether the 2 are separate genetic entities or a disease spectrum, and Schrander-Stumpel et al. (1993) regarded a family with type III syndactyly and subtle dental and facial findings as evidence of a clinical spectrum with ODD. Paznekas et al. (2003) identified mutations in the GJA1 gene, located at chromosome 6q22-q23, in all 17 families with ODD that they screened (16 different missense mutations and 1 codon duplication); syndactyly type III occurs in some cases of ODD.

### Animal Model

Richardson et al. (2004) reported that expression of Gja1 correlates with the phenotype observed in oculodentodigital syndrome/type III syndactyly.
