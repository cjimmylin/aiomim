### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene, which encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) of the major spliceosome, on chromosome 12.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, and seizures (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Most affected individuals show brain MRI abnormalities and dysmorphic facial features, and involvement of other organ systems, including visual, gastrointestinal, and skeletal, is frequent (Chen et al., 2024). The disorder is caused by de novo variants in a short, highly constrained central region of RNU4-2, and Chen et al. (2024) estimated that variants in this region explain about 0.4% of individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders.

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms recorded for 46 participants in the UK 100,000 Genomes Project (100KGP) who were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality and carried RNU4-2 variants. Of these 46 cases, 91% had intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay. Terms significantly overrepresented compared with other neurodevelopmentally affected participants included microcephaly in 57% (vs 18%), drooling in 13% (vs 1%), proportionate short stature in 28% (vs 7%), generalized hypotonia in 39% (vs 13%), seizure in 52% (vs 27%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion in 13% (vs 2%). No terms were significantly underrepresented.

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures, in whom extensive previous genetic investigations had not identified a cause. They presented detailed phenotyping obtained over a 5-year follow-up, including photographs showing facial features that they considered recognizable for this disorder.

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed phenotypic information on 49 individuals (21 females and 28 males; median age at last evaluation 10 years, range 2 to 38 years) with RNU4-2 variants. All had global developmental delay (severe in 34 and moderate in 10) and all 45 assessed had intellectual disability. Of individuals older than 5 years, 83% were ambulatory, but walking was delayed (average 3.4 years, range 17 months to 7.5 years), with some having a wide-based or ataxic gait. Only 3 individuals had fluent speech; 35 of 45 with speech abnormality were nonverbal and 10 had a few words. Other features included short stature (76%), microcephaly (77%, mostly congenital), hypotonia (87%), seizures (77%), intrauterine growth restriction (18%), behavioral issues (67%), autism spectrum disorder (48%), sleep issues (47%), hearing loss (22%), and vision issues (79%), including optic nerve hypoplasia, strabismus, nystagmus, and cortical blindness. Seizure onset ranged from the first year of life to 10 years (median 3 years) and seizure types included infantile spasms, focal and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. Brain MRI was abnormal in 91%, most frequently showing reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, and delayed myelination. Gastrointestinal problems included constipation, gastroesophageal reflux, and feeding difficulties, with 13 of 41 requiring a gastrostomy tube; skeletal abnormalities included osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, and hip dysplasia; and endocrine involvement included hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, and pituitary abnormalities. All but 3 individuals had dysmorphic facial features, which varied but included a myopathic face, deep-set eyes (some widely and some narrowly spaced), epicanthus, wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, large cupped ears, full cheeks, a distinctive mouth with full lips and downturned corners, high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. No significant differences between males and females were noted.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of ReNU syndrome is autosomal dominant; most cases result from de novo heterozygous variants (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Chen et al. (2024) found that in all 54 individuals in whom the parental origin could be confidently determined, the variant was on the maternal allele. Greene et al. (2024) observed one case that inherited the n.76C-T variant from an affected mother.

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) compared the burden of rare variants in 41,132 noncoding genes between 5,529 unrelated cases with intellectual disability and 46,401 unrelated controls in the 100KGP using whole-genome sequencing data, and identified a strong dominant association with rare variants in RNU4-2. Variants clustered in 2 contiguous regions (n.62-70 and n.73-79) in which no unaffected participant carried a rare variant; these regions map to a quasi-pseudoknot interaction between U4 and U6 and to U4/U6 stem III. Of 47 rare alleles in these regions, found in 47 cases, 37 were de novo and 10 had unknown inheritance. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, occurred de novo in 33 families. In replication in 3 further UK collections, they identified 27 additional probands with a rare variant in the 2 regions, of whom 26 were affected; 19 of the 26 had a de novo variant, 6 had unknown inheritance, and 1 inherited n.76C-T from an affected mother, bringing the number of unrelated cases to 73. One parent was found to be mosaic for n.65A-G. The n.76C-T variant was the only variant present in gnomAD (1 allele in 152,108), and an n.76C-G variant was observed in 1 participant without neurodevelopmental abnormality, suggesting to the authors that some changes at position 76 may have milder or benign effects.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 164 trios with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders and identified the recurrent de novo RNU4-2 variant n.64_65insT in a 5-year-old girl.

Chen et al. (2024) identified a recurrent single-base insertion in RNU4-2, n.64_65insT, that occurred de novo in 38 probands with undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders in the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project; 8 more individuals without data for both parents also had the variant, giving 46 (0.52% of 8,841 undiagnosed probands). The variant was not found in 3,408 probands with a known genetic diagnosis, 21,817 probands with other phenotypes, or 33,122 unaffected individuals, and was absent from gnomAD v4.0 and All of Us. They defined an 18-bp critical region (nucleotides 62-79) depleted of variation in the population and, including additional cohorts, identified heterozygous variants in this region in 115 individuals; de novo occurrence was confirmed in 86 with sequencing data from both parents. Most (89; 77.4%) had n.64_65insT; the second recurrent insertion, n.77_78insT, was found in 6 individuals, including 2 affected sibs; and 15 individuals had single-nucleotide variants (10 confirmed de novo). In 1 individual, the n.65A-G variant appeared to be mosaic in the mother, and in another, the n.76C-T variant was maternally inherited.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) suggested that the n.76C-T variant, which was inherited from an affected mother in 1 family and present once in gnomAD, may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases than the other variants, particularly n.64_65insT.

Chen et al. (2024) found that children with single-nucleotide variants had fewer reports of severe global developmental delay than those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40), and 2 of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV. They noted that SNVs may result in a milder phenotype but that this was based on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) noted that the n.64_65insT variant could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction supporting the quasi-pseudoknot structure, and proposed that a delay in U4-U6 unwinding, similar to that seen in WBP4 deficiency, might be the underlying mechanism.

Chen et al. (2024) performed RNA sequencing on blood from 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants (3 with n.64_65insT, 1 with n.77_78insT, and 1 with n.78A-C) and found increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites compared with controls (mean 8.8 vs 0.7 events). The unannotated 5-prime splice sites with increased use showed an increase in T at position +3 and C at positions +4 and +5, the positions that pair with the U6 ACAGAGA sequence. Twelve abnormal splicing events were shared by 2 or more affected individuals, none of which was found in 378 controls. Chen et al. (2024) also showed that RNU4-2 is expressed at a consistently higher level than its paralog RNU4-1 in human prefrontal cortex across development, and that chromatin around RNU4-2 is more accessible in prenatal cortex.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 is not captured by exome sequencing. Among 1,755 individuals with both genome data from the 100,000 Genomes Project and exome data from the DDD study, 22 had n.64_65insT, but only 2 had any exome read at the variant position, and in each case the read matched the reference sequence. The authors concluded that exome data have very low sensitivity for detecting these variants.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 variants in 46 of 9,112 (0.50%) neurodevelopmentally affected cases in the 100KGP, making RNU4-2 the most frequent cause of neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 was the second most frequent cause (21 of 5,527 cases, 0.38%), after MECP2.

Chen et al. (2024) found variants in the 18-bp critical region in 60 of 8,841 (0.68%) genetically undiagnosed probands with neurodevelopmental disorders in Genomics England, compared with 39 of 490,132 (0.008%) individuals in the UK Biobank. Assuming a 40% diagnostic yield before recruitment, they estimated that RNU4-2 variants explain 0.4% of all neurodevelopmental disorders. The rate was much lower in autism cohorts (3 of 7,149; 0.042%).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, n.64_65insT [NR_003137.2:n.64_65insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291839:T:TA]

Greene et al. (2024) found that the most common RNU4-2 variant in intellectual disability cases in the 100KGP, n.64_65insT, occurred de novo in 33 families. The variant lies in the region of U4 snRNA that forms the quasi-pseudoknot interaction with U6, and the authors noted that it could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction.

Schot et al. (2024) identified a de novo heterozygous n.64_65insT variant in a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) identified the n.64_65insT variant in 89 of 115 individuals with variants in the RNU4-2 critical region. In Genomics England it arose de novo in 38 probands sequenced with unaffected parents. It was absent from gnomAD v4.0 and All of Us and was seen in 1 individual in the UK Biobank. In all 46 individuals with n.64_65insT in whom the parent of origin could be resolved, the variant was on the maternal allele. RNA sequencing of blood from affected individuals showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites.

**.0002 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, n.76C-T [n.76C>T]

Greene et al. (2024) found the n.76C-T variant de novo in 4 families and inherited from an affected mother in 1 case. It was the only variant among the 74 pedigrees present in gnomAD (1 allele in 152,108), suggesting to the authors that it may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases. The variant was predicted to disrupt Watson-Crick base pairing in U4 stem III.

Chen et al. (2024) observed a maternally inherited n.76C-T variant in 1 individual with a neurodevelopmental disorder.

**.0003 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, n.65A-G [n.65A>G]

Greene et al. (2024) found the n.65A-G variant in a heterozygous proband in the 100KGP. His mother had a homozygous reference genotype call, but inspection of the aligned reads showed that she was probably mosaic.

Chen et al. (2024) reported 1 individual with the n.65A-G variant whose mother appeared to be mosaic (54 reference and 8 alternate reads).

**.0004 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, n.77_78insT [n.77_78insT; GRCh38:chr12:120291826:T:TA]

Chen et al. (2024) identified the second recurrent single-base insertion, n.77_78insT, in 6 individuals with a neurodevelopmental disorder, 2 of whom were affected sibs. The variant lies in the U4 region that base-pairs with U6 in stem III. One individual with this variant was included in their blood RNA-sequencing analysis, which showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites in individuals with RNU4-2 variants.
