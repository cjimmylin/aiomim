### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation, usually arising de novo, in the RNU4-2 gene (620823), which encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA, on chromosome 12q24.31.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder (Greene et al., 2024). Its features include moderate to severe global developmental delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, and severely impaired or absent speech (Chen et al., 2024; Greene et al., 2024). Many patients also have seizures, microcephaly, short stature, dysmorphic facial features, and brain abnormalities on imaging (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). The disorder is caused by heterozygous, mostly de novo, mutations in short, highly constrained regions of the noncoding RNU4-2 gene. This gene encodes U4 snRNA, a component of the major spliceosome. Greene et al. (2024) concluded that the disorder is one of the most prevalent monogenic neurodevelopmental disorders.

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 variants in patients with neurodevelopmental disorders in the 100,000 Genomes Project. They described a syndrome characterized by intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, seizures, and motor delay. They concluded that the RNU4-2-related disorder is among the most prevalent monogenic neurodevelopmental disorders.

Chen et al. (2024) identified mostly de novo RNU4-2 variants in a large series of individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. The variants were found first in the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project and then in further cohorts. All 49 patients assessed had global developmental delay, and all 45 assessed had intellectual disability. Hypotonia was present in 39 of 45. Speech was abnormal in 45 of 48, and 35 of 45 were nonverbal. Common findings were seizures (37 of 48), microcephaly (37 of 48), short stature (37 of 49), and abnormal brain MRI (41 of 45). The patients shared recurring facial features.

### Inheritance

ReNU syndrome shows autosomal dominant inheritance, and most mutations arise de novo. Of 26 affected probands studied by Greene et al. (2024), 19 had acquired the RNU4-2 variant de novo. In 6 probands the inheritance was unknown because parental genotype data were lacking. One proband had inherited the n.76C-T variant from an affected mother. ⚠︎

Chen et al. (2024) found that most variants were de novo, confirmed in 86 of 115 individuals. However, one SNV (n.76C-T) was maternally inherited. In another individual, the n.65A-G variant appeared to be mosaic in the mother.

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed whole-genome sequencing data from participants in the 100,000 Genomes Project. They found de novo variants in 2 regions of RNU4-2 in 47 cases with a neurodevelopmental syndrome. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, occurred as a de novo mutation in 33 different families.

Chen et al. (2024) performed genome sequencing in patients with neurodevelopmental disorders and found an excess of RNU4-2 variants. These variants were present in 60 of 8,841 genetically undiagnosed probands (0.68%) in Genomics England, compared with 39 of 490,132 individuals (0.008%) in the UK Biobank. The variants clustered in a critical 18-bp region in the center of RNU4-2. This region maps to 2 structural elements in the U4/U6 snRNA duplex, the T-loop and stem III. Both elements are thought to stabilize U4/U6 pairing and to position the U6 ACAGAGA sequence to receive the 5-prime splice site during initial spliceosome assembly. Most individuals (77.4%) carried the same recurrent single-base insertion, n.64_65insT. This variant was not absent from population databases, because it was seen in a single individual in the UK Biobank. RNA sequencing of blood samples showed that 5-prime splice site use was systematically disrupted in individuals with RNU4-2 variants. Chen et al. (2024) noted that the region lies within a highly conserved noncoding exon and is not included in commercially available clinical exome capture, which mainly targets protein-coding exons.

### Pathogenesis

Chen et al. (2024) proposed that inserting a single base into the T-loop or stem III region of U4 snRNA may destabilize the U4/U6 interaction, alter the positioning of the U6 ACAGAGA sequence, or both. This could disrupt the correct loading of the 5-prime splice site into the fully assembled spliceosome. They noted that this proposed effect was supported by the systematic disruption of 5-prime splice site use seen in RNA-sequencing data from patients with RNU4-2 variants.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 64T [n.64_65insT]

Greene et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous 1-bp insertion (n.64_65insT) in the RNU4-2 gene in patients with ReNU syndrome. The mutation had arisen de novo in 33 different families. It was the most common variant in the implicated regions of the gene.

Chen et al. (2024) found the n.64_65insT mutation in most individuals with RNU4-2 variants (77.4%). It was highly recurrent.
