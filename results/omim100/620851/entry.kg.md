### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene (620823) on chromosome 12q24.31. RNU4-2 is a noncoding gene that encodes the U4 snRNA of the major spliceosome.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is an autosomal dominant syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder. It is caused by mostly de novo variants in 2 regions of the RNU4-2 gene, which encodes the U4 snRNA of the major spliceosome U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP. The variants fall within an 18-bp region that is severely depleted of variation in the general population. The disorder is characterized by intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, seizures, and motor delay (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed HPO terms for 46 RNU4-2 cases from the UK 100,000 Genomes Project that were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Intellectual disability and neurodevelopmental delay were each present in 91% of cases, and motor delay was present in 61%. Compared with other cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality, several features were significantly overrepresented: microcephaly (57%), seizures (52%), generalized hypotonia (39%), proportionate short stature (28%), drooling (13%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion (13%).

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures whom they followed over 5 years. The authors considered the facial features in her photographs recognizable for this novel disorder.

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed phenotypes for 49 individuals (21 female and 28 male), with a median age of 10 years (range, 2 to 38 years). Of these individuals, 42 had the n.64_65insT variant, 3 had other insertions, and 4 had SNVs. All had moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability. Most (83%) learned to walk, but late (mean age, 3.4 years), and some had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Most were nonverbal. Other common features included short stature (76%), microcephaly (77%, mostly congenital), hypotonia (87%), seizures (77%; median age at onset, 3 years), abnormal brain MRI (91%), vision problems (79%), autism (48%), feeding difficulties, and constipation. Forty-five of 48 individuals had dysmorphic facial features. These varied but included a myopathic face with deep-set eyes, epicanthus, a wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, large cupped ears, full cheeks, full lips with downturned corners, a high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. The most frequent brain MRI findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, and delayed myelination. Many organ systems were involved: - skeletal: osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, and hip dysplasia - endocrine: hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, and pituitary abnormalities - visual: including cortical blindness - genitourinary, dental, and cutaneous Presentation did not differ significantly between males and females. Compared with all other NDD probands in Genomics England (GEL), the 46 individuals with the n.64_65insT variant were significantly enriched for HPO terms for global developmental delay, delayed gross and fine motor development, microcephaly, hypotonia, short stature, drooling, and absent speech.

### Inheritance

The heterozygous RNU4-2 mutations in ReNU syndrome mostly occur de novo, and the disorder follows an autosomal dominant pattern (Greene et al., 2024; Schot et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). In the 100,000 Genomes Project, Greene et al. (2024) found that 37 of the 47 variants were de novo and 10 had unknown inheritance. In their replication collections, 1 affected proband had inherited the n.76C>T variant from an affected mother. In another family, the mother of a proband with the n.65A>G variant was likely mosaic for the variant.

Chen et al. (2024) confirmed de novo occurrence by parental sequencing in 86 of 115 individuals. In all 54 individuals in whom the parental allele of origin could be resolved, the variant was on the maternal allele. This contrasts with the usual paternal bias for de novo mutations. The authors suggested that the bias could reflect negative selection in the male germline or imprinting. No maternal age effect was seen.

### Heterogeneity

Chen et al. (2024) examined 28 other brain-expressed snRNA genes. No other snRNA gene, or constrained subregion of one, was significantly enriched for de novo or biallelic variants in undiagnosed NDD probands; RNU12 showed only nominal enrichment of biallelic variants. Chen et al. (2024) also noted that RNU4-2 and the adjacent RNU4-1 gene on chromosome 12 are both 141 bp long and differ at 4 nucleotides, but variants in RNU4-1 were not enriched in NDD. Two lines of evidence supported RNU4-2 as the main U4 transcript in the brain: it was expressed at consistently higher levels than RNU4-1 across development in human prefrontal cortex, and prenatal cortex ATAC-seq showed much greater chromatin accessibility at RNU4-2. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) performed a genetic association analysis using whole-genome sequencing data from the 100,000 Genomes Project. They compared the burden of rare variants in 41,132 noncoding genes between 5,529 unrelated intellectual disability cases and 46,401 unrelated controls. This identified an extremely strong dominant association between rare variants in RNU4-2 and intellectual disability (posterior probability of association about 1; log Bayes factor 55). None of the 34 cases carrying the 3 top variants had previously received a diagnosis, and these cases were phenotypically more homogeneous than expected by chance. The authors found rare variants that were present only in affected participants in 2 contiguous regions of RNU4-2 (n.62-70 and n.73-79), within a locus markedly depleted of variation in gnomAD. Across 47 cases, 37 variants were de novo and 10 had unknown inheritance. In the quasi-pseudoknot region, the variants were: - n.63T>G - n.64_65insG - n.64_65insT - n.65A>G - n.65_66insT - n.67A>G - n.68_69insA In stem III, the variants were n.76C>T and n.77_78insT. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, arose de novo in 33 different families. In 3 replication collections (the NIHR BioResource, the 100,000 Genomes Project Pilot, and the UK Genomic Medicine Service), the authors found 27 further probands with a rare variant in the 2 regions; 26 were affected and 1 was unaffected. Of the 26 affected probands, 19 had a de novo variant, 6 had unknown inheritance, and 1 had inherited n.76C>T from an affected mother. This brought the total to 73 unrelated cases.

Schot et al. (2024) mined 164 WGS trios from individuals with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders who had received diagnostic genomic investigations at their clinic. In the girl they reported, they identified a recurrent de novo variant in RNU4-2 (NR_003137.2:n.64_65insT). Earlier extensive genetic investigations in this girl had not identified an alternative cause, and the authors concluded that the variant likely explained her phenotype.

Chen et al. (2024) identified heterozygous variants in RNU4-2 in 115 individuals with NDD. All variants lay within an 18-bp region that maps to the T-loop and stem III of the U4/U6 duplex. In the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project, a highly recurrent single-base insertion (n.64_65insT) arose de novo in 38 probands sequenced with both unaffected parents. Eight more individuals had findings consistent with de novo occurrence. All 46 had undiagnosed NDD, representing 0.52% of 8,841 undiagnosed NDD probands. The variant was absent from: - 3,408 diagnosed NDD probands - 21,817 non-NDD probands - 33,122 unaffected individuals - gnomAD v4.0 - All of Us It was present in only 1 individual in UK Biobank. Across all cohorts, 89 of the 115 affected individuals (77.4%) carried this variant. A second recurrent insertion, n.77_78insT, was found in 6 individuals, including 2 affected sibs. In total, 6 different single-base insertions in the critical region were found in 100 individuals; these included n.68_69insA and n.64_65insG. Single-base insertions in the region were present in 54 of 8,841 undiagnosed GEL NDD probands, compared with 2 of 490,132 UK Biobank individuals (OR = 1,531). Fifteen individuals had SNVs in the critical region. Ten of these SNVs were confirmed de novo, and all 15 individuals had phenotypes consistent with those of individuals with insertions. The SNVs clustered at both ends of the 18-bp region and were modestly enriched in GEL NDD probands compared with UK Biobank (OR = 9.51). The n.65A>G variant appeared to be mosaic in the mother of 1 individual, and the n.76C>T variant was maternally inherited in another. SNVs at the center of the region (nucleotides 71-74) were also found in non-NDD individuals and population controls.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) observed the n.76C>T variant de novo in 4 families and inherited from an affected mother in 1 family. It was the only variant among the 74 pedigrees that was present in gnomAD (allele count 1 in 152,108). The authors suggested that in some cases it may cause a less severe phenotype than other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. They also found the n.76C>G variant in 1 case without neurodevelopmental abnormality, which further suggested that some mutations at position 76 may have a limited or benign effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that children with SNVs had fewer reports of severe global developmental delay than children with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV, and fewer organ systems were involved in individuals with SNVs. The authors suggested that SNVs may cause a milder phenotype but noted that this needs confirmation in larger cohorts.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) reported that one variant region of RNU4-2 maps to the U4-U6 quasi-pseudoknot interaction and the other maps to U4-U6 stem III. They predicted that the variants destabilize the interactions of U4 snRNA with U6 snRNA and with RNA-binding proteins. G64 of U4 snRNA is thought to help stabilize the ACAGAGA loop of U6 snRNA, and the authors proposed that the n.64_65insT insertion could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the quasi-pseudoknot. Noting the similar phenotype of WBP4 deficiency, they suggested that delayed U4-U6 unwinding may be the underlying mechanism.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that the 18-bp critical region (nucleotides 62-79) is loaded into the active site of the BRR2 (SNRNP200) helicase. The n.64_65insT variant lies within a T-loop (quasi-pseudoknot), and n.77_78insT lies in the stem III region that pairs with U6. The authors proposed that single-base insertions destabilize the U4/U6 interaction and/or alter the positioning of the U6 ACAGAGA sequence that receives the 5-prime splice site. They analyzed blood RNA-seq data from 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants: 3 with n.64_65insT, 1 with n.77_78insT, and 1 with n.78A>C. Compared with controls, these individuals had significantly more abnormal splicing events at unannotated 5-prime splice sites (mean 8.8 vs 0.7). Splicing events shared by 2 or more patients were not found in any of 378 controls. At splice sites with increased use, positions +3 to +5, which pair with the U6 ACAGAGA sequence, shifted away from the consensus sequence. The authors concluded that the variants disrupt accurate 5-prime splice-site recognition during spliceosome activation.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) found that exome sequencing has very low sensitivity for detecting the n.64_65insT variant. Of 22 individuals with the variant who also had DDD exome data, only 2 had any read at the position, and in both cases the read showed the reference sequence. The authors stated that any variant detected in this way requires independent confirmation, and they noted that clinical exome sequencing does not capture the variant region. In the girl reported by Schot et al. (2024), the diagnosis was made only on reanalysis of whole-genome sequencing, after earlier extensive genetic investigations had been uninformative.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 cases in 46 of 9,112 (0.50%) 100,000 Genomes Project cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. This made RNU4-2 the most prevalent etiology for neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 was the second most prevalent etiology (21 of 5,527 cases, 0.38%), after MECP2 (24 cases).

Chen et al. (2024) found variants in the 18-bp RNU4-2 region in 60 of 8,841 (0.68%) genetically undiagnosed GEL NDD probands, compared with 39 of 490,132 (0.008%) UK Biobank individuals. Assuming a prior diagnostic yield of 40%, they estimated that RNU4-2 variants explain about 0.4% of all NDD. The rate was much lower in autism cohorts (3 of 7,149).
