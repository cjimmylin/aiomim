### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene (620823) on chromosome 12q24.31.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is an autosomal dominant syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder caused by de novo or inherited heterozygous variants in RNU4-2. RNU4-2 encodes the U4 snRNA of the major spliceosome U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP. Greene et al. (2024) described the syndrome as including intellectual disability, microcephaly, short stature, hypotonia, seizures and motor delay. Chen et al. (2024) found that the causative variants lie within an 18-bp region of RNU4-2 that maps to the T-loop and stem III of the U4/U6 duplex and is severely depleted of variation in the general population.

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed HPO terms for 46 cases with RNU4-2 variants from the UK 100,000 Genomes Project who were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Intellectual disability and neurodevelopmental delay were each present in 91% of cases, and motor delay in 61%. Compared with other cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality, several features were significantly overrepresented: microcephaly (57%), seizures (52%), generalized hypotonia (39%), proportionate short stature (28%), drooling (13%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion (13%).

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures, whom they followed over 5 years.

Chen et al. (2024) collected detailed phenotypes for 49 individuals (21 female and 28 male; median age 10 years, range 2 to 38). Of these, 42 had the n.64_65insT variant, 3 had other insertions, and 4 had single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). All had moderate to severe global developmental delay and intellectual disability. Most (83%) learned to walk, but late (mean age 3.4 years), and some had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Most were nonverbal. Other common features were short stature (76%), microcephaly (77%, mostly congenital), hypotonia (87%), seizures (77%; median age at onset 3 years), abnormal brain MRI (91%), vision problems (79%), autism (48%), feeding difficulties, and constipation. Of 48 individuals assessed, 45 had dysmorphic facial features. These varied, but included a myopathic face with deep-set eyes, epicanthus, a wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, large cupped ears, full cheeks, full lips with downturned corners, a high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. In most individuals brain MRI was abnormal, most often showing reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, and delayed myelination. Many organ systems were involved, including skeletal (osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, hip dysplasia), endocrine (hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, pituitary abnormalities), visual (including cortical blindness), genitourinary, dental, and cutaneous systems. Presentation did not differ significantly between males and females.

Chen et al. (2024) also compared 46 individuals carrying the n.64_65insT variant in the Genomics England (GEL) 100,000 Genomes Project with all other neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) probands in GEL. Individuals with the variant were significantly enriched for the HPO terms global developmental delay, delayed gross and fine motor development, microcephaly, hypotonia, short stature, drooling, and absent speech.

### Inheritance

Greene et al. (2024), Schot et al. (2024), and Chen et al. (2024) found that ReNU syndrome is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Most variants arose de novo. Greene et al. (2024) reported 1 proband who inherited the n.76C>T variant from an affected mother. Chen et al. (2024) reported affected sibs carrying the n.77_78insT variant.

Chen et al. (2024) used parental sequencing to confirm de novo occurrence in 86 of 115 individuals. In all 54 individuals in whom the parental allele of origin could be resolved, the variant was on the maternal allele, unlike the usual paternal bias of de novo mutations. The authors suggested that this could reflect negative selection in the male germline or imprinting. They saw no maternal age effect.

### Heterogeneity

Chen et al. (2024) examined 28 other brain-expressed snRNA genes. In undiagnosed NDD probands, no other snRNA gene, or constrained subregion of one, was significantly enriched for de novo or biallelic variants. RNU12 showed only nominal enrichment of biallelic variants. Variants in the adjacent paralog RNU4-1 were not enriched in NDD. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) ran a genetic association analysis on whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from the UK 100,000 Genomes Project. They compared the burden of rare variants in 41,132 noncoding genes between 5,529 unrelated intellectual disability cases and 46,401 unrelated controls. They identified an extremely strong dominant association between rare variants in RNU4-2 and intellectual disability (posterior probability of association about 1; log Bayes factor 55). None of the 34 cases carrying the 3 top variants had previously received a diagnosis, and these cases were phenotypically more homogeneous than expected by chance. Greene et al. (2024) found 8 rare variants that were present only in affected participants. The variants lay in 2 contiguous regions of RNU4-2 (n.62-70 and n.73-79), within a locus markedly depleted of variation in gnomAD. Of the variants in 47 cases, 37 were de novo and 10 had unknown inheritance. Seven variants lay in the quasi-pseudoknot region: n.63T-G, n.64_65insG, n.64_65insT, n.65A-G, n.65_66insT, n.67A-G, and n.68_69insA. Two variants lay in stem III: n.76C-T and n.77_78insT. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, arose de novo in 33 different families. In 1 pedigree, the n.65A>G variant was found in a heterozygous male proband. His mother was called homozygous for the reference allele, but inspection of the aligned WGS reads suggested that she was likely mosaic for the variant.

Greene et al. (2024) replicated their findings in 3 further collections: the NIHR BioResource, the 100,000 Genomes Project Pilot, and the UK Genomic Medicine Service. These collections contributed 27 more probands with a rare variant in the 2 RNU4-2 regions, of whom 26 were affected and 1 was unaffected. Among the 26 affected probands, 19 had a de novo variant, 6 had a variant of unknown inheritance, and 1 had inherited the n.76C-T variant from an affected mother. This brought the total to 73 unrelated cases.

Schot et al. (2024) mined 164 diagnostic WGS trios from individuals with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. They identified a recurrent de novo n.64_65insT variant (NR_003137.2) in the noncoding spliceosomal snRNA gene RNU4-2 in the girl described above, whose earlier extensive genetic investigations had found no alternative cause. The authors concluded that the variant likely explained her phenotype.

In 115 individuals with NDD, Chen et al. (2024) identified heterozygous variants in RNU4-2, all located within an 18-bp region. In the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project, they found a highly recurrent single-base insertion, n.64_65insT (GRCh38 chr12:120291839:T:TA). It arose de novo in 38 probands sequenced with both unaffected parents, and findings in 8 more individuals were consistent with de novo occurrence. All 46 individuals had undiagnosed NDD, representing 0.52% of 8,841 undiagnosed NDD probands. The variant was absent from 3,408 diagnosed NDD probands, 21,817 non-NDD probands, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and All of Us. It was seen in only 1 individual in UK Biobank. Across all cohorts, 89 of the 115 affected individuals (77.4%) carried this variant. A second recurrent single-base insertion, n.77_78insT (GRCh38 chr12:120291826:T:TA), was found in 6 individuals, including 2 affected sibs. Other single-base insertions included n.68_69insA and n.64_65insG. In total, 100 individuals carried 6 different single-base insertions in the critical region. Single-base insertions in this region were present in 54 of 8,841 undiagnosed GEL NDD probands, compared with 2 of 490,132 UK Biobank individuals (odds ratio 1,531).

Chen et al. (2024) also identified 15 individuals with SNVs in the RNU4-2 critical region. Ten of the SNVs were confirmed de novo, and all 15 individuals had phenotypes consistent with those of individuals with insertions. The SNVs clustered at both ends of the 18-bp region and were modestly enriched in GEL NDD probands compared with UK Biobank (odds ratio 9.51). In 1 individual the mother appeared to be mosaic for the n.65A-G variant, and in another individual the n.76C-T SNV was maternally inherited. SNVs at the center of the region (nucleotides 71-74) were also found in non-NDD individuals and in population controls.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) observed the n.76C-T variant de novo in 4 families and inherited from an affected mother in 1 family. Among the 74 pedigrees, it was the only variant present in gnomAD (allele count 1 in 152,108). The authors suggested that in some cases n.76C-T may cause a less severe phenotype than other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. The n.76C-G variant was seen in 1 case without neurodevelopmental abnormality, which further suggested that some changes at position 76 may have a limited or benign effect.

Chen et al. (2024) found that severe global developmental delay was reported less often in children with SNVs than in those with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV, and fewer organ systems were involved in individuals with SNVs. The authors suggested that SNVs may cause a milder phenotype but noted that this needs confirmation in larger cohorts.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) reported that one variant region of RNU4-2 maps to the U4-U6 quasi-pseudoknot interaction and the other maps to U4-U6 stem III. They predicted that the variants destabilize interactions of U4 snRNA with U6 snRNA and with RNA-binding proteins. They noted that G64 of U4 snRNA is thought to help stabilize the ACAGAGA loop of U6 snRNA, and they proposed that the n.64_65insT insertion could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the quasi-pseudoknot. Because WBP4 deficiency causes a similar phenotype, they suggested that delayed U4-U6 unwinding may be the underlying mechanism of disease.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that the 18-bp critical region (nucleotides 62-79) is loaded into the active site of the BRR2 (SNRNP200) helicase. The n.64_65insT variant lies within a T-loop (quasi-pseudoknot), and n.77_78insT lies in the stem III region that pairs with U6. The authors proposed that single-base insertions destabilize the U4/U6 interaction and/or alter the positioning of the U6 ACAGAGA sequence that receives the 5-prime splice site. They analyzed blood RNA-seq data from 5 individuals with RNU4-2 variants: 3 with n.64_65insT, 1 with n.77_78insT, and 1 with n.78A-C. Compared with controls, these individuals had significantly more abnormal splicing events at unannotated 5-prime splice sites (mean 8.8 vs 0.7). Splicing events shared by 2 or more patients were not found in any of 378 controls. At sites with increased use, positions +3 to +5, which pair with the U6 ACAGAGA sequence, had shifted away from the consensus. The authors concluded that the variants disrupt accurate recognition of 5-prime splice sites during spliceosome activation.

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 and its paralog RNU4-1 lie next to each other on chromosome 12; both are 141 bp long and they differ at 4 nucleotides. Across development in the human prefrontal cortex (BrainVar), RNU4-2 was consistently expressed at higher levels than RNU4-1. ATAC-seq of prenatal cortex showed much greater chromatin accessibility at RNU4-2. These data supported RNU4-2 as the main U4 transcript in the brain.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) found that exome sequencing has very low sensitivity for detecting the n.64_65insT variant. Of 22 individuals with the variant who also had exome data from the DDD study, only 2 had any read at the position, and in both cases the read showed the reference sequence. The authors stated that any variant detected by exome sequencing requires independent confirmation, and they noted that the variant region is not captured by clinical exome sequencing.

Schot et al. (2024) made the diagnosis in their patient by reanalyzing diagnostic WGS trio data, after earlier extensive genetic investigations had not identified a cause. ⚠︎

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found RNU4-2 variants in 46 of 9,112 cases (0.50%) in the 100,000 Genomes Project that were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. This made RNU4-2 the most prevalent cause of neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 was the second most prevalent cause (21 of 5,527 cases, 0.38%), after MECP2 (24 cases).

Chen et al. (2024) found variants in the 18-bp RNU4-2 region in 60 of 8,841 (0.68%) genetically undiagnosed GEL NDD probands, compared with 39 of 490,132 (0.008%) UK Biobank individuals. Assuming a prior diagnostic yield of 40%, they estimated that RNU4-2 variants explain about 0.4% of all NDD. The rate was much lower in autism cohorts (3 of 7,149).
