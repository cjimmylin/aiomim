### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that ReNU syndrome (RENU) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the RNU4-2 gene, which encodes the U4 small nuclear RNA (snRNA), on chromosome 12.

### Description

ReNU syndrome is a neurodevelopmental disorder. Its features include moderate to severe global developmental delay, intellectual disability, absent or very limited speech, hypotonia, microcephaly, short stature, seizures, abnormal brain MRI findings, and distinctive facial features. Many patients also have visual, gastrointestinal, and skeletal involvement. The disorder is caused by heterozygous, mostly de novo, variants in a short critical region of the noncoding RNU4-2 gene, which encodes the U4 snRNA of the major spliceosome. A single recurrent insertion, n.64_65insT, accounts for most cases (Greene et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024).

### Clinical Features

Greene et al. (2024) analyzed Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms for 46 participants in the 100,000 Genomes Project (100KGP) who had RNU4-2 variants and were coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality. Of these 46 cases, 91% had intellectual disability, 91% had neurodevelopmental delay, and 61% had motor delay. Compared with other cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality, the RNU4-2 cases were significantly enriched for several terms: microcephaly (57% vs 18%), drooling (13% vs 1%), proportionate short stature (28% vs 7%), generalized hypotonia (39% vs 13%), seizure (52% vs 27%), and abnormality of the upper lip vermilion (13% vs 2%). No terms were significantly underrepresented.

Schot et al. (2024) reported a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures. She was followed for 5 years, and clinical photographs showed recognizable facial features. Extensive previous genetic investigations had not found a cause.

Chen et al. (2024) found that 46 individuals in the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project (GEL) carried the recurrent n.64_65insT variant. Compared with all other probands with neurodevelopmental disorders, these individuals were significantly enriched for global developmental delay, delayed gross and fine motor development, microcephaly, hypotonia, short stature, drooling, and absent speech. Chen et al. (2024) also collected detailed phenotypic information on 49 individuals (21 females and 28 males; median age at last evaluation 10 years, range 2 to 38 years). Of these, 42 had n.64_65insT, 3 had other single-base insertions, and 4 had single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). All 49 had moderate to severe global developmental delay, and all assessed individuals had intellectual disability. Most (83%) achieved ambulation, but late, at an average age of 3.4 years (range 17 months to 7.5 years); some had a wide-based or ataxic gait. Only 3 individuals had fluent speech; some had a few words and most were nonverbal. Other features were short stature (76%), microcephaly (77%, mostly congenital), hypotonia (87%), seizures (77%), abnormal brain MRI (91%), behavioral issues (67%), autism spectrum disorder (48%), sleep issues (47%), hearing loss (22%), vision issues (79%), constipation (66%), gastroesophageal reflux (49%), feeding difficulties (76%), gastrostomy tube placement (32%), and intrauterine growth restriction (18%). Seizure onset varied, with a median of 3 years and a range from the first year of life to 10 years. Seizure types included infantile spasms, focal seizures, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, febrile seizures, and status epilepticus. The most frequent brain MRI findings were reduced white matter volume, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ventriculomegaly, and delayed myelination. Visual findings included optic nerve hypoplasia, cortical blindness, strabismus, and nystagmus. Skeletal findings included osteopenia, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, kyphosis, and hip dysplasia. Endocrine findings included hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency, and pituitary abnormalities. All but 3 individuals had dysmorphic facial features, which varied. They included a myopathic face, deep-set eyes (widely or narrowly spaced), epicanthus, a wide nasal bridge, anteverted nares or underdeveloped alae nasi, large cupped ears (some posteriorly rotated), full cheeks, full lips with downturned corners, a high-arched palate, and a large or protruding tongue. No significant differences were found between males and females.

### Inheritance

Greene et al. (2024) and Chen et al. (2024) found that the heterozygous RNU4-2 variants in ReNU syndrome mostly occurred de novo, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance; Chen et al. (2024) described the disorder as autosomal dominant. Greene et al. (2024) identified one case in which the n.76C-T variant was inherited from an affected mother. Chen et al. (2024) could confidently determine the parental allele of origin in 54 individuals, and in all 54 the de novo variant was on the maternal allele.

### Molecular Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) used whole-genome sequencing data from the 100,000 Genomes Project to compare the burden of rare variants in 41,132 noncoding genes between 5,529 unrelated intellectual disability cases and 46,401 unrelated controls. RNU4-2 was the most strongly associated gene. They identified 47 cases with rare variants in two contiguous regions of RNU4-2 (n.62-70 and n.73-79), and no unaffected participants had rare variants in these regions. Of the 47 variants, 37 were de novo and 10 were of unknown inheritance. The most common variant, n.64_65insT, occurred de novo in 33 different families, and one parent was mosaic for n.65A-G. In 3 replication collections, Greene et al. (2024) found 27 further probands with a variant in these regions, 26 of whom were affected. Of the 26 affected probands, 19 had de novo variants, 6 had variants of unknown inheritance, and 1 inherited n.76C-T from an affected mother. This brought the total number of unrelated cases to 73. One participant with n.76C-G did not have a neurodevelopmental abnormality. The variant regions map to the quasi-pseudoknot interaction between U4 and U6 snRNAs and to the U4-U6 stem III interaction.

Schot et al. (2024) reanalyzed whole-genome sequencing data from 164 trios with neurodevelopmental or multiple congenital anomaly disorders. In a 5-year-old girl with ReNU syndrome, they identified the recurrent de novo RNU4-2 variant n.64_65insT (NR_003137.2).

Chen et al. (2024) first identified the recurrent single-base insertion n.64_65insT (GRCh38 chr12:120291839:T:TA) in RNU4-2 as a de novo variant in 38 GEL probands sequenced with their unaffected parents. Searching probands without data for both parents found 8 more individuals, in all of whom the detectable inheritance was consistent with de novo occurrence. All 46 had undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders, representing 0.52% of 8,841 such probands. The variant was absent from 3,408 probands with genetically diagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders, 21,817 probands with other phenotypes, 33,122 unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and the All of Us dataset; it was seen in a single individual in the UK Biobank. Chen et al. (2024) defined an 18-bp critical region (n.62-79) that is depleted of variation in population datasets. Across GEL and additional cohorts, they identified 115 individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders and heterozygous variants in this region. Of these, 89 (77.4%) had n.64_65insT and 6 had n.77_78insT; the latter included 2 affected sibs. Where both parents were sequenced (86 individuals), the variants were confirmed to be de novo. In one individual the mother appeared to be mosaic for n.65A-G, and in another an SNV (n.76C-T) was maternally inherited. Single-base insertions in this region were strongly enriched in GEL probands with undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders compared with the UK Biobank (54 of 8,841 vs 2 of 490,132). SNVs showed a more modest enrichment. SNVs in individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders clustered at the two ends of the critical region, whereas SNVs in the central portion (particularly nucleotides 71-74) were also seen in individuals without neurodevelopmental disorders and in population controls. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Greene et al. (2024) observed n.76C-T as a de novo mutation in 4 families, as a variant inherited from an affected mother in 1 family, and once in gnomAD. They suggested that this variant may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases than the other variants, particularly n.64_65insT. They also observed n.76C-G in one individual without a neurodevelopmental abnormality and suggested that some changes at position 76 may have limited or benign effects.

Chen et al. (2024) found that the 4 children with SNVs had fewer reports of severe global developmental delay than individuals with single-base insertions (0 of 4 vs 34 of 40). Two of the 3 individuals with fluent speech had an SNV. Fewer organ systems were reported to be involved in individuals with SNVs. Chen et al. (2024) noted that this possible milder phenotype was based on only 4 fully phenotyped individuals and needed confirmation in larger cohorts.

### Pathogenesis

Greene et al. (2024) noted that the RNU4-2 variants lie in the central region of the U4/U6 snRNA duplex. They predicted that the variants disrupt either Watson-Crick interactions within U4 stem III or the configuration of the U4 quasi-pseudoknot, destabilizing interactions of U4 snRNA with U6 snRNA and with RNA-binding proteins. Because n.64_65insT could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction that supports the quasi-pseudoknot, they proposed that the disease mechanism may be a delay in U4-U6 unwinding, similar to that seen in WBP4 deficiency.

Chen et al. (2024) performed RNA sequencing on blood from 5 individuals: 3 with n.64_65insT, 1 with n.77_78insT, and 1 with n.78A-C. These individuals showed significantly increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites compared with both unmatched and matched controls. Of 12 abnormal splicing events shared by 2 or more affected individuals, 11 involved increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites, and none was seen in 378 controls. The 5-prime splice sites with increased use showed a shift away from consensus at positions +3, +4, and +5, which pair directly with the U6 ACAGAGA sequence. Chen et al. (2024) also found that RNU4-2 was expressed at consistently higher levels than its homolog RNU4-1 in human prefrontal cortex across development and had greater chromatin accessibility in prenatal brain. They concluded that RNU4-2 is the main U4 transcript in the brain.

### Diagnosis

Chen et al. (2024) noted that RNU4-2 is not included in standard exome capture. Of 22 individuals with n.64_65insT who also had exome data in the Deciphering Developmental Disorders cohort, none had a read showing the variant: 2 had a single read matching the reference sequence and 20 had no reads mapping to RNU4-2. Chen et al. (2024) concluded that detecting these variants from exome data has very low sensitivity.

### Population Genetics

Greene et al. (2024) found that 46 of 9,112 (0.50%) cases coded with neurodevelopmental abnormality in the 100,000 Genomes Project had RNU4-2 variants, making it the most prevalent cause of neurodevelopmental abnormality in that cohort. In the UK Genomic Medicine Service, RNU4-2 accounted for 21 of 5,527 (0.38%) such cases and was second only to MECP2. Chen et al. (2024) estimated that variants in the 18-bp critical region of RNU4-2 explain about 0.4% of all individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders. RNU4-2 variants were much less frequent in cohorts recruited mainly for autism spectrum disorder (3 of 7,149).

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 1-BP INS, 64T [n.64_65insT; NR_003137.2:n.64_65insT]

In the 100,000 Genomes Project, Greene et al. (2024) identified a heterozygous single-base insertion in RNU4-2, n.64_65insT, as a de novo mutation in 33 different families with intellectual disability. They noted that the G at position 64 of U4 snRNA is thought to contribute to the stability of the U6 ACAGAGA loop and that the insertion could disrupt the G64(U4)-A47(U6) interaction supporting the quasi-pseudoknot.

Schot et al. (2024) identified the de novo n.64_65insT mutation in a 5-year-old girl with severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, microcephaly, and seizures.

Chen et al. (2024) identified the n.64_65insT mutation (GRCh38 chr12:120291839:T:TA) in 89 of 115 individuals (77.4%) with neurodevelopmental disorders and variants in the RNU4-2 critical region, including 46 GEL probands with undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders. The variant was absent from GEL probands with other phenotypes, unaffected individuals, gnomAD v4.0, and All of Us, and was seen once in the UK Biobank. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in 8 individuals, and in 3 families de novo occurrence was confirmed by both short- and long-read trio sequencing. In all 46 carriers in whom parental origin could be resolved, the variant was on the maternal allele. RNA sequencing of blood from affected individuals showed increased use of unannotated 5-prime splice sites. ⚠︎

**.0003 RENU SYNDROME**  
RNU4-2, 76C-T [n.76C>T]

Greene et al. (2024) observed the heterozygous n.76C-T variant in RNU4-2 as a de novo mutation in 4 families. In 1 further family, it was inherited from an affected mother. The variant was present once in gnomAD (1 in 152,108 alleles). Greene et al. (2024) suggested that it may cause a less severe phenotype in some cases.

Chen et al. (2024) reported 1 individual in whom the n.76C-T variant was maternally inherited, and showed facial photographs of an individual with this variant at 22 months of age.
