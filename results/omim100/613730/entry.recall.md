### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene (606871) on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a severe autosomal recessive disorder characterized by hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts (Mochida et al. (2010)). Akawi et al. (2013) described a distinctive and catastrophic neonatal presentation, with enlarged ventricles. Affected children have severe neurologic impairment and often die in infancy (Akawi et al. (2013)).

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) described a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates in which several children were affected. They had congenital cataracts, subependymal calcification, and massive intracranial hemorrhage leading to destruction of brain tissue. ⚠︎

Akawi et al. (2013) reported additional affected individuals from 3 families with a similar phenotype. Two of the families were consanguineous. These patients further defined the clinical spectrum of the disorder.

### Inheritance

The disorder showed autosomal recessive inheritance in the consanguineous family reported by Mochida et al. (2010).

### Mapping

Mochida et al. (2010) used homozygosity mapping in the consanguineous family to map the disorder to chromosome 11q25.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of the consanguineous family, Mochida et al. (2010) found a homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene, which lies in the 11q25 candidate interval. JAM3 encodes a tight-junction protein that is present in vascular endothelium. The authors suggested that JAM3 is essential for maintaining the integrity of the cerebrovascular endothelium.

Akawi et al. (2013) found further homozygous JAM3 mutations in additional families with the disorder and carried out cellular studies of the mutant proteins.
