### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts (HDBSCC) is caused by homozygous mutation in the JAM3 gene on chromosome 11q25.

### Description

Hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and cataracts is a rare autosomal recessive disorder in which affected infants are born with congenital cataracts and develop severe intracranial hemorrhage with destruction of brain tissue and subependymal calcification (Mochida et al., 2010; Akawi et al., 2013). Massive intracranial hemorrhage distinguished these patients from cases with an overlapping presentation, such as some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome (Mochida et al., 2010). The course was catastrophic, often lethal in the first weeks of life (Akawi et al., 2013).

### Clinical Features

Mochida et al. (2010) studied a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates in which some members had severe hemorrhagic destruction of the brain, subependymal calcification, and congenital cataracts. The clinical presentation overlapped with some reported cases of pseudo-TORCH syndrome and with cases involving mutations in occludin, but massive intracranial hemorrhage distinguished these patients. Summarizing this original family, Akawi et al. (2013) stated that affected members were born with congenital cataracts and developed progressive hemorrhagic destruction of brain tissue, including the cerebral white matter and basal ganglia; 3 of 8 affected individuals died early in infancy. Brain imaging in all affected showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage with liquefaction and massive cystic degeneration resulting in large ventricles; renal anomalies and liver enlargement were also described in that family.

Akawi et al. (2013) reported affected children from 3 families of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin, 2 of which were consanguineous. In family 1, the parents were Turkish second cousins. Their first child, a preterm girl, had intracranial bleeding and died in the neonatal period. The second child, a girl with a birth weight of 2480 gm, was admitted on day 5 with treatment-resistant tonic seizures; she had a head circumference of 30 cm, sloping forehead, bulging anterior fontanel, bitemporal grooving, and bilateral nuclear cataracts. Brain CT showed subependymal calcification and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Serologic tests for toxoplasmosis, rubella, and cytomegalovirus were negative and platelets were normal. She developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus and died at 2 months of age.

In family 2 of Akawi et al. (2013), the parents were nonconsanguineous and of Afghani origin. The first affected sib, a boy, had bilateral cataracts and feeding problems from birth and presented at 2 weeks with vomiting, irritability, and a high-pitched cry. Brain MRI at 15 days showed grade IV intraventricular hemorrhage, severe ventricular dilatation, white matter abnormalities, and multiple subependymal cysts; renal ultrasound showed horseshoe kidneys. He died at 2 weeks of age, and extensive investigations, including coagulation studies, were normal. His affected brother had bilateral cataracts at birth, deteriorated on day 5 with a drop in hemoglobin, had bilateral epileptiform activity on EEG and extensive bilateral intracerebral hemorrhage, and died on the fifth day of life; he also had pelvicalyceal dilatation of the right kidney.

In family 3 of Akawi et al. (2013), the parents were Moroccan first cousins. A previous son born at 36 weeks had cerebral hemorrhage and hydrocephalus and died at 10 days, a stillborn child had hydrocephalus with necrotic degeneration of cerebral tissue, and the mother's brother died on the seventh day of life. The index patient, a girl, had central and nuclear bilateral cataracts at birth and on day 2 presented with irritability and a bulging anterior fontanel. Imaging showed intraventricular and multifocal intraparenchymal hemorrhages, subependymal calcification, edema, cystic changes of the brain parenchyma, dilated ventricles, and a thin corpus callosum. EEG showed electrical status epilepticus without clinical seizures. She had severe anemia with normal platelets, her head circumference reached 41 cm (+4 SD) on day 18, and she died at 39 days of age.

Akawi et al. (2013) noted that the phenotype of their patients was very similar to that of the original family: all had bilateral cataracts and progressive neurologic impairment, with seizures in some, and neuroimaging showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage with brain destruction and subependymal, basal ganglia, and white matter calcification. Renal anomalies and liver enlargement were more variable. They stated that the disorder was lethal in 7 of 10 cases, with death in the first few weeks of life, and that those who survived were severely retarded with severe spasticity and seizures.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of HDBSCC in the consanguineous family reported by Mochida et al. (2010) was consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Akawi et al. (2013) found homozygous mutations in 3 further families, with heterozygous carrier parents.

### Mapping

By homozygosity mapping in a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates, Mochida et al. (2010) mapped the disease locus to chromosome 11q25 with a maximum multipoint lod score of 6.15.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with HDBSCC, Mochida et al. (2010) identified a homozygous mutation in the canonical splice donor site of intron 5 of the JAM3 gene, found by sequencing genes in the 11q25 candidate interval. RT-PCR analysis of a patient lymphoblast cell line confirmed abnormal splicing, leading to a frameshift with early termination. In a table summarizing this mutation, Akawi et al. (2013) listed it as c.612+1G-T (c.747+1G-T in the earlier nomenclature), resulting in a 19-bp insertion and a frameshift (V205LfsX).

In affected children from 3 families of Turkish, Afghani, and Moroccan origin with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified 3 different homozygous mutations in the JAM3 gene by sequencing its coding exons: C219Y, E116K, and M1R. The mutations segregated with disease, with heterozygous parents and unaffected sibs who were homozygous wildtype or heterozygous; none was reported in dbSNP or the 1000 Genomes Project. In transfected HeLa cells, the C219Y mutant was retained predominantly in the endoplasmic reticulum, whereas the E116K mutant localized to the plasma membrane like wildtype protein; the M1R mutation affects the start codon and was predicted to impair protein synthesis.

### Animal Model

Akawi et al. (2013) reviewed that more than half of mice with homozygous mutation in Jam3 exhibit postnatal lethality and that Jam3 deficiency in mice caused nuclear congenital cataracts, whereas the brains of mice lacking Jam3 are relatively unaffected.

In affected members of a large consanguineous family from the United Arab Emirates with HDBSCC, Mochida et al. RT-PCR analysis of patient lymphoblasts confirmed abnormal splicing resulting in a frameshift with early termination.

Akawi et al. (2013) listed this homozygous splice site mutation as c.612+1G-T (c.747+1G-T based on NM_032801.3), with a 19-bp insertion leading to a frameshift (p.V205Lfs; p.V250LfsX26) and premature termination codon.

**.0002 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, CYS219TYR [c.656G>A; p.C219Y]

In a girl, born of Turkish parents who were second cousins, with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.656G-A transition in exon 6 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a cys219-to-tyr (C219Y) substitution in the Ig-like C2-type domain, disrupting one of the 2 disulfide bonds stabilizing the Ig folds. The parents were heterozygous. In transfected HeLa and COS7 cells, most of the mutant protein was retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and failed to reach the cell membrane.

**.0003 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, GLU116LYS [c.346G>A; p.E116K]

In 2 brothers, born of nonconsanguineous Afghani parents, with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.346G-A transition in exon 4 of the JAM3 gene, resulting in a glu116-to-lys (E116K) substitution in the Ig-like V-type domain. The parents were heterozygous. The mutant protein trafficked normally to the plasma membrane in transfected HeLa cells; the authors suggested that it may interfere with dimerization or with interaction with partner proteins.

**.0004 HEMORRHAGIC DESTRUCTION OF THE BRAIN, SUBEPENDYMAL CALCIFICATION, AND CATARACTS**  
JAM3, MET1ARG [c.2T>G; p.M1R]

In a girl, born of Moroccan first-cousin parents, with HDBSCC, Akawi et al. (2013) identified a homozygous c.2T-G transversion in the JAM3 gene, changing the initiator methionine to arginine (M1R). The mutation was predicted to impair protein synthesis. The parents were heterozygous.
