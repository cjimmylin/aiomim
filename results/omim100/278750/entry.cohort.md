### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that xeroderma pigmentosum, variant type (XPV) is caused by mutation in the gene encoding DNA polymerase eta (hRAD30), the human counterpart of yeast RAD30. ⚠︎

### Description

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant (XP-V) is an inherited disorder with an increased incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers (Masutani et al., 1999). XPV patients show the clinical features of xeroderma pigmentosum, including more skin cancer. However, their cells have a normal or nearly normal rate of nucleotide excision repair and are only slightly more sensitive than normal cells to UV cytotoxicity, although they are significantly more sensitive to the mutagenic effect of UV (Wang et al., 1991). Unlike cells from XP groups A to G, XP-V cells carry out normal nucleotide excision repair but are defective in replicating UV-damaged DNA (Lehmann et al., 1975; Masutani et al., 1999).

### Nomenclature

Cleaver et al. (1980) found that a pigmented xerodermoid cell culture could not be distinguished from the XP variant in UV-induced unscheduled DNA synthesis, strand breakage during repair, chain growth during semiconservative replication with or without caffeine, or recovery of replication. They concluded that the term 'pigmented xerodermoid' was redundant.

### Clinical Features

Itoh et al. (2000) studied 2 independent families that included 2 photosensitive patients identified as having xeroderma pigmentosum variant. One family was Japanese. In the patients' cells, recovery of replicative DNA synthesis after UV irradiation was reduced. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Itoh et al. (1996) found that caffeine enhanced the reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis after UV irradiation in XPV cells. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant is transmitted as an autosomal recessive trait. Johnson et al. (1999) identified nonsense or frameshift mutations in hRAD30 in XP variant cell lines. In a family reported by Itoh et al. (2000), the affected individual inherited 2 heterozygous missense mutations from heterozygous parents. The parents were not affected. ⚠︎

### Heterogeneity

Fujiwara et al. (1981) studied an 8-year-old boy (UV81KO) who had acute recurrent sunburns and no physical or neuromental retardation. His skin showed lowered minimal erythema doses between 280 and 300 nm. His fibroblasts were 5-fold more sensitive than normal cells to killing by 254-nm UV and had a higher baseline level of sister chromatid exchanges (10-12 per cell vs 5 per cell in normal cells). The cells showed no detectable defect in nucleotide excision repair or in replicative bypass. However, they failed to recover DNA synthesis after UV irradiation at doses at which normal cells recovered. Fujiwara et al. (1981) concluded that these features set the patient clearly apart from xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, the 11961 disorder, and Bloom syndrome. They proposed that he had a new photodermatosis with a defect in the recovery of DNA synthesis after UV exposure.

### Molecular Genetics

Using a cell-free trans-lesion synthesis assay, Masutani et al. (1999) isolated a DNA polymerase from HeLa cells that bypassed UV-induced thymine dimers in XP-V cell extracts. They identified it as a human homolog of yeast Rad30 (DNA polymerase eta), a member of the UmuC/DinB/Rev1 family of damage-bypass replication proteins. All XP-V cells they examined carried mutations in the DNA polymerase eta gene. Recombinant human DNA polymerase eta corrected the inability of XP-V cell extracts to replicate DNA past thymine dimers. The authors concluded that DNA polymerase eta could be the XPV gene product.

Johnson et al. (1999) identified nonsense or frameshift mutations in hRAD30, the human counterpart of yeast RAD30, in XP variant cell lines. Of the 8 mutations identified, 7 were predicted to result in a severely truncated hRad30 protein, indicating that defects in hRAD30 cause XP-V.

In a Japanese XPV family, Itoh et al. (2000) confirmed that the patient had inherited 2 heterozygous missense mutations from heterozygous parents. One was an A-to-G transition at nucleotide 1840 resulting in a lys535-to-glu (K535E) substitution. The other was an A-to-C transversion at nucleotide 2003 affecting lys589 (K589). Neither mutation was found in 52 unrelated Japanese individuals. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Lehmann et al. (1975) found that XP variant cells, which have normal excision repair, synthesized DNA abnormally after UV irradiation. Newly synthesized low-molecular-weight DNA took much longer than in normal cells to be converted to high-molecular-weight DNA. Caffeine strongly inhibited this slow conversion in XP variant cells but had no effect in normal cells. Two cell lines from excision-defective classes of xeroderma pigmentosum showed intermediate results: both the time needed to convert newly synthesized DNA to high molecular weight and the caffeine inhibition of this process fell between those of normal and XP variant cells.

Cleaver et al. (1980) proposed that XP variant cells lack a gene product that lets normal cells replicate DNA without interruption at UV-induced pyrimidine dimers. As a result, replication forks were blocked more often and at lower doses. They suggested that the link between this defect and the high rate of actinic carcinogenesis pointed to an important role for disturbed DNA replication in human carcinogenesis.

Wang et al. (1991) transfected an XP variant cell line with a UV-irradiated shuttle vector carrying the supF gene as a mutation target. Mutant frequency rose linearly with dose, with a slope 5 times steeper than in normal cells. Of 53 independent mutants, 52 had base substitutions, and 62 of the 64 substitutions involved a dipyrimidine. In XP variant cells, 28% of the mutations involved A.T base pairs, compared with 11% in normal cells. Most of these were at position 136, the middle of a run of 3 A.T base pairs. Assuming the excision rate is the same in both cell lines, the authors suggested that XP variant cells are less likely than normal cells to incorporate dAMP opposite photoproduct bases. They proposed that this could account for the UV hypermutability of XP variant cells.

Johnson et al. (1999) noted that yeast RAD30 encodes a DNA polymerase involved in error-free bypass of UV damage. They suggested that error-free replication of UV lesions by hRad30 plays an important role in minimizing the incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers.

Using DNA combing, Despras et al. (2010) showed that after UV irradiation, the translesion synthesis defect in XP-V cells led to a strong reduction in active replication forks and a high proportion of stalled forks. Low UVC doses caused extensive single-strand DNA formation and over-activation of the ATR/Chk1 pathway. Low-dose caffeine given after irradiation decreased Chk1 activation and abolished DNA synthesis in XP-V cells. Chk1 inhibition by UCN-01 increased fork stalling and, like caffeine, sensitized XP-V cells to UVC. The authors concluded that in the absence of pol-eta, Chk1 becomes essential for resuming replication through fork stabilization, which explains the caffeine sensitivity of XP-V cells. ⚠︎

### Diagnosis

Itoh et al. (1996) described a simple autoradiographic method for diagnosing XP variant. It measures 3 DNA repair markers in patient fibroblasts and compares them with adjacent normal cells on the same coverslips: unscheduled DNA synthesis (UDS), recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis after UV irradiation. Among the hereditary photosensitive disorders tested (XP, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome), only XPV cells showed normal UDS and normal recovery of RNA synthesis but reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis, at about 50% of control. Because each photosensitive disorder gave a different combination of the 3 markers, the authors proposed that the method also offers a systematic basis for diagnosing these diseases.

Itoh et al. (2000) found that recovery of replicative DNA synthesis after UV in XPV cells was 51% of normal and fell further to 36% in the presence of nontoxic caffeine. Cells from heterozygous parents of XPV patients had normal UDS, normal recovery of RNA synthesis, and normal recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. However, with 1 mM caffeine, their recovery of replicative DNA synthesis was reduced (53% of normal) and their UV survival was slightly reduced. The authors suggested that XPV heterozygotes can be identified by UV sensitivity in the presence of nontoxic caffeine.

### History

Cleaver (1972) described variants of xeroderma pigmentosum that had normal DNA repair and normal sensitivity to ultraviolet light. Robbins et al. (1974) reviewed xeroderma pigmentosum as an inherited disease with sun sensitivity, multiple cutaneous neoplasms, and abnormal DNA repair.
