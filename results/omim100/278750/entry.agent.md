### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that xeroderma pigmentosum, variant type (XPV) is caused by mutation in the gene encoding DNA polymerase eta (POLH, also called hRAD30). In one family, the patient had inherited two different missense mutations, one from each heterozygous parent. ⚠︎

### Description

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant (XPV) is an inherited disorder associated with clinical photosensitivity and an increased incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers (Masutani et al., 1999; Itoh et al., 2000). Unlike cells from patients in the excision-repair-deficient groups of xeroderma pigmentosum (XP-A to XP-G), XPV cells carry out normal nucleotide excision repair but are defective in replication of ultraviolet-damaged DNA, i.e., in postreplication repair (Lehmann et al., 1975; Masutani et al., 1999). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

The 'pigmented xerodermoid' had been defined on the basis of mild clinical symptoms suggesting a condition similar to but distinct from xeroderma pigmentosum. Cleaver et al. (1980) compared XP and pigmented xerodermoid cell cultures after ultraviolet irradiation, measuring unscheduled DNA synthesis, strand breakage during repair, chain growth during semiconservative DNA replication with or without caffeine, and recovery of DNA replication. They concluded that a pigmented xerodermoid cell culture was indistinguishable from the XP variant and that the term pigmented xerodermoid was therefore redundant.

### Clinical Features

Wang et al. (1991) noted that XP variant patients show the clinical characteristics of xeroderma pigmentosum, with increased frequencies of skin cancer, even though their cells have a normal or nearly normal rate of nucleotide excision repair of UV-induced DNA damage.

Itoh et al. (2000) studied 2 independent families that included 2 photosensitive patients identified as having xeroderma pigmentosum variant. The patients showed clinical photosensitivity and ultraviolet-induced skin neoplasias. ⚠︎

### Biochemical Features

Lehmann et al. (1975) found that cells from the class of xeroderma pigmentosum patients whose cells are completely normal in excision repair of UV-induced pyrimidine dimers have an abnormality in DNA synthesis after UV irradiation. The time taken to convert the initially low-molecular-weight DNA synthesized in UV-irradiated cells into high-molecular-weight DNA was much greater in these variants than in normal cells, and this slow conversion was drastically inhibited by caffeine, which had no effect in normal cells. Two cell lines from excision-repair-defective classes of XP showed intermediate effects.

Wang et al. (1991) noted that XP variant cells are only slightly more sensitive than normal cells to the cytotoxic effect of UV radiation but significantly more sensitive to its mutagenic effect. When an XP variant cell line was transfected with a UV-irradiated shuttle vector carrying the supF gene, the frequency of mutants increased linearly with dose, with a slope 5 times steeper than that seen with normal cells. Of 53 independent mutants, 52 contained base substitutions, and 62 of 64 substitutions involved a dipyrimidine. Twenty-eight percent of the mutations involved A:T base pairs, mostly at position 136, compared with only 11% in normal cells. Wang et al. (1991) suggested that XP variant cells are less likely than normal cells to incorporate dAMP opposite bases involved in photoproducts, which could account for their UV hypermutability.

### Inheritance

Johnson et al. (1999) noted that xeroderma pigmentosum is an autosomal recessive disease. Itoh et al. (2000) found that the clinically unaffected parents of an XPV patient were each heterozygous for a different missense mutation inherited by the patient, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Using a cell-free assay for translesion DNA synthesis, Masutani et al. (1999) isolated from HeLa cells a DNA polymerase that continues replication on damaged DNA by bypassing UV-induced thymine dimers in XP-V cell extracts. They showed that this polymerase is a human homolog of the yeast Rad30 protein, DNA polymerase eta. All XP-V cells examined carried mutations in the DNA polymerase eta gene, and recombinant human DNA polymerase eta corrected the inability of XP-V cell extracts to replicate DNA by bypassing thymine dimers.

Johnson et al. (1999) found that XP-V cell lines harbored nonsense or frameshift mutations in hRAD30, the human counterpart of yeast RAD30, which encodes a DNA polymerase involved in error-free bypass of UV damage. Of the 8 mutations identified, 7 would result in a severely truncated hRad30 protein.

In 1 of 2 Japanese families with XPV, Itoh et al. (2000) confirmed inheritance of 2 heterozygous missense mutations in the POLH gene: a 1840A-G transition resulting in a lys535-to-glu (K535E) substitution, and a 2003A-C transversion resulting in a missense change at lys589. Neither mutation was found in 52 unrelated Japanese individuals. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Cleaver et al. (1980) concluded that the defect in XP variant cells appears to be the loss of a gene product that permits normal cells to replicate DNA without interruption at UV-induced pyrimidine dimers, so that replication forks are blocked more frequently and at lower doses in XP variant cells. They suggested that the correlation between this defect and the high levels of actinic carcinogenesis in these patients pointed to an important role for perturbations in DNA replication in human carcinogenesis.

Using DNA combing, Despras et al. (2010) showed that the translesion synthesis defect in XP-V cells leads to a strong reduction in the number of active replication forks and a high proportion of stalled forks. Extensive regions of single-strand DNA formed during replication in irradiated XP-V cells, leading to overactivation of the ATR/Chk1 pathway after low UVC doses. A low concentration of caffeine added after irradiation significantly decreased Chk1 activation and abrogated DNA synthesis in XP-V cells, and inhibition of Chk1 by UCN-01 aggravated the replication defect by increasing fork stalling and sensitized XP-V cells to UVC as caffeine does. They concluded that pol-eta acts at stalled forks to resume their progression, and that in its absence Chk1 kinase becomes essential for replication resumption via fork stabilization.

### Diagnosis

Itoh et al. (1996) described a simple autoradiographic method for diagnosing XPV that measures 3 cellular markers after UV irradiation in patient fibroblasts compared with adjacent normal cells: unscheduled DNA synthesis, recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. Among hereditary photosensitive disorders, including xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome, only XPV cells showed normal unscheduled DNA synthesis and normal recovery of RNA synthesis but reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis (approximately 50% of control), a reduction enhanced in the presence of caffeine.

Itoh et al. (2000) reported that recovery of replicative DNA synthesis in XPV cells was 51 +/- 6% of normal, reduced to 36 +/- 5% in the presence of a nontoxic level of caffeine. Cells from heterozygous parents in 2 families showed normal unscheduled DNA synthesis, recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis, but reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis in the presence of 1 mM caffeine (53 +/- 8% of normal control). In colony-forming assays, heterozygous cells showed normal UV survival without caffeine but slightly reduced survival with 1 mM caffeine. The authors suggested that XPV heterozygotes can be identified by their sensitivity to UV irradiation in the presence of nontoxic levels of caffeine.

In the differential diagnosis, Fujiwara et al. (1981) studied an 8-year-old boy (UV81KO) with only acute recurrent sunburns and lowered minimal erythema doses, whose fibroblasts were 5-fold more sensitive than normal to 254-nm UV killing and showed defective recovery of DNA synthesis after UV irradiation, but no detectable defect in nucleotide excision repair or in replicative bypass (molecular-weight increase of pulse-chased DNA). Baseline sister chromatid exchanges were elevated (10-12 SCEs/cell vs 5 in normal cells). On the basis of clinical, cellular, and molecular characteristics, the authors distinguished this disorder from xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, and Bloom syndrome and proposed it as a new photodermatosis.
