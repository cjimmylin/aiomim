### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the variant type of xeroderma pigmentosum (XPV) is caused by mutation in the gene encoding DNA polymerase eta (POLH), the human homolog of yeast RAD30.

### Description

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant (XP-V) is an inherited disorder associated with an increased incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers (Masutani et al., 1999). Patients show the clinical features of xeroderma pigmentosum, which Robbins et al. (1974) described as an inherited disease with sun sensitivity, multiple cutaneous neoplasms, and abnormal DNA repair. Unlike cells from XP groups A to G, XP-V cells carry out normal or nearly normal nucleotide excision repair of UV-induced DNA damage but are defective in replicating UV-damaged DNA (Masutani et al., 1999; Wang et al., 1991). XP-V cells are only slightly more sensitive than normal cells to the cytotoxic effect of UV radiation, but they are significantly more sensitive to its mutagenic effect (Wang et al., 1991).

### Nomenclature

Cleaver et al. (1980) found that, in studies of DNA repair and replication after UV irradiation, a pigmented xerodermoid cell culture could not be told apart from the XP variant. They concluded that the term 'pigmented xerodermoid', which had been defined by mild clinical symptoms, was therefore redundant.

### Biochemical Features

Cleaver (1972) described variants of xeroderma pigmentosum that had normal DNA repair and normal sensitivity to ultraviolet light.

Lehmann et al. (1975) found that cells from the class of xeroderma pigmentosum patients whose excision repair of UV-induced pyrimidine dimers was completely normal (XP variants) had an abnormality in DNA synthesis after UV irradiation. These variant cells took much longer than normal cells to convert the low-molecular-weight DNA first made after UV irradiation into high-molecular-weight DNA. Caffeine strongly inhibited this slow conversion in UV-irradiated XP variant cells but had no effect in normal cells. Two excision repair-deficient XP cell lines showed intermediate effects, both in the time needed for the conversion and in the degree to which caffeine inhibited it.

Itoh et al. (2000) studied 2 independent Japanese families with 2 photosensitive patients identified as having XPV. Cells from the heterozygous parents showed normal unscheduled DNA synthesis, normal recovery of RNA synthesis, and normal recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. However, recovery of replicative DNA synthesis was reduced in the presence of 1 mM caffeine (53 +/- 8% of normal). In a colony-forming assay, UV survival of heterozygous cells was normal without caffeine but slightly reduced with caffeine. The authors suggested that XPV heterozygotes can be identified by UV sensitivity in the presence of nontoxic caffeine. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Itoh et al. (2000) confirmed that a patient with XPV had inherited 2 heterozygous missense mutations, and they identified heterozygous parents of XPV patients by a cellular phenotype.

### Heterogeneity

Fujiwara et al. (1981) described an 8-year-old boy (patient UV81KO) whose only symptom was acute recurrent sunburns, with no other physical or neuromental retardation. His skin had lowered minimal erythema doses at monochromatic wavelengths between 280 and 300 nm, without delayed peaking of erythema. His cultured skin fibroblasts were 5-fold more sensitive than normal cells to killing by 254-nm UV. They were also more sensitive to sunlamp UV-B, 4-nitroquinoline-1-oxide, and N-hydroxy-acetylaminofluorene, but not to mitomycin C or alkylating agents. Cells from obligate heterozygotes responded normally. The authors detected no defect in nucleotide excision repair or replicative bypass. Nevertheless, the patient's cells failed to recover DNA synthesis after 254-nm UV doses of 1 and 5 J/m2, doses at which normal cells recovered. Baseline sister chromatid exchanges were raised (10 to 12 per cell vs 5 per cell in normal cells), but the rate of UV-induced exchanges was normal. After comparing these features with xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, the 11961 disorder, and Bloom syndrome, Fujiwara et al. (1981) concluded that the UV81KO disorder was distinct from all of them. They proposed it as a new photodermatosis with a defect in the recovery of DNA synthesis after UV exposure.

### Molecular Genetics

Masutani et al. (1999) had previously used an improved cell-free assay to isolate a DNA polymerase from HeLa cells that, in XP-V cell extracts, continued replication on damaged DNA by bypassing UV-induced thymine dimers. They showed that this polymerase is a human homolog of yeast Rad30 (DNA polymerase eta), a member of a family of damage-bypass replication proteins that also includes E. coli UmuC and DinB and yeast Rev1. They found mutations in the DNA polymerase eta gene (POLH) in all XP-V cells they examined. Recombinant human DNA polymerase eta corrected the inability of XP-V cell extracts to replicate damaged DNA by bypassing thymine dimers. The authors concluded that DNA polymerase eta could be the XPV gene product.

Johnson et al. (1999) found nonsense or frameshift mutations in XP-V cell lines in hRAD30, the human counterpart of the yeast RAD30 gene. Of the 8 mutations they identified, 7 were predicted to result in a severely truncated hRad30 protein. The authors concluded that defects in hRAD30 cause XP-V.

In a Japanese family with XPV, Itoh et al. (2000) confirmed that the patient had inherited 2 heterozygous missense mutations. The first was an A-to-G transition at nucleotide 1840 resulting in a lys535-to-glu (K535E) substitution. The second was an A-to-C transversion at nucleotide 2003 that affected lys589. Neither mutation was found in 52 unrelated Japanese individuals. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Cleaver et al. (1980) irradiated XP variant and pigmented xerodermoid cells with UV light. They measured unscheduled DNA synthesis, strand breakage during repair, chain growth during semiconservative replication with or without caffeine, and recovery of DNA replication. The authors concluded that the common defect appeared to be loss of a gene product that allows normal cells to replicate DNA without interruption at UV-induced pyrimidine dimers. As a result, replication forks in XP variant cells were blocked more often and at lower doses. The authors suggested that this defect correlated with the high levels of actinic carcinogenesis seen in these patients. ⚠︎

Wang et al. (1991) transfected an XP variant cell line with a supF shuttle vector plasmid that had been irradiated with UV at 254 nm. Mutant frequency rose linearly with dose, with a slope 5 times steeper than previously seen in normal cells. In 52 of 53 independent mutants the change was a base substitution, and 62 of 64 substitutions involved a dipyrimidine. A.T base pairs were involved in 28% of mutations in XP variant cells, compared with 11% in normal cells, mostly at position 136. The authors suggested that XP variant cells are less likely than normal cells to incorporate dAMP opposite photoproducts, which could account for their UV hypermutability.

Johnson et al. (1999) noted that yeast RAD30 encodes a DNA polymerase involved in error-free bypass of UV damage. They suggested that error-free replication of UV lesions by hRad30 plays an important role in minimizing the incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers.

Using DNA combing, Despras et al. (2010) showed that in UV-irradiated XP-V cells, which lack DNA polymerase eta, the translesion synthesis defect caused a strong reduction in the number of active replication forks and a high proportion of stalled forks, in contrast to budding yeast. Extensive regions of single-strand DNA formed during replication, and the ATR/Chk1 pathway was overactivated after low UVC doses. Adding a low concentration of caffeine after irradiation lowered Chk1 activation and abolished DNA synthesis in XP-V cells. The Chk1 inhibitor UCN-01 worsened the replication defect in XP-V cells by increasing fork stalling, and like caffeine it made XP-V cells more sensitive to UVC. The authors concluded that pol eta acts at stalled forks to restart them. They further concluded that in the absence of pol eta, Chk1 becomes essential for replication to resume by alternative pathways through fork stabilization.

### Diagnosis

Itoh et al. (1996) described a simple autoradiographic method for diagnosing xeroderma pigmentosum variant. The method measures 3 cellular DNA repair markers after UV irradiation: unscheduled DNA synthesis (UDS), recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. Patient fibroblasts were plated on coverslips alongside normal control cells, and grain counts on nuclei were compared. Among the hereditary photosensitive disorders (XP, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome), only XP variant cells showed normal UDS and normal recovery of RNA synthesis with reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis (about 50% of control). Caffeine made this reduction greater. Because each of these disorders produces a different combination of the 3 markers, the method also provides a systematic basis for diagnosing them. Before this method, XP variant had been diagnosed by a postreplication repair assay together with clinical features.

Itoh et al. (2000) noted that among the hereditary photosensitive disorders, including other XP groups, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome, only XPV cells showed normal unscheduled DNA synthesis and normal recovery of RNA synthesis with reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis after UV irradiation (51 +/- 6% of normal). This reduction was enhanced to 36 +/- 5% in the presence of a nontoxic level of caffeine. The authors also proposed that heterozygous carriers could be identified by reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis and slightly reduced UV survival in the presence of caffeine. ⚠︎
