### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that xeroderma pigmentosum, variant type (XPV) is caused by mutation in the gene encoding DNA polymerase eta (POLH).

### Description

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant (XP-V) is an inherited disorder with the clinical features of xeroderma pigmentosum (XP), including an increased incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers (Wang et al., 1991; Masutani et al., 1999). Unlike cells from XP groups A to G, XP-V cells carry out normal or nearly normal nucleotide excision repair of UV-induced DNA damage but are defective in replicating UV-damaged DNA (Masutani et al., 1999). XP-V cells are only slightly more sensitive than normal cells to the cytotoxic effect of UV radiation but are significantly more sensitive to its mutagenic effect (Wang et al., 1991). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Cleaver et al. (1980) found that, in UV-irradiation studies of DNA repair and replication, a pigmented xerodermoid cell culture could not be told apart from the XP variant. They concluded that the term 'pigmented xerodermoid', which had been defined by mild clinical symptoms, was therefore redundant.

### Biochemical Features

Cleaver (1972) described variants of xeroderma pigmentosum that had normal DNA repair and normal sensitivity to ultraviolet light.

Lehmann et al. (1975) found that cells from XP patients whose excision repair of UV-induced pyrimidine dimers was completely normal (XP variants) had an abnormality in DNA synthesis after UV irradiation: the variant cells took much longer than normal cells to convert the low-molecular-weight DNA first made after UV irradiation into high-molecular-weight DNA. Caffeine strongly inhibited this slow conversion in UV-irradiated XP variant cells but had no effect in normal cells. Two excision repair-deficient XP cell lines showed intermediate effects, both in the time needed for the conversion and in the degree of caffeine inhibition.

Itoh et al. (2000) studied 2 independent Japanese families with 2 photosensitive patients identified as having XPV. Cells from the heterozygous parents showed normal unscheduled DNA synthesis, normal recovery of RNA synthesis, and normal recovery of replicative DNA synthesis, but reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis in the presence of 1 mM caffeine (53 +/- 8% of normal). In a colony-forming assay, heterozygote cells had normal UV survival without caffeine but slightly reduced survival with caffeine. The authors suggested that XPV heterozygotes can be identified by UV sensitivity in the presence of nontoxic caffeine. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Johnson et al. (1999) concluded that defects in hRAD30 (POLH) cause XP-V, and Itoh et al. (2000) confirmed inheritance of 2 different missense mutations from the parents of an XPV patient and found that cells from heterozygous parents had only subtle abnormalities, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Masutani et al. (1999) had used an improved cell-free assay to isolate a DNA polymerase from HeLa cells that, in XP-V cell extracts, continued replication on damaged DNA by bypassing UV-induced thymine dimers. They showed that this polymerase is a human homolog of yeast Rad30 (DNA polymerase eta), a member of a family of damage-bypass replication proteins that also includes E. coli UmuC and DinB and yeast Rev1. Masutani et al. (1999) found mutations in the DNA polymerase eta gene (POLH) in all XP-V cells they examined. Recombinant human DNA polymerase eta corrected the inability of XP-V cell extracts to replicate damaged DNA by bypassing thymine dimers. The authors concluded that DNA polymerase eta could be the XPV gene product.

Johnson et al. (1999) found nonsense or frameshift mutations in hRAD30 (POLH), the human counterpart of the yeast RAD30 gene, in XP-V cell lines. Of the 8 mutations identified, 7 were predicted to result in a severely truncated hRad30 protein. The authors concluded that defects in hRAD30 cause XP-V.

In 1 Japanese family with XPV, Itoh et al. (2000) confirmed inheritance of 2 heterozygous missense mutations: a 1840A-G transition resulting in a lys535-to-glu (K535E) substitution, and a 2003A-C transversion affecting lys589. Neither mutation was found in 52 unrelated Japanese individuals. ⚠︎

### Pathogenesis

Cleaver et al. (1980) irradiated XP variant and pigmented xerodermoid cells with UV light and measured unscheduled DNA synthesis, strand breakage during repair, chain growth during semiconservative replication with or without caffeine, and recovery of DNA replication. They concluded that the common defect appeared to be loss of a gene product that allows normal cells to replicate DNA without interruption at UV-induced pyrimidine dimers, so that replication forks were blocked more often and at lower doses in XP variant cells. They suggested that this defect correlated with the high levels of actinic carcinogenesis in these patients.

Wang et al. (1991) transfected an XP variant cell line with a supF shuttle vector plasmid irradiated with UV at 254 nm. Mutant frequency rose linearly with dose, with a slope 5 times steeper than previously seen in normal cells. In 52 of 53 independent mutants the change was a base substitution, and 62 of 64 substitutions involved a dipyrimidine. A.T base pairs were involved in 28% of mutations in XP variant cells versus 11% in normal cells, mostly at position 136. The authors suggested that XP variant cells are less likely than normal cells to incorporate dAMP opposite photoproducts, which could account for their UV hypermutability.

Johnson et al. (1999) noted that yeast RAD30 encodes a DNA polymerase involved in error-free bypass of UV damage, and suggested that error-free replication of UV lesions by hRad30 plays an important role in minimizing the incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers.

Using DNA combing, Despras et al. (2010) showed that the translesion synthesis defect in UV-irradiated XP-V cells, which lack DNA polymerase eta, caused a strong reduction in the number of active replication forks and a high proportion of stalled forks, in contrast to budding yeast. Extensive regions of single-strand DNA formed during replication, and the ATR/Chk1 pathway was overactivated after low UVC doses. Adding a low concentration of caffeine after irradiation lowered Chk1 activation and abolished DNA synthesis in XP-V cells. The Chk1 inhibitor UCN-01 worsened the replication defect in XP-V cells by increasing fork stalling and, like caffeine, made XP-V cells more sensitive to UVC. The authors concluded that pol eta acts at stalled forks to restart them, and that in its absence Chk1 becomes essential for replication to resume by alternative pathways through fork stabilization.

### Diagnosis

Itoh et al. (1996) described a simple autoradiographic method for diagnosing XP variant that measures 3 cellular markers after UV irradiation: unscheduled DNA synthesis (UDS), recovery of RNA synthesis, and recovery of replicative DNA synthesis. Patient fibroblasts were plated on coverslips alongside normal control cells, and grain counts on nuclei were compared. Among hereditary photosensitive disorders (XP, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome), only XP variant cells showed normal UDS and normal recovery of RNA synthesis but reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis (about 50% of control), and caffeine made this reduction greater. Because each of these disorders produces a different combination of the 3 markers, the method also gives a systematic basis for their diagnosis. Before this method, XP variant had been diagnosed using a postreplication repair assay together with clinical features.

Itoh et al. (2000) noted that, among hereditary photosensitive disorders including other XP groups, Cockayne syndrome, and UV-sensitive syndrome, only XPV cells showed normal unscheduled DNA synthesis and normal recovery of RNA synthesis but reduced recovery of replicative DNA synthesis after UV irradiation (51 +/- 6% of normal), a reduction enhanced to 36 +/- 5% in the presence of a nontoxic level of caffeine.
