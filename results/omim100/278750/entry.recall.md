### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the variant type of xeroderma pigmentosum (XPV) is caused by mutation in the gene encoding DNA polymerase eta (POLH; 603968) on chromosome 6p21.1.

### Description

Xeroderma pigmentosum variant type (XPV) is a disorder of clinical photosensitivity (Itoh et al., 2000) with high levels of actinic carcinogenesis (Cleaver et al., 1980). Most xeroderma pigmentosum cells are defective in excision repair, but XPV cells have normal excision repair (Lehmann et al., 1975). They are instead defective in the replication of ultraviolet-damaged DNA (Masutani et al., 1999). DNA polymerase eta, which performs translesion synthesis past UV photoproducts, is deficient in XPV (Despras et al., 2010).

### Clinical Features

Cleaver et al. (1980) noted that the 'pigmented xerodermoid' had previously been defined on the basis of mild clinical symptoms. These symptoms suggested that it might be similar to, but distinct from, xeroderma pigmentosum. Cleaver et al. (1980) equated the pigmented xerodermoid with the XP variant and noted high levels of actinic carcinogenesis in these patients.

Itoh et al. (2000) noted that patients with xeroderma pigmentosum variant show clinical photosensitivity.

### Biochemical Features

Cleaver (1972) described variants of xeroderma pigmentosum with normal DNA repair, which distinguished them from the repair-deficient forms of XP. ⚠︎

Lehmann et al. (1975) found that xeroderma pigmentosum cells with normal levels of excision repair had a defect in DNA synthesis after UV irradiation. These variant cells took much longer than normal cells to convert low-molecular-weight DNA synthesized after UV irradiation into high-molecular-weight DNA. Caffeine drastically inhibited this slow conversion.

### Inheritance

Johnson et al. (1999) described xeroderma pigmentosum as an autosomal recessive disease. They did not make this statement specifically about the variant form.

### Molecular Genetics

Masutani et al. (1999) isolated a DNA polymerase from HeLa cells that continued replication on damaged DNA in XPV cell extracts by bypassing UV-induced thymine dimers. They showed that this polymerase was a human homolog of the yeast Rad30 protein, DNA polymerase eta. All XPV cells they examined carried mutations in the DNA polymerase eta gene.

Johnson et al. (1999) independently showed that XPV cell lines carried nonsense or frameshift mutations in hRAD30 (POLH), the human counterpart of yeast RAD30.

### Pathogenesis

Johnson et al. (1999) noted that yeast RAD30 encodes a DNA polymerase involved in the error-free bypass of UV damage. They suggested that error-free replication of UV lesions by hRad30 plays an important role in minimizing the incidence of sunlight-induced skin cancers.

Despras et al. (2010) reported that, in the absence of pol eta, replication resumed through alternative pathways. ⚠︎
