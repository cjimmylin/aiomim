### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nanophthalmos-4 (NNO4) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TMEM98 gene (615949) on chromosome 17q11.2.

### Description

Nanophthalmos-4 is an autosomal dominant eye disorder in which the eyes are small, with a short axial length, and there is high hyperopia without other physical anomalies (Yu et al., 2009; Awadalla et al., 2014; Khorram et al., 2015). Angle-closure glaucoma has been reported in older affected individuals (Yu et al., 2009).

### Clinical Features

Yu et al. (2009) described a 5-generation Chinese family with isolated (simple) microphthalmia inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. The 9 affected members were aged 6 to 66 years. All had bilateral hyperopia ranging from +4.50 to +11.50 diopters (mean +8.00) and short axial length of 18.22 to 20.26 mm (mean 19.29 mm). Best corrected visual acuity was less than 0.5 in most eyes, and no family member had any other physical anomaly. Three patients, all older than 50 years, had angle-closure glaucoma or occludable anterior chamber angles. The glaucoma was treated by trabeculectomy but kept progressing, and 2 patients eventually developed atrophia bulbi despite repeated surgeries. The authors also noted learning disabilities in one-third of the cases. On histologic examination of sclera from affected members, the collagen fibers were diffusely swollen, twisted or frayed, and mixed with normal fibers. In the 2 atrophied globes, collagen alignment was disrupted, the sclera contained coil-like amorphous material and was 3 times normal thickness, and the cornea was also thickened. ⚠︎

Hu et al. (2011) studied a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia.

Awadalla et al. (2014) studied a large white family (NNO-SA1), recruited in South Australia, with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. Nanophthalmos was defined as an axial length less than 20.0 mm and/or a refractive error greater than +7.00. Of 35 available family members, 16 (46%) had nanophthalmos, with a mean refraction of +11.8 D and a mean axial length of 17.6 mm.

Khorram et al. (2015) studied a Caucasian family of German heritage living in the United States (pedigree 991-F) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos spanning at least 4 generations. Six of 10 examined members had nanophthalmos, and 3 more were affected by family history report. Affected members had a mean axial eye length of 17.1 +/- 0.31 mm and high hyperopia, with spherical equivalent refractions of +13.0 +/- 0.89 diopters, compared with +0.71 +/- 0.81 diopters in unaffected members.

Khorram et al. (2015) also described a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage from the Mariana Islands (pedigree 951-N) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. Eight of 10 examined members were affected, and 3 more were affected by family history report. Affected members had a mean axial eye length of 17.4 +/- 0.94 mm, compared with 22.7 +/- 0.38 mm in unaffected members, and hyperopia of +3.93 +/- 1.57 diopters. Axial lengths across the 2 families ranged from 15.9 to 18.4 mm. ⚠︎

### Inheritance

Autosomal dominant inheritance of nanophthalmos/simple microphthalmia was observed in the families reported by Yu et al. (2009), Hu et al. (2011), Awadalla et al. (2014), and Khorram et al. (2015).

### Mapping

In the Chinese family with autosomal dominant microphthalmia that they studied, Yu et al. (2009) found a normal karyotype in all affected members. Two-point linkage analysis with 23 microsatellite markers produced lod scores below 1 for every marker, excluding linkage to CHX10, PAX6, MFRP and to the loci on 14q32, 11p, and 2q11-q14. The authors predicted that a novel locus was responsible and concluded that the exclusion of all known loci suggested high genetic heterogeneity in microphthalmia.

In a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia, Hu et al. (2011) excluded previously reported microphthalmia loci and performed a genomewide scan with 382 microsatellite markers followed by fine mapping. The highest linkage was found at D17S1824 (maximum lod score of 4.97 at theta = 0.00). Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 21.57-cM interval between D17S900 and D17S1872 on chromosome 17p12-q12.

In family NNO-SA1, Awadalla et al. (2014) performed a genomewide linkage scan with a 10K SNP array and found significant linkage on chromosome 17p12-q12 between SNP markers rs2323659 and rs967293, with a maximum location score of 4.1.

Khorram et al. (2015) carried out linkage analysis with 6,020 SNPs. In pedigree 991-F, a 20.2-Mb segment of chromosome 17p12-q12 between SNPs rs530664 and rs16523 cosegregated with nanophthalmos; in pedigree 951-N, an overlapping 19.7-Mb region between rs917541 and rs16523 cosegregated with the disease. The authors noted that this region overlapped the locus previously linked to nanophthalmos in a Chinese pedigree.

### Molecular Genetics

In the Chinese family with microphthalmia mapping to 17p12-q12, Hu et al. (2011) screened 14 candidate genes in the linked region, including CRYBA1 and NCOR1, by PCR and direct sequencing, and used genomewide SNP genotyping to look for copy number variation. They found no mutation or CNV that accounted for the microphthalmia in the family. ⚠︎

In affected members of the white family (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos linked to 17p12-q12, Awadalla et al. (2014) used exome sequencing to identify a heterozygous missense mutation (c.577G-C; A193P) in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene. It was the only novel segregating variant within the linked region and was absent from the Exome Variant Server and from 285 local white controls. Using RT-PCR, Awadalla et al. (2014) found that TMEM98 was expressed in all ocular tissues tested, including the sclera and the optic nerve head. The authors concluded that TMEM98 most likely causes the disease in this family. ⚠︎

In the US family (pedigree 991-F) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Khorram et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing of an affected member and found 4 nonsynonymous variants in the linked chromosome 17 region. Only a heterozygous missense mutation in TMEM98 (H196P) cosegregated with nanophthalmos and was predicted to be pathogenic by SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62. In the Mariana Islands family (pedigree 951-N), Khorram et al. (2015) used Sanger sequencing to identify a heterozygous 34-bp deletion in TMEM98 in all 11 affected members tested; the deletion was absent from healthy family members. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, ALA193PRO [c.577G>C; p.A193P]

In affected members of a large white family from South Australia (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Awadalla et al. (2014) identified a heterozygous c.577G-C transversion in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene, resulting in an ala193-to-pro (A193P) substitution. The mutation, found by exome sequencing, was the only novel segregating variant within the linked 17p12-q12 region and was absent from the Exome Variant Server and from 285 local white controls. ⚠︎

**.0002 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, HIS196PRO [p.H196P]

In a Caucasian family of German heritage living in the United States (pedigree 991-F) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Khorram et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in the TMEM98 gene by exome sequencing. Of 4 nonsynonymous variants in the linked chromosome 17 region, only this mutation cosegregated with nanophthalmos and was predicted to be pathogenic by SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62. The mutation affects a highly conserved histidine and was absent from the Exome Variant Server and 1000 Genomes Project databases. ⚠︎

**.0003 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, 34-BP DEL, NT694 [c.694_721delagaatgaagactggatcgaagatgcctcgtaagg]

In all 11 affected members tested of a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage from the Mariana Islands (pedigree 951-N) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Khorram et al. (2015) identified by Sanger sequencing a heterozygous 34-bp deletion in the TMEM98 gene (c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg). The deletion spans the last 28 bp of exon 4 and the first 6 bp of intron 4, eliminates a splice site, and results in premature termination of translation. It was absent from healthy family members and from public databases.
