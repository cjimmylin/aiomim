### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that nanophthalmos-4 (NNO4) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the TMEM98 gene (615949) on chromosome 17.

### Description

Nanophthalmos is a congenital disorder in which the eye is small but otherwise structurally normal. Its main complications are severe hyperopia and angle-closure glaucoma (Awadalla et al., 2014). Khorram et al. (2015) described the clinical features as a short axial eye length (less than 20 mm), high hyperopia, and small corneal diameter. They also described thick, impermeable sclera, which brings an increased risk of complications after intraocular surgery. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Yu et al. (2009) studied a 5-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant simple microphthalmia. All 34 family members were examined, and 9 were affected, aged 6 to 66 years (mean, 42.8 years). Both eyes were affected from birth, and the family members had no other physical anomalies. Hyperopia ranged from +4.50 to +11.50 diopters (mean, +8.00 diopters). Axial length ranged from 18.22 mm to 20.26 mm (mean, 19.29 mm), compared with a normal length of 23 to 24 mm. Best corrected visual acuity was less than 0.5 in most eyes. Three patients, all older than 50 years, had angle-closure glaucoma or occludable anterior chamber angles and were treated by trabeculectomy, but the condition continued to progress. Two of them (III10 and III15) developed atrophia bulbi despite repeated surgery. Histologic examination of the sclera showed diffusely swollen collagen fibers that were twisted or frayed and mixed with normal fibers. In the 2 atrophied globes, the sclera was 3 times as thick as normal sclera and contained coil-like amorphous material, and the corneas were also thickened. Karyotypes of the 9 affected members were normal. In the discussion, Yu et al. (2009) noted learning disabilities in one-third of the cases.

Awadalla et al. (2014) studied a large white family (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, recruited through ophthalmology clinics in Adelaide, South Australia. Nanophthalmos was defined as an axial length of less than 20.0 mm and/or a refractive error greater than +7.00. Of the 35 available family members, 16 (46%) had nanophthalmos, with a mean refraction of +11.8 D and a mean axial length of 17.6 mm.

Khorram et al. (2015) studied 2 large pedigrees with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos with high penetrance over at least 4 generations. One was a Caucasian family of German heritage living in the United States (pedigree 991-F), and the other was a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage living in the Mariana Islands (pedigree 951-N). In pedigree 991-F, 6 of 10 examined members had nanophthalmos, and 3 more were affected according to family history. Their mean axial length was 17.1 +/- 0.31 mm, and spherical equivalent refraction was +13.0 +/- 0.89 diopters. In pedigree 951-N, 8 of 10 examined members had nanophthalmos, and 3 more were affected according to family history. Affected members had a mean axial length of 17.4 +/- 0.94 mm, compared with 22.7 +/- 0.38 mm in unaffected members, and a spherical equivalent refraction of +3.93 +/- 1.57 diopters. Across the patients with nanophthalmos, axial lengths ranged from 15.9 to 18.4 mm.

### Inheritance

Yu et al. (2009) and Awadalla et al. (2014) reported autosomal dominant inheritance of nanophthalmos (simple microphthalmia) in their families. Khorram et al. (2015) observed autosomal dominant transmission with high penetrance in 2 further pedigrees.

### Mapping

Hu et al. (2011) carried out a whole-genome scan with 382 microsatellite markers in a Chinese family with autosomal dominant congenital simple microphthalmia, after excluding the candidates previously linked to microphthalmia. The highest LOD score was 4.97 at D17S1824 (theta = 0.00). Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 21.57-cM interval between D17S900 and D17S1872 on chromosome 17p12-q12. Sequencing of 14 candidate genes, including CRYBA1 and NCOR1, and genomewide SNP analysis for copy number variation found no causative mutation or CNV.

In the large white family NNO-SA1 with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Awadalla et al. (2014) found significant linkage to chromosome 17 between SNPs rs2323659 and rs967293, with a maximum location score of 4.1.

Khorram et al. (2015) used SNP-based linkage analysis in 2 families. In pedigree 991-F, a 20.2-Mb segment of chromosome 17p12-q12 between rs530664 and rs16523 was inherited with nanophthalmos in all members of the family. In pedigree 951-N, an overlapping 19.7-Mb segment between rs917541 and rs16523 was likewise inherited with the disease.

### Heterogeneity

In a 5-generation Chinese family with autosomal dominant simple microphthalmia, Yu et al. (2009) excluded linkage to the reported candidate genes CHX10, PAX6, and MFRP and to loci on chromosomes 14q32, 11p, and 2q11-q14. All 23 markers gave LOD scores of less than 1. The authors predicted that a novel locus was responsible in this family.

### Molecular Genetics

In affected members of a large white family (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos linked to chromosome 17, Awadalla et al. (2014) used whole-exome sequencing to identify a missense mutation in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene (c.577G-C; ala193-to-pro, A193P) that segregated with the disease. The variant was not found in the Exome Variant Server database or in 285 local white controls. TMEM98 was expressed in all ocular tissues tested, including the sclera and optic nerve head. Awadalla et al. (2014) concluded that TMEM98 was most likely the cause of the disease in this family.

Khorram et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing on 1 affected member of pedigree 991-F, a Caucasian family from the United States with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos. Within the chromosome 17 linkage region, they found nonsynonymous variants in 4 genes. Only a his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in the TMEM98 gene was inherited with nanophthalmos, absent from healthy family members, and predicted to be pathogenic by SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62. Sanger sequencing confirmed that all other affected members of the pedigree carried the mutation. Khorram et al. (2015) then sequenced TMEM98 in 11 affected and 4 healthy members of pedigree 951-N from the Mariana Islands. All affected members carried a heterozygous 34-bp deletion (c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg) covering the last 28 bp of exon 4 and the first 6 bp of intron 4. The authors stated that the deletion removes a splice site and leads to premature termination of translation. Neither mutation was found in the Exome Variant Server or 1000 Genomes Project databases. ⚠︎

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, ALA193PRO [c.577G>C; p.Ala193Pro]

In affected members of a large white family (NNO-SA1) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Awadalla et al. (2014) identified a c.577G-C transversion in exon 8 of the TMEM98 gene, resulting in an ala193-to-pro (A193P) substitution. The mutation was found by exome sequencing within the chromosome 17 linkage region and segregated with the disease. It was not present in the Exome Variant Server database or in 285 local white controls.

**.0002 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, HIS196PRO [p.His196Pro]

In affected members of a Caucasian family of German heritage living in the United States (pedigree 991-F) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Khorram et al. (2015) identified a his196-to-pro (H196P) mutation in the TMEM98 gene. The mutation was found by exome sequencing and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. It was inherited with the disease, absent from healthy family members, and absent from the Exome Variant Server and 1000 Genomes Project databases. It affects a histidine that is highly conserved across species, and SIFT, PolyPhen, and BLOSUM62 all predicted it to be pathogenic.

**.0003 NANOPHTHALMOS 4**  
TMEM98, 34-BP DEL [c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg]

In all affected members of a Pacific Islander family of Micronesian heritage from the Mariana Islands (pedigree 951-N) with autosomal dominant nanophthalmos, Khorram et al. (2015) identified a heterozygous 34-bp deletion in the TMEM98 gene (c.694_721delAGAATGAAGACTGGATCGAAGATGCCTCgtaagg). The deletion covers the last 28 bp of exon 4 and the first 6 bp of intron 4. The authors stated that it removes a splice site and leads to premature termination of translation. The deletion was absent from healthy family members and from public sequence databases.
