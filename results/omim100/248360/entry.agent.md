### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MLYCD, also called MCD). ⚠︎

### Description

Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism. Wightman et al. (2003) characterized it by malonic aciduria, developmental delay, seizure disorder, hypoglycemia, and cardiomyopathy. Gao et al. (1999) listed mild mental retardation, seizures, hypotonia, cardiomyopathy, vomiting, hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and malonic aciduria as associated features. ⚠︎

### Clinical Features

Brown et al. (1984) described a child with repeated episodes of vomiting, some of which required hospitalization. They reported this patient as having a deficiency of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase, which they called a mitochondrial enzyme and an inborn error of metabolism not recognized before. The defect was first suspected because the child persistently excreted malonic and methylmalonic acids in the urine. Disturbances of lipid metabolism were also shown.

Haan et al. (1986) described a second child, a boy with a more severe deficiency of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase. He was mildly mentally retarded. During a urinary tract infection he presented with vomiting, a seizure, hypoglycemia, and mild metabolic acidosis. His urine contained increased amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, succinic, adipic, glutaric, and suberic acids. Mitochondrial malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity in extracts of cultured fibroblasts was 4% of the mean control value.

MacPhee et al. (1993) described 2 new cases of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, noting that until then the worldwide experience had been limited to 2 affected Australian children. The new patients were born in Scotland to consanguineous parents of Scottish/Irish origin. They were investigated during episodes of vomiting and febrile convulsions, together with developmental delay. Malonic aciduria and greatly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity were demonstrated.

Sacksteder et al. (1999) described malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency as a lethal disorder characterized by cardiomyopathy and developmental delay. They noted that affected patients have several phenotypes that resemble mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disorders.

De Wit et al. (2006) described a girl with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency. Brain MRI showed white matter abnormalities together with diffuse pachygyria and periventricular heterotopia, consistent with a malformation of cortical development. The patient had a strikingly low appetite. The authors recommended that patients with the deficiency undergo brain MRI, and that unexplained malformations of cortical development prompt metabolic screening.

### Biochemical Features

In the patient of Haan et al. (1986), a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet caused symptomatic hypoglycemia and moderate metabolic acidosis. On this diet the urine contained large amounts of the same organic acids and 3-hydroxybutyrate. On an isocaloric low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet, only relatively small amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, and succinic acid were excreted. Acute fat and lysine loads increased urinary excretion of malonic acid without changing excretion of the other organic acids. The authors concluded that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase prevents accumulation of malonyl-CoA in the mitochondrion, and that the enzyme matters most when fat is the main metabolic fuel.

### Inheritance

Wightman et al. (2003) described malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder. This is consistent with the 2 homozygous mutations that FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found in patients born to consanguineous parents, which segregated appropriately within the families.

### Molecular Genetics

Gao et al. (1999) isolated and sequenced the human malonyl-CoA decarboxylase gene. They identified the cDNA sequences by homology to the goose gene and determined the intron/exon boundaries by sequencing a PAC clone. They then identified the mutation underlying the deficiency in a patient who had clinical features of the defect, malonic aciduria, and markedly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity.

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) used RT-PCR analysis of fibroblast RNA from the 2 consanguineous Scottish patients reported earlier by MacPhee et al. (1993). They found 2 different homozygous mutations in the MCD gene. One was a 442C-G transversion that creates a premature stop codon (S148X). The other was an intronic mutation (IVS4-14A-G) that creates a new splice acceptor site, causing a 13-bp insertion in the mature RNA and a frameshift with predicted protein truncation. Both mutations segregated appropriately within the families and were absent from 100 normal unrelated individuals. ⚠︎

Sacksteder et al. (1999) identified a candidate gene for human MCD in a computer-based search for new peroxisomal proteins. Recombinant MCD protein had high intrinsic malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity. In a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency they found a severe mutation in the MCD gene (c.947-948delTT).

Wightman et al. (2003) sequenced the genomic MLYCD gene in 9 unrelated patients and identified 16 of 18 pathogenic alleles. Western blot analysis of fibroblasts from 8 of these patients and from 2 previously reported patients with homozygous MLYCD mutations used antisera raised to a C-terminal peptide. A 66-kD band was absent in 6 patients and substantially reduced in 3; 1 patient had increased protein levels with a prominent smeary 68- to 83-kD band. Immunocytochemistry of patient cell lines that expressed MLYCD showed apparent intracellular mislocalization. The extreme N-terminal mutation c.8G-A (G3D) mislocalized the protein to the plasma membrane. A 25-base deletion between the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiating codons (M1 and M40), and a point mutation that abolished the second of these (c.119T-C, M40T), both gave punctate perinuclear staining. None of these 3 mislocalizing mutations was predicted to alter catalytic function, so the authors proposed that correct subcellular localization of MLYCD is critical for its normal function.

In a girl with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, de Wit et al. (2006) found a normal MLYCD genomic sequence but no messenger product. They suggested an abnormality of transcription regulation.

### Pathogenesis

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found that the human MCD peptide has a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting sequence (-SKL). Subcellular fractionation of rat liver homogenates suggested that MCD is located in peroxisomes as well as mitochondria. Sacksteder et al. (1999) found MCD in both the cytoplasm and peroxisomes, and found MCD mRNA to be most abundant in cardiac and skeletal muscle. They proposed that cytoplasmic MCD regulates the abundance of cytoplasmic malonyl-CoA, and so the uptake and oxidation of fatty acids by mitochondria. They also proposed that peroxisomal MCD may break down malonyl-CoA produced inside peroxisomes by beta-oxidation of odd-chain-length dicarboxylic fatty acids.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, SER148TER [442C>G; S148X]

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found a homozygous 442C-G transversion in the MCD gene in a consanguineous Scottish patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, previously reported by MacPhee et al. (1993). The change creates a premature stop codon (S148X) in the N-terminal half of the protein. They identified it by RT-PCR analysis of fibroblast RNA. The mutation segregated appropriately within the family and was absent from 100 normal unrelated individuals.

**.0002 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, IVS4AS, A-G, -14 [IVS4-14A>G]

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found a homozygous intronic mutation (IVS4-14A-G) in the MCD gene in a consanguineous Scottish patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, previously reported by MacPhee et al. (1993). The mutation creates a new splice acceptor site. This causes a 13-bp insertion in the mature RNA and a frameshift with predicted protein truncation. The mutation segregated appropriately within the family and was absent from 100 normal unrelated individuals.

**.0003 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, 2-BP DEL, 947TT [c.947-948delTT]

In a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, Sacksteder et al. (1999) found a severe mutation in the MCD gene, a 2-bp deletion (c.947-948delTT). The abstract does not state its zygosity.

**.0004 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, GLY3ASP [c.8G>A; p.G3D]

Wightman et al. (2003) found an extreme N-terminal c.8G-A mutation in the MLYCD gene, giving a gly3-to-asp (G3D) substitution, in a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency. On immunocytochemistry the mutant protein was mislocalized to the plasma membrane. The authors suggested that a new targeting signal may lie in a conserved 4-amino acid N-terminal motif, and the mutation was not predicted to alter catalytic function.

**.0005 MALONYL-COA DECARBOXYLASE DEFICIENCY**  
MLYCD, MET40THR [c.119T>C; p.M40T]

Wightman et al. (2003) found a c.119T-C point mutation in the MLYCD gene, giving a met40-to-thr (M40T) substitution, in a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency. The mutation abolishes the second of the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiating codons (M40). On immunocytochemistry it showed punctate perinuclear staining, consistent with mislocalization, and it was not predicted to alter catalytic function.
