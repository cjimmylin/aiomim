### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is caused by homozygous mutation in the MLYCD gene (606761) on chromosome 16q23.3.

### Description

Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism characterized by malonic aciduria, developmental delay, seizure disorder, hypoglycemia, and cardiomyopathy (Wightman et al., 2003). Other reported features include mild mental retardation, hypotonia, vomiting, and metabolic acidosis (Gao et al., 1999). Sacksteder et al. (1999) described it as a lethal disorder characterized by cardiomyopathy and developmental delay, with phenotypes reminiscent of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disorders.

### Clinical Features

Brown et al. (1984) described a patient with deficiency of the mitochondrial enzyme malonyl-CoA decarboxylase, an inborn error of metabolism not recognized previously. The enzyme defect was first suspected because of persistent excretion of malonic and methylmalonic acids in the urine in a child with repeated episodes of vomiting, some requiring hospitalization. Disturbances of lipid metabolism were demonstrated.

Haan et al. (1986) reported a second child with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, a boy who was mildly mentally retarded and presented with vomiting, a seizure, hypoglycemia, and mild metabolic acidosis during a urinary tract infection. His urine contained increased amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, succinic, adipic, glutaric, and suberic acids. Mitochondrial malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity in cultured fibroblast extracts was 4% of the mean control value.

MacPhee et al. (1993) reported 2 Scots-born patients who were the offspring of consanguineous parents of Scots/Irish origin. They were investigated during episodes of vomiting and febrile convulsions associated with concomitant developmental delay. Malonic aciduria and grossly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity were demonstrated.

Gao et al. (1999) studied a patient with clinical features of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, malonic aciduria, and markedly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity.

de Wit et al. (2006) described a girl with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency whose brain MRI showed white matter abnormalities and additionally diffuse pachygyria and periventricular heterotopia, consistent with a malformation of cortical development. She had a strikingly low appetite.

Chapel-Crespo et al. (2019) reported the clinical, biochemical, and molecular characteristics of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency and long-term follow-up of 9 patients.

### Biochemical Features

In their patient, Haan et al. (1986) found that a high fat, low carbohydrate diet caused symptomatic hypoglycemia, moderate metabolic acidosis, and urinary excretion of large amounts of organic acids and 3-hydroxybutyrate, whereas an isocaloric low fat, high carbohydrate diet led to excretion of only relatively small amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, and succinic acids. Acute fat and lysine loads increased urinary malonic acid without affecting the other organic acids.

### Inheritance

Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. FitzPatrick et al. (1999) identified homozygous mutations in the MCD gene in 2 patients born to consanguineous parents, and the mutations segregated appropriately within the families. Wightman et al. (2003) described MLYCD deficiency as an autosomal recessive disorder.

### Molecular Genetics

By RT-PCR analysis of fibroblast RNA from 2 previously reported consanguineous Scottish patients with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, FitzPatrick et al. (1999) identified 2 different homozygous truncating mutations in the MCD (MLYCD) gene: a 442C-G transversion resulting in a premature stop codon (S148X) in the N-terminal half of the protein, and an intronic mutation (IVS4-14A-G) generating a novel splice acceptor sequence that resulted in a 13-bp insertion in the mature RNA, causing a frameshift with predicted protein truncation. Both mutations segregated appropriately within the families and were not found in 100 normal unrelated individuals, which confirmed the cloned transcript as the human MCD ortholog.

Sacksteder et al. (1999) found that a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency had a severe mutation in the MCD gene (c.947-948delTT), confirming that this gene encodes human malonyl-CoA decarboxylase.

Gao et al. (1999) determined the genetic mutation underlying malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency in a patient with clinical features of the defect, malonic aciduria, and markedly reduced enzyme activity.

By genomic sequencing of the MLYCD gene in 9 unrelated patients with MLYCD deficiency, Wightman et al. (2003) identified 16 of 18 pathogenic alleles. Western blot analysis of patient fibroblasts (from 8 of these patients and 2 previously reported patients with homozygous mutations) using C-terminal antisera showed that the 66-kDa band was absent in 6 patients and substantially reduced in 3; 1 patient showed increased protein with a smeary 68- to 83-kDa band. Mutations studied for subcellular localization included an extreme N-terminal mutation (c.8G-A; G3D), a 25-base deletion between the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiating codons (M1 and M40), and a point mutation ablating the second of these (c.119T-C; M40T).

In a girl with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, de Wit et al. (2006) found normal genomic MLYCD sequence but no MLYCD messenger product, suggesting an abnormality of transcription regulation.

### Pathogenesis

Haan et al. (1986) suggested that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase serves an important mitochondrial function by preventing accumulation of malonyl-CoA, and that its importance is most apparent when fat is the main metabolic fuel.

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting sequence (-SKL) in human MCD, and subcellular fractionation of rat liver homogenates strongly suggested that MCD activity was localized to peroxisomes as well as to mitochondria.

Sacksteder et al. (1999) found that the MCD protein carries a canonical type-1 peroxisomal targeting signal (SKL), that recombinant MCD had high intrinsic malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity, and that MCD resides in both the cytoplasm and peroxisomes. MCD mRNA was most abundant in cardiac and skeletal muscle. They proposed that cytoplasmic MCD regulates cytoplasmic malonyl-CoA abundance and thus mitochondrial fatty acid uptake and oxidation, and that peroxisomal MCD may degrade intraperoxisomal malonyl-CoA generated by peroxisomal beta-oxidation of odd chain-length dicarboxylic fatty acids.

Wightman et al. (2003) found that the N-terminal G3D mutation mislocalized MLYCD to the plasma membrane, suggesting a novel targeting signal in a 4-amino acid conserved N-terminal motif, whereas the 25-base deletion between the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiation codons and the M40T mutation showed punctate perinuclear staining. Because none of these mutations was predicted to alter catalytic function, they concluded that correct subcellular localization of MLYCD is likely critical for normal function.

### Diagnosis

MacPhee et al. (1993) presented total ion current chromatograms of urinary organic acid profiles obtained by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry in patients with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency.

de Wit et al. (2006) recommended that patients with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency be evaluated with brain MRI and that unexplained malformations of cortical development prompt metabolic screening.

### History

MacPhee et al. (1993) noted that before their report, worldwide experience of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency had been limited to 2 affected Australian children.
