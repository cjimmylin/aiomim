### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency is caused by homozygous mutation in the MLYCD gene (606761).

### Description

Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MLYCD) deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by malonic aciduria, developmental delay, seizures, hypoglycemia, and cardiomyopathy (Wightman et al., 2003). Gao et al. (1999) listed mild mental retardation, seizures, hypotonia, cardiomyopathy, vomiting, hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and malonic aciduria among its features. Sacksteder et al. (1999) described it as a lethal disorder characterized by cardiomyopathy and developmental delay. Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase catalyzes the proton-consuming conversion of malonyl-CoA to acetyl-CoA and CO2 (Sacksteder et al., 1999; Gao et al., 1999).

### Clinical Features

Brown et al. (1984) described a child with deficiency of the mitochondrial enzyme malonyl-CoA decarboxylase, an inborn error of metabolism that had not been recognized before. The child had repeated episodes of vomiting, some of which required hospitalization.

Haan et al. (1986) described a boy, the second reported child with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, whose enzyme defect was more severe. He was mildly mentally retarded. During a urinary tract infection he presented with vomiting, a seizure, hypoglycemia, and mild metabolic acidosis.

MacPhee et al. (1993) described 2 Scottish-born children with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency whose consanguineous parents were of Scots/Irish origin. The children were investigated during episodes of vomiting and febrile convulsions, and they also had developmental delay. Before this report, the disorder had been reported in only 2 Australian children.

de Wit et al. (2006) described a girl with MCD deficiency whose brain MRI showed white matter abnormalities together with diffuse pachygyria and periventricular heterotopia, consistent with a malformation of cortical development. She had a strikingly low appetite. The authors noted that this was interesting in light of the proposed role of malonyl-CoA in feeding control, but that it remained to be confirmed in other patients.

Chapel-Crespo et al. (2019) reported the clinical, biochemical, and molecular characteristics of malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, with long-term follow-up of 9 patients.

### Biochemical Features

Brown et al. (1984) first suspected malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency in their patient because the child persistently excreted malonic and methylmalonic acids in the urine. They also demonstrated disturbances of lipid metabolism.

In their patient, Haan et al. (1986) found increased urinary amounts of malonic, methylmalonic, succinic, adipic, glutaric, and suberic acids. Mitochondrial malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity in extracts of his cultured fibroblasts was 4% of the mean control value. A high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet led to symptomatic hypoglycemia, moderate metabolic acidosis, and urinary excretion of large amounts of the same organic acids and of 3-hydroxybutyrate. On an isocaloric low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet, he excreted only relatively small quantities of malonic, methylmalonic, and succinic acids. Acute fat and lysine loads increased urinary malonic acid excretion without affecting excretion of the other organic acids.

MacPhee et al. (1993) found malonic aciduria and greatly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity in their 2 Scottish patients. They presented urinary organic acid profiles obtained by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and compared the clinical and biochemical features of these patients with those of the Australian patients.

### Inheritance

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found homozygous MCD mutations in 2 patients with MCD deficiency from consanguineous Scottish families, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. Wightman et al. (2003) also described the disorder as autosomal recessive.

### Molecular Genetics

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) studied 2 previously reported patients with MCD deficiency from consanguineous Scottish families. By RT-PCR analysis of fibroblast RNA from one patient, they identified a homozygous nonsense mutation in the MCD (MLYCD) gene (S148X) that created a premature stop codon in the N-terminal half of the protein. In the second patient, they identified a homozygous intronic mutation (IVS4-14A-G) that created a novel splice acceptor site, causing a 13-bp insertion in the mature RNA, a frameshift, and predicted truncation of the protein. Each mutation segregated appropriately within the family and was absent from 100 normal unrelated individuals.

Gao et al. (1999) identified the human malonyl-CoA decarboxylase cDNA by its homology to the goose gene. The 1,479-bp human cDNA was 70% identical to the goose sequence. The intron/exon boundaries, which they determined by sequencing a PAC clone, were completely conserved between the 2 species. They found the mutation underlying malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency in a patient who had clinical features of the defect, malonic aciduria, and markedly reduced malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity.

Sacksteder et al. (1999) found the MCD gene in a computer-based search for new peroxisomal proteins. In a patient with malonyl-CoA decarboxylase deficiency, they identified a severe mutation in the MCD gene, a 2-bp deletion (c.947-948delTT). Together with the high malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity of the recombinant protein, the mutation confirmed that the gene encodes human MCD.

Wightman et al. (2003) sequenced the genomic MLYCD gene in 9 unrelated patients with MLYCD deficiency and identified 16 of the 18 pathogenic alleles. They recorded the variants in a newly created Human MLYCD Allelic Variant Database. Western blot analysis of fibroblast cell lines from 8 of their patients and 2 previously reported patients with homozygous MLYCD mutations showed that the 66-kDa MLYCD band was absent in 6 patients and substantially reduced in 3. One patient had increased protein levels, with a prominent smeary band at 68 to 83 kDa. The mutations included an extreme N-terminal mutation (G3D), whose mutant protein mislocalized to the plasma membrane on immunocytochemistry; a mutation (M40T) that ablates the second of the putative mitochondrial and peroxisomal initiating codons; and a 25-base deletion between the putative mitochondrial (M1) and peroxisomal (M40) initiating codons. The protein from the last 2 mutants showed punctate perinuclear staining.

In their patient with MCD deficiency, de Wit et al. (2006) found a normal MLYCD genomic sequence but no MLYCD messenger product, suggesting an abnormality of transcription regulation.

### Pathogenesis

Haan et al. (1986) suggested that malonyl-CoA decarboxylase serves an important function in the mitochondrion by preventing accumulation of malonyl-CoA, and that its importance is best seen when fat is the main metabolic fuel. They noted that the mechanisms by which malonyl-CoA produces its complex metabolic effects remained to be elucidated.

FitzPatrick et al. (1999) found that the human MCD peptide carries a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting sequence (-SKL). Subcellular fractionation of rat liver homogenates showed a distribution of MCD enzyme activity that strongly suggested MCD is located in peroxisomes as well as mitochondria.

Sacksteder et al. (1999) found that recombinant MCD protein had high intrinsic malonyl-CoA decarboxylase activity and carried a canonical type-1 peroxisomal targeting signal (serine-lysine-leucine at the C terminus). Subcellular fractionation showed that MCD is located in both the cytoplasm and peroxisomes. They proposed that cytoplasmic MCD regulates the amount of cytoplasmic malonyl-CoA and, through it, mitochondrial fatty acid uptake and oxidation. They noted that MCD mRNA is most abundant in cardiac and skeletal muscle and that patients have phenotypes reminiscent of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation disorders. They also suggested that peroxisomal MCD may degrade intraperoxisomal malonyl-CoA produced by beta-oxidation of odd chain-length dicarboxylic fatty acids.

Wightman et al. (2003) found apparent intracellular mislocalization of MLYCD in patient cell lines that still expressed the protein. None of the 3 mislocalizing mutations was predicted to alter catalytic function, so the authors concluded that correct subcellular localization of MLYCD is likely critical for normal function. They suggested that a novel targeting signal may reside in a conserved 4-amino acid N-terminal motif.

### Diagnosis

de Wit et al. (2006) recommended that patients with MCD deficiency be evaluated with brain MRI, and that unexplained malformations of cortical development prompt metabolic screening.

By RT-PCR analysis of fibroblast RNA, they identified a homozygous 442C-G transversion in the MCD gene, resulting in a ser148-to-ter (S148X) substitution and a premature stop codon in the N-terminal half of the protein. The mutation segregated appropriately within the family and was not present in 100 normal unrelated individuals.
