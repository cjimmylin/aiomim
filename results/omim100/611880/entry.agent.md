### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that dilated cardiomyopathy-2A (CMD2A) is caused by mutation in the gene encoding cardiac troponin I (TNNI3). Murphy et al. (2004) identified a TNNI3 mutation in a family with autosomal recessive dilated cardiomyopathy, and Carballo et al. (2009) reported TNNI3 missense mutations in families with autosomal dominant dilated cardiomyopathy. ⚠︎

### Description

Idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy is a common cause of heart failure. Familial isolated dilated cardiomyopathy is usually inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, but autosomal recessive forms have also been described (Goldblatt et al., 1987; Seliem et al., 2000; Murphy et al., 2004).

### Clinical Features

Goldblatt et al. (1987) described dilated cardiomyopathy in 3 members of a consanguineous Madeira Portuguese family. Following a review of the literature, they concluded that the autosomal recessive form of dilated cardiomyopathy appeared to be more common than previously reported.

Seliem et al. (2000) evaluated 55 consecutive cases of dilated cardiomyopathy in patients younger than 10 years of age, seen during a 5-year interval in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. The 55 cases represented 20% of the offspring of 41 families of Arab descent; in 19 families (46%) the parents were first cousins, and there was no obvious consanguinity in 22 families (54%). Echocardiography was the primary diagnostic modality. Age at presentation was less than 30 months in 95% (range, 1 to 100 months); 38% of patients were male and 62% female. Twenty-five patients (46%) died, 15 (27%) improved, and 15 (27%) recovered. The left ventricular shortening fraction at diagnosis ranged from 5 to 28% and did not differ among those who died, improved, or recovered.

Carballo et al. (2009) reported that 2 TNNI3 missense mutations were each associated with severe and early-onset familial dilated cardiomyopathy. Of the 5 mutation carriers, 3 required cardiac transplantation, at ages 6, 15, and 24 years.

### Inheritance

Goldblatt et al. (1987) described autosomal recessive inheritance of dilated cardiomyopathy in a consanguineous Madeira Portuguese kindred. In a study of 41 Saudi Arabian families of Arab descent with infantile dilated cardiomyopathy, Seliem et al. (2000) performed complex segregation analysis using the mixed model of inheritance and found that a model of recessive inheritance best fit the data. They suggested that recessively inherited dilated cardiomyopathy may be infrequently reported because it is difficult to recognize in populations in which consanguineous marriage is uncommon and the number of children per family is small.

Carballo et al. (2009) reported TNNI3 mutations in autosomal dominant dilated cardiomyopathy, indicating that both recessive (Murphy et al., 2004) and dominant inheritance occur with TNNI3-related dilated cardiomyopathy. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Seliem et al. (2000) suggested that, in the setting of consanguineous marriage, homozygosity mapping in the Saudi Arabian families they studied should lead to identification of the gene(s) causing dilated cardiomyopathy.

### Molecular Genetics

Murphy et al. (2004) used mutation analysis suitable for identifying both dominant and recessive mutations to screen the cardiac troponin I gene (TNNI3) in 235 patients with dilated cardiomyopathy. They identified a novel TNNI3 mutation in a family with recessive disease. Functional studies showed impairment of troponin interactions that could lead to diminished myocardial contractility. Murphy et al. (2004) stated that TNNI3 was the first recessive gene identified for dilated cardiomyopathy and suggested that other such genes could be found by mutation analyses designed to identify homozygous mutations.

Carballo et al. (2009) screened TNNI3 exons by heteroduplex analysis in a panel of 96 probands with dilated cardiomyopathy, sequenced exons with aberrant profiles, and evaluated variants by segregation analysis and study of normal controls. They identified 2 novel TNNI3 missense mutations, lys36-to-gln (K36Q) and asn185-to-lys (N185K), each associated with severe, early-onset familial disease. Troponin reconstituted with either mutant troponin I gave lower maximum actin-tropomyosin-activated myosin ATPase rates and lower Ca(2+) sensitivity than wildtype, and mutant thin filaments had reduced Ca(2+) affinity. The authors noted that these functional alterations closely mirrored those of proven dilated cardiomyopathy mutations in other thin filament proteins, and that these were the first reported autosomal dominant dilated cardiomyopathy-causing mutations in TNNI3.
