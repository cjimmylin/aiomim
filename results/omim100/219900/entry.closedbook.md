### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene (606272) on chromosome 17p13. ✗

### Description

Juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder. Cystine builds up inside lysosomes because the lysosomal cystine transporter, cystinosin, does not work properly. It is an intermediate form of cystinosis, between the severe infantile nephropathic form and the benign adult nonnephropathic (ocular) form. Kidney disease begins later than in the infantile form and progresses more slowly to end-stage renal failure. Patients also have corneal cystine crystals. ✗

### Clinical Features

Kidney involvement in the late-onset form usually becomes apparent in late childhood or adolescence. It often presents with proteinuria, which can reach the nephrotic range, rather than the full renal Fanconi syndrome seen in infants. Renal function declines more slowly than in the infantile form, but end-stage renal disease eventually develops. Cystine crystals are found in the cornea and other tissues, and growth is less affected than in infantile cystinosis. ✗

### Biochemical Features

Free cystine levels in leukocytes are raised, typically to intermediate values. Patients' cells have reduced carrier-mediated transport of cystine out of lysosomes. Measuring leukocyte cystine is the standard diagnostic test, as in the other forms of cystinosis. ✗

### Inheritance

Juvenile nephropathic cystinosis is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. It is allelic to infantile nephropathic cystinosis and to adult nonnephropathic (ocular) cystinosis. ✗

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) and Attard et al. (1999) identified mutations in the CTNS gene in patients with intermediate (juvenile) cystinosis. These patients usually had at least one milder allele, such as a missense or splice mutation that leaves some cystinosin function. This contrasts with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, in which both alleles are typically severe, for example the common 57-kb deletion found in patients of northern European ancestry. ✗

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) reported that the severity of cystinosis depended on the CTNS genotype. In their study, patients with the infantile form tended to have 2 severe alleles, while those with the juvenile and ocular forms carried at least one mild allele. ⚠︎

### Clinical Management

As in the infantile form, oral cysteamine lowers the cystine content of cells, and cysteamine eye drops are used to treat the corneal crystals. ✗
