### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis (intermediate cystinosis) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene. ⚠︎

### Description

Late-onset cystinosis is a rarer form of cystinosis, a disorder caused by a defect in the transport of cystine across the lysosomal membrane. Attard et al. (1999) characterized late-onset cystinosis by onset of symptoms between 12 and 15 years of age, in contrast to the infantile nephropathic form, which is characterized by early onset of renal proximal tubular dysfunction.

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) reported adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of the disorder.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis, together with an unusual clinical variant of the disorder.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Thoene et al. (1999) described 6 patients with the intermediate form of cystinosis.

### Inheritance

Attard et al. (1999) noted that infantile nephropathic cystinosis is an autosomal recessive disease, and they regarded late-onset cystinosis as a rarer form of the same disorder. The finding by Thoene et al. (1999) that intermediate cystinosis results from homozygosity or from the combination of 2 different mutations in the CTNS gene is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) studied 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis and found mutations in the CTNS gene, 2 of which had not been described before. The disease arose either from the combination of 1 mild mutation with 1 mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or from homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation. Transfection of cystinotic fibroblasts with normal cDNA, or with a cDNA derived from a mutation causing intermediate cystinosis, partially corrected the phenotype.

Attard et al. (1999) screened the CTNS gene in patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, in patients with late-onset cystinosis, and in patients whose phenotype did not fit the classical definitions. They found 23 different mutations, 14 of them novel. The mutations found in 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis affected regions of the cystinosin protein that are not functionally important, which the authors suggested accounted for the milder phenotype. In 3 further patients, cystinosis began before age 7 years, but the disease ran a milder course than the infantile nephropathic form. The authors suggested that the missense mutations in these patients allowed production of functional protein.

Macias-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 cystinosis patients from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Attard et al. (1999) found that the severity of the cystinosis phenotype varied with the type of CTNS mutation. Of 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, 12 had 2 severely truncating mutations, consistent with loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions that were predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains and abolish protein function. In contrast, the mutations in 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis affected functionally unimportant regions of cystinosin.
