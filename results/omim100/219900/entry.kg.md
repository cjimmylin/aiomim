### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that late-onset juvenile or adolescent nephropathic cystinosis is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the CTNS gene.

### Description

Late-onset (juvenile or adolescent) cystinosis is a rarer form of cystinosis than the infantile nephropathic form. Symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age (Attard et al., 1999), and renal failure can develop (Langman et al., 1985). Goldman et al. (1971) compared the adolescent form with the infantile and adult forms of cystinosis.

### Clinical Features

Goldman et al. (1971) described adolescent cystinosis and compared it with the infantile and adult forms of the disorder.

Spear et al. (1971) described polykaryocytosis (multinucleated cells) of the visceral glomerular epithelium in cystinosis. They also described an unusual clinical variant of cystinosis.

Langman et al. (1985) reported renal failure in a sibship with late-onset cystinosis.

Attard et al. (1999) described late-onset cystinosis as a rarer form of the disorder in which symptoms begin between 12 and 15 years of age.

### Inheritance

Thoene et al. (1999) and Attard et al. (1999) established autosomal recessive inheritance of intermediate cystinosis. Thoene et al. (1999) found that patients were either compound heterozygous or homozygous for mutations in the CTNS gene. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Thoene et al. (1999) identified CTNS mutations in 6 patients with intermediate cystinosis. Two of the mutations had not been reported before. In these patients the disease resulted either from compound heterozygosity for one mild mutation and one mutation known to cause nephropathic cystinosis, or from homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation. Transfecting cystinotic fibroblasts with normal CTNS cDNA partially corrected the cells' phenotype. Transfecting them with a cDNA carrying a mutation that causes intermediate cystinosis did the same. ⚠︎

Attard et al. (1999) screened the CTNS gene in 3 groups of patients: those with infantile nephropathic cystinosis, those with late-onset cystinosis, and those whose phenotype did not fit the classical definitions. They found 23 different CTNS mutations, 14 of them novel. In 2 patients with late-onset cystinosis, the mutations affected regions of cystinosin that are not functionally important. The authors concluded that this accounted for the milder phenotype.

Macías-Vidal et al. (2009) analyzed the CTNS gene in 32 patients with cystinosis from Spain.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Thoene et al. (1999) studied 6 patients and concluded that intermediate cystinosis results from having at least one mild CTNS mutation. This could be one mild mutation together with a mutation that causes nephropathic cystinosis, or homozygosity for a predicted mild mutation.

Attard et al. (1999) studied 25 patients with infantile nephropathic cystinosis. Twelve had 2 severely truncating CTNS mutations, consistent with loss of functional protein. The other 13 had missense mutations or in-frame deletions predicted to disrupt transmembrane domains and abolish protein function. Three other patients had onset of cystinosis before 7 years of age but a milder course than the infantile nephropathic form, and they carried CTNS missense mutations. The authors suggested that these mutations allow production of functional protein and may mark regions of cystinosin that are not functionally important.
