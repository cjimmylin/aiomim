### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that developmental delay with variable intellectual disability and dysmorphic facies (DIDDF) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the JARID2 gene (601594) on chromosome 6p22.3.

### Description

JARID2-related neurodevelopmental syndrome is caused by JARID2 haploinsufficiency. It features developmental delay and cognitive impairment that ranges from borderline intellectual functioning to severe intellectual disability. Patients may also have hypotonia, gait disturbance, autistic features and behavior abnormalities. The facial features include a high anterior hairline, broad forehead, prominent supraorbital ridges, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, midface hypoplasia, depressed nasal bridge, bulbous nasal tip and full lips (Barøy et al., 2013; Verberne et al., 2022).

### Clinical Features

Barøy et al. (2013) reported 4 unrelated patients with de novo 6p22.3 deletions that included JARID2. Patient 1 was a 15-year-old Norwegian girl born to nonconsanguineous healthy parents. She had global developmental delay and moderate intellectual disability (IQ 35-49). She walked at 3.5 years and had severe expressive language delay. Other findings were periventricular white matter changes, syncope episodes suspected to be seizures, hyperactivity, sleeping difficulties, dyspraxia and an unsteady, broad-based gait. Skeletal findings included pes planovalgus, short metacarpals and first metatarsals, kyphosis and fifth-finger clinodactyly. Her face showed prominent supraorbital ridges, deep-set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, midface hypoplasia, hypotelorism, strabismus and a bulbous nasal tip. Her muscle tone was normal. Patient 2 was a 4-year-old boy born to nonconsanguineous healthy Norwegian and English parents. He had moderate intellectual disability, delayed eye contact, severe language delay, repetitive behavior and syncope episodes suspected to be seizures. He also had an atrial septal defect that closed spontaneously. His gait was broad-based and unsteady, and he seemed unable to stand on one leg at a time. He had the same facial features as patient 1, along with pes planovalgus and syndactyly of toes 2 to 4. His cerebral MRI was normal. ⚠︎

Patient 3 of Barøy et al. (2013) was a 6.5-year-old girl born to nonconsanguineous healthy Norwegian parents. In the neonatal period she had hypotonia, an umbilical hernia and apnea with cyanosis. Testing with the Bayley II at 5 years 10 months showed cognitive function corresponding to 12 to 13 months, suggesting severe intellectual disability (IQ 20-34). Evaluation with the ADOS showed behavior consistent with autism spectrum disorder. She also had severe language delay, hyperactivity, an unsteady gait and sleeping difficulties. Her face showed prominent supraorbital ridges, deep-set eyes, hypertelorism and midface hypoplasia, and her frontal teeth were irregularly placed. Patient 4 was a 6.5-year-old boy born to nonconsanguineous Lebanese parents. He had early-onset muscular hypotonia. Testing with the WPPSI-III at 6.5 years gave a borderline IQ of 74. He had language delay, dyspraxia, balance and coordination problems and gait disturbance. His facial features were prominent supraorbital ridges, deep-set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, midface hypoplasia, strabismus, a bulbous nasal tip, a short philtrum, full lips and a pointed chin. He also had syndactyly of toes 2 and 3, a sandal gap and fifth-finger clinodactyly. His cerebral MRI and EEG were normal. On photographs of 7 of 10 previously published patients with deletions that contained JARID2, the authors confirmed the same facial pattern. Impaired balance or coordination and/or gait disturbance had been noted in 4 of those 10 patients. ⚠︎

Verberne et al. (2021) reported 16 individuals from 15 families with a deletion or single-nucleotide variant in JARID2. All had developmental delay and/or intellectual disability. They shared some clinical characteristics, such as facial features, with individuals who had larger deletions involving JARID2.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported 7 individuals from 5 families with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants. Their findings highlighted palatal abnormalities and heart defects as part of the phenotype. In some families, the variant had been inherited from a mildly affected parent.

Verberne et al. (2022) reported 11 patients with JARID2 variants. Patient 1 was a 14-year-old boy with mild intellectual disability (IQ 61-74), autistic features, a broad forehead, infraorbital dark circles, pes planovalgus and mild hypermetropia, without hypotonia. Patient 2 was a 21-year-old woman with borderline intellectual functioning (IQ 82) and behavior abnormalities. She had a high anterior hairline, deeply set eyes, midface hypoplasia, a short philtrum, a submucous cleft palate and a bifid uvula. Patient 3 was an 18-year-old woman with mild intellectual disability (IQ 50) and autistic features. Her facial features were a high anterior hairline, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles, a depressed nasal bridge, a bulbous nasal tip, a short philtrum and full lips. She also had fetal finger pads and slight bilateral tapering of digits II and V. Patient 4 was a 14-year-old boy with moderate intellectual disability, behavior abnormalities and autism spectrum disorder. He had a small posterior fossa cyst or mega cisterna magna, a high anterior hairline, a short philtrum, full lips and 2 cafe-au-lait macules. Patient 5 was a 5-year-old boy with developmental delay, hypotonia, an arachnoid cyst, a broad forehead, deeply set eyes, right cryptorchidism and congenital torticollis.

Patients 6 and 7 of Verberne et al. (2022) were daughter and mother. The 6-year-old daughter had developmental delay but normal intellectual functioning. She previously had a rigid walking pattern, and she had a high anterior hairline, infraorbital dark circles, a bulbous nasal tip and a supernumerary tooth. Her 36-year-old mother had learning difficulties and a bulbous nasal tip. Patient 8 was a 9-year-old boy with mild intellectual disability, behavior abnormalities, autism spectrum disorder, mild hypotonia and gait disturbance. His facial features were a broad forehead, high anterior hairline, prominent supraorbital ridges, deeply set eyes, infraorbital dark circles and a slightly depressed nasal bridge. He also had kyphoscoliosis and bladder spasticity, and his spinal cord MRI was normal.

Verberne et al. (2022) also described 3 patients who carried JARID2 missense variants of uncertain significance. Patient 9 was a 43-year-old woman with learning difficulties (IQ 79), behavior abnormalities, autism spectrum disorder, convergent strabismus, camptodactyly of the fifth fingers and 2-3 toe syndactyly. Patient 10 was a 12-year-old boy with mild intellectual disability (IQ 66). Neither patient 9 nor patient 10 showed the facial dysmorphisms associated with JARID2-neurodevelopmental syndrome. Patient 11 was an 8-year-old boy with developmental delay and normal intellectual functioning. He had hypotonia that was later followed by severe global spasticity, lack of myelination on brain MRI and a neurogenic bladder. He also had a broad forehead, high anterior hairline, deeply set eyes, midface hypoplasia and full lips. The authors noted that severe global spasticity and lack of myelination were not known to be associated with JARID2-neurodevelopmental syndrome.

### Inheritance

The transmission pattern of DIDDF is autosomal dominant. Most reported variants arose de novo (Barøy et al., 2013; Verberne et al., 2022). Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) reported JARID2 variants inherited from mildly affected parents, which showed that the disorder has variable expressivity. Verberne et al. (2022) reported a JARID2 deletion that a mother with learning difficulties transmitted to her affected daughter. ⚠︎

### Molecular Genetics

Barøy et al. (2013) studied about 3,600 patients with neurodevelopmental disorders by array CGH. In 4 patients they found de novo heterozygous 6p22.3 deletions that shared a 189-kb smallest region of overlap (SRO I) containing JARID2 and DTNBP1. Three of the deletions were 2.3 to 4.8 Mb in size and also included ATXN1. The fourth, in patient 4, was 189 to 241 kb and removed exons 3 to 18 of JARID2 and exons 7 to 10 of DTNBP1. The authors judged DTNBP1 unlikely to explain the phenotype and considered JARID2 the critical gene. In these 4 patients, JARID2 expression in leukocytes was significantly lower than in 8 controls (p ≤ 0.01). Patient 3 also carried a 463-kb duplication of chromosome 4q23. It had been inherited from a healthy parent, and its gene content did not appear relevant to the clinical presentation. A fifth patient had a de novo 116- to 163-kb deletion that included GMPR and part of ATXN1 but not JARID2. She had mild intellectual disability without dysmorphic features.

Zahir et al. (2016) had earlier found a CNV involving only exon 6 of JARID2, apparently de novo, in 7 patients with intellectual disability. Exon 6 encodes one of a series of repeated motifs. When they studied this variant in 2 cohorts with a genotype-phenotype assessment, they concluded that the exon 6 CNV was benign, with a population frequency above 14%. They noted that it could still have a contributory effect.

Verberne et al. (2021) used chromosomal microarray, exome sequencing and GeneMatcher to identify deletions or single-nucleotide variants involving JARID2 in 16 individuals from 15 families. Several variants were predicted to cause haploinsufficiency through nonsense-mediated mRNA decay. The authors concluded that JARID2 haploinsufficiency leads to a clinically distinct neurodevelopmental syndrome. They noted that JARID2 regulates histone methyltransferase complexes and is expressed in human neurons.

Cadieux-Dion et al. (2022) identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic heterozygous JARID2 variants in 7 individuals from 5 families. Intragenic JARID2 copy number variants were highly prevalent in their series, and the authors emphasized the importance of exon-level analysis. ⚠︎

Verberne et al. (2022) reported heterozygous JARID2 variants in 11 patients. Eight patients had variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Four had de novo intragenic deletions (patients 1, 2, 3 and 8). Patient 4 had a de novo frameshift duplication, c.2866dupG (Glu956GlyfsTer72). Patient 5 had a de novo splice site variant, c.2731+1G-C. Patients 6 and 7, daughter and mother, shared a deletion. Three patients carried missense variants of uncertain significance. Patients 9 and 11 were unrelated and both had R788Q. In patient 9 the variant was de novo, and she also carried 2 de novo VUSs in TNRC18, a gene with no known disease association. In patient 11, R788Q was inherited from an apparently unaffected mother. Patient 10 had a de novo E644K variant in the BRIGHT/ARID domain, a change predicted to lead to a normal functioning protein. In DNA methylation analysis, the 3 missense VUS carriers clustered with controls and received MVP scores near zero. Given their nonspecific phenotypes and the absence of characteristic facial features, the authors suggested that these missense variants might very well be benign. They noted, however, that a negative episignature result does not rule out pathogenicity. ⚠︎

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Barøy et al. (2013) found that the 3 patients whose deletions removed both JARID2 and ATXN1 had lower cognitive function than patients missing only one of the 2 genes. They suggested that loss of both genes made the intellectual disability more severe.

### Pathogenesis

Verberne et al. (2022) detected no significant differentially methylated regions with DMRcate in patients with pathogenic JARID2 variants. Among the 150 episignature probes, however, they found 7 small regions, 2 of which were annotated to genes linked to abnormal neurologic development (NLGN2 and ADGRL2). Gene set enrichment analysis did not yield consistent pathways, apart from enrichment for the endochondral ossification pathway.

### Diagnosis

Barøy et al. (2013) recommended sequencing JARID2 in patients with a similar phenotype when no deletion is found.

Verberne et al. (2022) identified a DNA methylation episignature of 150 CpG probes in peripheral blood. It was derived from 8 patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic JARID2 variants (6 deletions, 1 frameshift and 1 splice site variant) compared with 56 matched controls. Hierarchical clustering, MDS and leave-one-out cross-validation showed robust, reproducible separation. A support vector machine classifier showed full sensitivity and specificity against controls and against 57 other neurodevelopmental disorders.

JARID2, 189-KB DEL

Barøy et al. (2013) studied a 6.5-year-old Lebanese boy (patient 4) with DIDDF and found a de novo heterozygous 189- to 241-kb deletion on chromosome 6p22.3 (15405377-15594945 bp, hg19). The deletion removed exons 3 to 18 of JARID2 and exons 7 to 10 of DTNBP1, and it defined the smallest region of overlap among the 4 patients with JARID2 deletions. The patient had borderline intellectual functioning (IQ 74), early-onset hypotonia, language delay, gait disturbance and the characteristic facial features. JARID2 expression in leukocytes of the patients with deletions was significantly reduced compared with controls.
