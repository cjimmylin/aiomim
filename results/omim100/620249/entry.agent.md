### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that the mild variant of congenital myopathy-10B (CMYO10B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutation in the MEGF10 gene on chromosome 5.

### Description

The mild variant of congenital myopathy-10B (CMYO10B) is an autosomal recessive myopathy with onset in infancy or childhood of predominantly axial and proximal weakness, often beginning in the neck, together with facial weakness, joint contractures, scoliosis, and respiratory insufficiency that develops later in childhood or beyond. Muscle biopsy may show minicores, sometimes together with dystrophic or myofibrillar changes (Boyden et al., 2012; Croci et al., 2022). The disorder is milder than the severe early-onset form of MEGF10-related myopathy with areflexia, respiratory distress, and dysphagia (EMARDD) (Takayama et al., 2016; Harris et al., 2018). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Croci et al. (2022) used the term mild variant EMARDD (mvEMARDD) for the milder, chronic forms of MEGF10 myopathy, which have a more favorable course and later onset than classic EMARDD. Takayama et al. (2016) and Harris et al. (2018) called the milder phenotype minicore myopathy and a milder form with cores in the muscle biopsy, respectively.

### Clinical Features

Boyden et al. (2012) reported a nonconsanguineous Caucasian family of mixed European ancestry (family 1030) in which 3 of 4 sibs had a myopathy that resembled multiminicore disease in some respects. Weakness began by 12 months of age, at first in the neck. It affected the whole body but was mainly axial and proximal, with facial weakness, and it stabilized in adolescence. Neck contractures were seen in infancy, and contractures of the elbows, knees, and heel cords had developed by adulthood. Serum creatine kinase measured before age 10 years was mildly elevated. All 3 patients had scoliosis from childhood that was treated with spinal fusion. From age 10 to 11 years, all 3 developed respiratory problems treated with nocturnal bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation. One patient was born with a cleft palate, a second had a midline ridge on her palate in adulthood, and all 3 had hypernasal speech. Muscle biopsies showed dystrophic features, including fatty replacement of myofibers, increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue, and isolated regenerating fibers. Moth-eaten fibers and minicores were also present. Rimmed vacuoles, inclusions, central cores, nemaline rods, and ragged red fibers were absent. There was a slight predominance of type I fibers. Before genetic testing, the patients had been diagnosed with limb-girdle muscular dystrophy or spinal muscular atrophy.

Boyden et al. (2012) also described a female Caucasian patient (subject 105-1) of distant Portuguese ancestry who had been diagnosed with multiminicore disease. She presented at several weeks of age with hypotonia, a weak neck, and a floppy head. She had a markedly high-arched palate, failure to thrive due to poor feeding, blue-tinged sclerae, and hyperflexible joints. She had frequent acute pulmonary infections in childhood but no chronic breathing problems. At age 10 years, serum CK was mildly elevated at 302 U/L, and mild scoliosis was present; it progressed to 20 degrees by age 12 years. Her weakness was mild, nonprogressive, and mainly proximal, and included facial weakness. Muscle biopsy showed minicores, which were confirmed by electron microscopy.

Takayama et al. (2016) reported 2 Japanese patients with MEGF10 mutations, one with the EMARDD phenotype and the other with the milder phenotype.

Harris et al. (2018) reported a 66-year-old man with a 20-year history of progressive proximal and distal weakness of the upper and lower limbs, facial weakness, and dysphagia. He developed respiratory failure requiring ventilation in his fifties, while still ambulant. Muscle biopsy showed myopathic changes with aggregation of myofibrillar proteins. The authors stated that their findings extended the MEGF10 phenotype to include onset in the fifth decade.

Croci et al. (2022) reported a 22-year-old Italian man born to healthy consanguineous parents. He was born at 33 weeks' gestation and needed resuscitation for asphyxia. He had a weak suck, and elbow contractures and scoliosis were present from the first months of life. Motor development and intellect were normal. He developed slowly progressive muscle weakness with impaired walking, rhinolalia, dysphagia, and respiratory involvement. The case report states that he needed nocturnal noninvasive ventilation from age 16 years, whereas the discussion states that he needed respiratory support at age 6. Serum CK, brain and spine MRI, ECG, and echocardiogram were normal. Nerve conduction was normal apart from reduced compound muscle action potential amplitudes. Follow-up examination showed axial hypotonia, generalized muscle hypotrophy and weakness, preserved facial expression, cleft palate, global areflexia, and contractures of the elbows and ankles. He could walk without support but used a manual wheelchair for longer distances. He had Achilles tendon tenotomy and vertebral arthrodesis. From age 17 years, he had swallowing difficulties and recurrent pulmonary infections due to aspiration of food. A first muscle biopsy at age 5 years showed nonspecific changes: fiber size variation, internal nuclei, and fibrosis. A second biopsy showed myofibrillar alterations, including whorled fibers, cytoplasmic inclusions, and minicores.

### Inheritance

Boyden et al. (2012) found autosomal recessive inheritance in a nonconsanguineous family in which 3 of 4 sibs were affected and carried compound heterozygous MEGF10 mutations. Croci et al. (2022) reported a patient born to consanguineous parents who was homozygous for a MEGF10 mutation; both parents were heterozygous carriers.

### Mapping

Boyden et al. (2012) performed a genomewide linkage scan under a recessive model in family 1030 and found positive LOD scores at 16 loci. MEGF10 lay within a linkage peak on chromosome 5 that reached the maximum possible LOD score of 1.329.

### Molecular Genetics

In the 3 affected sibs of family 1030 with a congenital myopathy with minicores, Boyden et al. (2012) identified compound heterozygous missense mutations in the MEGF10 gene: c.976T-C (C326R) and c.2320T-C (C774R). The mutations were found by whole-genome sequencing of 1 affected sib, with variants filtered using linkage data. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the mutations were in trans and segregated with the disorder in all 6 family members. Neither mutation was present in dbSNP, the 1000 Genomes Project, the Exome Sequencing Project, or 56 internal genomes. The c.976T-C and c.2320T-C mutations were absent from 715 and 645 genotyped controls, respectively. Both mutations replace cysteine residues in atypical EGF-like domains that are completely conserved in 43 species. Knockdown of megf10 in zebrafish with 4 different morpholinos caused curved tails, impaired motility, unhatched eggs, reduced birefringence, and disorganized muscle tissue.

Boyden et al. (2012) also sequenced the MEGF10 open reading frame in 190 patients with genetically unexplained myopathies. They found a single heterozygous mutation, c.211C-T (R71W), in subject 105-1, whose clinical and histologic presentation resembled that of family 1030. The mutation was absent from 699 controls but was carried by an unaffected parent. No second mutation was found, either by sequencing of muscle-derived cDNA or by a search for structural changes. The authors suggested that the patient might carry a second MEGF10 mutation they had been unable to detect.

In a Japanese patient with the milder phenotype, Takayama et al. (2016) identified compound heterozygous mutations in the MEGF10 gene: a c.2981-2A-G splice site mutation and a novel missense mutation, cys810-to-tyr (C810Y).

By exome sequencing in a 66-year-old man with adult-onset MEGF10 myopathy, Harris et al. (2018) identified 2 novel mutations in the MEGF10 gene: an essential splice site mutation (c.1426+1G-T) and a missense mutation, c.352T-C, resulting in a cys118-to-arg (C118R) substitution.

By exome sequencing in a 22-year-old Italian man born to consanguineous parents, Croci et al. (2022) identified a homozygous c.2096G-C mutation in the MEGF10 gene, resulting in a cys699-to-ser (C699S) substitution. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation, and both parents were heterozygous carriers. The variant was absent from ClinVar and gnomAD, affected a conserved residue, and was predicted to be pathogenic by several in silico tools. It was classified as likely pathogenic under ACMG criteria. Functional studies were not performed.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Boyden et al. (2012) noted that the C774R mutation they found had also been reported in compound heterozygosity with a frameshift mutation in a family with the more severe EMARDD phenotype. The phenotype of their missense-mutation families was generally similar to that of patients with null mutations but less severe, and minicores were prominent in their patients but had not been noted in biopsies from families with null mutations. They suggested that the patients in family 1030 might retain a small amount of residual MEGF10 function, although genetic background or other factors could also explain the differences between families.

Takayama et al. (2016) stated that cysteine mutations in the extracellular EGF-like domain may be responsible for the milder phenotype but that this relationship was not conclusive. Their 2 Japanese patients illustrated the correlation: the patient with EMARDD had a homozygous frameshift mutation, and the patient with the milder phenotype carried a splice site mutation and a cysteine missense mutation.

Harris et al. (2018) reviewed 20 reported patients with MEGF10 mutations and found a genotype-phenotype correlation. Patients with at least 1 null mutation had onset of respiratory dysfunction in the first year of life. Patients with 2 missense mutations had respiratory dysfunction at age 10 years or much later. The patient reported by Harris et al. (2018), who carried a splice site mutation and a missense mutation, also had late-onset respiratory dysfunction.

Croci et al. (2022) noted that their patient, although homozygous for a missense mutation, had an early-onset, progressive myopathy. A first muscle biopsy showed nonspecific changes, and a later biopsy showed myofibrillar alterations and minicores. They proposed that minicores, which mark the late-onset group, may result from a longstanding myofibrillar pathologic process that begins in infancy. They also suggested that MEGF10-related phenotypes form a continuum rather than a dichotomy between EMARDD and the mild variant.

### Pathogenesis

Holterman et al. (2007) identified Megf10 as a marker of quiescent mouse satellite cells. It was also expressed in skeletal myoblasts but not in differentiated myofibers. Megf10 silencing in activated satellite cells on individual fibers or in cultured myoblasts caused precocious differentiation and depletion of the self-renewing Pax7+/MyoD- population, and it downregulated Notch signaling components in MyoD -/- myoblasts. ⚠︎

Boyden et al. (2012) hypothesized that the C326R and C774R mutations, which they presumed disrupt disulfide bonds, cause loss of MEGF10 function. Excessive differentiation of satellite cells could then deplete the pool of self-renewing cells available to regenerate damaged muscle, which might explain the dystrophic features in patient biopsies. They suggested satellite cell dysfunction as a novel myopathic mechanism.

### Animal Model

Boyden et al. (2012) knocked down megf10 in zebrafish with 4 splice-blocking morpholinos. At 4 days after fertilization, morphants had curled or bent tails, impaired swimming, and reduced motility in response to touch. They also showed decreased birefringence, disrupted somite boundaries, gaps between myofibers, and, on electron microscopy, widespread myofibrillar disorganization and decreased striation.
