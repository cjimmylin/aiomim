### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding titin (TTN; 188840) on chromosome 2q31.

### Description

Myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is an autosomal dominant myopathy that begins in adulthood. Patients have weakness that may be mainly proximal, distal, or respiratory. Respiratory muscle weakness appears early, often while patients can still walk, and may be the presenting feature (Edstrom et al., 1990; Birchall et al., 2005; Pfeffer et al., 2012). Muscle biopsy shows myofibrillar changes with eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions and rimmed vacuoles. Muscle MRI shows selective early involvement of the semitendinosus muscle (Pfeffer et al., 2012; Pfeffer et al., 2014).

### Nomenclature

Pfeffer et al. (2014) proposed that hereditary myopathy with early respiratory failure (HMERF) caused by TTN mutations be classified nosologically as 'MFM-titinopathy.'

### Clinical Features

Edstrom et al. (1990) described 16 patients from 7 pedigrees with an unusual limb-girdle myopathy that began in adulthood. All patients had prominent early weakness of the respiratory muscles. In many, the first neurologic examination was prompted by acute respiratory incapacity. Other early symptoms were neck flexor weakness and sometimes foot extensor weakness. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed a myopathy, and serum muscle enzyme levels were normal or slightly elevated. Muscle biopsies showed myofibrillar changes, including plaques that stained strongly with rhodamine-conjugated phalloidin, a marker for F-actin. On electron microscopy, the plaques consisted of moderately dense thin filaments and were related to splitting of Z discs or extended from Z discs.

Nicolao et al. (1999) studied 2 Swedish families with autosomal dominant myopathy. Affected members had proximal weakness and early respiratory failure. Their muscle biopsies showed characteristic cytoplasmic bodies.

Chinnery et al. (2001) described a 3-generation family with a novel autosomal dominant myopathy. They clinically assessed 24 family members, 11 of whom were affected. Affected members presented in mid-adult life with weakness of the tibialis anterior, and weakness of the hip and shoulder girdles appeared later in the course. Affected members of the second generation presented earlier, and their disease progressed faster, than those of the first generation. Lung function testing showed progressive global weakness of the respiratory muscles that could be detected from the time of presentation, and in some patients the diaphragm was preferentially involved. Muscle pathology was studied in 7 of the 11 affected individuals, 1 of them at autopsy, and correlated closely with clinical involvement. Clinically unaffected muscles showed minimal change. Moderately affected muscles showed fiber splitting, eosinophilic inclusions, and rimmed vacuoles with basophilic rims. In severely involved muscles, the fibers had been replaced by fat and fibrous connective tissue. The inclusions were Congophilic and reacted with antibodies to desmin, beta-amyloid, and phosphorylated tau protein.

Birchall et al. (2005) performed muscle MRI in 7 patients (age range 37 to 66 years; 4 male) with autosomal dominant myopathy with early respiratory failure. They described the disorder as a dominant limb myopathy that begins insidiously in late adult life, with respiratory failure occurring while patients are still ambulant. MRI showed selective early involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus muscles that could not be detected clinically. Closely related muscles progressed at different rates.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) defined the phenotype in 31 carriers of a TTN mutation from 3 families who had been followed for up to 31 years. Patients presented with weakness that was mainly distal, mainly proximal, or mainly respiratory. Age at onset was highly variable, ranging from early adulthood to a mild phenotype in advanced age. In nearly all patients, weakness began earlier and was more severe in the lower limbs. Seven patients had axial weakness. Respiratory function declined gradually over time, and no patient had cardiomyopathy. On muscle MRI in 21 affected individuals, 20 had the characteristic involvement of the semitendinosus, including 3 who were presymptomatic. The most frequent histopathologic findings were eosinophilic inclusions (inclusion bodies) and rimmed vacuoles, but pathology was nonspecific in a minority of patients.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) described 11 patients from 5 British families with the TTN C30071R mutation. Onset was typically in the fourth or fifth decade, with tripping, falling, myalgia, and difficulty walking. Creatine kinase ranged from normal to 1195 IU/L. The disease progressed slowly and involved proximal, distal, and axial muscles. Only 1 patient lost the ability to walk, after about 25 years. Pulmonary function was abnormal in 10 of 11 patients, indicating respiratory muscle weakness, and 4 required nocturnal noninvasive ventilation 20 to 30 years after onset. Features not previously noted included scapular winging, which was often asymmetric, contractures of the elbows and/or Achilles tendons, variable spinal rigidity, and muscle hypertrophy. Cardiac involvement was limited to mild left ventricular impairment in 1 patient and supraventricular tachycardia in 1. Muscle MRI in 3 patients, 1 of them presymptomatic, showed early involvement of the semitendinosus and peroneus longus muscles. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic or dystrophic changes. Rimmed vacuoles were present in 3 biopsies, and inclusions that were eosinophilic and dark blue on Gomori trichrome were present in 3. In 6 of 7 biopsies, desmin, myotilin, p62, and ubiquitin had accumulated in the cytoplasm. Three biopsies showed cytoplasmic aggregates with a 'cheetah skin' appearance, best seen with myotilin staining, and the inclusions did not fully overlap with the protein accumulations. The authors concluded that these features supported a diagnosis of myofibrillar myopathy.

### Inheritance

Edstrom et al. (1990) found evidence of autosomal dominant inheritance in most of their 16 patients from 7 pedigrees. Autosomal dominant inheritance was also reported by Nicolao et al. (1999), Chinnery et al. (2001), and Pfeffer et al. (2012), in whose families the TTN mutation segregated with the phenotype.

### Mapping

Nicolao et al. (1999) performed a genome-wide linkage screen in 2 Swedish families and mapped the disorder to chromosome 2q24-q31. The maximum combined 2-point lod score was 4.87 at theta = 0 with marker D2S1245. Haplotype analysis placed the gene within a 17-cM region between D2S2384 and D2S364.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) combined genome-wide linkage with whole-exome sequencing in a family with autosomal dominant myopathy with early respiratory failure. They identified a shared 2.93-Mbp haplotype on chromosome 2 that contained the likely causal variant in the TTN gene.

### Heterogeneity

Xiang et al. (1999) studied a French family with autosomal dominant myopathy whose clinical and muscle biopsy findings were similar to those of the Swedish families mapped to 2q24-q31. The 2q24-q31 region was completely excluded in this family. A genome-wide scan with polymorphic microsatellite markers gave a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.11, the highest possible in this family, for markers in a 4-cM region between D2S1272 and D2S1260. The authors suggested that the gene for the French form probably lies on chromosome 2q21.

In their family with distal-onset myopathy and early respiratory failure, Chinnery et al. (2001) found no linkage to any of the known loci for distal myopathies. They concluded that the disorder was genetically and phenotypically distinct.

### Molecular Genetics

Lange et al. (2005) reported that a human mutation in the protein kinase domain of titin (TTN) causes hereditary muscle disease by disrupting the TK-nbr1-p62-MuRF2 signaling pathway.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) identified a variant in the TTN gene (g.274375T-C; cys30071-to-arg, C30071R) that segregated with the phenotype in 21 individuals from the original family. It also segregated in 9 members of a second family with the same selective pattern of muscle involvement on MRI, and in a third familial case with a similar phenotype.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) screened 127 unrelated UK probands with undiagnosed myopathy compatible with myofibrillar myopathy (MFM). They used Sanger sequencing of the 119th fibronectin type III (FN3) domain and part of the kinase domain of TTN. The heterozygous C30071R mutation was found in 5 of the 127 probands (3.9%), including 4 of 72 (5.5%) with a clinical diagnosis of MFM. The mutation cosegregated with disease in 3 of the families (F.1, F.2, and F.4). No patient had a mutation at residue 32450 in the kinase domain.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) also identified a novel heterozygous TTN variant, g.274436C-T (pro30091-to-leu, P30091L), in a patient (F.6) with adult-onset myopathy compatible with MFM. The variant affects a highly conserved residue in the 119th FN3 domain. The patient had proximal weakness, mild neck weakness, calf hypertrophy, scapular winging, and respiratory involvement (FVC 52%). The biopsy showed rimmed vacuoles and accumulation of myotilin and p62. The variant was absent from 400 control chromosomes, but a healthy 61-year-old brother also carried it. The authors suggested that the variant may be neutral, although they could not exclude reduced penetrance.

In a Brazilian patient (F.7), Pfeffer et al. (2014) identified another novel heterozygous TTN variant in the 119th FN3 domain, g.274599C-G (asn30145-to-lys, N30145K). The patient had adult-onset proximal limb weakness, severe weakness of ankle dorsiflexion, and scapular winging. Several relatives were affected but were not available for study. MRI showed involvement of the semitendinosus, tibialis anterior, and peroneus longus muscles, and the biopsy showed rimmed vacuoles and cytoplasmic bodies. The variant was absent from 400 control chromosomes, but segregation could not be assessed.

### Pathogenesis

Edstrom et al. (1990) suggested that the muscle biopsy changes indicated defective myofibrillogenesis, and they discussed the possibility that actin polymerization was defective.

Lange et al. (2005) identified a signaling complex in which the titin protein kinase domain (TK) interacts with the zinc finger protein nbr1 through a mechanically inducible conformation. Nbr1 targets the ubiquitin-associated protein p62/SQSTM1 to sarcomeres. p62 in turn interacts with MuRF2, a muscle-specific RING-B-box E3 ligase that binds the transactivation domain of serum response factor (SRF). Mechanical inactivity caused MuRF2 to move into the nucleus, which reduced nuclear SRF and repressed transcription. The authors suggested that the titin kinase domain controls muscle gene expression and protein turnover in response to mechanical load.

### Diagnosis

Birchall et al. (2005) concluded that subclinical involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus muscles on MRI is specific to autosomal dominant myopathy with early respiratory failure. They stated that MRI allows early noninvasive diagnosis of at-risk individuals, which is otherwise difficult because onset is insidious in late adult life and clinical and pathologic muscle involvement is highly selective.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) suggested that the disorder be considered in patients with adult-onset proximal or distal myopathy and early respiratory failure, even if pathology is nonspecific. They recommended muscle MRI as an initial investigation, with a positive result prompting TTN screening. They noted that whole-exome sequencing offers a reliable, cost-effective way to screen the 363-exon TTN gene.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) recommended sequencing the 119th FN3 domain of TTN in the diagnostic workup of myofibrillar myopathy, particularly when respiratory involvement is early and muscle MRI is supportive. They cautioned that TTN is highly polymorphic: exome data from 239 subjects contained 71 novel coding variants, none of them in the 119th FN3 domain.

### Clinical Management

Pfeffer et al. (2014) recommended cardiac surveillance for patients with TTN mutations.

### Population Genetics

Nicolao et al. (1999) found that affected members of 2 Swedish families shared an identical haplotype, suggesting a common origin.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) found that the probands of 5 new and 3 previously reported families with the TTN C30071R mutation shared a common core haplotype of 171 kb on chromosome 2. The probability of such sharing by chance in 8 unrelated families was less than 10(-6). The small size of the shared region suggested that the mutation is ancient. The authors attributed its frequency, 5.5% of undiagnosed UK patients with myofibrillar myopathy, to a British founder effect.
