### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9), also called hereditary myopathy with early respiratory failure (HMERF), is caused by heterozygous mutation in the gene encoding titin (TTN) on chromosome 2q. ⚠︎

### Description

Myofibrillar myopathy-9 with early respiratory failure (MFM9) is an autosomal dominant myopathy that starts in adulthood. Respiratory muscle weakness appears early, often while patients can still walk, and limb weakness may be distal, proximal, or both (Edstrom et al., 1990; Chinnery et al., 2001; Pfeffer et al., 2012). Muscle biopsy shows myofibrillar abnormalities, including eosinophilic inclusions, rimmed vacuoles, and cytoplasmic accumulation of myofibrillar proteins. Muscle MRI shows a characteristic pattern of selective involvement, including the semitendinosus muscle (Birchall et al., 2005; Pfeffer et al., 2012, 2014). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Pfeffer et al. (2014) found that the clinical, MRI, and histologic features of patients with the TTN C30071R mutation supported a diagnosis of myofibrillar myopathy. They suggested that HMERF caused by TTN mutations be classified nosologically as 'MFM-titinopathy.'

### Clinical Features

Edstrom et al. (1990) reported 16 patients from 7 pedigrees with an unusual adult-onset limb-girdle myopathy. In most cases there was evidence of autosomal dominant inheritance. All patients had early respiratory muscle weakness, and in many of them acute respiratory incapacity was the reason for the first neurologic examination. Other early symptoms were neck flexor weakness and sometimes foot extensor weakness. Electrophysiologic studies confirmed a myopathy, and serum muscle enzymes were normal or slightly elevated. On light microscopy, muscle biopsies showed myofibrillar changes, including plaques that stained strongly with rhodamine-conjugated phalloidin, a marker for F-actin. On electron microscopy, these plaques consisted of moderately dense thin filaments that were related to splitting of Z discs or extended from Z discs. Edstrom et al. (1990) suggested that these changes reflected defective myofibrillogenesis, possibly due to defective actin polymerization. ⚠︎

Nicolao et al. (1999) studied 2 Swedish families with autosomal dominant myopathy, proximal weakness, early respiratory failure, and characteristic cytoplasmic bodies on muscle biopsy. Xiang et al. (1999) described a French family with a similar clinical phenotype and similar muscle pathology.

Chinnery et al. (2001) described a novel autosomal dominant myopathy in a 3-generation family. They assessed 24 individuals clinically, 11 of whom were affected, and examined muscle pathology in 7 affected individuals, including 1 at autopsy. The disorder began in mid-adult life with tibialis anterior weakness. Affected members of the second generation presented earlier, and their disease progressed faster, than those of the first generation. Lung function tests showed progressive global respiratory muscle weakness that was detectable from presentation, with preferential involvement of the diaphragm in some cases. Hip girdle and shoulder girdle weakness developed later. Clinical and pathologic features correlated closely. Clinically unaffected muscles showed minimal pathologic change. Moderately affected muscles showed fiber splitting, eosinophilic inclusions, and vacuoles with basophilic rims. In severely involved muscles, fat and fibrous connective tissue had replaced muscle fibers. The inclusions were Congophilic and reacted with antibodies to desmin, beta-amyloid, and phosphorylated tau protein.

Birchall et al. (2005) performed muscle MRI in 7 patients with autosomal dominant myopathy with early respiratory failure, aged 37 to 66 years (4 male). MRI showed selective early involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus muscles that could not be detected clinically, and closely related muscles progressed at different rates. Birchall et al. (2005) concluded that these findings were specific to the disorder and allowed early noninvasive diagnosis in individuals at risk.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) studied 31 TTN mutation carriers and followed them for up to 31 years. The carriers came from the original family reported by Chinnery et al. (2001), a second family, and a third familial case. Patients presented with predominantly distal, proximal, or respiratory muscle weakness. Age at onset varied widely, from early adulthood to a mild phenotype in advanced age. In nearly all patients, muscle weakness began earlier and was more severe in the lower extremities. Seven patients also had axial muscle weakness. Respiratory function gradually deteriorated over time. No patient had cardiomyopathy despite up to 31 years of follow-up. Muscle MRI in 21 affected patients showed characteristic abnormalities, with semitendinosus involvement in 20, including 3 presymptomatic patients. Muscle histopathology most often showed eosinophilic inclusions (inclusion bodies) and rimmed vacuoles, but findings were nonspecific in a minority of patients.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) described 5 new British families with the TTN C30071R mutation. Onset was typically in the fourth or fifth decade, with tripping and falling, myalgia, and difficulty walking. CK ranged from normal to 1195 IU/L (average 730 IU/L). The disease progressed slowly and involved proximal, distal, and axial muscles. All patients remained ambulant except one, who lost ambulation after about 25 years of disease. Ten of 11 patients had abnormal pulmonary function tests indicating respiratory muscle weakness. Four patients required nocturnal noninvasive ventilation approximately 20 to 30 years after onset. Features not previously documented in this condition included scapular winging, which was often asymmetric, elbow and/or Achilles tendon contractures, and a variable degree of spinal rigidity. Some patients had hypertrophy of the calf or quadriceps muscles. Cardiac function was affected in 2 patients: 1 had mild left ventricular impairment, possibly related to respiratory dysfunction, and 1 had supraventricular tachycardia. Muscle MRI showed early involvement of the semitendinosus and peroneus longus muscles, including in a presymptomatic individual. Muscle biopsies showed myopathic or dystrophic changes, including variation in fiber size, central nuclei, fiber splitting, and increased perimysial and endomysial connective tissue. Some biopsies had rimmed vacuoles and eosinophilic inclusions that stained dark blue with Gomori trichrome. Six of 7 biopsies showed variable cytoplasmic accumulation of desmin, myotilin, p62, and ubiquitin. In 3 biopsies, the cytoplasmic aggregates had a 'cheetah skin' appearance, particularly on myotilin staining. Cytoplasmic inclusions seen with H&E and Gomori trichrome did not fully overlap with regions of protein accumulation.

### Inheritance

Edstrom et al. (1990) found evidence of autosomal dominant inheritance in most of the families they reported. Nicolao et al. (1999), Xiang et al. (1999), and Chinnery et al. (2001) also reported autosomal dominant transmission. Pfeffer et al. (2012) showed that a heterozygous TTN mutation segregated with the disorder. ⚠︎

### Mapping

Nicolao et al. (1999) performed a genome-wide linkage screen in 2 Swedish families with autosomal dominant myopathy with proximal weakness and early respiratory failure. They established linkage to chromosome 2q24-q31, with a maximum combined 2-point lod score of 4.87 at theta = 0 for marker D2S1245. Haplotype analysis placed the disease gene in a 17-cM region between D2S2384 and D2S364. Affected individuals in both families shared an identical haplotype, suggesting a common origin.

Xiang et al. (1999) completely excluded the 2q24-q31 region in a French family with a similar phenotype and pathology. A genome-wide scan gave a maximum 2-point lod score of 2.11, the highest achievable in this family, for markers in a 4-cM region between D2S1272 and D2S1260. This suggested that the gene responsible in this family was likely located on chromosome 2q21.

Chinnery et al. (2001) found that the disorder in their 3-generation family was not linked to any of the known loci associated with distal myopathies.

### Heterogeneity

Xiang et al. (1999) excluded the 2q24-q31 locus, which had been linked to the disorder in Swedish families (Nicolao et al., 1999), in a French family with a similar phenotype. They suggested a second, likely locus on chromosome 2q21 in that family.

### Molecular Genetics

Lange et al. (2005) identified a signaling complex in which the titin kinase domain interacts with the zinc finger protein NBR1 through a mechanically inducible conformation. NBR1 targets p62/SQSTM1 to sarcomeres, and p62 in turn interacts with MURF2, a muscle-specific E3 ligase that is a ligand of the transcription factor SRF. Lange et al. (2005) reported that a human mutation in the titin kinase domain causes hereditary muscle disease by disrupting this pathway.

Pfeffer et al. (2012) combined genome-wide linkage analysis with whole-exome sequencing. They identified a likely causal TTN variant, g.274375T-C, which predicts a cys30071-to-arg (C30071R) substitution, within a 2.93-Mbp haplotype on chromosome 2 that was shared by affected individuals. The mutation segregated with the phenotype in 21 individuals from the original family of Chinnery et al. (2001), in 9 individuals from a second family with the same highly selective pattern of muscle involvement on MRI, and in a third familial case with a similar phenotype.

Pfeffer et al. (2014) noted that 2 TTN mutations had been described in 8 apparently unrelated HMERF families: C30071R in the 119th fibronectin-3 (FN3) domain and R32450W in the kinase domain. They Sanger-sequenced both regions in 127 undiagnosed probands whose clinical presentation was compatible with myofibrillar myopathy. The heterozygous C30071R mutation was found in 5 unrelated probands (3.9%), and in families where segregation could be tested, it cosegregated with the disease. No patient had a mutation at position 32450. Two further probands carried novel heterozygous variants in the 119th FN3 domain: pro30091-to-leu (P30091L) and asn30145-to-lys (N30145K). Neither variant was found in 400 ethnically matched control chromosomes or in public databases. However, P30091L was also present in the healthy 61-year-old brother of the proband, which suggested it might be a neutral variant, although variable penetrance could not be excluded. Segregation could not be tested for N30145K. Pfeffer et al. (2014) considered the pathogenicity of both variants uncertain.

### Diagnosis

Birchall et al. (2005) reported that selective, subclinical involvement of the semitendinosus and obturator externus muscles on MRI allowed early noninvasive diagnosis in individuals at risk. Pfeffer et al. (2012) recommended considering the disease in patients with adult-onset proximal or distal myopathy and early respiratory failure, even when muscle pathology is nonspecific. They suggested muscle MRI as an initial investigation and, if findings are characteristic, screening for TTN mutations, for example by whole-exome sequencing. Pfeffer et al. (2014) recommended sequencing the 119th FN3 domain of TTN as part of the diagnostic workup for myofibrillar myopathy, particularly in patients with early respiratory involvement and supportive MRI findings.

### Clinical Management

Pfeffer et al. (2014) noted that respiratory involvement often leads to the need for noninvasive ventilation. Major cardiac involvement did not appear to be part of the disorder, but because of titin's role in myocardial function, they recommended cardiac surveillance for all patients with TTN mutations.

### Population Genetics

Pfeffer et al. (2014) found the TTN C30071R mutation in 3.9% of 127 undiagnosed British probands with phenotypes compatible with myofibrillar myopathy, and in 5.5% of those with a clinical diagnosis of myofibrillar myopathy. All 8 families with this mutation shared a core haplotype of 171 kbp, and the probability of this occurring by chance in 8 unrelated families was less than 10(-6). Pfeffer et al. (2014) concluded that the mutation's frequency was likely due to a British founder effect. They also concluded that the very small shared haplotype suggested an ancient mutation.
