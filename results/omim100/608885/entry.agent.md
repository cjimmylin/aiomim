### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis with neurologic defects (SDCHCN) is caused by mutation in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes the glucose transporter GLUT1.

### Description

Stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis (sdCHC) is a rare form of hereditary stomatocytosis in which the red cell membrane leaks sodium and potassium, and the leak becomes much worse in the cold. Patients have hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly. They also have neurologic and other defects, including cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder (Fricke et al., 2004; Flatt et al., 2011). Flatt et al. (2011) regarded the disorder as a novel form of GLUT1 deficiency syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied 2 patients with congenital hemolytic anemia caused by increased permeability of the red cell membrane to Na and K. One patient had hereditary stomatocytosis and the other had cryohydrocytosis.

Fricke et al. (2004) described 3 new isolated cases and 1 new pedigree with stomatin-deficient hereditary stomatocytosis, and expanded the description of 1 previously reported case. In all of these patients, red cells leaked cations markedly at 37 degrees C and had markedly abnormal intracellular Na and K concentrations. The cases differed in how the passive K leak depended on temperature. In 3, the leak showed a simple monotonic temperature dependence. In the other 2, the leak was lowest at about 20 to 25 degrees C, so that the cells were extremely leaky at 0 degrees C, which is the cryohydrocytosis phenotype. Fricke et al. (2004) stated that these 2 patients were the only known cases of stomatin-deficient cryohydrocytosis. Both patients had a congenital syndrome of mental retardation, seizures, cataracts, and massive hepatosplenomegaly. The authors suggested that this probably defined a new hematoneurologic syndrome.

Flatt et al. (2011) summarized the features of the 2 previously reported cases of sdCHC. Both had a cold-induced cation leak, hemolytic anemia, and hepatosplenomegaly, together with cataracts, seizures, mental retardation, and movement disorder. ⚠︎

Bawazir et al. (2012) reported an infant who presented with erratic hyperkalemia, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, anemia, hepatic dysfunction, and microcephaly. The infant later developed seizures and had delayed developmental milestones. MRI and CT scans of the brain showed multiple abnormalities, including periventricular calcification. Vision was impaired by both cataracts and retinal dysfunction. The red cells were extremely leaky, which caused the hemolysis. Their loss of potassium depended on temperature, which explained the hyperkalemia as pseudohyperkalemia. The authors described this as the fourth pedigree reported with the syndrome.

### Biochemical Features

Lande et al. (1982) studied red cell membrane proteins from 2 patients, one with hereditary stomatocytosis and one with cryohydrocytosis. On 1-dimensional SDS gel electrophoresis, the band 7 proteins separated into 3 components of approximately 30,000, 28,000, and 26,000 daltons, and the 28,000-dalton component was decreased in both patients. Two-dimensional electrophoresis split this component into 2 proteins, designated 22 x 8 and 60 x 8. In both patients, 22 x 8 was completely absent, whereas 60 x 8 was present as usual. All band 7 proteins were present in normal subjects and in reticulocytosis controls. The authors raised the possibility that loss of this protein was responsible for the increased Na and K permeability.

On Western blotting of red cell membranes, Fricke et al. (2004) found a deficiency of the 32-kD membrane protein stomatin in all of their cases. Immunocytochemistry showed that the deficiency was not complete. On typical blood films, some red cells were stomatin-positive while most were negative, although in 1 case only a minority were negative. All platelets and neutrophils were stomatin-positive.

### Molecular Genetics

Flatt et al. (2011) found mutations in the SLC2A1 gene, which encodes GLUT1, in 2 patients with sdCHC. When expressed in Xenopus oocytes, the 2 mutations caused both loss of glucose transport and a cation leak. Using a 3-dimensional model of GLUT1, the authors proposed possible mechanisms for the phenotypes of the 2 mutations.

In an infant with cation-leaky GLUT1 deficiency syndrome, Bawazir et al. (2012) identified a 3-bp deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deleted isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1.

### Pathogenesis

Flatt et al. (2011) found that red cells lose stomatin during erythropoiesis. The loss occurred during reticulocyte maturation and involved endocytosis.

Bawazir et al. (2012) proposed that the multisystem disease probably results from 2 combined effects. One is deficient glucose transport at the blood-brain barrier, as in typical GLUT1 deficiency. The other is the harmful osmotic effect of a cation-leaky membrane protein in cells that express GLUT1, notably the red cell.

Bawazir et al. (2012) reported an infant with pseudohyperkalemia, hemolysis, seizures, cataracts, and developmental delay. In this patient, they identified a 3-bp deletion in the SLC2A1 gene that deleted isoleucine-435 or isoleucine-436 of GLUT1. The patient's red cells were extremely leaky to cations and lost potassium in a temperature-dependent way.
