### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, language delay, and skeletal defects with or without seizures (NEDHLSS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene (114205) on chromosome 12p13.33.

### Description

NEDHLSS is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by heterozygous variants in CACNA1C and seen mainly without the classic cardiac and other features of Timothy syndrome. Features vary and include developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, hypotonia, ataxia, and epilepsy. Skeletal abnormalities are common, including clubfoot, finger contractures, adducted thumbs, hip dysplasia, kyphoscoliosis, and syndactyly. Rodan et al. (2021) found that patients with de novo nontruncating variants tended to have more severe delay and motor impairment and more often had epilepsy. Patients with truncating variants mainly had expressive language deficits, and autism was more common in this group.

### Clinical Features

Kosaki et al. (2018) reported a 5-year-old girl with cutaneous syndactyly, developmental delay, and pulmonary hypertension. She did not have QT prolongation. The authors stated that she was the first patient with syndactyly and a CACNA1C mutation who did not have QT prolongation, a feature long considered obligatory for Timothy syndrome.

Bozarth et al. (2018) reported a girl (patient 1) with neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy. She was born at 38 weeks' gestation to nonconsanguineous parents of English (maternal) and Ukrainian (paternal) ancestry. At birth she had profound axial and appendicular hypotonia, laryngomalacia with stridor, bilateral clubfeet, bilateral 3-4 finger and 2-3 toe syndactyly, and camptodactyly. Her dysmorphic facial features included micrognathia, downturned corners of the mouth, frontal bossing, and very scant hair, and she had bilaterally inverted nipples. She also had mild optic nerve hypoplasia, severe gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, and anal stenosis. A gastrostomy tube was needed at 8 months because of severe dysphagia. At 18 months she had made no progress in developmental milestones, had profound hypotonia, and could not visually track or fixate. She had severe disturbances of the sleep-wake cycle. Seizures began within a few hours of birth, with numerous subtle tonic seizures and prolonged tonic and tonic-myoclonic seizures each day. At 3 months she was diagnosed with epileptic tonic spasms that did not respond to multiple antiepileptic drugs. Clobazam substantially reduced the seizure burden. Her first EEG, at 5 days, showed diffuse excessive fast activity, and this pattern persisted beyond the neonatal period. Brain MRI at 7 days and 12 months was normal. An echocardiogram and a 24-hour Holter monitor were normal at 13 months.

Rodan et al. (2021) described 25 individuals (13 male and 12 female) from 22 families with heterozygous CACNA1C variants and mainly neurologic manifestations. Age at the most recent follow-up ranged from 10 months to 40 years (median, 6.8 years). All 14 individuals with nontruncating variants had developmental delay or intellectual disability. Nine were nonverbal at the most recent follow-up. Six were nonambulatory, not counting a 10-month-old infant with severe global developmental delay. One had a formal diagnosis of autism and 6 others had autistic features. Twelve of the 14 were hypotonic and 4 had abnormal balance and coordination (ataxia). Ten had epilepsy, with onset between 2 months and 8 years; both generalized and focal epilepsies occurred, seizures were fever-sensitive in 3, and epilepsy was medically refractory in 4. Seizure types included infantile spasms (West syndrome), which progressed to Lennox-Gastaut syndrome in 1 patient. EEG findings were inconsistent across the cohort, although 1 individual had excessive diffuse fast (beta) activity. Brain MRI was normal in all who were imaged, apart from an incidental arachnoid cyst in 1 and prominent cerebellar folia in another.

Among the patients with nontruncating variants reported by Rodan et al. (2021), 12 of 14 had orthopedic abnormalities. Two had bilateral clubfoot and congenital finger contractures, and 2 others had persistently adducted thumbs. Other findings were kyphosis or scoliosis (2 of 14), congenital hip dysplasia (3 of 14), syndactyly (2 of 14), and joint hypermobility (3 of 14). Further variable features included hyperopia (4 of 14), strabismus (4 of 14), tremor (2 of 14), and leg spasticity (1 of 14). Two patients were microcephalic. Three had short stature, including the 2 with clubfoot. Prenatal findings included reduced fetal movement (3 of 14), bilateral clubfoot on ultrasound (2 of 14), and oligohydramnios (2 of 14). On the cardiac side, 1 individual had a mildly prolonged QTc at 7 years, and Holter monitoring showed significant QT prolongation. Another had a possible ventricular conduction delay. The other 9 individuals who had ECGs had normal results. Facial dysmorphism was minor and not consistent between patients.

Rodan et al. (2021) also described 11 individuals from 8 families with truncating CACNA1C variants. Unlike the patients with nontruncating variants, most had essentially normal early motor development and walked independently. Ten of the 11 had delayed expressive language. Five had been diagnosed with autism, 3 of whom were considered nonverbal. Only 1 of the 11 had a history of seizures, and that individual was seizure-free without treatment. Three had hypotonia, 4 had poor balance or coordination, and 2 had tremor. One individual with a de novo truncating variant had paroxysmal dyskinesia. Another, with a paternally inherited variant, had a paroxysmal movement disorder in infancy that later resolved. Cognition ranged from normal to moderate intellectual disability. Recurrent infections were reported in 4 of the 11, one of whom had hypogammaglobulinemia. All 5 ECGs performed in this group were normal.

### Inheritance

Transmission of NEDHLSS is autosomal dominant. The CACNA1C variant arose de novo in the patients reported by Kosaki et al. (2018) and Bozarth et al. (2018), and in all patients with nontruncating variants reported by Rodan et al. (2021). In the cohort of Rodan et al. (2021), 3 truncating variants were paternally inherited and segregated with neurodevelopmental abnormalities in the families. Penetrance and expressivity appeared to vary among family members.

### Molecular Genetics

In a 5-year-old girl with NEDHLSS, Kosaki et al. (2018) used exome analysis to identify a previously undescribed de novo heterozygous mutation in the CACNA1C gene, an arg1024-to-gly (R1024G) substitution. ⚠︎

In a girl with NEDHLSS and neonatal-onset epileptic encephalopathy, Bozarth et al. (2018) used trio exome sequencing to identify a de novo heterozygous c.4087G-A transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a val1363-to-met (V1363M) substitution. The variant affected a highly conserved residue and was absent from gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes. Structural modeling placed V1363 in the fifth transmembrane helix of domain IV, facing the pore-forming sixth helix. Exome sequencing also found a de novo heterozygous terminal deletion of MSH2. ⚠︎

Rodan et al. (2021) identified heterozygous CACNA1C variants in 25 individuals from 22 families with mainly neurologic phenotypes. The cases were ascertained through GeneMatcher and ClinVar, and the variants were found by exome sequencing or an intellectual disability gene panel. Thirteen unrelated individuals had missense variants and 1 had an in-frame deletion, all de novo. Eleven individuals from 8 families had predicted truncating variants, including 1 canonical splice variant. Three of the truncating variants were inherited (paternally) and the rest arose de novo. None of the variants were present in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, or the Exome Variant Server. Whole-cell patch clamp studies in HEK293 cells showed loss of function for L1408V, gain of function for L657F, a modest, nonsignificant current increase for L614P, and no significant effect for L614R. In their discussion, the authors grouped L614P with L614R as having a neutral effect.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Rodan et al. (2021) observed apparent phenotypic differences between patients with nontruncating and truncating CACNA1C variants, while noting that their sample was small. Patients with nontruncating (missense or in-frame deletion) variants, all de novo, had more severe global developmental delay or intellectual disability and motor impairment. About half were nonverbal and nonambulatory, and more than two-thirds had epilepsy. Patients with truncating variants had more selective delay of expressive language with largely normal early motor development, and autism was more common (5 of 11). The study was underpowered to compare phenotypes between missense variants with predicted gain of function and those with predicted loss of function.

In a boy (P7) with NEDHLSS, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1841T-G transversion in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu614-to-arg (L614R) substitution. In a 24-year-old man (P8) with NEDHLSS, Rodan et al. (2021) identified a de novo heterozygous c.1841T-C transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in a leu614-to-pro (L614P) substitution. Rodan et al. (2021) identified de novo heterozygous variants causing a val1411-to-leu (V1411L) substitution in CACNA1C in 2 unrelated patients with NEDHLSS. In a family with NEDHLSS (family 22), Rodan et al. (2021) identified a heterozygous c.5860C-T transition in the CACNA1C gene, resulting in an arg1989-to-ter (R1989X) substitution, in 3 male family members. Two boys aged 2 and 3 years inherited the variant from their father; inheritance was unknown in a 32-year-old male. The boys had language delay and spoke only a few words, and both were diagnosed with autism and had hypotonia. The 32-year-old had a learning disability and was the only individual with a truncating variant who had seizures. The variant lies in the last exon (exon 45 of 45), where it may escape nonsense-mediated decay. ⚠︎
