### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that otofaciocervical syndrome-1 (OTFCS) is caused by heterozygous mutation in the EYA1 gene (601653) on chromosome 8q13.3. ⚠︎

### Description

Otofaciocervical syndrome (OFC) is a developmental disorder with features that include facial dysmorphism, external ear anomalies with preauricular pits, hearing impairment, branchial cysts or fistulas, anomalies of the vertebrae and shoulder girdle, and mild intellectual disability (Pohl et al., 2013). It resembles branchiootorenal (BOR) syndrome, an autosomal dominant disorder of hearing loss, branchial defects, ear pits, and renal abnormalities, but has clinical features beyond those of BOR syndrome (Rickard et al., 2001). In particular, OFC shows trophic changes of the face and shoulder girdle in addition to the BOR malformations, which are developmental defects of branchial arch structures, the middle and/or inner ear, and the kidney. Whether OFC and BOR are the same disease has been debated (Estefanía et al., 2006). ⚠︎

### Nomenclature

Fára et al. (1967) reported a familial disorder that they called oto-facio-cervical dysmorphia.

### Clinical Features

Fára et al. (1967) described a familial form of oto-facio-cervical dysmorphia.

Dallapiccola and Mingarelli (1995) described a sporadic patient with OFC syndrome. The distinguishing features were a long face with a narrow nose, high-arched palate, prominent and dysmorphic ears, long neck, sloping shoulders and clavicles, winged, low and laterally set scapulae, tetralogy of Fallot, and deafness caused by cochlear malformation. The patient did not have preauricular tags, lacrimal duct stenosis, or renal malformations.

### Cytogenetics

Rickard et al. (2001) concluded that OFC syndrome is a contiguous gene deletion syndrome involving EYA1 on 8q13.3. They compared the boundaries of the large deletion in their OFC patient with other reported rearrangements of the region. This narrowed the critical region for Duane syndrome, an eye movement disorder, to the interval between markers D8S553 and D8S1797, a genetic distance of about 1 cM.

### Heterogeneity

Dallapiccola and Mingarelli (1995) noted that some features in the original family, including lateral cervical fistulae, had led to the suggestion that OFC syndrome might be a variant of branchiootorenal (BOR) syndrome. They concluded that their sporadic patient supported OFC syndrome being a unique, clinically recognizable entity, because the patient lacked preauricular tags, lacrimal duct stenosis, and renal malformations but had distinct facial and radiographic findings and markedly downward-sloping shoulders. In contrast, Rickard et al. (2001) concluded from finding an EYA1 deletion in a patient with OFC syndrome that OFC syndrome is allelic with BOR syndrome.

Pohl et al. (2013) found that an autosomal recessive form of otofaciocervical syndrome in a large consanguineous Turkish family was caused by a homozygous mutation in the PAX1 gene (167411).

### Molecular Genetics

Rickard et al. (2001) developed a fluorescent quantitative multiplex PCR dosage assay for EYA1 exons 7, 9, and 13. Combined with microsatellite marker analysis, the assay identified de novo deletions of the EYA1 gene and the surrounding region in 2 patients with complex phenotypes that included features of BOR syndrome. One of these patients, who had OFC syndrome, carried a large deletion of the EYA1 gene region. The authors concluded that this confirmed that OFC syndrome is allelic with BOR syndrome.

In a patient with otofaciocervical syndrome, Estefanía et al. (2006) found a single-nucleotide substitution at a splice site of the EYA1 gene. They concluded that OFC can be caused by point mutations that alter the EYA1 reading frame, not only by major rearrangements, a finding that argued against the hypothesis that OFC results from contiguous deletions of EYA1 and adjacent genes.

In a large consanguineous Turkish family with otofaciocervical syndrome, Pohl et al. (2013) performed whole-exome sequencing on a single pooled DNA sample from 4 affected individuals and identified a homozygous c.497G-T mutation in the PAX1 gene, predicted to cause a gly166-to-val (G166V) substitution within the highly conserved paired-box domain. Filtering found it to be the only novel homozygous variant, and it cosegregated with the disease in the family. In a dual luciferase reporter assay in HEK293T cells testing transactivation of a regulatory sequence in the Nkx3-2 promoter, a direct target of mouse Pax1, the mouse equivalent of the mutation, Pax1 G157V, showed significantly reduced transactivation compared with wildtype Pax1. The authors concluded that the mutant protein had reduced DNA-binding affinity and that the mutation is hypofunctional.
