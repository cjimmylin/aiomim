### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that colorectal cancer (CRC) can be caused by somatic mutation in several genes, including PPARG (601487) on chromosome 3p25.2, KRAS (190070) on chromosome 12p12.1, MYH11 (160745) on chromosome 16p13.11, BRAF (164757) on chromosome 7q34, TCF7L2 (602228) on chromosome 10q25.2-q25.3, RSPO2 (610575) on chromosome 8q23.1, and RSPO3 (610574) on chromosome 6q22.33, and by germline mutation in the FAN1 gene (613534) on chromosome 15q13.3.

### Description

Vilar and Gruber (2010) noted that approximately 15% of colorectal cancers display microsatellite instability (MSI), due either to epigenetic silencing of MLH1 or to a germline mutation in one of the mismatch repair genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2. Most MSI colorectal cancers are sporadic and caused by MLH1 promoter hypermethylation, whereas 2 to 3% of all colorectal cancers are caused by germline mismatch repair gene mutations (Lynch syndrome). Fearnhead et al. (2004) noted that clear-cut mendelian traits such as familial adenomatous polyposis and hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer account for less than 4% of colorectal cancers, and that another 20% are thought to arise in people with a significant inherited multifactorial susceptibility that is not obviously familial.

### Clinical Features

Lovett (1976) took family histories from 209 British patients treated for cancer of the colon or rectum and identified 3 features of an index case associated with a higher likelihood that other family members had also had cancer of the large bowel: multiple benign or malignant tumors in the large bowel, a history of a previous primary carcinoma or adenoma of the large bowel, and early age of onset of bowel carcinoma.

Lovett (1976) described a family with a high incidence of early-onset gastrointestinal carcinoma, with affected individuals proven in 4 generations. The management of unaffected family members was discussed and a follow-up regimen was suggested.

Sivak et al. (1981) reported a kindred with the familial cancer syndrome in which every confirmed affected member had at least 1 primary carcinoma of the colon. The average age at which cancer appeared was 38 years, and 23% of the cancer patients had multiple primary neoplasms. Colonoscopy found no unsuspected cancers in family members at risk.

Budd and Fink (1981) described a family in which 17 members across 3 generations had cancers. Fourteen had adenocarcinoma of the colon, mostly the mucoid variant. Other tumors in the family were endometrial adenocarcinoma, atypical endometrial hyperplasia, uterine leiomyosarcoma, transitional cell carcinoma of the bladder, and renal cell carcinoma.

Bamezai et al. (1984) described a nonconsanguineous Indian Sikh kindred of 46 individuals in which 8 had site-specific colon (cecum) cancer not associated with polyposis. The authors considered the clinical features indicative of a 'cancer family syndrome.'

Burt et al. (1985) studied a large Utah pedigree that had multiple cases of common colorectal cancer but no recognizable inheritance pattern. Pedigree members and spouse controls were screened with flexible proctosigmoidoscopy. One or more adenomatous polyps were found in 21% of family members (41 of 191), compared with 9% of controls (12 of 132) (P less than 0.005).

In a Finnish population-based study, Mecklin (1987) found that patients with cancer family syndrome were young, accounting for 29 to 39% of colorectal carcinoma patients under 50 years of age, and that their tumors were located predominantly (65%) in the right hemicolon. The author concluded that the high frequency of hereditary cases indicated the importance of taking the family history of every new patient.

Vilar and Gruber (2010) summarized the clinical and pathologic profile of MSI colorectal cancers. These tumors tend to be right-sided and diagnosed at lower pathologic stages, with high histologic grade, mucinous features, prominent tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, absence of dirty necrosis, and a Crohn-like host response. Sporadic MSI cases are generally diagnosed after age 70 years, whereas familial cases are diagnosed before age 50 years.

Neklason et al. (2010) described a large Utah kindred (K5275), identified through the Utah Population Database, with a statistical excess of colorectal cancer (familial standardized incidence ratio 12.4) and no clinical or molecular features of known hereditary colon cancer syndromes. In the 4 generations studied, the average age at colon cancer diagnosis was 46 years and the average age at detection of the first adenoma was 49.5 years. Of 32 kindred members who had colonoscopy, 3 had CRC, 9 had adenomatous polyps, and 22 had no adenomas; no one had more than 5 adenomas. No affected member had advanced adenomas, but 2 of the 3 cancers (diagnosed at ages 42 and 35) were metastatic at diagnosis, which the authors took as a subtle suggestion that adenomas may advance rapidly to metastasis.

### Inheritance

Kluge (1964) reported on familial cancer of the colon.

Lovett (1976) took family histories from 209 patients being treated for cancer of the colon or rectum at St Mark's Hospital and obtained death certificates for parents and sibs who had died. Among these relatives, deaths from bowel cancer were more than 3 times the number expected for a comparable group from the general population.

Sivak et al. (1981) found that transmission of the cancer trait in their colon cancer kindred was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Budd and Fink (1981) concluded that the mucoid colonic carcinoma syndrome in their 3-generation family was inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, and suggested that the defective autosomal locus was pleiotropic, causing several types of extracolonic cancer.

Bamezai et al. (1984) found that site-specific colon cancer in an Indian Sikh kindred showed an autosomal dominant transmission pattern, probably with 100% penetrance. They suggested that the responsible gene may have arisen by germline mutation in the first generation and remained heterozygous in affected individuals.

Burt et al. (1985) used likelihood methods to compare random occurrence of cancer and polyps with autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant inheritance in a Utah pedigree. The analysis suggested that the excess of discrete adenomatous polyps and colorectal cancers was caused by an inherited autosomal dominant susceptibility gene, rather than a recessive gene or chance. The authors suggested that this type of inheritance may be more common than previously recognized.

Cannon-Albright et al. (1988) studied 670 persons in 34 kindreds by flexible proctosigmoidoscopy; kindreds were ascertained through a single person with an adenomatous polyp or a cluster of relatives with colon cancer, and had common colorectal cancer rather than familial polyposis coli or nonpolyposis inherited colorectal cancer. Likelihood analysis strongly supported dominant inheritance of susceptibility to colorectal adenomas and cancers, with a gene frequency of 19%. Under the most likely model, adenomatous polyps and colorectal cancers occurred only in genetically susceptible persons (95% CI for this proportion, 53 to 100%).

Houlston et al. (1992) performed complex segregation analysis of a published series of consecutive pedigrees ascertained through patients treated for colorectal cancer. The analysis favored a dominant gene or genes with a frequency of 0.006 and a lifetime penetrance of 0.63.

Ponz de Leon et al. (1992) performed classical segregation analysis on 605 families of colorectal carcinoma probands ascertained through the Cancer Registry of the Province of Modena, Italy. Of these, 28 were suspected HNPCC families and 577 were presumed non-HNPCC. Among the non-HNPCC families, both parents were affected in 11, 1 parent in 130, and neither parent in 436. In the suspected HNPCC families, segregation was compatible with dominant transmission of susceptibility to carcinoma. In families with 1 affected parent, the segregation frequency was almost exactly equal to that in families in which both parents were normal, and a model of dominant transmission of susceptibility through a major gene with greatly reduced penetrance in heterozygotes fitted the data acceptably.

Fearnhead et al. (2004) used multiple colorectal adenomas as a model to test the hypothesis that rare variants account for the multifactorial inherited susceptibility to colorectal cancer (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

### Cytogenetics

Bamezai et al. (1984) observed a low mitotic index and no structural or numerical chromosomal aberrations in the 2 probands available for study from an Indian Sikh kindred with site-specific colon cancer.

Pathak and Goodacre (1986) suggested that a deletion of the short arm of chromosome 12 was a specific abnormality in a limited number of colorectal carcinoma samples. They proposed that fragile sites expressed at these specific chromosomal regions in normal peripheral blood cultures might identify individuals predisposed to develop a particular type of neoplasm.

Using autosomal RFLPs as clonal markers, Fearon et al. (1987) found somatic loss of chromosome 17p sequences in more than 75% of the colorectal carcinomas examined, but such loss was rare in adenomas. The authors suggested that a gene on 17p may be associated with progression from the benign to the malignant state.

Okamoto et al. (1988) used polymorphic DNA markers to look for somatic loss of heterozygosity in 38 tumors from 25 patients with familial polyposis coli and in 20 sporadic colon carcinomas from 19 patients. Allele loss on chromosome 22 was frequent, with some additional losses on chromosomes 5, 6, 12q, and 15. They also found frequent allele loss with C11p11, a DNA probe linked to the familial polyposis coli gene, in both familial and sporadic colon carcinomas, but not in benign adenomas. The authors suggested that more than 1 chromosomal locus may be involved in the development of familial and sporadic colon carcinomas.

Vogelstein et al. (1988) found that chromosome 5 sequences linked to the familial adenomatous polyposis gene were not lost in adenomas from polyposis patients but were lost in 29 to 35% of adenomas and carcinomas from other patients. A specific region of chromosome 18 was deleted in 73% of carcinomas and 47% of advanced adenomas but in only 11 to 13% of earlier-stage adenomas, and chromosome 17p sequences were usually lost only in carcinomas (75%).

Law et al. (1988) studied allelic loss in colorectal carcinomas, microdissecting tumor cells from 24 carcinomas, engrafting 9 more in nude mice, and flow-sorting nuclei from 2 others to obtain purer tumor samples. Loss of heterozygosity was found for chromosome 5 alleles in 6 of 31 informative cancers (19%), for chromosome 17 in 19 of 34 (56%), and for chromosome 18 in 17 of 33 (52%).

Wildrick and Boman (1988) compared matched normal and tumor DNA from patients with sporadic colon carcinoma using a probe for the glucocorticoid receptor (GRL) locus on 5q11-q13, which lies close to 1 of 2 proposed sites of a constitutional deletion seen in a patient with familial adenomatous polyposis. At least 27% of tumors showed tumor-specific allele loss at GRL. The authors concluded that this supports the hypothesis that hereditary and sporadic forms of colon cancer result from mutations of the same gene.

Vogelstein et al. (1989) studied polymorphic DNA markers from every nonacrocentric autosomal arm in 56 paired colorectal carcinoma and adjacent normal colonic mucosa specimens, an analysis they called an 'allelotype.' Allelic deletions were very common: for every polymorphic marker tested, 1 allele was lost in at least some tumors, and some tumors lost more than half of their parental alleles.

Delattre et al. (1989) investigated multiple genetic alterations in colorectal cancers, including changes in DNA content, ras oncogene mutations, and deletions involving chromosomes 5, 17, and 18. Deletions and mitotic abnormalities were both significantly more frequent in distal than in proximal tumors, and deletions were significantly associated with each other and with change in DNA content.

Konstantinova et al. (1991) analyzed chromosome abnormalities in 15 colorectal tumors. Rearrangements of 17p leading to deletion of the arm or part of it were found in 12 tumors, and loss of 1 chromosome 17 homolog in 2 others; loss of chromosome 18 was found in 12 of 13 cases with fully identified numerical abnormalities. Other losses involved chromosome 5 (6 tumors), 21 (5), and 4, 15, and 22 (4 each); gains included extra copies of chromosome 20 (6 tumors), 8q (5), and 13q (6), and rearrangements of 1p and 11q were each found in 6 tumors. Unlike other series, their tumors showed a high frequency of sex chromosome loss (X missing in 4 of 7 tumors from females; Y absent in 5 of 8 tumors from males) and rearrangements of 9q in 8 cases, with the breakpoint at 9q22 in 5. Cytologic signs of gene amplification (double minutes or multiple microchromosomes) were seen in 6 tumors.

Bass et al. (2011) found an average of 75 somatic rearrangements per tumor (range, 5-182) across 9 colorectal cancer genomes. Three tumors showed clustered networks of balanced interchromosomal translocations between pairs of chromosomes, including chromosomes 8 and 20 in 1 tumor and chromosomes 5 and 11 in another, which the authors described as a variant of the chromothripsis pattern. They also found small deletions that removed the first exon of EGFR and the 3-prime section of PTEN.

Among 257 tumors profiled by SNP arrays, the Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) found deletion of 18p and 18q (including SMAD4) in 66% and of 17p and 17q (including TP53) in 56%, 28 recurrent deletion peaks, and 17 regions of focal amplification, including an ERBB2-containing amplicon at 17q21.1 in 4% of tumors and a 100- to 150-kb amplification at 11p15.5 in 7% of tumors that included IGF2 and miR-483, whose expression was elevated. By low-pass whole-genome sequencing of 97 tumors, they identified 3 cases in which the first 2 exons of NAV2 on chromosome 11 were fused to the 3-prime coding portion of TCF7L1 on chromosome 2, with the predicted fusion protein lacking the TCF3 beta-catenin-binding domain; 21 translocations involving TTC28 predicted its inactivation.

In 74 tumor-normal pairs, Seshagiri et al. (2012) found IGF2 overexpression in 8 of 68 colon tumors (12%), 7 of which had focal amplification of the IGF2 locus. They also found KRAS amplification (10 of 74) and MYC amplification (17 of 74). Deletions involved FHIT (22 of 74), APC, PTEN, and SMAD3. Chromosome 18q (carrying SMAD4 and SMAD2) was frequently lost and chromosome 20q was frequently gained.

### Mapping

Sivak et al. (1981) performed HLA typing in a kindred with inherited colon adenocarcinoma, with data from direct typing or inference available for 66 living and deceased individuals. In the offspring of 4 informative matings there were only 3 crossovers out of a possible 17, and the total lod score was 1.06 at a recombination fraction of 0.20.

Daley et al. (2008) performed a genomewide linkage scan of 194 kindreds with familial colorectal neoplasia from the colon neoplasia sibling study. Families were classified into 5 phenotypic subgroups (severe histopathology, oligopolyposis, young, colon/breast, and multiple cancer), and affected, unaffected, and discordant sib pairs were analyzed. Sib-pair linkage statistics and Haseman-Elston regression identified 19 linkage peaks, with notable results for chromosomes 1p31.1, 15q14-q22, 17p13.3, and 21. There was strong evidence for linkage at marker D1S1665 on chromosome 1p31.1 in the multiple-cancer subgroup (p = 0.00007). A linkage peak on chromosome 15q14-q22 was found in the full sample (p = 0.018) and in the oligopolyposis (p = 0.003) and young-onset (p = 0.0009) subgroups; this region includes the HMPS/CRAC1 locus associated with hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome in Ashkenazi families, and the authors provided evidence linking this region in families of European descent with oligopolyposis and/or young age at onset (51 years or younger). In the colon/breast cancer subgroup, they found linkage to BRCA2 and identified a second locus near D21S1437 on chromosome 21 segregating with, but distinct from, BRCA2. Linkage to 17p13.3 at marker D17S1308 in the breast/colon subgroup identified HIC1 as a candidate gene.

Neklason et al. (2010) performed genomewide STR and SNP linkage scans of the Utah kindred K5275 and identified a single major locus on chromosome 13q that segregated with adenomatous polyps and colon cancer. Under an autosomal dominant model, the maximum 2-point lod score was 2.43 at D13S251. Nonparametric analysis gave a maximum NPL score of 24.12 (lod 2.99, p = 0.001) at D13S251. The nonrecombinant region spanned rs1870836 (13q22.2) to D13S265 (13q31.3), about 21 Mb containing 27 RefSeq genes.

### Heterogeneity

Wood et al. (2007) stated that their findings on the genomic landscapes of breast and colorectal cancers have implications for understanding the nature and heterogeneity of human cancers and for using personal genomics for tumor diagnosis and therapy.

In the K5275 kindred, Neklason et al. (2010) excluded Lynch syndrome because 2 colorectal cancers were microsatellite stable, and they found no evidence of polyposis syndromes. Lod scores were negative near the known familial colon cancer genes APC, MYH, CTNNB1, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, STK11, PTEN, and BMPR1A. One marker near SMAD4 was positive, but the other markers near SMAD4 gave negative lod scores.

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) found that, excluding hypermutated cancers, colon and rectal carcinomas had indistinguishable patterns of copy number, CIMP, mRNA, and miRNA changes; however, tumors from the right/ascending colon were more likely to be hypermethylated and hypermutated, with three-quarters of hypermutated samples coming from the right colon.

### Molecular Genetics

Perucho et al. (1981) transfected DNA from cultured human tumor cell lines into NIH/3T3 mouse cells and concluded that 5 of the lines contained a gene or genes able to transform mouse cells, and that at least 3 different transforming genes were present among these 5 lines. Three of the lines, 2 from lung carcinomas and 1 from a colon carcinoma, transferred the same or closely related human genes. The authors suggested that, if these genes were mediating tumorigenesis, overlapping pathways leading to tumorigenesis may arise independently.

By combining DNA hybridization analyses with tissue sectioning techniques, Bos et al. (1987) found ras gene mutations in over a third of human colorectal cancers. Most of the mutations were at codon 12 of the c-Ki-ras (KRAS) gene.

Vogelstein et al. (1988) looked for ras gene mutations and allelic deletions of chromosomes 5, 17, and 18 in 172 colorectal tumor specimens representing different stages of neoplasia: 40 predominantly early-stage adenomas from 7 patients with familial adenomatous polyposis, 40 adenomas from 33 patients without polyposis, and 92 carcinomas from 89 patients. Ras gene mutations occurred in 58% of adenomas larger than 1 cm and 47% of carcinomas, but in only 9% of adenomas under 1 cm.

Vogelstein et al. (1989) found new DNA fragments, absent from normal tissue, in 5 of 56 colorectal carcinomas; these fragments contained repeated sequences of the variable number of tandem repeat (VNTR) type. Delattre et al. (1989) found that the frequency of somatic c-Ki-ras mutations in colorectal cancer did not differ between proximal and distal tumors.

Kikuchi-Yanoshita et al. (1992) looked for p53 mutations and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in 274 colorectal tumors of 4 histopathologic grades. In 160 tumors from 40 patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), p53 mutations were found in 0 of 58 adenomas with moderate dysplasia, 3 of 37 severe adenomas, 6 of 40 intramucosal carcinomas, and 10 of 25 invasive carcinomas. Both mutation and LOH were seen in 1 of 33 severe adenomas, 6 of 24 intramucosal carcinomas, and 10 of 25 invasive carcinomas, and every intramucosal and invasive carcinoma with a mutation had lost the other p53 allele. Similar results were obtained in 114 tumors from 86 patients without FAP: no adenoma had both a p53 mutation and LOH, but both changes were present in intramucosal and invasive carcinomas. Of the 56 p53 mutations found, the codons most often affected were 175, 238, 245, 248, 273, and 282, 4 of which encode arginine; GC-to-AT transitions made up 39 of 54 mutations (72%).

Sarraf et al. (1999) identified 4 somatic mutations in the PPARG gene among 55 sporadic colon cancers: 1 nonsense, 1 frameshift, and 2 missense mutations. Each mutation greatly impaired the function of the protein. The authors concluded that colon cancer in humans is associated with loss-of-function mutations in PPARG.

Rajagopalan et al. (2002) found that somatic BRAF mutations in colorectal cancers occurred only in tumors that did not carry KRAS mutations, and concluded that this gave genetic support for the idea that BRAF and KRAS mutations have equivalent effects in tumorigenesis. Gruber et al. (2002) studied BLM heterozygosity in relation to the risk of colorectal cancer. Bardelli et al. (2003) performed a mutational analysis of the tyrosine kinome in colorectal cancers. Samuels et al. (2004) reported a high frequency of mutations in the PIK3CA gene in human cancers.

Fearnhead et al. (2004) screened germline DNA from 124 patients with 3 to 100 histologically proven synchronous or metachronous colorectal adenomas. All patients were tested for the APC (611731) variants I1307K and E1317Q, and variants in AXIN1, CTNNB1, MLH1, and MSH2 were sought. Thirty of the 124 patients (24.9%) carried potentially pathogenic germline variants, compared with 55 (about 12%) of 483 random controls. The authors suggested that this highly significant difference indicated that many rare variants together contribute to inherited susceptibility to colorectal adenomas.

Parsons et al. (2005) analyzed human colorectal cancers for mutations in 340 serine/threonine kinases and found mutations in 8 genes, including 3 members of the phosphatidylinositol-3-OH kinase (PI3K) pathway. The authors suggested that this mutational activation of a key cell-signaling pathway may provide new targets for therapeutic intervention.

Boraska Jelavic et al. (2006) genotyped the TLR2 (603028) GT microsatellite polymorphism, the TLR2 arg753-to-gln polymorphism, and the TLR4 (603030) asp299-to-gly (D299G) and thr399-to-ile polymorphisms in 89 Croatian patients with sporadic colorectal cancer and 88 age- and sex-matched controls. In patients, TLR2 GT microsatellite alleles with 20 and 21 GT repeats were less frequent (p = 0.0044 and p = 0.001, respectively) and the allele with 31 GT repeats was more frequent (p = 0.0147). The mutant asp299-to-gly allele of TLR4 was slightly more frequent in patients (p = 0.0269). The authors reported associations of the TLR2 GT microsatellite polymorphism and the TLR4 D299G polymorphism with sporadic colorectal cancer.

Sjoblom et al. (2006) sequenced 13,023 well-annotated human protein-coding genes in 11 breast and 11 colorectal cancers. Individual tumors accumulated an average of about 90 mutant genes, but only a subset of these contributed to the neoplastic process. Using stringent criteria, they identified 189 genes (an average of 11 per tumor) that were mutated at significant frequency; most were not previously known to be genetically altered in tumors and were predicted to affect functions including transcription, adhesion, and invasion.

Three groups disputed the statistical analysis of Sjoblom et al. (2006). Forrest and Cavet (2007) stated that the earlier analysis used point probabilities when it should have used P values for the hypotheses being tested; when they reran the analysis with exact P values, far fewer genes had mutation rates that reached statistical significance. Getz et al. (2007) raised 2 statistical concerns and found that, once these were addressed, few genes with significantly elevated mutation rates remained; they concluded that more samples were needed to achieve sufficient power. Rubin and Green (2007) reanalyzed the somatic mutation data using what they considered more appropriate statistical methods and background mutation rate assumptions and concluded that few if any of the newly reported candidate cancer genes had significantly elevated mutation rates.

Wood et al. (2007) sequenced exons representing 20,857 transcripts from 18,191 RefSeq genes in DNA from 11 colorectal and 11 breast tumors. They concluded that the genomic landscapes of these cancers consist of a handful of commonly mutated gene 'mountains' and a much larger number of gene 'hills' mutated at low frequency, and described statistical and bioinformatic tools that may help identify mutations with a role in tumorigenesis.

Alhopuro et al. (2008) screened patients with colorectal cancer, Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, and juvenile polyposis for MYH11 mutations. They found somatic protein-elongating frameshift mutations in MYH11 in 55% of colorectal cancers with microsatellite instability, and 2 somatic missense mutations, R501L and K1044N, in 1 microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer. All of the mutations tested produced unregulated myosin molecules with constitutive motor activity.

Vilar and Gruber (2010) noted that BRAF mutations, mostly V600E in exon 15, are present in 10% of colorectal cancers and do not occur together with KRAS mutations. BRAF mutations are more frequent in sporadic MSI tumors with MLH1 hypermethylation than in hereditary (Lynch syndrome) cases. KRAS mutations, present in about 40% of primary colorectal tumors, are more frequent in microsatellite-stable tumors (those with chromosomal instability) than in MSI tumors. PI3K pathway genes are altered in about 40% of colorectal tumors; PIK3CA mutation rates were reported at 10 to 30%, other genes in the pathway (PDK1, AKT2, PAK4, IRS2, INSRR, ERBB4, PTEN) were also mutated, and PTEN was both mutated and frequently epigenetically silenced in MSI tumors.

In the Utah kindred K5275 with linkage to 13q, Neklason et al. (2010) sequenced 8 candidate cancer-related genes in the linked region (KLF5, KLF12, LMO7, RNF219, SPRY2, GPC5, MYCBP2, and POU4F1) and found no unambiguously deleterious mutation. Polymorphisms that segregated with the phenotype included SNPs in SPRY2, RNF219, LMO7, and KLF12 and a novel SNP in the POU4F1 promoter; whether these SNPs were causative or in disequilibrium with the causal change could not be determined.

Ward et al. (2011) screened 2 European-origin cohorts with sporadic colorectal adenocarcinoma and 2 age- and sex-matched control cohorts for the most common European PKHD1 (606702) mutation, T36M (107C-T). In the first study, they found 6 T36M heterozygotes among 1,601 controls (0.37%) and none among 1,842 colorectal cancer patients (p = 0.01). In the second study, they found 9 among 2,002 controls (0.45%) and 1 among 1,925 cancer patients (0.05%) (p = 0.022). Combined, T36M was found in 15 of 3,603 controls (0.42%) and 1 of 3,767 colorectal cancer patients (0.027%) (p = 0.0002; odds ratio 0.072). The authors concluded that heterozygous PKHD1 mutations are not a risk factor for colorectal cancer and are protective.

Bass et al. (2011) performed whole-genome sequencing on 9 primary colorectal adenocarcinomas and matched adjacent nontumor tissue, at average coverages of 30.7x and 31.9x. The mutation rate was about 5.9 per Mb (range, 4.0-9.8), with a strong predilection for C-to-T transitions at CpG sites. KRAS, APC, and TP53 were mutated in 5, 7, and 6 tumors, respectively, and scored as significantly recurrent. Of 11 rearrangements predicted to produce in-frame fusions, 1 joined the first 3 exons of VTI1A to exon 4 of the adjacent TCF7L2 gene; screening cDNA from 97 primary colorectal cancers found in-frame VTI1A-TCF7L2 fusions in 3 tumors (3%). They also found a TCF7L2 point mutation affecting a splice site in 1 tumor.

Dorard et al. (2011) identified a mutant of HSP110, HSP110-delta-E9, in colorectal cancers with microsatellite instability. The mutant was generated from an aberrantly spliced mRNA, lacked the HSP110 substrate-binding domain, and was expressed at variable levels in almost all MSI CRC cell lines and primary tumors tested.

Montagut et al. (2012) identified an acquired somatic mutation in the EGFR (131550) ectodomain, S492R, in 2 of 10 colorectal cancer patients with disease progression after cetuximab treatment. The mutation prevented cetuximab binding and conferred resistance to cetuximab.

Diaz et al. (2012) retrospectively analyzed serial serum samples from 28 patients with chemorefractory metastatic colorectal cancer receiving panitumumab monotherapy for circulating mutant KRAS DNA. Of 24 patients whose tumors were initially KRAS wildtype, 9 (38%) developed detectable KRAS codon 12/13 mutations in serum during therapy, 3 of whom developed multiple different KRAS mutations (2 in 1 patient and 4 in 2 others); none developed BRAF mutations. The authors suggested that emergence of KRAS mutations is a mediator of acquired resistance to EGFR blockade and can be detected noninvasively.

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) performed a genome-scale analysis of 276 colorectal carcinoma samples, including exome sequencing of 224 tumor-normal pairs, DNA copy number, promoter methylation, and mRNA and microRNA expression, with low-depth whole-genome sequencing of 97 samples. Overall, 16% of tumors were hypermutated; three-quarters of these had high microsatellite instability (MSI-H), usually with hypermethylation and MLH1 silencing, and one-quarter had somatic mismatch-repair gene and POLE mutations. Among 30 hypermutated tumors with complete data, 23 (77%) were MSI-H, including 19 with MLH1 methylation (17 with CIMP); the remaining 7, including the 6 with the highest mutation rates, lacked MSI-H, CIMP, or MLH1 methylation but usually had somatic mutations in mismatch-repair genes or POLE aberrations.

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) identified 24 significantly mutated genes. Among nonhypermutated tumors the most frequently mutated were APC, TP53, KRAS, PIK3CA, FBXW7, SMAD4, TCF7L2, and NRAS, with CTNNB1, SMAD2, FAM123B (WTX), and SOX9 also frequently mutated; all 9 SOX9 mutant alleles in nonhypermutated tumors were frameshift or nonsense, and ATM and ARID1A had a disproportionate number of frameshift or nonsense mutations. In hypermutated tumors, there were frequent mutations in ACVR2A, APC, TGFBR2, MSH3, MSH6, SLC9A9, and TCF7L2, along with mostly BRAF V600E mutations; TP53 (60% vs 20%) and APC (81% vs 51%) were significantly less frequently mutated in hypermutated than nonhypermutated tumors, indicating that the 2 groups progress through different sequences of genetic events. Mutations or amplifications of ERBB family genes were found in 13% of nonhypermutated and 53% of hypermutated cases, including recurrent ERBB2 (164870) V842I and ERBB3 (190151) V104M mutations in 4 and 2 nonhypermutated cases, respectively, which the authors suggested be evaluated as predictors of response to ERBB-targeted agents.

Seshagiri et al. (2012) sequenced the exomes of 72 primary colon tumor-normal pairs (15 MSI, 57 MSS) and also analyzed RNA-seq, SNP-array, and whole-genome data, identifying 36,303 protein-altering somatic changes. In MSS tumors they found 23 significantly mutated genes, including known genes (KRAS, APC, TP53, SMAD4, FBXW7, PIK3CA) and new ones such as ATM, as well as new recurrent mutations in TCF7L2, TET2 and TET3, and ERBB3. TCF12 (600480) mutations were found in 5 MSI colon tumors; 3 of the 5 hit a hotspot at arg603 in the helix-loop-helix domain, and the authors stated that these probably abolish DNA binding. ERBB3 was mutated in 6 of 72 tumors (8%), and all 3 TET genes were mutated. Using RNA-seq, they found a recurrent EIF3E-RSPO2 fusion in 2 of 68 MSS colon tumors (3%) and PTPRK-RSPO3 fusions in 5 of 68 colon tumors (8%). Fusion proteins were secreted and activated Wnt signaling in reporter assays, and the authors concluded that R-spondins probably act as drivers in human colorectal cancer.

Douillard et al. (2013) tested tumors from 639 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer without KRAS exon 2 mutations, enrolled in the PRIME trial, for mutations in KRAS exons 3 and 4, NRAS exons 2, 3, and 4, and BRAF exon 15. A total of 108 patients (17%) with nonmutated KRAS exon 2 had other RAS mutations. These mutations were associated with inferior progression-free and overall survival with panitumumab-FOLFOX4, consistent with findings in patients with KRAS exon 2 mutations.

Segui et al. (2015) performed exome sequencing on 3 members of a family with colorectal cancer that met the Amsterdam criteria for hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer and had mismatch repair-proficient tumors. All 3 carried a germline nonsense variant in FAN1, which encodes a nuclease involved in DNA interstrand cross-link repair. Sequencing of FAN1 in 176 additional families with a history of colorectal cancer, with in vitro functional analyses of the mutant FAN1 proteins found, detected FAN1 mutations in about 3% of families that met the Amsterdam criteria and had mismatch repair-proficient cancers with no previously associated mutations. The authors concluded that these findings link colorectal cancer predisposition to the Fanconi anemia DNA repair pathway.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Vogelstein et al. (1989) found that patients whose colorectal carcinomas had more than the median percentage of allelic deletions had a considerably worse prognosis than the other patients, even though primary tumor size and stage were very similar in the 2 groups. The authors suggested that tumor allelotype may provide a molecular tool for better estimating prognosis in colorectal cancer.

Rajagopalan et al. (2002) reported that BRAF mutation in colorectal tumors was linked to the tumors' mismatch-repair status, which the authors stated emphasizes the role of repair processes in shaping the mutation spectra of human cancer.

Dorard et al. (2011) found that survival and response to chemotherapy in patients with MSI colorectal cancer were associated with the level of HSP110-delta-E9 expression in the tumor, and suggested that HSP110 may be a major determinant of both prognosis and treatment response in colorectal cancer.

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) found molecular signatures associated with tumor aggressiveness, including somatic mutations in APC, TP53, PIK3CA, BRAF, and FBXW7; FBXW7 mutations (38 cases) and distant metastasis (32 cases) never co-occurred.

Seshagiri et al. (2012) found that RSPO2 and RSPO3 fusions were mutually exclusive with each other. The fusions occurred in tumors without APC mutation or copy loss (P = 0.038), except for 1 RSPO2-fusion tumor with a single APC allele, and they were also mutually exclusive with CTNNB1 mutations. Every fusion-positive tumor carried a KRAS or BRAF mutation. Fusion-positive tumors showed upregulation of Wnt target genes similar to APC-mutant tumors, which the authors suggested means the fusions act analogously to APC mutations.

Douillard et al. (2013) found that BRAF mutations were a negative prognostic factor in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer.

### Pathogenesis

Fearon et al. (1987) used X-linked RFLPs to study X chromosome inactivation in colorectal tumors from females. All 50 tumors examined, 20 carcinomas and 30 adenomas of either familial or spontaneous type, had monoclonal inactivation patterns, which the authors concluded supported a monoclonal origin for colorectal neoplasms.

Bos et al. (1987) concluded that ras gene mutations in colorectal tumors usually occur before malignancy develops, suggesting that they are an early event in colorectal tumor development.

Vogelstein et al. (1988) found that ras mutations and deletions of chromosomes 5, 17, and 18 accumulated in a fashion paralleling the clinical progression of colorectal tumors, consistent with a model in which development of cancer often involves mutational activation of an oncogene coupled with loss of several tumor suppressor genes. Fearon and Vogelstein (1990) proposed a genetic model for colorectal tumorigenesis.

Law et al. (1988) concluded that the familial polyposis coli gene on chromosome 5 appears to act as a true dominant for adenomatosis but not as a common recessive gene for colon cancer, and suggested that simple mendelian models in which alleles are lost at a single locus may not be suitable for understanding common human solid tumors.

Delattre et al. (1989) concluded that their data strongly supported the hypothesis that proximal and distal colon carcinomas might differ in the genetic mechanisms underlying their initiation and/or progression.

Kikuchi-Yanoshita et al. (1992) found high p53 polyadenylated RNA expression in every invasive carcinoma, whether or not a mutation or LOH was present. p53 protein immunostaining was negative in almost all adenomas but positive in 86% of invasive carcinomas with a p53 mutation. The authors suggested that changes in both p53 alleles through mutation and LOH, leading to abnormal protein accumulation, are involved in the conversion of adenoma to early carcinoma.

Laird et al. (1995) stated that their findings in ApcMin mice (see ANIMAL MODEL) are consistent with DNA methyltransferase playing a role in generating the C-to-T transitions that occur at high frequency in human colorectal tumors.

Lengauer et al. (1997) found that colorectal tumors without microsatellite instability had a striking defect in chromosome segregation, with chromosomes gained or lost at rates above 10(-2) per chromosome per generation. This chromosomal instability reflected an ongoing cellular defect that lasted throughout the lifetime of the tumor cell and was not simply related to chromosome number. Microsatellite instability was a recessive trait, whereas chromosomal instability appeared to be dominant. The authors concluded that persistent genetic instability may be critical for the development of all colorectal cancers and that such instability can arise through 2 distinct pathways.

Lengauer et al. (1997) found striking differences in the expression of exogenously introduced retroviral genes among colorectal cancer cell lines. Loss of expression was associated with DNA methylation and could be reversed by the demethylating agent 5-azacytidine. Because genetic instability correlated strikingly with methylation capacity, the authors suggested that methylation abnormalities may play a role in chromosome segregation in cancer cells. Jones and Gonzalgo (1997) asked whether altered DNA methylation and genome instability might be a new pathway to cancer.

Cui et al. (2003) noted that loss of imprinting (LOI) of the IGF2 gene, an epigenetic change, is found in the normal colonic mucosa of about 30% of colorectal cancer patients but in only 10% of healthy people.

Batlle et al. (2005) found that most human colorectal cancers lose expression of EphB receptors, which are Tcf4 target genes, at the adenoma-carcinoma transition, even though Wnt signaling remains constitutively active. Loss of EphB expression correlated strongly with degree of malignancy, and the authors concluded that it is a critical step in colorectal cancer progression.

Greaves et al. (2006) showed that nononcogenic mitochondrial DNA mutations established in colonic crypt stem cells spread through the normal human colon by crypt fission: mutated cytochrome c oxidase-deficient crypts undergoing fission and neighboring mutated crypts shared the same mitochondrial genotype, which differed from adjacent cytochrome c oxidase-positive crypts; mutated crypts were clustered throughout the colon, and patches of cytochrome c oxidase-deficient crypts increased in size with age. The authors suggested this may have implications for the growth and spread of colorectal neoplasms.

Alhopuro et al. (2008) showed that the MYH11 R501L and K1044N missense mutations and the frameshift mutations all produced unregulated myosin molecules with constitutive motor activity, similar to the mutant myosin that causes the zebrafish meltdown phenotype. The authors suggested that unregulated MYH11 may affect cellular energy balance or disturb cell lineage decisions in tumor progenitor cells.

McMurray et al. (2008) showed that a large proportion of genes controlled synergistically by loss-of-function p53 and Ras activation ('cooperation response genes') were critical to the malignant state of murine and human colon cells. In gene perturbation experiments, 14 of 24 cooperation response genes contributed to tumor formation, compared with only 1 of 14 perturbations of nonsynergistically responding genes. The authors suggested that synergistic control of gene expression by oncogenic mutations is an underlying key to malignancy and provides a rationale for identifying intervention targets.

Vilar and Gruber (2010) noted that MSI colorectal cancers usually have a stable karyotype, unlike chromosomally unstable tumors. In mismatch repair-deficient tumors, frameshift mutations occur in coding microsatellites of more than 30 genes, including TGFBR2, IGF2R, BAX, MSH3, MSH6, MRE11A, and RAD50. MRE11A mutations are found in 70 to 85% of MSI tumors and may increase sensitivity to irinotecan.

Neklason et al. (2010) performed CGH on a metastatic colon cancer from an obligate carrier of the 13q haplotype in kindred K5275 (diagnosed at age 42). It showed duplication of most of 13q, plus losses typical of sporadic CRC (APC, TP53, DCC, SMAD4). In STR peak-ratio analysis, all 3 family cancers showed a copy number gain at 1 of 3 13q markers, whereas 2 adenomas showed no change. Because 13q is gained in 30 to 50% of CRCs and correlates with metastasis, the authors suggested that the locus may harbor a gain-of-function cancer progression gene.

Ward et al. (2011) hypothesized that reduced fibrocystin activity could cause mitotic instability and centrosome amplification, so that premalignant cells have too much genomic instability to form aggressive tumors, which would explain the protective effect of the PKHD1 T36M mutation against colorectal carcinoma.

Bass et al. (2011) showed that the colorectal cancer cell line NCI-H508 carries a deletion producing an in-frame fusion of VTI1A exon 2 to TCF7L2 exon 5. Knockdown of the fusion transcript with shRNA sharply reduced anchorage-independent growth of NCI-H508 cells but not of fusion-negative DLD-1 cells. NCI-H508 also depended on beta-catenin, and the authors suggested that VTI1A-TCF7L2 is expressed in the setting of activated beta-catenin.

Dorard et al. (2011) found that HSP110-delta-E9 disrupted the normal cellular localization of HSP110 and its interaction with other HSPs, and, in a dominant-negative manner, abolished the chaperone activity and antiapoptotic function of HSP110. Overexpression of HSP110-delta-E9 made cells more sensitive to oxaliplatin and 5-fluorouracil.

Prahallad et al. (2012) found that inhibiting BRAF V600E in colon cancer cells caused rapid feedback activation of EGFR, which allowed the cells to keep proliferating despite BRAF inhibition. Melanoma cells express low levels of EGFR and were not subject to this feedback; ectopic expression of EGFR in melanoma cells was sufficient to cause resistance to PLX4032.

Using mathematical modeling of serial circulating tumor DNA data, Diaz et al. (2012) concluded that KRAS mutations conferring resistance to panitumumab were present in expanded subclones before therapy began, and estimated that about 42 nucleotides (in perhaps about 10 genes) could confer resistance, with each large metastatic lesion expected to harbor hundreds to thousands of resistant cells before treatment. They suggested that resistance is a 'fait accompli' and that combination therapies targeting at least 2 pathways would be required for longer remissions.

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) found that IGF2 overexpression (by amplification or otherwise) was nearly mutually exclusive with PI3K pathway-activating events (PIK3CA, PIK3R1, PTEN), and that high IRS2 expression was mutually exclusive with IGF2 overexpression and PI3K pathway mutations, strongly suggesting that the IGF2-IGF1R-IRS2 axis signals to PI3K in CRC. In integrated analysis of 195 tumors, WNT signaling was altered in 93% of tumors, including biallelic APC inactivation or activating CTNNB1 mutations in about 80%, with FZD10 overexpressed in about 17%; 55% of nonhypermutated tumors had KRAS, NRAS, or BRAF alterations; TGF-beta pathway alterations occurred in 27% of nonhypermutated and 87% of hypermutated tumors; and TP53 was altered in 59% of nonhypermutated cases. PARADIGM analysis showed changes in MYC transcriptional targets in nearly 100% of tumors, suggesting an important role for MYC.

Seshagiri et al. (2012) expressed RSPO fusion constructs in 293T cells and showed that the fusion proteins were secreted. In a TOPbrite luciferase reporter assay, conditioned media containing the fusion proteins activated Wnt signaling, an effect further potentiated by added Wnt; the same activity was seen in HT-29 colon cancer cells. The authors concluded that R-spondins probably act as drivers in human colorectal cancer.

In a review, Schweiger et al. (2013) described colorectal cancer as a complex disease with several layers, involving both the genome and the epigenome, with direct effects on gene and microRNA expression signatures, and noted that many tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes are frequently mutated in colorectal cancer and might drive the malignant transformation.

### Diagnosis

In a pilot study of 172 patients seen at a colonoscopy clinic, Cui et al. (2003) found that IGF2 LOI in lymphocytes was associated with CRC risk. The adjusted odds ratio was 5.15 for patients with a positive family history (95% CI, 1.70-16.96; P = 0.002), 3.46 for patients with adenomas (95% CI, 1.14-11.37; P = 0.026), and 21.7 for patients with CRC (95% CI, 3.48-153.6; P = 0.0005). The authors suggested that LOI, which can be assayed with a DNA-based blood test, may be a valuable predictive marker of an individual's CRC risk.

Vilar and Gruber (2010) described detection of MSI using the Bethesda panel of 5 microsatellite markers (BAT25, BAT26, D5S346, D2S123, D17S250), with tumors classified as MSI-high (2 or more unstable loci), MSI-low, or microsatellite stable. Immunohistochemistry for MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 indicates which gene is most likely inactivated. Loss of MLH1 is typically accompanied by loss of PMS2, and MLH1 promoter methylation is typically accompanied by somatic BRAF V600E.

Diaz et al. (2012) found that in 3 of 4 metastatic colorectal cancer patients with KRAS-mutant archival tumors, the identical KRAS mutation was detectable in pretreatment serum, whereas no KRAS mutations were detected in pretreatment serum of patients with KRAS-wildtype tumors, supporting circulating tumor DNA as a 'liquid biopsy.'

### Clinical Management

Williams (1978) described a further 'cancer family' and recommended that cancer patients from such families receive careful lifelong follow-up, that all members of cancer families be screened for cancer of the uterus and colon, and that they be taught methods of cancer detection and the importance of early diagnosis.

Baron et al. (1999) conducted a randomized, double-blind trial in which 930 subjects (mean age 61 years; 72% men) with a recent history of colorectal adenomas received calcium carbonate (3 g, i.e., 1,200 mg elemental calcium, daily) or placebo, with follow-up colonoscopies 1 and 4 years after the qualifying examination. Among the 832 subjects who completed both follow-up examinations, at least 1 adenoma was diagnosed between the first and second follow-up endoscopies in 31% of the calcium group and 38% of the placebo group (adjusted risk ratio 0.81; 95% CI 0.67-0.99; P = 0.04). The adjusted ratio of the average number of adenomas was 0.76, and the effect was independent of initial dietary fat and calcium intake. The authors concluded that calcium supplementation is associated with a significant though moderate reduction in the risk of recurrent colorectal adenomas.

Sandler et al. (2003) conducted a randomized, double-blind trial in which 635 patients with previous colorectal cancer received either 325 mg of aspirin per day or placebo. Among the 517 patients with at least 1 colonoscopy (median 12.8 months after randomization), 1 or more adenomas were found in 17% of the aspirin group and 27% of the placebo group (P = 0.004). The adjusted relative risk of any recurrent adenoma with aspirin was 0.65 (95% CI, 0.46-0.91), and time to detection of a first adenoma was longer with aspirin (hazard ratio 0.64). The authors concluded that daily aspirin use is associated with a significant reduction in the incidence of colorectal adenomas in patients with previous colorectal cancer.

Baron et al. (2003) performed a randomized, double-blind trial of aspirin in 1,121 patients with a recent history of histologically documented colorectal adenomas, assigned to placebo (372), 81 mg aspirin (377), or 325 mg aspirin (372) daily. Among the 1,084 patients (97%) with follow-up colonoscopy at least 1 year after randomization, the incidence of 1 or more adenomas was 47% with placebo, 38% with 81 mg aspirin, and 45% with 325 mg aspirin (global P = 0.04); relative risks of any adenoma were 0.81 (95% CI, 0.69-0.96) for 81 mg and 0.96 (95% CI, 0.81-1.13) for 325 mg. For advanced neoplasms (adenomas at least 1 cm or with tubulovillous or villous features, severe dysplasia, or invasive cancer), relative risks were 0.59 (95% CI, 0.38-0.92) with 81 mg aspirin and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.55-1.23) with 325 mg aspirin compared with placebo. The authors concluded that low-dose aspirin has a moderate chemopreventive effect on adenomas in the large bowel.

Vilar and Gruber (2010) reviewed a meta-analysis of 32 studies with 7,642 cases, 1,277 of them MSI, which confirmed that MSI colorectal cancers have a better prognosis and spread less often to lymph nodes and distant sites than microsatellite-stable cancers. Data from 6 randomized trials suggested that 5-FU-based adjuvant chemotherapy gives no benefit, and might even be harmful, in stage II MSI tumors. Evidence for a benefit from irinotecan in MSI tumors remained preliminary and conflicting.

Montagut et al. (2012) found that cells with the EGFR S492R mutation, which confers resistance to cetuximab, retained binding to and were growth inhibited by panitumumab, and a patient with cetuximab resistance whose tumor harbored the acquired S492R mutation responded to treatment with panitumumab.

Prahallad et al. (2012) found that colon cancers with the BRAF V600E mutation respond poorly to the BRAF inhibitor PLX4032 (vemurafenib). In an RNA-interference screen of kinases in human cells, blocking EGFR was strongly synergistic with BRAF V600E inhibition, and in multiple BRAF V600E-mutant colon cancers, inhibiting EGFR with cetuximab, gefitinib, or erlotinib was strongly synergistic with BRAF V600E inhibition both in vitro and in vivo. The authors suggested that BRAF V600E-mutant colon cancers, about 8 to 10% of all colon cancers, might benefit from combined BRAF and EGFR inhibitors.

Diaz et al. (2012) found that in KRAS-wildtype metastatic colorectal cancer patients treated with panitumumab, the appearance of circulating KRAS mutations was very consistent, generally occurring 5 to 6 months after treatment initiation (23 +/- 5 weeks), and mutant KRAS was detected before radiographic progression in 3 of 9 cases with an average lead time of 21 weeks. Circulating tumor DNA levels generally paralleled CEA. Progression-free and overall survival did not differ significantly between those who did and did not develop secondary KRAS mutations.

Douillard et al. (2013) found that among 512 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer without RAS mutations, panitumumab-FOLFOX4 improved progression-free survival compared with FOLFOX4 alone (10.1 vs 7.9 months; hazard ratio 0.72; P = 0.004) and improved overall survival (26.0 vs 20.2 months; hazard ratio 0.78; P = 0.04). The authors concluded that additional RAS mutations predicted a lack of response to panitumumab-FOLFOX4.

### Population Genetics

Mecklin (1987) studied all 468 colorectal carcinoma patients diagnosed in 1 Finnish county (0.25 million inhabitants) from 1970 to 1979 and found that the cancer family syndrome type of hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal carcinoma was the most common verifiable risk factor, involving 3.8 to 5.5% of patients. The frequencies of familial adenomatosis and ulcerative colitis were 0.2% and 0.6%, respectively. Because diagnosis relied on family history with cancer in 2 or more generations, the author suggested that the observed frequency of 4 to 6% for the syndrome may be an underestimate.

Cannon-Albright et al. (1988) suggested that an inherited susceptibility to colonic adenomatous polyps and colorectal cancer is common (estimated gene frequency 19%) and is responsible for the majority of clinically observed colonic neoplasms, and that genetic and environmental factors interact in polyp formation and transformation; they supported screening of first-degree relatives of colorectal cancer patients.

Houlston et al. (1992) estimated that dominant susceptibility genes accounted for 81% of colorectal cancer in patients under 35 years of age, whereas by age 65 about 85% of cases were phenocopies.

Vilar and Gruber (2010) noted that population-based studies have reported MSI in 8 to 20% of colorectal cancers, with MSI more common in stage II (about 20%) than in stage III (about 12%) or stage IV (about 4%) tumors. Two small retrospective studies suggested a higher frequency of MSI tumors in African American (20-45%) and Egyptian (37%) populations.

Ward et al. (2011) found that T36M made up 13.1% of PKHD1 mutations in a European ARPKD cohort. Using the T36M frequency in controls, they estimated the carrier frequency of all PKHD1 mutations in the European population at 3.2% (1 in 31), higher than the previous estimate of 1 in 70.

### Animal Model

Laird et al. (1995) studied the effects of reduced DNA methyltransferase activity on ApcMin-induced intestinal neoplasia in mice, combining heterozygosity for the DNA methyltransferase gene with a weekly dose of the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-aza-deoxycytidine. In these treated heterozygous Min mice, the average number of intestinal adenomas fell from 113 in control mice to only 2. The authors concluded that DNA methyltransferase activity contributes substantially to tumor development in this model, and that their results argue against an oncogenic effect of DNA hypomethylation.

Batlle et al. (2005) found that reducing EphB activity in Apc(Min/+) mice accelerated tumorigenesis in the colon and rectum and led to the formation of aggressive adenocarcinomas.

Seshagiri et al. (2012) reported that Rspo2 was a top candidate cancer gene in Sleeping Beauty transposon mutagenesis screens in mouse models of colorectal cancer. In these mouse tumors, transposon insertions in Apc and in Rspo2 were mostly mutually exclusive. One tumor with an insertion near the Rspo2 transcription start site had about 20-fold higher Rspo2 mRNA than adjacent normal tissue.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
KRAS, CODON 12 MUTATIONS

By combining DNA hybridization analyses with tissue sectioning techniques, Bos et al. (1987) found ras gene mutations in over a third of human colorectal cancers, most of them at codon 12 of the KRAS gene. They concluded that ras gene mutations usually occur before malignancy develops and are an early event in colorectal tumor development.

**.0002 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
PPARG, 1-BP DEL, 472A [c.472dela]

In a sporadic colon cancer, Sarraf et al. (1999) identified a somatic 1-bp deletion in the PPARG gene (c.472delA), resulting in a frameshift and deletion of the entire ligand-binding domain.

**.0003 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
PPARG, GLN286PRO [p.Q286P]

In a sporadic colon cancer, Sarraf et al. (1999) identified a somatic gln286-to-pro (Q286P) mutation in the PPARG gene. The mutant retained the ligand-binding domain but could not activate transcription because it failed to bind ligands.

**.0004 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
PPARG, ARG288HIS [p.R288H]

In a sporadic colon cancer, Sarraf et al. (1999) identified a somatic arg288-to-his (R288H) mutation in the PPARG gene. The mutant responded normally to synthetic ligands but showed greatly decreased transcription and ligand binding when exposed to natural ligands.

**.0005 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
PPARG, LYS319TER [p.K319*]

In a sporadic colon cancer, Sarraf et al. (1999) identified a somatic lys319-to-ter (K319X) mutation in the PPARG gene. The truncated protein retained part of the ligand-binding domain but could not activate transcription because it failed to bind ligands.

**.0006 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
MYH11, ARG501LEU [p.R501L]

In a microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer, Alhopuro et al. (2008) identified a somatic arg501-to-leu (R501L) mutation in the MYH11 gene, together with a second somatic missense mutation, K1044N. The mutation produced an unregulated myosin molecule with constitutive motor activity, similar to the mutant myosin that causes the zebrafish meltdown phenotype.

**.0007 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
MYH11, LYS1044ASN [p.K1044N]

In the same microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer that carried the R501L mutation, Alhopuro et al. (2008) identified a somatic lys1044-to-asn (K1044N) mutation in the MYH11 gene. The mutation produced an unregulated myosin molecule with constitutive motor activity.

**.0008 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
BRAF, VAL600GLU [p.V600E]

Vilar and Gruber (2010) noted that BRAF mutations, mostly val600-to-glu (V600E) in exon 15, are present in 10% of colorectal cancers and do not occur together with KRAS mutations, and that they are more frequent in sporadic MSI tumors with MLH1 hypermethylation than in Lynch syndrome cases.

Prahallad et al. (2012) found that colon cancers with the BRAF V600E mutation respond poorly to the BRAF inhibitor PLX4032 (vemurafenib) because BRAF V600E inhibition causes rapid feedback activation of EGFR; inhibiting EGFR was strongly synergistic with BRAF V600E inhibition in vitro and in vivo.

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) found mostly BRAF V600E mutations in hypermutated colorectal tumors.

**.0009 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
TCF7L2, VTI1A-TCF7L2 FUSION

By whole-genome sequencing of 9 colorectal cancers, Bass et al. (2011) identified a rearrangement joining the first 3 exons of VTI1A to exon 4 of the adjacent TCF7L2 gene on chromosome 10, caused by an approximately 540-kb deletion. Screening cDNA from 97 primary colorectal cancers found in-frame VTI1A-TCF7L2 fusions in 3 tumors (3%), all of which also carried APC alterations. The fusion protein lacks the N-terminal beta-catenin-binding domain of TCF4. In the colorectal cancer cell line NCI-H508, which carries an in-frame VTI1A exon 2-TCF7L2 exon 5 fusion, shRNA knockdown of the fusion transcript sharply reduced anchorage-independent growth.

**.0010 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
TCF7L2, EXON 10 SPLICE SITE MUTATION

In 1 colorectal tumor, Bass et al. (2011) found a point mutation in TCF7L2 affecting the splice site at the 3-prime end of exon 10, the exon encoding the HMG-box DNA-binding domain. The authors considered this mutation likely deleterious.

**.0011 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
EGFR, SER492ARG [p.S492R]

Montagut et al. (2012) identified an acquired somatic ser492-to-arg (S492R) mutation in the EGFR ectodomain in 2 of 10 colorectal cancer patients with disease progression after cetuximab treatment. The mutation prevented cetuximab binding and conferred resistance to cetuximab, whereas cells with the mutation retained binding to and were growth inhibited by panitumumab; a patient whose tumor harbored the mutation responded to panitumumab.

**.0012 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
ERBB2, VAL842ILE [p.V842I]

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) found a recurrent somatic val842-to-ile (V842I) mutation in the ERBB2 gene in 4 nonhypermutated colorectal cancers and suggested that it be evaluated as a predictor of response to ERBB-targeted agents.

**.0013 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
ERBB3, VAL104MET [p.V104M]

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network (2012) found a recurrent somatic val104-to-met (V104M) mutation in the ERBB3 gene in 2 nonhypermutated colorectal cancers and suggested that it be evaluated as a predictor of response to ERBB-targeted agents.

**.0014 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
RSPO2, EIF3E-RSPO2 FUSION

Using RNA-seq, Seshagiri et al. (2012) found a recurrent EIF3E(exon 1)-RSPO2(exon 2) fusion in 2 of 68 MSS colon tumors (3%), expected to produce functional RSPO2 driven by the EIF3E promoter. Whole-genome sequencing showed a 158-kb deletion in 1 tumor and a 113-kb deletion in the other, each placing EIF3E exon 1 next to the 5-prime end of RSPO2, and RSPO2 expression was elevated in the fusion-positive tumors. Fusion proteins were secreted and activated Wnt signaling in reporter assays.

**.0015 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
RSPO3, PTPRK(E1)-RSPO3(E2) FUSION

Seshagiri et al. (2012) found PTPRK-RSPO3 fusions, caused by simple or complex inversions on chromosome 6q, in 5 of 68 colon tumors (8%). In 4 tumors, the in-frame PTPRK(e1)-RSPO3(e2) fusion retains the entire RSPO3 coding sequence but uses the PTPRK secretion signal in place of the RSPO3 signal. Fusion proteins were secreted and activated Wnt signaling in reporter assays.

**.0016 COLORECTAL CANCER, SOMATIC**  
RSPO3, PTPRK(E7)-RSPO3(E2) FUSION

In 1 colon tumor, Seshagiri et al. (2012) found an in-frame PTPRK(e7)-RSPO3(e2) fusion encoding a protein of about 70 kD made of PTPRK amino acids 1-387 joined to RSPO3 amino acids 34-272. Fusion proteins were secreted and activated Wnt signaling in reporter assays.

**.0017 COLORECTAL CANCER**  
FAN1, NONSENSE MUTATION

By exome sequencing of 3 members of a family with colorectal cancer that met the Amsterdam criteria for hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer and had mismatch repair-proficient tumors, Segui et al. (2015) found that all 3 carried a germline nonsense variant in the FAN1 gene, which encodes a nuclease involved in DNA interstrand cross-link repair.
