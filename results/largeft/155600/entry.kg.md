## MELANOMA, CUTANEOUS MALIGNANT, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO, 1; CMM1

### TEXT

This entry describes susceptibility to cutaneous malignant melanoma (CMM1), which in many families occurs with distinctive precursor nevi (variously called the B-K mole syndrome, the familial atypical multiple mole-melanoma syndrome, or the dysplastic nevus syndrome), and for which Bale et al. (1989) mapped a susceptibility locus to chromosome 1p36 by linkage analysis, a localization that other studies did not confirm.

### Description

Familial cutaneous malignant melanoma is often accompanied by distinctive precursor melanocytic lesions. Clark et al. (1978) called these lesions B-K moles, and Lynch et al. (1978) described the familial atypical multiple mole-melanoma (FAMMM) syndrome, a cutaneous phenotype that they suggested signifies melanoma risk. Lynch et al. (1983) described FAMMM as an autosomal dominantly inherited susceptibility to multiple atypical moles that tend to transform to cutaneous malignant melanoma. Elder et al. (1980) found identical dysplastic nevi in patients with nonfamilial melanoma and called this the dysplastic nevus syndrome (DNS). They suggested that these patients are also at increased risk of primary cutaneous malignant melanoma.

### Nomenclature

Clark et al. (1978) designated the clinical and histologic features of the distinctive melanocytic moles in melanoma families the 'B-K mole syndrome.' Lynch et al. (1978) used the term 'familial atypical multiple mole-melanoma syndrome' for the concordance of melanoma and multiple large atypical moles in a family. Greene et al. (1980) proposed a nomenclature for precursor naevi in cutaneous malignant melanoma. Elder et al. (1980) introduced the term 'dysplastic nevus syndrome' (DNS) for sporadic, nonfamilial melanoma occurring with dysplastic nevi, which they described as the counterpart of the familial B-K mole syndrome. Elder et al. (1982) gave their definition of the dysplastic nevus syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Familial occurrence of cutaneous melanoma was described in a number of early reports. MOSCHELLA (1961) reported malignant melanoma of the skin in sisters, and SCHOCH (1963) reported a pedigree and cytogenetic study of familial malignant melanoma. Katzenellenbogen and Sandbank (1966) reported malignant melanomas in twins, Smith et al. (1966) reported on familial melanoma, and Andrews (1968) reported malignant melanoma in siblings. Anderson (1971) described the clinical characteristics of the genetic variety of cutaneous melanoma in man. Robinson (1972) and Wallace et al. (1973) also reported familial melanoma.

Reimer et al. (1978) studied 7 consecutive melanoma-prone families. Pigmented lesions with distinctive clinical and histologic characteristics were present in 18 of 20 melanoma patients (90%) and in 24 of 43 first-degree relatives (56%). Recognizing these lesions led to the detection of early-stage melanoma in 6 family members. Greene et al. (1978) suggested that familial melanoma may be related to an inherited syndrome of precursor cutaneous lesions. They noted that recognizing this syndrome in relatives of melanoma patients identifies a subset of family members at high risk, which makes early diagnosis and treatment easier.

Clark et al. (1978) described distinctive melanocytic moles in 37 patients from 6 melanoma families and named the condition the 'B-K mole syndrome.' Among examined family members, 15 of 17 patients with melanoma and 22 of 41 nonmelanoma relatives had the unique moles. Clinically, patients had from fewer than 10 to more than 100 moles. The moles were prominent on the upper trunk and extremities and varied in size (5 to 15 mm), outline, and color. Histologically, B-K moles showed atypical melanocytic hyperplasia, lymphocytic infiltration, delicate fibroplasia, and new blood vessels, occurring within a compound nevus or de novo.

Lynch et al. (1978) described a family that showed concordance for malignant melanoma and a cutaneous phenotype of multiple large moles. The moles varied in size and color (reddish-brown to bright red) and showed pigmentary leakage. The authors called this the familial atypical multiple mole-melanoma syndrome and suggested that the cutaneous phenotype signifies melanoma risk.

Lynch et al. (1980) carried out clinical, pathologic, and genetic studies of 3 kindreds with the FAMMM syndrome. Clinical expression varied widely, from apparent absence of disease through minimal, moderate, and florid manifestations. At the extreme, one patient had 9 separate primary melanomas over 18 years. On histology, the FAMMM moles were compound nevocellular nevi with variable melanocytic dysplasia, more frequent fibroplasia, and chronic inflammation in the papillary dermis. The degree of dysplasia varied markedly both between and within families.

Elder et al. (1980) reviewed clinical photographs of 79 prospectively studied cases of nonfamilial cutaneous malignant melanoma. Fifteen of these patients had moles on the covered buttock area. Seven of the 15 had large, clinically atypical nevi, and biopsies of these nevi showed severe melanocytic dysplasia. Residual elements of melanocytic dysplasia were found in 5 of the primary melanomas in this group. The dysplastic nevi were identical to the precursor lesion of melanoma in B-K mole syndrome families, and the authors stated that this was the first description of this form of dysplasia in nonfamilial melanoma.

Kraemer et al. (1983) wrote about dysplastic nevi in relation to the risk of cutaneous melanoma.

Lynch et al. (1983) described the FAMMM moles. They ranged in color from black to brown, tan, red, or pink and were occasionally variegated. These compound nevi were macular or papular, had regular or irregular borders, measured 1 cm or more, and were found mainly on areas not exposed to the sun. Their number ranged from none to more than 100 per patient, and they had a propensity to transform to cutaneous malignant melanoma. Histopathologically, the nevi showed dysplastic changes in melanocytes, fibroplasia, focal chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate, and new blood vessel formation in the papillary dermis, although not every lesion showed these findings. In a single FAMMM kindred, some members had minimal or absent cutaneous expression, and the family had a more diverse range of neoplasms. The authors attributed this to pleiotropic effects of the cancer-prone FAMMM genotype.

Arndt (1984) discussed congenital nevi and dysplastic nevi as precursors to malignant melanoma. Greene et al. (1985) described the familial dysplastic nevus syndrome and characterized dysplastic nevi as acquired precursors of cutaneous malignant melanoma. Bale et al. (1985) described dysplastic nevi as a well-characterized precursor of hereditary cutaneous malignant melanoma.

Rhodes et al. (1985) studied 39 probands with small (less than 40 mm) congenital nevomelanocytic nevi (CNN) who had been registered by photograph in 1982 at a children's hospital dermatology service. Of the probands' 65 sibs, 8 (12.3%) also had one or more small CNN. This rate was 11 times the 1.1% prevalence of CNN in newborn infants in the same city. In 5 of the 39 families, a parent also had CNN.

Kopf et al. (1986) analyzed consecutive questionnaires completed prospectively by 1,169 patients with cutaneous malignant melanoma, 69 of whom reported a family history of melanoma. Compared with nonfamilial cases, familial melanoma was significantly associated with younger age at first diagnosis, smaller lesion diameter, and lower Clark level. Familial cases also had less frequent nonmelanoma skin cancer and a lower prevalence of noncutaneous cancer. The authors suggested that greater awareness of melanoma among family members could explain some of these observations.

Hartley et al. (1987) described 7 cases of malignant melanoma in close relatives of children with osteosarcoma and chondrosarcoma.

Vasen et al. (1989) studied 9 families with the dysplastic nevus syndrome identified since 1982 in the Leiden area of the Netherlands. In these families, 38 persons had a total of 50 primary melanomas. Nineteen melanomas were diagnosed before a screening program began, 11 were found at the first examination of the families, and 20 were found during follow-up.

In 6 melanoma-prone families studied by Bale et al. (1989), 34 family members had cutaneous malignant melanoma, and 31 of these also had histologically confirmed dysplastic nevi. Another 24 family members had dysplastic nevi alone.

Kefford et al. (1991) studied 119 members of 8 newly described Australian families with hereditary melanoma. Thirty had cutaneous malignant melanoma, and only 8 of these also had dysplastic nevi. Another 15 family members had dysplastic nevi without melanoma. The pedigrees fell into 3 groups: hereditary melanoma with no associated dysplastic nevi, hereditary melanoma in which occasional affected individuals had dysplastic nevi, and hereditary melanoma with dysplastic nevi.

Tucker et al. (2002) followed 33 melanoma-prone families for up to 25 years. Each family had more than 2 living members with invasive melanoma. A total of 844 family members were examined and photographed, melanocytic lesions were photographed in sequence, and lesions were examined histologically when removed. All of the families had members with dysplastic nevi and melanoma. Most dysplastic nevi remained stable or regressed, and few changed in a way that should raise concern for melanoma. The authors concluded that melanomas can be found early with careful surveillance.

### Other Features

Potter et al. (2002) described a 32-year-old woman with a history of metastatic melanoma who developed melanoma-associated retinopathy with reduced vision in both eyes. Electroretinography showed a 'negative' B-wave, and Goldmann perimetry showed paracentral and central scotomas. Western blotting of her serum showed antibodies to retinal transducin, but immunohistochemistry showed no immunoreactivity to retinal bipolar cells. The authors concluded that melanoma-associated retinopathy can be related to a variety of antiretinal antibodies, and they identified transducin as a novel antigen in this condition.

Alexander et al. (2004) studied contrast sensitivity in 2 patients with melanoma-associated retinopathy, aged 57 and 61 years, who had normal Snellen visual acuity. Both had lower contrast sensitivity than 10 normal observers. With the steady-pedestal paradigm, the loss was greatest at the lowest spatial frequency (0.25 cpd). With the pulsed-pedestal paradigm, it was greatest at 1 cpd. Sensitivity was normal at 8 cpd. The authors concluded that the deficits were not specific to the magnocellular pathway but depended on spatial frequency, and that they were consistent with retinal bipolar cell dysfunction.

Constantinescu et al. (2007) identified 5 cases of malignant melanoma in the DATATOP clinical trial cohort of patients with early Parkinson disease, compared with 1.5 expected for a standard healthy population. The standardized event ratio was 3.3 (95% CI, 1.1-7.8). They found no association between levodopa therapy and melanoma incidence.

Ferreira et al. (2007) carried out a cross-sectional survey in which a dermatologist examined 150 Parkinson disease (PD) patients and 146 age-matched controls. Neoplastic or preneoplastic skin lesions were found in 35 PD patients (23.3%) and 20 controls (13.7%) (OR 1.92; 95% CI 1.05-3.51). The difference was no longer significant after adjustment for gender. The authors judged the results nonconclusive but consistent with an increased risk of skin cancer in PD.

Gao et al. (2009) examined natural hair color in early adulthood and PD risk in 38,641 US men and 93,661 US women. They noted that red hair is a risk factor for melanoma. PD risk rose as hair color became lighter: compared with black hair, the pooled adjusted relative risks were 1.40 for brown, 1.61 for blond, and 1.93 for red hair. In a nested case-control study (272 PD cases and 1,185 controls), the Cys/Cys genotype of the MC1R arg151-to-cys (R151C) variant was associated with higher PD risk than Arg/Arg (adjusted relative risk 3.15; 95% CI 1.06-9.37). The authors described this variant as the SNP most consistently associated with melanoma risk. They suggested that the links of both diseases to light hair color and to this variant may partly explain why melanoma occurs more often in people with PD, rather than this being an adverse effect of levodopa. Herrero Hernandez (2009) discussed pigmentation as a clue to the relationship between Parkinson disease and melanoma.

### Inheritance

Several early reports addressed whether malignant melanoma is hereditary. These included SALAMON et al. (1963), TURKINGTON (1965), Anderson et al. (1967), Lynch and Krush (1968), and Wallace et al. (1971).

Reimer et al. (1978) concluded that the precursor-lesion syndrome in 7 melanoma-prone families appeared to be an autosomal dominant trait. Lynch et al. (1978) found that transmission of the atypical multiple mole phenotype in their reported family, and in several other families, was consistent with a simple autosomal dominant factor. Lynch et al. (1980) observed vertical transmission of cutaneous malignant melanoma and/or FAMMM moles, including father-to-son transmission, with no sex predilection, in 3 kindreds. They concluded that this was consistent with an autosomal dominant gene with markedly variable expressivity.

Pellegris et al. (1982) concluded that their findings in 6 families were consistent with 2 complementary factors, one of them in the HLA complex, promoting familial malignant melanoma with dominant inheritance and high penetrance. Happle et al. (1982) argued in favor of polygenic inheritance of precursor nevi.

Greene et al. (1983) did segregation analyses on 401 family members from 14 high-risk kindreds with multiple cases of cutaneous malignant melanoma (CMM) and the dysplastic nevus syndrome (DNS). Pedigree analysis fit an autosomal dominant mode of inheritance for the familial CMM trait. A similar model probably also applied to DNS, but segregation analysis could not confirm a major locus for it.

Using a life-table and disease-free survival approach, Bale et al. (1986) estimated the lifetime incidence of melanoma in sibs and offspring of individuals with dysplastic nevi to be 46%. They considered this consistent with highly penetrant autosomal dominant inheritance. Bale and Chakravarti (1987) reported evidence for autosomal dominance of a CMM/dysplastic nevus gene and for its pleiotropy, meaning that one gene accounts for both traits. Pascoe (1987) discussed the inheritance of CMM and DNS.

Traupe et al. (1989) estimated mutation rates to test whether the dysplastic nevus syndrome associated with familial melanoma is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. The rate estimated directly from sporadic occurrence was exceedingly high (0.9%-2.5%), whereas the rate estimated with Haldane's formula was 0.007% to 0.02%. From these figures they calculated that newly arising mutations would outnumber eliminated ones by 100:1, so that within a few generations 10% of the world population would be affected. They concluded that this apparent lack of genetic equilibrium is not compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance of the dysplastic nevus syndrome.

Kefford et al. (1991) found an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance in all 8 Australian families with hereditary melanoma that they studied.

### Cytogenetics

Pathak et al. (1983) karyotyped 9 established melanoma cell lines from 2 female and 6 male patients. Every line had an aneuploid stemline chromosome number, and G-banding showed chromosome 6 involved in marker chromosomes in all of them. Including previously reported cases, 38 of 47 karyotyped cell lines or primary melanoma cells (80.8%) had a marker chromosome involving chromosome 6. Becher et al. (1983) also found that chromosome 6 was frequently involved in structural aberrations in malignant melanoma cells. Trent et al. (1983) found deletion or translocation of 6q in 4 of 5 melanomas and suggested that 6q may be a specific site of chromosome aberration in malignant melanoma.

Becher et al. (1983) analyzed short-term cultures of 4 malignant melanomas with Giemsa banding. One tumor was near-diploid and 3 were near-triploid, and they identified 27 abnormal chromosomes, mostly translocations, followed by deletions and isochromosomes. Every case had markers derived from chromosomes 1 and 6, and 3 cases had abnormalities of chromosome 7. Each marker was unique to its case. The authors suggested that abnormalities of chromosomes 6 and 7 may be characteristic of melanoma, and that chromosome 1 aberrations may be a general feature of human neoplasia.

Balaban et al. (1984) studied melanocytic lesions from 17 patients: 5 nevi (3 of them dysplastic), 1 early primary melanoma in radial growth phase, 1 advanced primary melanoma with multiple metastases, and 10 metastatic lesions. The nevi had normal karyotypes, and each tumor had a predominantly abnormal karyotype. The early melanoma was pseudodiploid and carried a 6p;22 translocation. Ten of 11 advanced melanomas had aberrations of chromosome 1. In 8 lesions, deletions or translocations of 1p involved the proximal segment 1p12-1p22 a total of 9 times. Six advanced tumors had extra 7q material, and 9 melanomas had chromosome 6 alterations, including 3 with extra copies of 6p. In 1 advanced tumor, the primary lesion and 5 metastases removed over 18 months had nearly identical karyotypes, which the authors took as evidence that the neoplasm was clonal. They suggested that genes important in melanoma carcinogenesis are located on proximal 1p, on 7q, and on chromosome 6.

Ochi et al. (1984) studied a metastatic melanoma from a 54-year-old white woman cytogenetically on 3 occasions. They found 2 clones, one hypodiploid and one hypertriploid, which shared the marker chromosomes der(6)t(6; 17) and der(17)t(1; 17). Rey et al. (1985) found a pseudodiploid modal chromosome number and 5 marker chromosomes, including an i(6p), in a melanoma metastatic to the brain.

Dracopoli et al. (1987) screened RFLPs at 61 autosomal and 7 X-linked loci in cell lines from 6 independent metastases and in autologous B cells from a patient with melanoma. Tumor cells showed loss of heterozygosity at 8 of 16 informative autosomes and at all 3 informative X-linked loci, generally without karyotypic abnormalities in the affected regions. Dracopoli et al. (1989) found loss of heterozygosity at chromosome 1p loci in 15 of 35 (43%) melanomas and 11 of 21 (52%) melanoma cell lines.

Millikin et al. (1991) used RFLP analysis with 5 markers on 6q and 1 on 6p to study 20 paired tumor and normal DNA samples from melanoma patients. They found loss of heterozygosity on 6q at 21 of 53 informative loci (40%). Five patients had allele losses consistent with loss of the entire 6q arm or of an entire chromosome 6, and 4 others had terminal 6q deletions. The highest frequency of 6q allelic loss (60%) was in the region defined by c-MYB and ESR (6q22-23 and 6q24-27). LOH at loci on chromosomes 1, 11, 16, and 17 occurred in only 5% of cases. The authors concluded that loss of 6q sequences is a nonrandom and possibly biologically relevant event in melanoma.

### Mapping

Hawkins et al. (1981) reported evidence for linkage between HLA and malignant melanoma. Pellegris et al. (1982) did HLA-A, -B, and -C typing in 6 families with familial malignant melanoma and found the HLA-B12 antigen in 5 of them. Linkage could be analyzed in 3 families and was possible in 2 but not in the third.

Greene et al. (1983) did linkage analysis with 23 genetic markers in 14 kindreds with familial melanoma and DNS. They suggested that a DNS/CMM susceptibility gene may lie on the short arm of chromosome 1, within 30 map units of the Rh locus (maximum lod score 2.00).

Bale et al. (1985) did detailed HLA typing in 11 families with hereditary CMM and dysplastic nevi and re-analyzed published data from 19 other families. In the pooled data from all 30 families, with CMM as the trait, linkage to HLA was rejected for all recombination fractions below 40%. When the combined CMM/dysplastic nevus trait was analyzed in 18 families, there was strong evidence against linkage to the major histocompatibility complex.

Bale et al. (1989) studied 99 relatives and 26 spouses in 6 melanoma-prone families. Cosegregation analysis of the CMM-dysplastic nevus trait with 26 polymorphic DNA markers on chromosome 1p placed a melanoma susceptibility gene between the anonymous marker D1S47 and the pronatrodilatin gene (PND) in band 1p36. The odds were greater than 260,000:1 in favor of linkage.

Cannon-Albright et al. (1990) genotyped 3 Utah kindreds with multiple cases of melanoma for PND and D1S47. They found no evidence for linkage whether melanoma alone or a combined melanoma/dysplastic nevus phenotype was analyzed. Multipoint analysis excluded the locus from a 55-cM region containing the PND-D1S47 interval. Kefford et al. (1991) found strongly negative linkage between the melanoma/dysplastic nevus trait and 8 chromosome 1p markers, including D1S47 and PND, in 8 Australian families. This effectively excluded the putative gene in these families from distal 1p. Nancarrow et al. (1992) studied 7 Australian kindreds, 3 of them the largest reported worldwide. They excluded a melanoma gene from a 40-cM region spanning the D1S47-PND interval and extending about 15 cM on either side, and also from about 20 cM around the MYCL1/D1S57 loci.

Fountain et al. (1992) performed loss-of-heterozygosity studies in melanoma with 8 markers spanning 9pter to proximal 9q. Two or more loci were hemizygously reduced in 12 of 14 (86%) informative metastatic melanoma tumor and cell line DNAs. Homozygous deletions of D9S126 were found in 2 of 20 (10%) melanoma cell lines, defining a critical region of 2-3 Mb on 9p21 that likely contains a melanoma tumor-suppressor gene. Several candidate genes, including IFNB1, the IFNA gene cluster, GGTB2, and the TYRP locus, were eliminated because they lie outside the homozygously deleted regions.

Goldstein et al. (1993) studied markers D1S47, PND, and D1S160 in 7 new families (set B) and in updated versions of 6 previously reported families (set A) with CMM/dysplastic nevi. With only CMM considered affected, the maximum lod score was 3.12 at theta = 0.10 with D1S47 and 1.76 at theta = 0.10 with D1S160, and PND showed no evidence for linkage. Under CMM/DN models, there was strong evidence for linkage to all 3 markers in set A but not in set B.

Goldstein et al. (1996) carried out two-trait-locus, two-marker-locus linkage analysis in 19 CMM/DN kindreds that had previously shown significant evidence of linkage and heterogeneity to both 1p and 9p. For CMM alone, the highest lod score came from a single-locus heterogeneity model (Z = 8.48 at theta = 0.0). Lod scores on 9p were about 2 times greater than on 1p. After conditioning on linkage to the other locus, only the 9p locus consistently showed significant linkage to CMM alone.

Gillanders et al. (2003) carried out a genomewide linkage scan in 49 Australian pedigrees with at least 3 cases of CMM, in which CDKN2A and CDK4 involvement had been excluded. The highest 2-point parametric lod score was 1.82 (theta = 0.2) at D1S2726 on 1p22. In 9 families selected for early age at onset, the parametric lod score at D1S2779 was 4.65 (theta = 0) and the nonparametric lod score was 4.19. Further typing in this subset gave 7 adjacent markers with lod scores above 3, the highest being a parametric lod of 4.95 (theta = 0) and a nonparametric lod of 5.37 at D1S2776. In 33 additional multiplex families from several continents, there was further evidence for linkage to 1p22, again strongest in families with the earliest mean age at diagnosis. A nonparametric ordered sequential analysis reached a lod score of 6.43 at D1S2779 when the 15 families with the earliest onset were included. The authors concluded that there was significant evidence for a novel melanoma susceptibility gene in 1p22.

Bishop et al. (2009) carried out a genome-wide association study of melanoma in the GenoMEL consortium. They typed 317K tagging SNPs in 1,650 cases and 4,336 controls of European ancestry. Replication used 1,149 cases and 964 controls from GenoMEL and a population-based Leeds, UK, study of 1,163 cases and 903 controls. Five loci reached P < 5 x 10(-7), and 3 were replicated: 16q24 encompassing MC1R, 11q14-q21 encompassing TYR, and 9p21 adjacent to MTAP and flanking CDKN2A. The strongest association was at 16q24, where rs258322 had a combined P of 2.54 x 10(-27) and a per-allele odds ratio of 1.67 (95% CI 1.52-1.83). rs8059973 appeared to be associated independently. On chromosome 11, rs1393350 gave the strongest evidence (P = 2.41 x 10(-14); OR 1.29), and the TYR coding variant rs1126809 was also associated (OR 1.27, P = 1.17 x 10(-6)). At 9p21, rs7023329 within MTAP reached P = 4.03 x 10(-7) (OR 0.85), and a second independent association was with rs1011970, which lies 246 kb centromeric within ANRIL. The authors noted that common variants at 9p21 had not previously been associated with melanoma.

Falchi et al. (2009) carried out a genome-wide association study of total body nevus count in 1,524 healthy adult female twins from the TwinsUK registry. They replicated the results in 4,107 adolescents of European ancestry from the Brisbane Twin Nevus Study. The strongest association was with rs4636294 in the 5-prime UTR of MTAP on 9p21, next to CDKN2A (combined P = 3.4 x 10(-15)). A second locus on 22q13.1 was led by rs2284063 (combined P = 3.4 x 10(-8)) in the intron between exons 2 and 3 of PLA2G6. Each locus explained roughly 0.7 to 3.0% of the variance in nevus count.

### Heterogeneity

Pellegris et al. (1982) suggested 2 types of familial malignant melanoma. The more frequent type, seen in 5 of 6 families, apparently segregated with the HLA complex, and the other, seen in 1 family, did not. Bale et al. (1985) found significant heterogeneity among subgroups of families in HLA linkage analysis. They suggested that preferential reporting of positive linkage results and misclassification of susceptibility status may have contributed to the earlier belief that the gene was linked to HLA.

Hartley et al. (1987) discussed an association between malignant melanoma and certain childhood cancers, namely adrenal cortical carcinoma, osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and retinoblastoma. They proposed that in some families melanoma may be another manifestation of the gene defect causing susceptibility to the tumors of the SBLA cancer family syndrome.

Cannon-Albright et al. (1990) suggested that diagnostic or genetic heterogeneity could explain why they found no 1p36 linkage in Utah kindreds. Kefford et al. (1991) suggested that hereditary melanoma is heterogeneous both genetically and in its association with the dysplastic nevus syndrome. Nancarrow et al. (1992) noted that there was considerable controversy over linkage of a CMM/dysplastic nevus gene to distal 1p, and that no study had supported linkage of familial melanoma alone to these markers.

Goldstein et al. (1993) found significant evidence for genetic heterogeneity of CMM/DN when families were split by the proportion of CMM/DN cases (with D1S47 and D1S160) and by the occurrence of immune-related tumors (with D1S160). Only a subset of families appeared linked to 1p. They concluded that diagnostic, clinical, and genetic heterogeneity likely explained earlier failures to confirm 1p linkage. Goldstein et al. (1996) suggested that a 1p locus (or loci) contributed to both CMM and CMM/DN, whereas a 9p locus contributed more to CMM alone. Under both 2-locus models, 1p lod scores were higher for CMM/DN than for CMM alone (epistatic, 4.63 vs 3.83; heterogeneity, 4.94 vs 3.80). In contrast, 9p lod scores were substantially lower for CMM/DN (epistatic, 4.64 vs 7.06; heterogeneity, 5.38 vs 7.99). Five kindreds showed evidence of linkage (Z > 0.7) to both regions.

Gillanders et al. (2003) noted that mutations in CDKN2A and CDK4 account for only 20 to 25% of families with multiple cases of CMM, and they localized an additional susceptibility locus to 1p22 in families without CDKN2A or CDK4 involvement.

Curtin et al. (2005) compared genome-wide DNA copy-number changes and BRAF and N-RAS mutation status in 126 melanomas. The tumors fell into 4 groups with different ultraviolet exposure: 30 from skin with chronic sun-induced damage, 40 from skin without such damage, 36 acral, and 20 mucosal. The groups differed significantly in regional copy-number changes and BRAF mutation frequencies. Copy-number changes alone classified samples into the 4 groups with 70% accuracy, separated the 2 sun-exposure groups with 84% accuracy, and separated acral from mucosal melanoma with 89% accuracy.

Bishop et al. (2009) also confirmed melanoma associations at a chromosome 20 region containing the pigmentation gene ASIP (rs1885120; OR 1.58, P = 1.25 x 10(-8)) and at a chromosome 22 region previously identified in a GWAS of nevus count (rs2284063; P = 2.40 x 10(-9)).

### Molecular Genetics

Fung et al. (2003) described eMelanoBase, an online locus-specific database set up by the international Melanoma Genetics Consortium. It collects germline variants at loci implicated in familial melanoma susceptibility, mainly CDKN2A and rarely CDK4. They noted that CDKN2A encodes 2 unrelated proteins from alternatively spliced transcripts read in different frames: p16INK4A from the alpha transcript (exons 1-alpha, 2, and 3) and p14ARF from the beta transcript (exons 1-beta and 2). Exon 2 mutations can impair both proteins.

Horn et al. (2013) used linkage analysis and high-throughput sequencing in a melanoma-prone family and identified a germline mutation in the promoter of the TERT gene (187270) that segregated with disease. The mutation created a new binding motif for Ets transcription factors and ternary complex factors (TCFs) near the transcription start site, and it increased transcription up to twofold in reporter gene assays.

Association Studies. In European populations comprising 2,121 individuals with cutaneous melanoma, 2,163 with basal cell carcinoma, and over 40,000 controls, Gudbjartsson et al. (2008) found that a haplotype near ASIP conferred risk of cutaneous melanoma (OR = 1.45, P = 1.2 x 10(-9)). The TYR (606933) variant encoding arg402-to-gln (R402Q), previously shown to affect eye color and tanning response, conferred risk of cutaneous melanoma (OR = 1.21, P = 2.8 x 10(-7)). An eye color variant in TYRP1 was also associated with melanoma (OR = 1.15, P = 4.6 x 10(-4)). All of these associations were robust to adjustment for pigmentation.

In a Spanish case-control study of 131 consecutive melanoma patients and 245 controls frequency-matched for sex and age, Fernandez et al. (2008) genotyped 23 SNPs in 6 pigmentation pathway genes. The variant allele of the c.1122C-G (phe374-to-leu, F374L; rs16891982) SNP in SLC45A2 (606202) was associated with protection from melanoma (OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.24-0.70; P = 0.008 after adjustment for multiple testing).

Rafnar et al. (2009) studied rs401681 at the TERT-CLPTM1L locus on chromosome 5p15.33. Its C allele was a risk allele for basal cell carcinoma and for lung, bladder, prostate, and cervical cancer. In 2,443 melanoma cases and 30,839 controls from Iceland, Sweden, and Spain, however, rs401681(C) was significantly associated with protection against cutaneous melanoma (OR = 0.88, P = 8.0 x 10(-4)). rs401681 lies in an intron of CLPTM1L (612585) and is in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs2736098, a synonymous SNP (ala305-to-ala) in exon 2 of TERT. rs401681(C) was not associated with pigment phenotypes or with TERT or CLPTM1L RNA expression in whole blood or adipose tissue.

Falchi et al. (2009) found that the effects of the nevus-count SNPs rs4636294 (MTAP; 156540) and rs2284063 (PLA2G6; 603604) were additive. TwinsUK subjects homozygous for the risk alleles at both SNPs had a median of 30 nevi, compared with 12.5 in those homozygous for the protective alleles. In Australian subjects the corresponding medians were 116 and 79. Both loci were associated with melanoma in the Australian Q-MEGA study (1,734 cases) and in the Leeds, UK, study (1,274 incident cases and 123 familial cases without CDKN2A mutations). The strongest associations were rs10757257 at 9p21 (combined OR 1.23, P = 3.4 x 10(-8)) and rs132985 at 22q13 (combined OR 1.23, P = 2.6 x 10(-7)). In the Leeds sample, subjects homozygous for the risk alleles at both loci had about double the melanoma risk (OR 1.94).

Somatic Mutations. In a systematic screen of signaling pathway genes in human cancer, Davies et al. (2002) found somatic missense mutations in the BRAF gene (164757) in 66% of malignant melanomas. All mutations lay within the kinase domain, and a single substitution, val599-to-glu (V599E), accounted for 80% of them. Pollock et al. (2003) found BRAF mutations causing the V599E substitution in 41 of 60 (68%) melanoma metastases, 4 of 5 (80%) primary melanomas, and, unexpectedly, 63 of 77 (82%) nevi. They suggested that activation of the RAS/RAF/MAPK pathway in nevi is a critical step in starting melanocytic neoplasia but is not enough on its own to cause melanoma.

Curtin et al. (2005) found somatic BRAF or N-RAS mutations in 81% of melanomas from skin without chronic sun-induced damage. Most melanomas from chronically sun-damaged skin, acral sites, and mucosal sites had mutations in neither gene, and these often had increased copy numbers of CDK4 and CCND1. The authors concluded that melanoma develops through distinct genetic pathways.

Pleasance et al. (2010) sequenced the whole genome of COLO-829, a melanoma cell line from an untreated metastasis in a 43-year-old man with no identified skin primary, and compared it with a lymphoblastoid line from the same patient. They identified 33,345 somatic base substitutions, 66 confirmed small insertions/deletions, and 37 confirmed somatic rearrangements. Of the base substitutions, 292 were in protein-coding sequences and 187 of these were nonsynonymous. The genome carried BRAF V600E and a 12-kb internal homozygous deletion of PTEN (601728), predicted to cause premature termination. A previously known 2-bp deletion in CDKN2A (600160) was found only by a targeted search of sequence reads. Other findings were heterozygous somatic missense mutations in SPDEF (608144; S229L and D283H), MMP28 (608417; G297E), and UVRAG (602493; N561K). Sequencing SPDEF in 48 more metastatic melanomas found a further somatic mutation, W158X, and the authors noted that more melanomas would need to be analyzed to determine the significance of the SPDEF mutations.

Flaherty et al. (2010) noted that 40 to 60% of melanomas carry an activating BRAF mutation and that 90% of reported BRAF mutations are V600E, which constitutively activates BRAF and MAP kinase signaling. Chapman et al. (2011) noted that other activating mutations include V600K and V600R. In their trial, 20 of 675 randomized patients had non-V600E mutations (19 V600K and 1 V600D), identified by Sanger and 454 sequencing.

Wei et al. (2011) performed whole-exome sequencing of 14 matched normal and metastatic tumor DNAs from untreated melanoma patients. They identified 68 genes that appeared to be somatically mutated at elevated frequency, with a high prevalence of C>T/G>A transitions and CC>TT/GG>AA dinucleotide substitutions consistent with ultraviolet exposure. BRAF V600E was present in 7 of 14 samples. A recurrent somatic TRRAP (603015) mutation, c.2165C-T (ser722-to-phe, S722F), was found in 6 of 167 melanomas (about 4%), including the A375 cell line. The residue is highly conserved, and sequencing all TRRAP coding exons in 25 further melanomas found no other nonsynonymous changes. Because no truncating mutations were found and all alterations occurred at one position, the authors suggested that TRRAP may be a new oncogene. They also found 34 distinct somatic GRIN2A (138253) mutations in 135 samples (25.2%). These included 2 mutation clusters, 3 recurrent alterations (S278F, E371K, and E1175K), and 5 nonsense mutations. The authors said the tumorigenic mechanism was unclear but that the frequency strongly suggested a major role in melanoma.

Stark et al. (2011) performed whole-exome sequencing of 8 metastatic melanoma cell lines and matched lymphoblastoid lines and identified 3,215 somatic alterations, 2,139 of them predicted to alter protein structure, with an ultraviolet mutation signature. Among 85 melanoma cell lines, somatic nonsynonymous mutations were found in MAP3K5 (602448) in 8 (9%) and in MAP3K9 (600136) in 13 (15%). The mutations were mutually exclusive with one exception, were confirmed in matched tumor DNA in all but one sample, and did not correlate with mutations or deletions of BRAF, NRAS, CDKN2A, PTEN, or TP53. Loss of heterozygosity was found at MAP3K5 in 85% (98/115) and at MAP3K9 in 67% (77/115) of melanoma samples. The MAP3K9 nonsense mutation W333X reduced kinase activity by 95%, and the MAP3K5 kinase-domain mutation I780F reduced it by 93%. The MAP3K5 mutation E663K reduced phosphorylation of myelin basic protein by 30%. Structural modeling predicted disruptive effects for MAP3K9 R160C, P263L, and E319K. The authors proposed that the heterozygous mutations are inactivating and reduce gene function through haploinsufficiency.

Nikolaev et al. (2011) exome sequenced 7 melanoma cell lines and germline cells from the same donors and found high numbers of somatic mutations bearing the hallmark of UV-induced DNA repair. Gain-of-function somatic mutations in MAP2K1 and MAP2K2 were present in 2 melanomas carrying non-canonical BRAF mutations. These mutations caused constitutive ERK phosphorylation and higher resistance to MEK inhibitors, and they recurred in a larger cohort at an overall frequency of 8%. The authors also frequently found somatic missense and nonsense mutations in FAT4, LRP1B, and DSC1.

In sporadic melanoma, Horn et al. (2013) found recurrent somatic TERT promoter mutations with an ultraviolet signature. They were present in 125 of 168 (74%) metastatic melanoma cell lines, 45 of 53 (85%) corresponding metastatic tumor tissues, and 25 of 77 (33%) primary melanomas. Most occurred at 2 positions and created Ets/TCF binding motifs.

Huang et al. (2013) analyzed published melanoma whole-genome sequences and found 2 somatic TERT promoter mutations in 17 of 19 (89%) tumors: chr5 1,295,228 C-T (C228T) and 1,295,250 C-T (C250T). Both are C-to-T transitions at dipyrimidine motifs, suggesting UV-induced damage, and both lie within 100 bp of the transcription start site. Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutations, and one of them was found in 33 of 51 (65%) further tumor/normal pairs, giving 50 of 70 (71%) melanomas in total. The 2 mutations never occurred together (P = 5.4 x 10(-7)). Two tumors with C228T also had an adjacent change at 1,295,229, forming a CC-TT dinucleotide transition. C228T or C250T was also present in 24 of 150 (16%) cancer cell lines, including 5 of 6 melanoma lines.

Hayward et al. (2017) carried out whole-genome sequencing of cutaneous, acral, and mucosal melanomas. Significantly mutated genes in cutaneous melanoma included BRAF, CDKN2A, NRAS, and TP53. Acral and mucosal melanomas were dominated by structural changes and by mutation signatures of unknown cause. TERT promoter mutations were the most frequent somatic mutations in melanoma, but neither TERT promoter nor ATRX mutations were associated with greater telomere length.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Of the 33 melanoma-prone families studied by Tucker et al. (2002), 17 had germline CDKN2A mutations, 2 had CDK4 mutations, and 14 had no mutation identified in either gene. The melanomas and dysplastic nevi did not appear to differ by mutation type.

Fernandez et al. (2008) found that the protective SLC45A2 F374L variant allele was consistently associated with dark hair, dark skin, dark eye color, solar lentigines, and childhood sunburns. They stated that this was the first description of SLC45A2 as a melanoma susceptibility gene in a light-skinned population.

Bishop et al. (2009) found no significant pairwise interactions between the top SNPs at 9p21, 11q, and 16q24, consistent with a multiplicative model. Associations were similar across case categories (family history, multiple primaries, and early onset), although for some SNPs the effect was marginally weaker in early-onset melanoma. Effect sizes were notably homogeneous across European-ancestry populations living at different latitudes.

### Pathogenesis

Clark et al. (1978) photographically documented the transformation of 2 B-K moles into malignant melanomas, which supported the origin of familial melanomas from heritable melanocytic lesions.

Pathak et al. (1983) proposed that a genetic factor controlling melanoma initiation lies in the distal segment of 6q. They suggested that loss of this segment, or of all of 6q, makes a melanoblast or melanocyte malignant.

Howell et al. (1984) found that skin fibroblasts from 5 patients with hereditary CMM and the dysplastic nevus syndrome were all more sensitive than normal to killing by 4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide (4NQO), with survival curves 2- to 3-fold steeper. Only 1 strain was hypersensitive to simulated sunlight. Of 2 strains tested at the 6-thioguanine-resistance locus, both were hypermutable after 4NQO, but only 1 showed a higher than normal mutant frequency after simulated sunlight. The authors suggested that hypersensitivity to mutagens contributes to melanoma risk in these patients.

Bale et al. (1986) analyzed linkage between the melanoma locus and a hypothetical dysplastic nevus locus and obtained a maximum lod score of 3.857 at theta = 0.08. All families giving evidence for linkage were in coupling phase, and the maximum likelihood estimate of theta did not differ significantly from 0 (P = 0.1). They concluded that the dysplastic nevus and melanoma traits may be pleiotropic effects of a single highly penetrant autosomal dominant gene.

Dracopoli et al. (1987) found that 6 metastases from one patient shared inactivation of the same X chromosome and identical segregations at loci on chromosomes 9 and X, supporting monoclonal origin. From the patterns of loss they constructed a genealogy of tumor progression. Dracopoli et al. (1989) analyzed multiple metastases from a single patient and samples from a family with hereditary melanoma. They concluded that loss of heterozygosity at distal 1p is a late event in tumor progression rather than the second mutation expected if melanoma arose by a cellular recessive mechanism.

Gilchrest et al. (1999) reviewed the pathogenesis of melanoma induced by ultraviolet radiation. Pleasance et al. (2010) found that most somatic base substitutions in the COLO-829 melanoma genome were C-to-T/G-to-A transitions. Of 510 dinucleotide substitutions, 360 were CC-to-TT/GG-to-AA, and 92% of C-to-T mutations occurred at the 3-prime base of a pyrimidine dinucleotide, which they attributed to ultraviolet light. C-to-T mutations were less frequent on transcribed than on nontranscribed strands (2,773 vs 4,058) and were less prevalent in genic regions and highly expressed genes, consistent with transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair. In a 1q region of loss of heterozygosity, C-to-T changes made up 82% of early mutations but 53% of late mutations, and the authors suggested that ultraviolet exposure ceased later in tumor evolution.

Davies et al. (2002) showed that mutant BRAF proteins had elevated kinase activity and transformed NIH3T3 cells. Cancer cell lines carrying V599E did not require RAS function for growth.

Zhang et al. (2006) used array-based comparative genomic hybridization to analyze 283 miRNA genes in 227 ovarian cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma specimens. Copy number alterations were found at 85.9% of miRNA-containing loci in melanoma, and there were frequent copy number abnormalities of DICER1, Argonaute-2, and other miRNA-associated genes.

Schatton et al. (2008) identified a subpopulation of cells enriched for human malignant-melanoma-initiating cells, defined by expression of ABCB5. ABCB5+ tumor cells in patients had a primitive molecular phenotype and correlated with clinical melanoma progression. Passeron et al. (2009) found that SOX9 was increasingly downregulated as melanocytes progressed to premalignant, malignant, and metastatic states. SOX9 overexpression caused cell cycle arrest by increasing p21 transcription and restored retinoic acid sensitivity by downregulating PRAME.

Falchi et al. (2009) found that adjusting the SNP-melanoma association for nevus count in the Leeds subsample (903 cases, 485 controls) moved the odds ratio toward 1.0. They took this as consistent with melanoma risk at these loci being mediated through nevus count.

Wei et al. (2011) found that mutant TRRAP S722F transformed NIH 3T3 cells more efficiently than wildtype. shRNA knockdown of TRRAP substantially increased apoptosis, with increased PARP cleavage, in melanoma lines carrying the mutation (63T and A375), but had little effect on wildtype lines. The authors concluded that mutant TRRAP is essential for melanoma cell survival. In a pathway analysis, they also found the glutamate signaling pathway highly significantly mutated in melanoma, with frequent mutations in GRIN2A and PLCB4.

Stark et al. (2011) found that expression of MAP3K9 W333X in HEK293T cells reduced signaling through MAP2K4/7-JNK and MEK1/2-ERK. MAP3K5 I780F reduced MAP3K5 phosphorylation, JNK signaling, and phospho-MEK1/2, and E663K also decreased phospho-MEK1/2. siRNA knockdown of MAP3K9 in UACC903 and UACC647 melanoma cells increased cell viability after temozolomide treatment, which suggested that reduced MAP3K9 activity can contribute to chemoresistance.

Huang et al. (2013) showed that the TERT promoter mutations C228T and C250T each create the same 11-bp sequence containing a consensus ETS binding motif. In luciferase assays in 5 cell lines, each mutation increased TERT core promoter activity about 2- to 4-fold. Together these mutations were more frequent than BRAF and NRAS mutations. The authors proposed that they may be early driver events acting through TERT dysregulation.

Straussman et al. (2012) showed that HGF was necessary and sufficient to make BRAF-mutant melanoma cells resistant to RAF inhibition. It was the only one of 22 receptor tyrosine kinase ligands tested that gave substantial resistance. HGF activated MET and caused sustained reactivation of the MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways. Wilson et al. (2012) found that HGF was one of very few of 446 secreted factors that rescued BRAF-mutant melanoma cells from PLX4032 (vemurafenib). HGF also significantly reduced PLX4032 sensitivity in 5 of 12 additional BRAF-mutant lines, MET expression correlated with rescue, and crizotinib blocked it. Das Thakur et al. (2013) found that vemurafenib-resistant patient-derived xenograft tumors had elevated BRAF(V600E) expression and became drug-dependent. Sun et al. (2014) found that SOX10 suppression activated TGF-beta signaling and upregulated EGFR and PDGFRB, conferring resistance to BRAF and MEK inhibitors. Hayward et al. (2017) reported that most melanomas had potentially actionable mutations, mostly in MAPK and phosphoinositol kinase pathway components.

### Clinical Management

Lynch et al. (1980) noted that management of FAMMM patients is difficult because it is not possible to know which moles need biopsy or which will need wider excision. They stressed that patients have a lifelong susceptibility to multiple melanomas.

Vasen et al. (1989) found that melanomas detected by screening in dysplastic nevus syndrome families were at an earlier stage. Melanoma in situ accounted for 1 of 19 melanomas diagnosed before screening, 2 of 11 found at the first examination, and 7 of 20 found during follow-up. Mean invasive thickness in these groups was 1.75, 0.80, and 0.54 mm. Sixteen of 19 prescreening melanomas (84%) were Clark level III or IV, whereas 15 of 20 follow-up melanomas (75%) were Clark level I or II. The authors concluded that screening can lead to earlier detection, curative treatment, and better prognosis.

Hejny et al. (2001) described 7 patients who developed retinopathy during high-dose interferon alfa-2b adjuvant treatment for high-risk melanoma. In 5 patients, visual acuity was normal and the retinopathy resolved after treatment was stopped. The other 2 had severe, unresolved retinopathy with vision loss to counting fingers or hand motions.

Flaherty et al. (2010) conducted a phase 1 trial of the oral BRAF inhibitor PLX4032. Among 16 patients with V600E melanoma who received 240 mg or more twice daily, 10 had a partial response and 1 had a complete response. In an extension cohort of 32 patients treated at 960 mg twice daily, 26 (81%) responded (2 complete and 24 partial responses), and estimated median progression-free survival was more than 7 months. None of 5 patients without BRAF mutations showed tumor regression. Biopsies showed markedly lower phosphorylated ERK, cyclin D1, and Ki-67 at day 15. Dose-limiting toxicities (grade 3 rash, fatigue, and arthralgia) occurred at 1120 mg twice daily. Cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas, nearly all of keratoacanthoma type, developed in 31% of the extension cohort.

Bollag et al. (2010) described the structure-guided discovery of PLX4032, which selectively blocked RAF/MEK/ERK signaling in BRAF-mutant cells and had no effect on wildtype BRAF xenografts. Two responders in the dose escalation carried BRAF V600K rather than V600E and had 71% and 100% reductions in tumor dimensions. More than 80% inhibition of ERK phosphorylation correlated with regression. The keratoacanthoma-type squamous cell carcinomas were attributed in part to paradoxical pathway activation through RAF dimerization in cells with activated RAS.

In a phase 3 trial, Chapman et al. (2011) randomized 675 patients with previously untreated metastatic BRAF V600E melanoma to vemurafenib or dacarbazine. At 6 months, overall survival was 84% with vemurafenib and 64% with dacarbazine. Vemurafenib reduced the relative risk of death by 63% and the risk of death or progression by 74%. Median progression-free survival was 5.3 versus 1.6 months, and confirmed response rates were 48% versus 5%. Four of 10 vemurafenib-treated patients with V600K mutations had partial responses. Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, keratoacanthoma, or both developed in 61 patients (18%).

Straussman et al. (2012) found stromal HGF in pretreatment biopsies from 23 of 34 patients with BRAF V600E melanoma, and these patients responded significantly worse to BRAF inhibition. Wilson et al. (2012) found that higher pretreatment plasma HGF in 126 patients treated with PLX4032 was associated with worse progression-free survival (hazard ratio 1.42) and overall survival (hazard ratio 1.8). Das Thakur et al. (2013) found that intermittent vemurafenib dosing prevented drug-resistant disease in mouse xenografts over 200 days. Sun et al. (2014) found acquired EGFR expression in 6 of 16 tumors after resistance to BRAF or MEK inhibitors. Drug-induced enrichment of SOX10-low/EGFR-high cells reversed off drug, and the authors suggested that a 'drug holiday' could restore sensitivity.

Van Allen et al. (2015) performed whole-exome sequencing of pretreatment tumors from 110 patients with metastatic melanoma treated with ipilimumab. Clinical benefit was significantly associated with nonsynonymous mutational load (P = 0.0076) and predicted neoantigen load (P = 0.027), but no gene, including BRAF or NRAS, was enriched for mutations in either group. Expression of GZMA, PRF1, CTLA4, and PD-L2 was higher in patients who benefited.

Schatton et al. (2008) found that a systemically given anti-ABCB5 monoclonal antibody induced antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity in melanoma-initiating cells and inhibited tumor growth. Passeron et al. (2009) found that PGD2 increased SOX9 expression and restored retinoic acid sensitivity, and that combined PGD2 and retinoic acid substantially decreased melanoma growth in human ex vivo and mouse in vivo models.

### Animal Model

Noonan et al. (2001) showed in a genetically engineered mouse model that a single dose of burning ultraviolet radiation given to neonates, but not to adults, was necessary and sufficient to induce tumors resembling human melanoma, with high penetrance. They concluded that this supported epidemiologic evidence that childhood sunburn poses a significant risk of developing melanoma.

In serial human-to-mouse xenotransplantation experiments, Schatton et al. (2008) found that ABCB5+ melanoma cells were more tumorigenic than ABCB5- cells and re-established tumor heterogeneity. ABCB5+ cells gave rise to both ABCB5+ and ABCB5- progeny, whereas ABCB5- cells gave rise, at lower rates, only to ABCB5- cells. Passeron et al. (2009) showed that SOX9 overexpression inhibited melanoma tumorigenicity in mice and in a human ex vivo model. Wilson et al. (2012) found in xenografts of BRAF-mutant melanoma lines that MET activation strongly reduced the growth-suppressive effect of PLX4032, whereas MET inhibition enhanced tumor regression.

### History

Norris (1820) reported a case of 'fungoid disease.' Hecht (1989) reviewed this 1820 description by Norris as an account of hereditary malignant melanoma of the skin.
