### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that cutaneous malignant melanoma (CMM1) can be caused by somatic mutation in the BRAF gene (164757) on chromosome 7q34 or the TRRAP gene (603015) on chromosome 7q22.1. A locus for familial susceptibility to cutaneous malignant melanoma-dysplastic nevus was mapped to chromosome 1p36, but this linkage was not confirmed in other families.

### Description

Cutaneous malignant melanoma clusters in some families. In many of these families, affected individuals and their relatives have distinctive pigmented precursor lesions, which have been called B-K moles, familial atypical multiple moles (FAMMM), or dysplastic nevi. These lesions have been proposed as markers of high melanoma risk (Reimer et al., 1978; Clark et al., 1978; Lynch et al., 1983). Pedigree studies have suggested autosomal dominant inheritance of familial melanoma (Greene et al., 1983). In malignant melanomas, somatic missense mutations in BRAF are frequent (Davies et al., 2002).

### Nomenclature

Clark et al. (1978) called the melanoma precursor lesion in familial melanoma the 'B-K mole syndrome,' and Lynch et al. (1978, 1980) used the term familial atypical multiple mole-melanoma (FAMMM) syndrome. Elder et al. (1980) proposed the term dysplastic nevus syndrome for patients with dysplastic nevi. These nevi were identical to the precursor lesion of the familial B-K mole syndrome. Greene et al. (1980) proposed a nomenclature for the precursor naevi of cutaneous malignant melanoma, and Elder et al. (1982) gave their definition of the dysplastic nevus syndrome.

### Clinical Features

Moschella (1961) reported malignant melanoma of the skin in sisters.

Schoch (1963) reported a pedigree study and cytogenetic study of familial malignant melanoma.

Katzenellenbogen and Sandbank (1966) reported malignant melanomas in twins.

Andrews (1968) reported malignant melanoma in siblings.

Anderson (1971) described the clinical characteristics of the genetic variety of cutaneous melanoma in man.

Reimer et al. (1978) studied 7 consecutive melanoma-prone families. Pigmented lesions with distinctive clinical and histologic characteristics occurred in 18 of 20 melanoma patients (90%) and in 24 of 43 first-degree relatives (56%). Recognizing these lesions led to the detection of early-stage melanoma in 6 family members.

Greene et al. (1978) proposed that melanoma occurring in families may be related to an inherited syndrome of precursor skin lesions with distinct clinical and histologic features. They suggested that recognizing this syndrome in relatives of melanoma patients identifies family members at high risk, which allows earlier diagnosis and treatment.

Clark et al. (1978) described distinctive melanocytic moles, which they called the 'B-K mole syndrome,' in 37 members of 6 melanoma families. Among the family members they examined, 15 of 17 patients with melanoma and 22 of 41 nonmelanoma relatives had the moles. A patient could have from fewer than 10 to more than 100 moles. They were prominent on the upper trunk and extremities and varied in size (5 to 15 mm), outline, and color. Histologically, B-K moles showed atypical melanocytic hyperplasia, lymphocytic infiltration, delicate fibroplasia, and new blood vessels, and arose within a compound nevus or de novo. The transformation of 2 B-K moles into malignant melanomas was documented photographically.

Lynch et al. (1978) described a family showing concordance for malignant melanoma and a cutaneous phenotype of multiple large moles of variable size and color (reddish-brown to bright red) with pigmentary leakage. They proposed that this phenotype indicated melanoma risk and could be added to the cancer-related genodermatoses.

Lynch et al. (1980) performed clinical, pathologic, and genetic studies of 3 kindreds with FAMMM syndrome. One patient had 9 separate primary melanomas in 18 years. Histologically, the FAMMM moles were compound nevocellular nevi with varying degrees of melanocytic dysplasia, an increased occurrence of fibroplasia, and chronic inflammation in the papillary dermis. Expression of the phenotype ranged from an apparent lack of disease through minimal, moderate, and florid manifestations. The degree of dysplasia in moles varied markedly between and within families.

Elder et al. (1980) reviewed clinical photographs of 79 prospectively studied cases of non-familial cutaneous malignant melanoma. Fifteen patients had moles on the covered buttock area. Seven of them had large, clinically atypical nevi, and biopsies of these nevi showed severe melanocytic dysplasia. Residual melanocytic dysplasia was found in 5 of the primary melanomas in this group. The authors proposed that patients with dysplastic nevi represent a distinctive syndrome, the dysplastic nevus syndrome, at increased risk of primary cutaneous melanoma. They noted that this was the first description of such dysplasia in non-familial melanoma.

Kraemer et al. (1983) wrote about dysplastic naevi in relation to the risk of cutaneous melanoma.

Lynch et al. (1983) described the FAMMM syndrome as an autosomal dominantly inherited susceptibility to multiple atypical moles. The moles were black, brown, tan, red, or pink, sometimes variegated. They measured 1 cm or larger, were macular or papular with regular or irregular borders, and were found mostly on areas not exposed to the sun. A patient could have few or none, or more than 100. Histopathology showed dysplastic melanocytes, fibroplasia, a focal chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate, and new blood vessel formation in the papillary dermis, though not in every case. In a single FAMMM kindred, some members had minimal or no skin signs and others had a wider range of neoplasms. The authors attributed both observations to pleiotropic effects of the cancer-prone FAMMM genotype.

Greene et al. (1985) described the familial dysplastic nevus syndrome and identified dysplastic nevi as acquired precursors of cutaneous malignant melanoma.

Kopf et al. (1986) analyzed prospectively completed questionnaires on 1,169 cases of cutaneous malignant melanoma. Sixty-nine patients reported a family history of melanoma. Compared with nonfamilial cases, familial melanoma was significantly associated with a younger age at first diagnosis, a smaller lesion diameter, a lower Clark level, and fewer nonmelanoma skin cancers and noncutaneous cancers. The authors suggested that greater awareness of melanoma among family members could account for some of these observations.

Hartley et al. (1987) described 7 cases of malignant melanoma in close relatives of children with osteosarcoma and chondrosarcoma. They proposed that in certain families, malignant melanoma may be another manifestation of the gene defect that causes susceptibility to the tumors of the SBLA cancer family syndrome. These tumors include childhood adrenal cortical carcinoma, osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and retinoblastoma.

Vasen et al. (1989) studied 9 families with the dysplastic nevus syndrome identified since 1982 in the Leiden area of the Netherlands. A total of 50 primary melanomas were diagnosed in 38 persons.

Kefford et al. (1991) assessed 119 members of 8 Australian families with hereditary melanoma. Thirty had cutaneous malignant melanoma, and only 8 of these also had dysplastic nevi. Another 15 family members had dysplastic nevi without melanoma.

Tucker et al. (2002) followed 33 melanoma-prone families for up to 25 years. Each family had more than 2 living members with invasive melanoma, and all had members with both dysplastic nevi and melanoma. The authors examined and photographed 844 family members. Most dysplastic nevi remained stable or regressed, and few changed in a way that should raise concern for melanoma. They concluded that melanomas can be detected early with careful surveillance.

### Other Features

Potter et al. (2002) described a 32-year-old woman with a history of metastatic melanoma who presented with bilateral decreased visual acuity. Electroretinography showed a 'negative' B-wave, and Goldmann perimetry showed paracentral and central scotomas. Her serum contained antibodies to retinal transducin but showed no immunoreactivity to retinal bipolar cells. The authors concluded that melanoma-associated retinopathy (MAR) can be related to a variety of antiretinal antibodies and that transducin is a novel MAR antigen.

Alexander et al. (2004) studied 2 patients with MAR, aged 57 and 61 years, who had normal Snellen visual acuity but reduced contrast sensitivity. The loss was not specific to the magnocellular pathway. It depended on spatial frequency and was greatest at low to intermediate frequencies. The authors concluded that this pattern was consistent with the retinal bipolar cell dysfunction presumed to underlie MAR.

Constantinescu et al. (2007) identified 5 cases of malignant melanoma in the DATATOP clinical trial cohort of patients with early Parkinson disease. About 1.5 cases would have been expected in a standard healthy population, giving a standardized event ratio of 3.3 (95% CI, 1.1-7.8). Melanoma incidence was not associated with levodopa therapy.

In a cross-sectional survey, Ferreira et al. (2007) found neoplastic or preneoplastic skin lesions in 35 of 150 patients with Parkinson disease (23.3%) and in 20 of 146 age-matched controls (13.7%). The difference lost statistical significance after adjustment for gender. The authors called their results nonconclusive but in agreement with earlier studies suggesting an increased risk of skin cancer in Parkinson disease.

In 2 prospective US cohorts, Gao et al. (2009) found that the risk of Parkinson disease (PD) increased as hair color got lighter. In a nested case-control study, homozygosity for the melanoma-associated MC1R Arg151Cys variant was associated with red hair and with a higher PD risk (adjusted RR 3.15 vs Arg/Arg homozygotes). The authors suggested that the higher-than-expected co-occurrence of melanoma and PD may be explained, at least in part, by the association of both diseases with lighter hair color and the MC1R Arg151Cys variant, rather than by levodopa treatment. Herrero Hernández (2009) proposed pigmentation as a clue to understanding the link between Parkinson disease and melanoma.

### Inheritance

Salamon et al. (1963) addressed whether malignant melanoma is hereditary, and Turkington (1965) addressed a familial factor in malignant melanoma.

Reimer et al. (1978) concluded that the preneoplastic syndrome of distinctive pigmented lesions in melanoma-prone families appeared to be an autosomal dominant trait. Lynch et al. (1978) found that the multiple-mole phenotype was transmitted in their family, and in several others then under study, in a pattern consistent with a simple autosomal dominant factor. In 3 FAMMM kindreds, Lynch et al. (1980) observed vertical transmission of cutaneous malignant melanoma and/or FAMMM moles, including father-to-son transmission, with no sex predilection. Together with other reports, they considered this consistent with an autosomal dominant gene with markedly variable expressivity.

Happle et al. (1982) argued for polygenic inheritance of precursor nevi.

Greene et al. (1983) collected data on 401 members of 14 high-risk kindreds with familial cutaneous malignant melanoma (CMM) and dysplastic nevus syndrome (DNS). Pedigree analysis was compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance of the familial CMM trait. A similar model probably applied to DNS, but segregation analysis could not confirm a major locus.

Using life-table and disease-free survival analysis, Bale et al. (1986) estimated a lifetime melanoma incidence of 46% in the sibs and offspring of individuals with dysplastic nevi. They considered this consistent with a highly penetrant autosomal dominant mode of inheritance. Bale and Chakravarti (1987) presented evidence for autosomal dominance and pleiotropy of the CMM/dysplastic nevus gene.

Traupe et al. (1989) estimated mutation rates for the dysplastic nevus 'syndrome.' Derived directly from sporadic occurrence, the rate was exceedingly high (0.9-2.5%). By Haldane's formula it would be 0.007-0.02%, so that new mutations would outnumber eliminated ones by 100:1. They concluded that this apparent lack of genetic equilibrium is not compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance of the dysplastic nevus syndrome.

Kefford et al. (1991) found an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance in all 8 of the Australian hereditary melanoma families they studied.

### Cytogenetics

Pathak et al. (1983) analyzed G-banded karyotypes of 9 established human malignant melanoma cell lines and found chromosome 6 involved in marker chromosomes in all of them. In their cases plus the literature, 38 of 47 (80.8%) karyotyped melanoma cell lines or primary melanoma cells had a marker involving chromosome 6. They proposed that a genetic factor determining melanoma initiation lies in the distal segment of 6q, and that deletion of this segment or of the entire 6q makes the melanoblast or melanocyte malignant.

On cytogenetic analysis of malignant melanoma cells from a number of cases, Becher et al. (1983) found frequent involvement of chromosome 6 in structural aberrations. In another study, Becher et al. (1983) analyzed G-banded chromosomes in short-term cultures of 4 malignant melanomas. All 4 had markers derived from chromosomes 1 and 6, and 3 had abnormalities of chromosome 7. The authors suggested that abnormalities of chromosomes 6 and 7 may be characteristic of malignant melanoma.

Trent et al. (1983) found deletion or translocation of chromosome 6q in 4 of 5 human malignant melanomas. They suggested that 6q may be a specific site of chromosome aberration in malignant melanoma.

Balaban et al. (1984) studied the chromosomes of melanocytic lesions from 17 patients. The 5 nevi, 3 of them dysplastic, had normal karyotypes, whereas each tumor had a mostly abnormal karyotype. Chromosome 1 was involved in 10 of 11 advanced melanomas. In 9 of these, deletions or translocations of 1p involved the proximal segment 1p12-p22. Six advanced melanomas had extra 7q material, and 9 melanomas had changes in chromosome 6. In one advanced tumor, the primary lesion and 5 metastases removed one after another over 18 months had nearly identical karyotypes, which the authors took as evidence of clonality. They suggested that genes important in melanoma development lie on proximal 1p, on 7q, and on chromosome 6.

Ochi et al. (1984) karyotyped a recurrent metastatic malignant melanoma in a 54-year-old white woman on 3 separate occasions. They found 2 clones, one hypodiploid and one hypertriploid, that shared the marker chromosomes der(6)t(6; 17) and der(17)t(1; 17).

Rey et al. (1985) found a pseudodiploid modal chromosome number and 5 marker chromosomes, including an i(6p), in a malignant melanoma that had metastasized to the brain.

Dracopoli et al. (1987) studied cell lines from 6 independent metastases of a single melanoma patient. They found loss of heterozygosity on 8 of the 16 informative autosomes and at all 3 informative X-linked loci. With one exception, the regions with loss of heterozygosity showed no karyotypic abnormalities.

Using RFLP analysis of 20 matched pairs of melanoma tumor and normal DNA, Millikin et al. (1991) found loss of heterozygosity on 6q at 21 of 53 informative loci (40%). Allelic loss was most frequent (60%) in the region defined by c-MYB and ESR (6q22-q23 and 6q24-q27). Loss of heterozygosity at loci on chromosomes 1, 11, 16, and 17 occurred in only 5% of cases. The authors concluded that loss of 6q sequences is a nonrandom and possibly biologically relevant event in malignant melanoma.

### Mapping

Hawkins et al. (1981) reported evidence for linkage between HLA and malignant melanoma. Pellegris et al. (1982) typed HLA-A, -B, and -C in 6 families with familial malignant melanoma and found HLA-B12 in 5 of them. Linkage between HLA and familial melanoma could be analyzed in 3 families and was possible in 2 of them but not in the third.

Greene et al. (1983) performed linkage analysis with 23 genetic markers in 14 melanoma-prone kindreds. Their results suggested that a DNS/CMM susceptibility gene may lie on the short arm of chromosome 1, within 30 map units of the Rh locus (maximum lod score = 2.00).

Bale et al. (1985) performed HLA typing in 11 families with hereditary cutaneous malignant melanoma and dysplastic nevi and reanalyzed published data from 19 other families. With CMM as the trait, linkage to HLA was rejected for all recombination fractions below 40% in the pooled 30 families. Analysis of the combined CMM/dysplastic nevus trait in 18 families also gave strong evidence against linkage.

Bale et al. (1986) analyzed linkage between the melanoma locus and a hypothetical dysplastic nevus locus and obtained a maximum lod score of 3.857 at theta = 0.08. All families giving evidence for linkage were in the coupling phase, and the maximum likelihood estimate of theta did not differ significantly from 0. The authors concluded that dysplastic nevi and melanoma may be pleiotropic effects of a single, highly penetrant autosomal dominant gene.

Bale et al. (1989) evaluated 99 relatives and 26 spouses in 6 families predisposed to melanoma. Thirty-four family members had cutaneous malignant melanoma, and 31 of them also had histologically confirmed dysplastic nevi. Another 24 family members had dysplastic nevi alone. Multipoint linkage analysis with 26 polymorphic DNA markers on 1p mapped a CMM-dysplastic nevus susceptibility gene to 1p36, between D1S47 and the pronatrodilatin gene (PND). The odds were greater than 260,000:1 in favor of linkage at this location.

Cannon-Albright et al. (1990) genotyped 3 Utah kindreds with multiple cases of melanoma for the distal 1p markers PND and D1S47. They found no evidence of linkage, whether they analyzed melanoma alone or a combined melanoma/dysplastic nevus phenotype. Multipoint analysis excluded the locus from a 55-cM region that included the PND-D1S47 interval.

In 8 Australian families with hereditary melanoma, Kefford et al. (1991) studied 8 polymorphic DNA markers on 1p, including D1S47 and PND. They found a strongly negative probability of linkage and effectively excluded the putative melanoma susceptibility gene from distal 1p in these families.

Nancarrow et al. (1992) studied linkage to 1p markers in 7 Australian familial melanoma kindreds, 3 of which were the largest reported worldwide. They excluded a melanoma gene from a 40-cM region spanning the D1S47-PND interval and extending about 15 cM beyond each marker. They also excluded about 20 cM around the MYCL1/D1S57 loci.

Fountain et al. (1992) found hemizygous loss at 2 or more of 8 chromosome 9p markers in 12 of 14 (86%) informative metastatic melanoma tumor and cell line DNAs. Homozygous deletions of D9S126 occurred in 2 of 20 (10%) melanoma cell lines. They defined a small critical region of 2 to 3 Mb on 9p21 likely to contain a melanoma tumor suppressor gene. IFNB1, the IFNA gene cluster, GGTB2, and the TYRP locus were excluded as candidates because they lay outside the homozygously deleted regions.

Goldstein et al. (1993) studied 7 new families (set B) and updated versions of 6 previously reported families (set A) with markers D1S47, PND, and D1S160. Models II and III showed strong evidence for linkage to D1S47, D1S160, and PND in the set A pedigrees but not in the set B families. In all 13 families, the maximum lod score between CMM alone and D1S47 was 3.12 at theta = 0.10, and between CMM alone and D1S160 it was 1.76 at theta = 0.10. PND showed no evidence for linkage to CMM alone.

Goldstein et al. (1996) performed 2-trait-locus, 2-marker-locus linkage analysis in 19 CMM/dysplastic nevus kindreds that earlier analyses had shown to have linkage and heterogeneity to both 1p and 9p. For CMM alone, the single-locus heterogeneity model gave the highest lod score (Z = 8.48 at theta = 0.0). The evidence for linkage to 9p was much stronger than for 1p, with lod scores about 2 times greater.

In a genome-wide association study, the GenoMEL consortium (Bishop et al., 2009) genotyped 317K tagging SNPs in 1,650 selected melanoma cases and 4,336 controls of European ancestry. Replication was carried out in 1,149 cases and 964 controls from GenoMEL and in a population-based Leeds study of 1,163 cases and 903 controls. Three loci were replicated: 16q24 encompassing MC1R, 11q14-q21 encompassing TYR, and 9p21 adjacent to MTAP and flanking CDKN2A. The strongest association was with rs258322 on 16q24 (per-allele OR 1.67). At 9p21, rs7023329 within MTAP was associated (overall P = 4.03 x 10(-7)), and rs1011970, which lies 246 kb centromeric within ANRIL and overlaps CDKN2B, was independently associated. At the TYR locus, rs1393350 was associated (P = 2.41 x 10(-14)).

### Heterogeneity

Pellegris et al. (1982) suggested that there may be 2 types of familial melanoma: a more frequent type that apparently segregates with the HLA complex and another that does not.

Cannon-Albright et al. (1990) suggested that diagnostic or genetic heterogeneity might explain why their results differed from the 1p36 linkage reported by Bale et al. (1989). Kefford et al. (1991) found that their pedigrees fell into 3 groups: melanoma without dysplastic nevi, melanoma with occasional dysplastic nevi, and melanoma with dysplastic nevi. They suggested that hereditary melanoma is heterogeneous in its genetic basis and in its association with the dysplastic nevus syndrome. Nancarrow et al. (1992) noted that mapping of a CMM/dysplastic nevus gene to distal 1p was highly controversial and that no study had supported linkage of familial melanoma alone to these markers.

Goldstein et al. (1993) found significant evidence for heterogeneity of CMM/dysplastic nevus linkage to 1p markers when families were divided by the proportion of CMM/dysplastic nevus cases (D1S47 and D1S160) or by the occurrence of immune-related tumors (D1S160). Only a subset of families appeared to be linked to 1p. The authors concluded that diagnostic, clinical, and genetic heterogeneity likely explained earlier failures to confirm linkage. Goldstein et al. (1996) found that a 1p locus (or loci) appeared to contribute to both CMM and CMM/dysplastic nevi, whereas a 9p locus contributed more to CMM alone. After conditioning on linkage to the other locus, only the 9p locus consistently showed significant evidence of linkage to CMM alone.

Among the 33 melanoma-prone families studied by Tucker et al. (2002), 17 had mutations in CDKN2A, 2 had mutations in CDK4, and 14 had no mutation in either gene.

Gillanders et al. (2003) performed a genomewide linkage scan in 49 Australian pedigrees, each with at least 3 cases of CMM and no CDKN2A or CDK4 involvement. In 9 families selected for early age at onset, the highest parametric lod score was 4.95 (theta = 0) at D1S2776 on 1p22. In 33 additional multiplex families from several continents, they found further evidence for linkage to 1p22, again strongest in families with the earliest mean age at diagnosis. They noted that mutations in CDKN2A and CDK4 account for only 20 to 25% of families with multiple cases of melanoma, and concluded that a novel melanoma susceptibility gene lies in 1p22.

Bishop et al. (2009) confirmed association of melanoma with the chromosome 20 region containing ASIP and with a chromosome 22 nevus-count locus (rs2284063). The effects at the 3 main loci (16q24, 11q14-q21, and 9p21) did not interact and fit a multiplicative model. Effect sizes were notably homogeneous across European-ancestry populations living at different latitudes.

### Molecular Genetics

Dracopoli et al. (1989) found loss of constitutional heterozygosity at chromosome 1p loci in 15 of 35 (43%) melanomas and 11 of 21 (52%) melanoma cell lines. They also analyzed multiple metastases from a single patient, and melanoma and lymphoblastoid samples from a family with hereditary melanoma. They concluded that loss of heterozygosity on distal 1p is a late event in tumor progression and not the second mutation that a cellular recessive mechanism for melanoma would predict.

Davies et al. (2002) found somatic missense mutations in the BRAF gene in 66% of malignant melanomas and at lower frequency in a wide range of other human cancers. All mutations were within the kinase domain, and a single substitution, V599E, accounted for 80% of them. Mutated BRAF proteins had elevated kinase activity and transformed NIH3T3 cells, and RAS function was not required for the growth of cancer cell lines carrying the V599E mutation.

Tucker et al. (2002) studied 33 melanoma-prone families in which all families had members with dysplastic nevi and melanoma. They found mutations in CDKN2A (600160) in 17 families and in CDK4 (123829) in 2 families.

Pollock et al. (2003) analyzed microdissected melanoma and nevus samples. They found BRAF mutations resulting in the V599E substitution in 41 of 60 (68%) melanoma metastases and 4 of 5 (80%) primary melanomas. Unexpectedly, the same mutations were present in 63 of 77 (82%) nevi.

Curtin et al. (2005) compared genome-wide DNA copy-number changes and BRAF and NRAS mutation status in 126 melanomas from 4 groups with differing ultraviolet exposure: 30 from skin with chronic sun-induced damage, 40 from skin without such damage, 36 acral, and 20 mucosal. Mutations in BRAF or NRAS were present in 81% of melanomas on skin without chronic sun-induced damage, whereas most melanomas in the other groups had mutations in neither gene. Melanomas with wildtype BRAF or NRAS frequently had increased copy number of CDK4 and CCND1.

In European populations comprising 2,121 individuals with cutaneous melanoma, 2,163 with basal cell carcinoma, and over 40,000 controls, Gudbjartsson et al. (2008) found that a haplotype near ASIP conferred significant risk of cutaneous melanoma (OR = 1.45, P = 1.2 x 10(-9)). The TYR variant encoding R402Q (OR = 1.21) and an eye color variant in TYRP1 (OR = 1.15) were also associated with melanoma risk. The associations of all 3 variants held after adjustment for pigmentation.

In a Spanish case-control study of 131 consecutive melanoma patients and 245 sex- and age-matched controls, Fernandez et al. (2008) found that the variant allele of the SLC45A2 c.1122C-G (phe374-to-leu; rs16891982) polymorphism was associated with protection from malignant melanoma (OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.24-0.70). The protective allele was also associated with dark hair, dark skin, and dark eye color. The authors proposed SLC45A2 as a melanoma susceptibility gene in a light-skinned population.

In 2,443 melanoma cases and 30,839 controls from Iceland, Sweden, and Spain, Rafnar et al. (2009) found that the C allele of rs401681, which lies in an intron of CLPTM1L at the TERT-CLPTM1L locus on 5p15.33, was associated with protection against cutaneous melanoma (OR = 0.88, P = 8.0 x 10(-4)). The same allele was associated with increased risk of basal cell carcinoma and of several other cancers. It was not associated with pigment phenotypes.

In a genome-wide association study of nevus count in 1,524 UK female twins, with replication in 4,107 Australian adolescents, Falchi et al. (2009) found strongly associated variants in MTAP, next to CDKN2A on 9p21 (rs4636294, combined P = 3.4 x 10(-15)), and in PLA2G6 on 22q13.1 (rs2284063, combined P = 3.4 x 10(-8)). Variants at both loci were associated with melanoma risk in 3,131 melanoma cases from 2 independent studies. In the Leeds sample, subjects homozygous for the risk alleles at both loci had about double the risk (OR = 1.94).

Pleasance et al. (2010) sequenced the genome of the malignant melanoma cell line COLO-829 and a lymphoblastoid line from the same patient. The cell line had been derived from a metastasis in a 43-year-old man in whom no skin primary was identified. They identified 33,345 somatic base substitutions, 292 of them in protein-coding sequences, along with small indels and 51 predicted somatic rearrangements. Genome-wide sequencing showed BRAF V600E and an internal 12-kb homozygous deletion of PTEN. A 2-bp deletion in CDKN2A was found only on a targeted search of sequence reads. They also identified 2 somatic missense mutations in SPDEF, and a further somatic SPDEF mutation (W158X) in 1 of 48 additional metastatic melanomas.

Wei et al. (2011) performed whole-exome sequencing of 14 matched normal and metastatic melanoma DNAs and identified 68 genes that appeared to be somatically mutated at elevated frequency. In the TRRAP gene, a recurrent mutation, a c.2165C-T transition resulting in a ser722-to-phe (S722F) substitution, occurred in 6 of 167 affected individuals (about 4%). Mutant TRRAP transformed NIH 3T3 cells more efficiently than wildtype. Knockdown of TRRAP by shRNA increased apoptosis in melanoma lines carrying the mutation but had minimal effect in wildtype lines, suggesting that TRRAP acts as an oncogene. The authors also identified 34 distinct GRIN2A mutations in 135 samples (25.2%), including 3 recurrent alterations (S278F, E371K, and E1175K) and 5 nonsense mutations. The mechanism by which GRIN2A mutations act was unclear.

Stark et al. (2011) sequenced the exomes of 8 metastatic melanoma cell lines and matched lymphoblastoid lines. In a prevalence screen, they found somatic nonsynonymous mutations in MAP3K5 in 8 of 85 melanoma cell lines and in MAP3K9 in 13 of 85. With one exception, mutations in the 2 genes did not occur together. Loss of heterozygosity was found at MAP3K5 in 85% of melanoma samples and at MAP3K9 in 67%.

Nikolaev et al. (2011) performed exome sequencing of 7 melanoma cell lines and donor-matched germline cells. In 2 melanomas with noncanonical BRAF mutations, they found gain-of-function mutations in MAP2K1 (MEK1) and MAP2K2 (MEK2) that caused constitutive ERK phosphorylation and higher resistance to MEK inhibitors. In a larger cohort, recurrent somatic MAP2K1 and MAP2K2 mutations occurred at an overall frequency of 8%. Somatic mutations were also frequent in FAT4, LRP1B, and DSC1.

Horn et al. (2013) studied a melanoma-prone family by linkage analysis and high-throughput sequencing and identified a disease-segregating germline mutation in the promoter of the TERT gene. The mutation created a new binding motif for Ets/TCF transcription factors and caused up to a 2-fold increase in transcription in reporter assays. In sporadic melanoma, recurrent somatic TERT promoter mutations with an ultraviolet signature were present in 125 of 168 (74%) metastatic melanoma cell lines, 45 of 53 (85%) corresponding metastatic tumor tissues, and 25 of 77 (33%) primary melanomas.

Huang et al. (2013) found 2 mutually exclusive somatic C-T mutations (C228T and C250T) in the TERT core promoter in 50 of 70 (71%) melanomas. Both lay within 100 bp of the transcription start site and carried a UV-damage signature. Each created a consensus ETS binding motif and increased transcription from the TERT promoter about 2- to 4-fold in luciferase reporter assays.

Hayward et al. (2017) analyzed whole-genome sequences of cutaneous, acral, and mucosal melanomas. Cutaneous melanoma was heavily mutated, with mutation signatures attributable to ultraviolet radiation, and its significantly mutated genes were BRAF, CDKN2A, NRAS, and TP53. TERT promoter mutations were the most frequent mutations in melanoma but were not associated with greater telomere length.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Tucker et al. (2002) found that melanomas and dysplastic nevi did not appear to differ between families with CDKN2A mutations, CDK4 mutations, or no identified mutation.

Falchi et al. (2009) found that UK twins homozygous for the risk variants at both rs4636294 and rs2284063 had more than twice as many nevi as those homozygous for the protective alleles (median 30 vs 12.5). When the association between melanoma and these SNPs was adjusted for nevus count, the odds ratio moved toward 1.0. The authors interpreted this as consistent with melanoma risk at these loci being mediated through nevus count.

### Pathogenesis

Howell et al. (1984) compared skin fibroblast strains from patients with hereditary cutaneous malignant melanoma and the dysplastic nevus syndrome. All 5 strains tested were more sensitive than normal to killing by 4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide (4NQO), with 2- to 3-fold steeper survival curves, but only 1 was hypersensitive to killing by simulated sunlight. Two strains tested for mutagenicity were both hypermutable after 4NQO exposure. The authors suggested that hypersensitivity to mutagens contributes to melanoma risk in these patients.

Dracopoli et al. (1987) found that all 6 metastases from a melanoma patient had inactivated the same X chromosome and showed identical segregation at loci on chromosomes 9 and X, supporting a monoclonal origin. They proposed a model in which metastatic cells accumulate selectively neutral and advantageous segregations. Dracopoli et al. (1989) suggested that frequent loss of heterozygosity on 1p in melanoma, neuroblastoma, and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 is a common late event in the progression of neuroectodermal tumors.

Gilchrest et al. (1999) reviewed the pathogenesis of melanoma induced by ultraviolet radiation. Pleasance et al. (2010) found that most somatic substitutions in the COLO-829 melanoma genome were C-T/G-A transitions, 92% of them at the 3-prime base of pyrimidine dinucleotides, indicating that most were caused by UV light-induced DNA damage. Wei et al. (2011) likewise found a predominance of C-T/G-A transitions and CC-TT/GG-AA dinucleotide substitutions among melanoma somatic mutations, consistent with UV exposure.

Pollock et al. (2003) concluded that mutational activation of the RAS/RAF/MAPK pathway in nevi is a critical step in the initiation of melanocytic neoplasia but is not sufficient alone for melanoma tumorigenesis. Curtin et al. (2005) concluded that the genetic alterations in melanomas at different sites and with different sun exposure indicate distinct genetic pathways in melanoma development.

Schatton et al. (2008) identified a subpopulation of human malignant melanoma-initiating cells defined by expression of the chemoresistance mediator ABCB5. In serial human-to-mouse xenotransplantation, ABCB5+ cells had greater tumorigenic capacity than ABCB5- bulk populations and re-established tumor heterogeneity. Lineage tracking showed that ABCB5+ cells could self-renew and give rise to both ABCB5+ and ABCB5- progeny.

Passeron et al. (2009) found that SOX9 was increasingly downregulated as melanocytes progressed to premalignant, malignant, and metastatic states. In melanoma cell lines, overexpression of SOX9 caused cell cycle arrest by increasing p21 transcription and restored sensitivity to retinoic acid by downregulating PRAME.

Stark et al. (2011) found that the MAP3K9 W333X and MAP3K5 I780F mutants reduced kinase activity by 95% and 93%, respectively. Expressing these mutants reduced downstream JNK and MEK/ERK signaling, and knocking down MAP3K9 increased melanoma cell viability after temozolomide treatment.

Das Thakur et al. (2013) modeled vemurafenib resistance in a patient-derived BRAF(V600E) melanoma xenograft. Resistant tumors still depended on BRAF(V600E)-MEK-ERK signaling because they expressed more BRAF(V600E). Resistant cells became dependent on the drug for proliferation, and stopping the drug caused established resistant tumors to regress. Sun et al. (2014) found that suppressing SOX10 in melanoma cells activated TGF-beta signaling, which upregulated EGFR and PDGFRB and conferred resistance to BRAF and MEK inhibitors.

### Diagnosis

Reimer et al. (1978) suggested that the distinctive precursor pigmented lesions may serve as a cutaneous marker for identifying persons at high risk of melanoma.

### Clinical Management

Lynch and Krush (1968) discussed the implications of heredity in malignant melanoma for early cancer detection. Lynch et al. (1980) noted that managing FAMMM patients is difficult because it cannot be known for certain which moles need biopsy or wider excision. They stressed the lifelong susceptibility to multiple melanomas. Kopf et al. (1986) concluded that recognizing familial malignant melanoma has practical implications for early detection and prompt intervention.

Vasen et al. (1989) compared melanomas in 9 Dutch families with the dysplastic nevus syndrome. Invasive melanomas diagnosed before screening began had an average thickness of 1.75 mm, compared with 0.80 mm for those found at the first family examination and 0.54 mm for those found during follow-up. Sixteen of 19 melanomas found before screening were Clark level III or IV, whereas 15 of 20 found during follow-up were Clark level I or II. The authors concluded that screening can detect melanomas at an earlier stage and improve prognosis.

Hejny et al. (2001) reported 7 patients who developed retinopathy during high-dose interferon alfa-2b adjuvant therapy for high-risk melanoma. In 5 patients the retinopathy resolved after treatment was stopped, whereas 2 had severe, unresolved vision loss. The authors recommended monitoring patients until the retinopathy resolves.

Davies et al. (2002) suggested that BRAF may provide new therapeutic opportunities in malignant melanoma. Bollag et al. (2010) described the structure-guided discovery of PLX4032 (RG7204), a selective inhibitor of oncogenic B-RAF(V600E) kinase activity. It caused regression of BRAF-mutant xenografts but had no effect on xenografts with wildtype BRAF.

In a phase 1 dose-escalation trial of PLX4032, Flaherty et al. (2010) set the recommended phase 2 dose at 960 mg twice daily. Sixteen patients with V600E-mutant melanoma received 240 mg or more twice daily, and 10 had a partial response and 1 a complete response. In an extension cohort of 32 patients with V600E-mutant metastatic melanoma (19 males and 13 females), 24 had a partial response and 2 a complete response. None of 5 patients whose melanoma lacked a BRAF mutation had tumor regression. Responses lasted from 2 to more than 18 months, and some tumors became resistant. Cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas, nearly all of the keratoacanthoma type, developed in 8 patients (15%) in the dose-escalation cohort and 10 (31%) in the extension cohort. In paired biopsies, phosphorylated ERK, cyclin D1, and Ki-67 were markedly reduced at day 15 of treatment.

Bollag et al. (2010) reported that clinical response to PLX4032 required greater than 80% inhibition of cytoplasmic ERK phosphorylation. Two responders carried BRAF V600K rather than V600E.

In a phase 3 randomized trial of 675 patients with previously untreated metastatic melanoma carrying the BRAF V600E mutation, Chapman et al. (2011) compared vemurafenib with dacarbazine. Vemurafenib reduced the relative risk of death by 63% and the risk of death or disease progression by 74%. At 6 months, overall survival was 84% with vemurafenib and 64% with dacarbazine. Confirmed response rates were 48% and 5%, respectively. Four of 10 patients with BRAF V600K mutations responded to vemurafenib. Eighteen percent of vemurafenib-treated patients developed cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, keratoacanthoma, or both.

Straussman et al. (2012) and Wilson et al. (2012) found that HGF, acting through the MET receptor, conferred resistance to RAF inhibitors in BRAF-mutant melanoma cells, and that MET inhibition with crizotinib reversed this resistance. Straussman et al. (2012) found that patients whose tumors had stromal HGF responded significantly worse to BRAF inhibitor treatment. Wilson et al. (2012) found that higher pretreatment plasma HGF was associated with worse progression-free and overall survival in patients treated with PLX4032.

Das Thakur et al. (2013) found that intermittent vemurafenib dosing prevented lethal drug-resistant disease in xenograft models. Sun et al. (2014) found that 6 of 16 melanomas acquired EGFR expression after becoming resistant to BRAF or MEK inhibitors. In mixed cell populations, cells with low SOX10 and high EGFR were enriched during drug treatment, and this enrichment reversed when treatment stopped. The authors suggested this may explain why some patients regain drug sensitivity after a 'drug holiday.'

Snyder et al. (2014) performed whole-exome sequencing of melanomas from 64 patients treated with anti-CTLA-4 antibodies. Mutational load was significantly associated with long-term clinical benefit but alone was not sufficient to predict it, and a neoepitope signature correlated with overall survival. In 110 patients treated with ipilimumab, Van Allen et al. (2015) found that patients with clinical benefit had significantly higher nonsynonymous mutational and neoantigen loads. However, they found no recurrent predictive neoantigen peptides, and the previously reported tetrapeptide signature was not enriched in responders.

### Animal Model

Using a genetically engineered mouse model, Noonan et al. (2001) showed that a single dose of burning ultraviolet radiation given to neonates, but not to adults, was necessary and sufficient to induce tumors resembling human melanoma with high penetrance. The authors concluded that this supported epidemiologic evidence that childhood sunburn poses a significant risk of melanoma.

Gaffal et al. (2011) gave 15 newborn Hgf-Cdk4(R24C) mice on the C57BL/6 background a single erythemogenic dose of UVB (6 kJ/m2) and compared them with 30 untreated mice. Neonatal UVB exposure shortened the latency and accelerated the growth of primary melanomas, and it shortened the time from melanoma onset to melanoma-related death (61 vs 96 days). It did not change the development of melanocytic nevi. Lung metastases were more frequent in irradiated mice (73% vs 47%). The UVB-induced tumors contained very few infiltrating immune cells.

### History

Norris (1820) reported a case of what he termed 'fungoid disease.' Hecht (1989) reviewed this 1820 description by Norris of hereditary malignant melanoma of the skin.

Bale et al. (1985) observed significant heterogeneity among subgroups of families. They suggested that preferential reporting of positive linkage results and misclassification of subjects' susceptibility status may have contributed to the earlier belief that the hereditary melanoma gene was linked to HLA.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 MELANOMA, CUTANEOUS MALIGNANT, SOMATIC**  
BRAF, VAL599GLU [p.V599E]

Davies et al. (2002) found somatic missense mutations in the BRAF gene in 66% of malignant melanomas. All were within the kinase domain, and a single substitution, val599-to-glu (V599E), accounted for 80% of them. Mutated BRAF proteins had elevated kinase activity and transformed NIH3T3 cells, and RAS function was not required for the growth of cancer cell lines carrying V599E.

**.0002 MELANOMA, CUTANEOUS MALIGNANT, SOMATIC**  
BRAF, VAL600GLU [p.V600E]

In the melanoma cell line COLO-829, derived from a metastasis in a 43-year-old man, Pleasance et al. (2010) identified a somatic BRAF val600-to-glu (V600E) mutation by genome-wide sequencing.

Flaherty et al. (2010) treated patients with metastatic melanoma whose tumors harbored the V600E BRAF mutation with the mutant BRAF inhibitor PLX4032. Of 16 such patients in the dose-escalation cohort who received 240 mg or more twice daily, 11 (69%) responded. Of 32 patients in the extension cohort, 26 (81%) responded. None of 5 patients whose melanoma lacked a BRAF mutation had tumor regression.

**.0003 MELANOMA, CUTANEOUS MALIGNANT, SOMATIC**  
TRRAP, SER722PHE [c.2165C>T; p.S722F]

By exome sequencing of matched normal and metastatic melanoma DNAs, Wei et al. (2011) identified a recurrent somatic mutation in the TRRAP gene in 6 of 167 affected individuals (about 4%). The mutation is a c.2165C-T transition resulting in a ser722-to-phe (S722F) substitution. Mutant TRRAP transformed NIH 3T3 cells more efficiently than wildtype. Knockdown of TRRAP by shRNA increased apoptosis in melanoma lines carrying the mutation but had minimal effect in wildtype lines.
