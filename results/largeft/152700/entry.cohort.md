### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that susceptibility to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is conferred by variation in a number of genes. These include heterozygous mutation in the TREX1 gene (606609) on chromosome 3p21.31, the DNASE1 gene (125505) on chromosome 16p13.3, and the FASLG gene (134638) on chromosome 1q24.3; homozygous deficiency of the C4A gene (120810) on chromosome 6p21.33; low copy number of the FCGR3B gene (610665) on chromosome 1q23.3; and polymorphisms in the FCGR2B gene (604590) on chromosome 1q23.3, the PTPN22 gene (600716) on chromosome 1p13.2, the CRP gene (123260) on chromosome 1q23.2, the TNFSF4 gene (603594) on chromosome 1q25.1, and the TNFAIP3 gene (191163) on chromosome 6q23.3.

### Description

Systemic lupus erythematosus is a multisystem autoimmune inflammatory disease that mainly affects women. International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) gave the female-to-male ratio as 9:1. Prokunina et al. (2002) described SLE as a complex autoimmune disease affecting 0.05% of the Western population. The disease clusters in families: Kelly et al. (2002) cited lambda(s) estimates of 10 to 20 and a monozygotic twin concordance rate greater than 20%. SLEGEN et al. (2008) estimated concordance at about 30% in monozygotic twins and about 3% in dizygotic twins, with a sibling relative risk ratio of 29.

### Clinical Features

Tuffanelli (1971) described lupus erythematosus panniculitis (lupus profundus), a form of lupus erythematosus that affects the subcutaneous fat.

Reed et al. (1972) described hereditary inflammatory vasculitis with persistent nodules. They considered it a genetically determined new entity, probably related to lupus erythematosus.

Schaller (1972) reported an illness resembling lupus erythematosus in the mothers of boys with chronic granulomatous disease.

Kohler et al. (1974) reported identical twin boys who had hereditary angioedema and 'familial' lupus erythematosus.

Yocum et al. (1975) studied a pair of monozygotic twins who were discordant for SLE. Both twins had biologically false-positive serology at an early age, but only one developed SLE. The twins did not differ in their immune responses to tetanus toxoid, keyhole limpet hemocyanin, or DNCB, in delayed sensitivity, or in antiviral antibody titers.

Arnett and Shulman (1976) studied 8 families with familial SLE. In one family, the affected parent had SLE and Hashimoto thyroiditis. When they compared familial cases with matched nonfamilial controls on 23 clinical and laboratory features, they found no real differences, and they concluded that familial SLE is probably not a separate disease from nonfamilial SLE. They also examined 27 unaffected first-degree relatives. Two sisters had thyroid disease, persistent leukopenia, and a raised sedimentation rate; their mother had SLE and Hashimoto thyroiditis, and their sib had SLE. The other 25 relatives were normal clinically, hematologically, biochemically, and serologically.

Brustein et al. (1977) reported a woman with discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) whose child developed DLE lesions at 2 months of age. At age 28 months, the original lesions persisted and new lesions continued to develop. A second child developed a probable lupus erythematosus rash at 1 week of age and was 11 months old at the time of the report.

Weinberg et al. (1980) studied a large pedigree segregating elliptocytosis, multiple lipomatosis, and a biological false-positive serologic test for syphilis (BFPSTS). Two female members of the pedigree with BFPSTS developed SLE, which agreed with the previously described association. The analysis did not support any conclusion about the causal relationship between the 2 traits.

Exner et al. (1980) detected a lupus anticoagulant in plasma from 2 pairs of sibs using the kaolin clotting time mixing test, and presented strong evidence for this inhibitor in a third sib pair. They concluded that the lupus anticoagulant may often be an acquired coagulation defect that requires a genetic predisposition.

Reveille et al. (1983) studied 8 families, each with 2 or more members with SLE (22 patients), and 40 of their relatives. Four of the 22 patients had another immune disease: 3 had thyroid disease and 1 had progressive systemic sclerosis. Antibodies to dsDNA, Ro(SSA), Sm, or nRNP were found only in the patients with SLE. Of the 40 relatives without SLE, 7 (18%) had other immune diseases, including thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, ITP, and Henoch-Schonlein purpura. Eleven relatives (28%) had ANA and 11 (28%) had antibodies to ssDNA.

Lahita et al. (1983) described several families in which SLE occurred mainly in males, chiefly in sons and their fathers. Females in some of these families had other autoimmune diseases, such as idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, or had symptoms suggesting a lupus diathesis.

Stohl et al. (1985) identified 3 black Jamaicans with SLE whose T helper/inducer cells lacked the T4 epitope (the T4 epitope-deficient phenotype). All 3 patients had lymphadenopathy as part of their disease.

Mackie et al. (1987) studied 3 families with more than 1 member affected by SLE or lupus-like disease. Of the 19 persons studied, 11 had SLE or lupus-like disease. Eight of these 11 had a prolonged kaolin partial thromboplastin time (KPTT) and other evidence of lupus anticoagulant, but only 5 of the 11 had high anticardiolipin titers.

Sibley et al. (1993) described a family in which 3 members developed SLE complicated by ischemic vasculopathy with digital ischemia. The 3 patients had remarkably similar calendar years of onset, ages at onset, and clinical courses, although their genotypes differed. The authors discussed how heredity and environment might each have contributed to this concordance.

García et al. (1996) compared autoantibody profiles in 222 African American patients with SLE, 94 with lupus nephritis and 128 without. Positive anti-Ro/SS-A, anti-Sm, and anti-nRNP without anti-La/SS-B occurred in 14 (14.9%) patients with nephritis and 15 (11.7%) without it. Only anti-dsDNA antibodies differed significantly between the 2 groups. The authors concluded that African American patients with lupus nephritis do not have a specific or distinctive autoantibody profile.

Elçioglu and Hall (1998) described 2 sibs with chondrodysplasia punctata whose mother had SLE, and they reported the radiologic features of the sibs. After comparison with other forms of chondrodysplasia punctata, they drew attention to a possible association between the skeletal phenotype and maternal SLE.

Austin-Ward et al. (1998) reported a newborn whose mother had been diagnosed with SLE before the pregnancy. The infant had neonatal lupus, chondrodysplasia punctata, and other findings resembling the congenital anomalies associated with oral anticoagulants, although there was no history of exposure. The authors speculated that the combined action of the mother's various autoantibodies may produce the full spectrum of manifestations.

Toriello (1998) also reported chondrodysplasia punctata in association with maternal SLE.

Kelly et al. (1999) reported a male infant whose neonatal radiographs showed stippled epiphyses and who developed a typical lupus erythematosus rash. The infant's skin findings led to a diagnosis of SLE in his mother. Over 3 years of follow-up, the child had strikingly short stature, midface hypoplasia, and anomalous digital development. The stippled epiphyses resolved slowly, and cognitive development was near normal. The authors reviewed the literature supporting maternal lupus as one cause of chondrodysplasia punctata.

Stein et al. (2002) analyzed 372 affected individuals from 160 multiplex SLE pedigrees, 25 of which included at least 1 affected male. Renal disease was significantly more frequent in female family members with an affected male relative than in those without one (68% vs 43%; P = 0.002). This trend persisted after stratification by race and was most pronounced in European Americans.

Quintero-Del-Rio et al. (2002) noted that renal disease occurs in 40 to 75% of patients with SLE and contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality.

From a collection of 184 pedigrees multiplex for SLE, Scofield et al. (2003) selected the 38 pedigrees that included an SLE patient with thrombocytopenia (platelets less than 100,000/microL); 13 of these pedigrees were African American. Thrombocytopenia was associated with nephritis, serositis, neuropsychiatric involvement, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, anti-double-stranded DNA, and antiphospholipid antibody. SLE was more severe in families with a thrombocytopenic patient, whether or not the individual patient had thrombocytopenia. The authors concluded that thrombocytopenia marks a severe familial form of SLE.

Kamat et al. (2003) reported identical triplets who developed SLE and were diagnosed in reverse birth order at ages 8, 9, and 11 years. All 3 children had skin rash, fatigue, and biopsy-proven glomerulonephritis, at different ages. Laboratory findings were similar and included antinuclear antibodies, anti-native DNA, anti-double-stranded DNA, and low complement levels. Although the triplets were genetically identical, each showed different clinical signs and symptoms of SLE.

Kozlowski et al. (2004) reported 2 brothers from Cape Town with chondrodysplasia punctata (CDP); the younger was more severely affected. Their mother had longstanding disseminated lupus erythematosus and epilepsy, which were treated with chloroquine and other agents during both pregnancies. The parents were nonconsanguineous, and the family history was otherwise unremarkable. Noting 7 earlier reports of CDP with maternal lupus erythematosus, the authors concluded that maternal lupus must be regarded as another cause of CDP.

Xing et al. (2005) added 392 individuals from 181 new families to an earlier sample. They replicated the finding that females with SLE who have affected male relatives have a higher prevalence of renal disease than females with SLE who do not, and they concluded that multiplex families with an affected male form a distinct subpopulation.

Bialas et al. (2017) noted that up to 75% of patients with SLE have neuropsychiatric symptoms. These range from anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment to seizures and, rarely, psychosis, and they usually appear at diagnosis or within the first year.

### Other Features

As part of a series of family studies in SLE, Larsen and Godal (1972) studied thyroid diseases and thyroid antibodies in SLE families.

DeHoratius et al. (1975) found anti-RNA antibodies in 82% of 28 SLE probands and anti-native DNA antibodies in 68%. Among 124 family members of the probands, 16% had anti-RNA antibodies, compared with 5% of 76 control family members. In SLE families, these antibodies occurred only in close household contacts of the probands (21% of 94); they were present in consanguineous household contacts but not in nonconsanguineous ones. Anti-native DNA antibodies were found in 6% of close household contacts, which did not differ significantly from the 1% in control families. Lymphocytotoxic antibodies were found in 57% of SLE family members overall and in 68% of close household contacts.

Beaucher et al. (1977) reported a family with lupus erythematosus in which the household dogs had antibodies to DNA.

Halpern et al. (1985) examined sera from 27 members of 3 kindreds with familial SLE. High-titer reactivity with the anti-idiotype 3I, an idiotype found on anti-DNA antibodies, was present in 6 of 8 patients with SLE and in 15 of 19 family members without SLE. By Millipore filter assay, anti-DNA antibodies were found in 4 of the 8 patients and in only 2 of the 19 unaffected relatives. The authors concluded that expression of the idiotype was significantly increased in relatives of patients with SLE and usually occurred without anti-DNA activity.

### Biochemical Features

Stohl et al. (1985) found that peripheral blood mononuclear cells from T4 epitope-deficient family members showed impaired B-cell differentiation after pokeweed mitogen stimulation, compared with cells from T4 epitope-intermediate relatives. Helper/suppressor T-cell ratios, autoantibodies, and proliferation in response to mitogens and in mixed lymphocyte reactions did not correlate with T4 epitope phenotype.

Mackie et al. (1987) found raised anticardiolipin levels in subjects with lupus anticoagulant, although the length of the KPTT did not correlate closely with anticardiolipin titer.

Levcovitz et al. (1988) found that PHA-stimulated lymphocytes from all patients with SLE produced 2 classes of newly synthesized DNA on sucrose density gradients. One was a large-molecular-weight fraction that sedimented with control DNA. The other was an excess low-molecular-weight (LMW) DNA fraction that control lymphocytes did not have. The excess LMW-DNA did not depend on disease activity or drug therapy.

In SLE patients who expressed both C4A and C4B isotypes, Sturfelt et al. (1990) found that plasma C4B/C4A quotients were fairly stable regardless of disease activity. This argued against preferential breakdown of either isotype during complement activation.

In agreement with earlier reports, Napirei et al. (2000) found lower serum DNASE1 activity in patients with SLE than in normal subjects.

Zhang et al. (2001) found that raised B lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) levels in patients with SLE were partly associated with higher levels of IgG, IgM, and IgA anti-dsDNA antibodies but not with disease activity. They suggested that BLyS may be a useful marker for early activation of an autoimmune diathesis.

Sthoeger et al. (2009) found that the mean serum level of the alpha-defensin human neutrophil peptide-1 (HNP-1) was significantly higher in patients with SLE than in controls (11.07 vs 0.12 ng/microl). Sixty percent of patients had very high serum levels, and 50% had raised alpha-defensin gene expression in polymorphonuclear cells. High levels correlated with disease activity but not with neutrophil count. Human beta-defensin-2 could not be detected in SLE sera by ELISA, and its mRNA levels were low and similar to those in controls.

### Inheritance

Early family studies of SLE included those of POLLAK (1964), who studied antinuclear antibodies in the families of patients with SLE; LEONHARDT (1964); Larsen (1972), who studied probands from central eastern Norway; and First (1973), who reported familial SLE. Lappat and Cawein (1968) reported a family study of procainamide-induced SLE and asked whether a pharmacogenetic polymorphism was involved.

Block et al. (1975) studied 12 twin pairs in which one or both twins had SLE. Four of 7 definitely monozygotic pairs (57%) were concordant for clinical SLE that met the preliminary criteria of the American Rheumatism Association. All 3 dizygotic pairs were discordant, although one clinically well co-twin had marked serologic abnormalities. In the monozygotic pairs, concordance was 71% for antinuclear factor and 87% for hypergammaglobulinemia, which agreed closely with 12 previously described monozygotic sets. When they compared the twins with other first-degree relatives, the authors concluded that SLE has a strong genetic component and that nongenetic and environmental factors also contribute to its expression.

Arnett and Shulman (1976) analyzed their 8 families together with 1 deceased pair and 31 families from the literature. These included 8 pairs of identical twins, 1 pair of nonidentical twins, 16 sib combinations, and 15 parent-offspring combinations. Disease presentation was markedly concordant between identical twins and between parents and offspring, but not between sibs, which supported genetic influences on SLE. The authors found 4 father-offspring pairs, which they said made transmission of SLE around the time of birth less likely.

Miller and Schwartz (1979) used an in vitro suppressor-cell assay to study 15 patients with SLE, 50 of their clinically healthy family members, and 41 normal persons. Suppressor-cell function was impaired in 11 patients and did not correlate with disease activity. Abnormal suppressor-cell activity was also found in 13 first-degree relatives, 12 of whom were women, and did not correlate with lymphocytotoxic antibodies in the relatives.

In the large pedigree studied by Weinberg et al. (1980), 180 relatives were tested. BFPSTS appeared to be inherited as a dominant trait, but the likelihood analysis was inconclusive, and no linkage was found between BFPSTS and any blood group or enzyme marker.

Lahita et al. (1983) suggested that, in families in which SLE occurred mainly in males, the sons had inherited the disease from their fathers.

From family studies, Stohl et al. (1985) concluded that the T4 epitope phenotype appeared to be inherited in an autosomal codominant fashion and was not linked to the HLA locus. In one family, the asymptomatic T4 epitope-deficient brother of a patient had lymphadenopathy in the same distribution as his sister with SLE.

Levcovitz et al. (1988) studied a 4-generation family in which 2 first cousins had SLE. Excess LMW-DNA in stimulated lymphocytes was found in both affected cousins, in the sib parents of the patients, in a common grandparent, in 4 of 9 sibs of one patient, and in 5 of 7 at-risk children. The trait occurred in both males and females, and male-to-male transmission was observed. The authors concluded that excess LMW-DNA in stimulated lymphocytes was inherited as an autosomal dominant trait in this family.

Sakane et al. (1989) studied 34 clinically healthy family members of 6 SLE probands. Phytohemagglutinin-induced IL-2 activity was impaired in 15 of 29 blood relatives but in none of 5 unrelated household members, which suggested that impaired IL-2 activity correlates strongly with genetic relationship.

Gaffney et al. (1998) concluded that their genome screen of 105 sib-pair families supported the hypothesis that multiple genes, including one in the HLA region, influence susceptibility to SLE. Shai et al. (1999) found no single locus with overwhelming evidence for linkage. They suggested that no 'major' susceptibility genes segregate in SLE families and that SLE more likely involves several genes of moderate effect.

Stein et al. (2002) concluded that the higher prevalence of renal disease previously reported in men with SLE reflects, in large part, a familial rather than a sex-based difference, at least in multiplex SLE families. Differences in hematologic and immunologic manifestations also appeared to have a familial basis, whereas arthritis and dermatologic features appeared to be influenced mostly by sex.

Molokhia et al. (2003) concluded that their admixture data favored an additive genetic model for the ethnic difference in SLE risk between west Africans and Europeans over an environmental explanation or an overdominant model.

In 18 African American families with affected male members, Xing et al. (2005) found that identity-by-descent sharing at 13q32 suggested a dominant mode of inheritance.

International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) found by stepwise logistic regression that 6 SNPs contributed independently to SLE susceptibility (C statistic 0.67). Together these SNPs explained about 15% of the sibling risk ratio of 29, an estimate the authors described as optimistic. They found no evidence of epistasis among the associated SNPs. Gateva et al. (2009) found no evidence of nonadditive interactions among 26 confirmed SLE risk alleles. HLA-DRB1 (DR3), IRF5, and STAT4 each accounted for more than 1% of the genetic variance, and together the 26 loci explained an estimated 8% of total genetic susceptibility.

### Mapping

Reveille et al. (1983) found HLA-DR2 and DR3 each in 36% of patients with familial SLE, which did not differ significantly from relatives or controls. In an analysis of 14 sib pair sets, 7 of them from published reports, HLA haplotypes were distributed randomly with respect to SLE. Heterozygous C2 deficiency was found in 1 of 7 kindreds studied; it followed an A25, B18, DR7 haplotype but was inherited by only 1 of 3 affected family members. The authors concluded that genetic factors other than HLA and complement deficiencies, and/or environmental factors, are needed for SLE to be expressed.

Fronek et al. (1986) found a similar distribution of restriction fragment length polymorphisms (RFLPs) of the T cell receptor beta chain genes in patients with SLE, their relatives, and normal controls. They suggested that these genes are not coinherited with SLE susceptibility genes. Wong et al. (1988) used Southern blots to study RFLPs of the T cell receptor alpha, beta, and gamma chain genes in 5 multiplex SLE families, 14 unrelated SLE patients, and 14 controls. RFLP patterns did not differ significantly among the groups, and none of the 3 genes cosegregated with SLE in the multiplex families.

Gaffney et al. (1998) carried out a genome-wide microsatellite screen in 105 SLE sib-pair families. Multipoint nonparametric analysis gave the strongest evidence for linkage near HLA on 6p11-p21 (D6S257, lod = 3.90), with further regions at 16q13 (D16S415, lod = 3.64), 14q21-q23 (D14S276, lod = 2.81), and 20p12 (D20S186, lod = 2.62). Nine more regions had lod scores of 1.00 or greater: 1p36, 1p13, 1q42, 2p15, 2q21-q33, 3cen-q11, 4q28, 11p15, and 15q26.

Shai et al. (1999) performed a genome-wide screen with 350 polymorphic markers (average spacing 12 cM) in 188 SLE patients from 80 multiplex families of Mexican American and Caucasian ancestry. Nonparametric multipoint linkage analysis suggested predisposing loci on chromosomes 1 and 18. Clear support for loci at 1q44 and 1p36 came only from the Mexican American families, and the authors suggested that each ethnic group should be considered separately.

Lindqvist et al. (2000) performed genome scans in Icelandic and Swedish families with multiple patients with SLE. In the Icelandic families, 5 regions had lod scores greater than 2.0: 4p15-p13 (Z = 3.20), 9p22 (Z = 2.27), 19q13 (Z = 2.06), 19p13 (D19S247, Z = 2.58), and 2q37 (D2S125, Z = 2.06). The first 3 are homologous to murine regions containing the lmb2, sle2, and sle3 loci. In the Swedish families, only 2q11 (D2S436, Z = 2.13) and 2q37 (D2S125, Z = 2.18) exceeded 2.0. With the 2 family sets combined, the lod score at D2S125 was 4.24, and the authors designated this new locus on 2q37 hSLE1.

In a second genome screen of 82 new SLE sib-pair families, Gaffney et al. (2000) found the best intervals at 7p22 (lod 2.87), 7q21 (lod 2.40), 10p13 (lod 2.24), and 7q36 (lod 2.15), and 8 more intervals had lod scores of 1.00 to 1.67. A combined analysis of 187 sib-pair families from the 2 Minnesota cohorts gave significant linkage at 6p11-p21 (D6S426, lod 4.19) and 16q13 (D16S415, lod 3.85).

In 126 pedigrees multiplex for SLE, which included 469 sib pairs and 175 affected relative pairs, Gray-McGuire et al. (2000) found a novel linkage to chromosome 4p16-15.2 (P = 0.0003, lod = 3.84) using revised multipoint Haseman-Elston regression and conditional logistic regression. They confirmed this linkage in European American families from an independent collection and presented evidence of epistasis between 4p16-15.2 and 5p15 in European American families. Their data also supported published results for 9 putative SLE regions.

Graham et al. (2002) mapped the HLA region in a large collection of SLE families using dense microsatellites. They identified 3 class II haplotypes showing transmission distortion, which carried DRB1*1501/DQB1*0602, DRB1*0801/DQB1*0402, and DRB1*0301/DQB1*0201. Using ancestral recombinants, they narrowed the DRB1*1501 and DRB1*0801 haplotypes to a region of approximately 500 kb, and they concluded that HLA class II haplotypes containing DRB1 and DQB1 alleles are strong risk factors for SLE.

Quintero-Del-Rio et al. (2002) analyzed 75 SLE pedigrees in which at least 1 patient met the American College of Rheumatology renal criterion. In European American pedigrees, they found a lod score of 3.16 at 10q22.3 and a sib-pair regression P value of 0.0000008, and they designated this locus SLEN1. In African American pedigrees, they found linkage to 2q34-35 (P = 0.000001), designated SLEN2. In 35 pedigrees with 2 or more patients with renal disease, African American families gave a lod score of 3.34 at 11p15.6 (SLEN3). Sib-pair analysis in these 35 pedigrees also suggested linkage at 4q13.1 in European Americans and at 11p13 and 3q23 in African Americans.

Kelly et al. (2002) reviewed linkage studies in multiplex SLE pedigrees, which had identified 60 susceptibility loci. Seven met the threshold for significant linkage: 1q22-q23, 1q41, 2q37, 4p16, 6p21-p11, 16q13, and 17p13. The linkages at 1q41, 4p16, and 6p21 had been independently confirmed, and there was suggestive supporting evidence for 1q22-q23 and 2q37.

Johanneson et al. (2002) genotyped 87 multicase SLE families of European (Iceland, Sweden, England, Norway, Italy, Greece) and recently admixed (Mexico, Colombia, United States) origin for 62 chromosome 1 microsatellite markers. Six previously described regions (1p36, 1p21, 1q23, 1q25, 1q31, and 1q43) had lod scores of 1.50 or higher, and their contributions differed by population. A significant 3-point lod score of 3.79 at 1q31, to which families from all populations contributed, led the authors to conclude that 1q31 contains a major SLE susceptibility gene.

In the 38 pedigrees with a thrombocytopenic SLE patient, Scofield et al. (2003) found linkage at 1q22-23 (maximum lod = 3.71). In the 13 African American pedigrees, they found linkage at 11p13 (maximum lod = 5.72).

Xu et al. (2004) noted that the gene encoding COX-2 lies in a lupus-susceptibility region on chromosome 1.

In a genome-wide scan of 64 multiplex SLE families with at least 1 affected male, Xing et al. (2005) found the strongest signal at 13q32 (lod = 3.08) in 18 African American families. Multipoint model-based analysis gave a lod score of 3.13 under a dominant model, with a disease allele frequency of 0.0004 and penetrance of 0.5. Fine mapping in these families and 3 more African American male families placed the locus in a region tightly linked to D13S892, confirming earlier linkage to 13q32. The authors suggested that this gene is specific to African Americans with a form of SLE in which males are at high risk.

Lee and Nath (2005) carried out a genome search meta-analysis of 12 genome scans from 9 studies, which together included 605 families with 1,355 affected individuals of Caucasian, African American, and Hispanic ethnicity. Two regions reached genome-wide significance: 6p22.3-p21.1, which contains HLA, and 16p12.3-q12.2. Twelve other bins were considered highly likely to contain susceptibility loci, including the novel regions 4q32.1-q34.3 and 13q13.2-q22.2. In Caucasians alone, the strongest linkage was at 6p21.1-q15, and a novel region at 3q22.1-q25.33 may represent an ethnicity-specific linkage.

In a targeted scan of 1q21-43 in 126 lupus multiplex families with 151 affected sib pairs, Wu et al. (2007) found evidence of linkage at 1q32.2 (nonparametric linkage score 2.52, P = 0.006).

Remmers et al. (2007) fine-mapped the STAT1-STAT4 region in a chromosome 2q interval linked to rheumatoid arthritis and identified a STAT4 intron 3 haplotype, which they then tested in lupus case-control series (see Molecular Genetics).

Genomewide Association Studies Hom et al. (2008) genotyped more than 500,000 SNPs in 1,311 SLE cases and 1,783 controls, all North Americans of European descent, plus 1,557 additional public controls. They replicated the findings in 793 cases and 857 controls from Sweden. Variation upstream of BLK and C8orf13 on 8p23.1 was associated with SLE (rs13277113; OR, 1.39; P = 1 x 10(-10)), as were variants on 16p11.22 near ITGAM and ITGAX (rs11574637; OR, 1.33; P = 3 x 10(-11)).

International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) scanned 317,501 SNPs in 720 women of European ancestry with SLE and 2,337 controls, and replicated the findings in 1,846 affected women and 1,825 controls. In addition to HLA and IRF5, they found replicated association at 16p11.2 (ITGAM), 11p15.5 (KIAA1542), 3p14.3 (PXK), and 1q25.1 (rs10798269).

Kozyrev et al. (2008) found an association between SLE and a nonsynonymous BANK1 SNP (rs10516487, R61H) in a scan of 85,042 SNPs, and replicated it in 4 case-control sets (combined P = 3.7 x 10(-10); OR = 1.38).

Graham et al. (2008) performed a genomewide association scan of 311,238 SNPs in 431 unrelated SLE cases and 2,155 controls. Three regions reached genomewide significance: HLA, IRF5/TNPO3, and a novel locus on 6q23 within TNFAIP3.

Han et al. (2009) performed a genomewide association study in a Chinese Han population (1,047 cases and 1,205 controls) and replicated 78 SNPs in 3,152 cases and 7,050 controls. They identified 9 new susceptibility loci: ETS1, IKZF1, RASGRP3, SLC15A4, TNIP1, 7q11.23, 10q11.22, 11q23.3, and 16p11.2. They also confirmed association with BLK, IRF5, STAT4, TNFAIP3, TNFSF4, 6q21, and 22q11.21.

Gateva et al. (2009) genotyped SNPs from 2,466 regions with nominal association in a genome-wide scan in 1,963 cases and 4,329 controls from the US and Sweden. They identified 5 new loci at genomewide significance: TNIP1 (OR = 1.27), PRDM1 (OR = 1.20), JAZF1 (OR = 1.20), UHRF1BP1 (OR = 1.17), and IL10 (OR = 1.19). The TNIP1 intronic variant rs7708392 (combined P = 3.8 x 10(-13)) appeared distinct from the nearby psoriasis risk variants. The intergenic PRDM1-ATG5 variant rs6568431 appeared distinct from the previously reported ATG5 risk allele rs2245214. The JAZF1 promoter variant rs849142 had previously been associated with type 2 diabetes and height. The IL10 variant rs3024505 was identical to a SNP associated with ulcerative colitis and type 1 diabetes.

Gateva et al. (2009) also tested 25 SNPs from 23 previously reported SLE loci and found further evidence of association for 21. Nine reached genomewide significance: HLA-DRB1 (DR3), IRF5, TNFAIP3, BLK, STAT4, ITGAM, PTPN22, PHRF1 (KIAA1542), and TNFSF4. There was no association for LYN, SCUBE1, TLR5, or LY9. At the IRAK1-MECP2 locus on Xq28, the strongest association was with SNPs near IRAK1. They identified 21 more candidate loci with P < 1 x 10(-5), including IRF8, TAOK3, LYST, and IL12RB2. Of 35 loci associated with other autoimmune diseases, 5 showed suggestive association with SLE: IFIH1, CFB, CLEC16A, IL12B, and SH2B3.

Using the Immunochip, Zhao et al. (2017) found the strongest association in Chinese at rs73366469 in the GTF2IRD1-GTF2I intergenic region on 7q11.23 (P = 3.8 x 10(-29), OR = 2.88). The association was modestly confirmed in European Americans but not seen in African Americans. A SNP in intron 9 of GTF2I, rs117026326, accounted for this signal (see Molecular Genetics for NCF1).

### Heterogeneity

Batchelor et al. (1980) found that the distribution of DR antigens in hydralazine-induced SLE differed significantly from that in idiopathic SLE, which supported the view that the 2 are separate entities.

Stohl et al. (1985) suggested that SLE with the T4 epitope-deficient phenotype may be a distinct subset with its own clinical and immunologic features.

Quintero-Del-Rio et al. (2002) concluded that multiple genetic linkages are related to the renal criterion in SLE. Kelly et al. (2002) observed that SLE linkages usually dominated in one ethnic group or another, which suggested that the responsible polymorphisms would also vary by ethnicity.

Fanciulli et al. (2007) found that low FCGR3B copy number was also associated with Wegener granulomatosis and microscopic polyangiitis, but not with the organ-specific autoimmune disorders Graves disease and Addison disease. In contrast, Willcocks et al. (2008) found that ANCA-associated systemic vasculitis was associated with high FCGR3B copy number (P = 10(-8)) in 556 patients compared with 286 controls. When the 2 diseases were compared directly, patients with SLE had a lower mean FCGR3B/CD36 ratio than patients with vasculitis (P = 0.0028).

Han et al. (2009) found genetic heterogeneity in SLE susceptibility between Chinese Han and European populations.

### Molecular Genetics

Fielder et al. (1983) studied the families of 29 patients with SLE and 42 normal subjects. Null (silent) alleles for C4A, C4B, or C2 were found in 24 of the 29 patients (83%) and in 18 of the 42 controls (43%). HLA-DR3 was found in 20 patients (69%) and in 7 of 39 controls (18%). Because DR3 was in strong linkage disequilibrium with the C4A and C4B null alleles, the authors could not separate the contributions of the 2 factors. They suggested that the complement null alleles found in most patients are relevant to susceptibility. Green et al. (1986) applied the empirical logistic method to reported data and concluded that the association with C4 null alleles is primary and that the DR3 association is probably secondary.

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found homozygous C4A deficiency in 13 of 80 (16%) patients with SLE from a defined population in southern Sweden.

Wu et al. (1996) screened DNA from 75 patients with SLE by SSCP analysis of the FASL extracellular domain. They found a heterozygous variant in only 1 patient, who had lymphadenopathy: an 84-bp deletion in exon 4 predicted to cause an in-frame deletion of 28 amino acids. The authors suggested that FASL mutations are an uncommon cause of SLE.

Linker-Israeli et al. (1999) genotyped an AT-rich minisatellite in the 3-prime flanking region of IL6 and the 5-prime promoter-enhancer in 146 patients with SLE and 139 controls. Minisatellite allele distributions differed significantly between patients and controls in both Caucasians and African Americans. Short alleles (792 bp or less) were seen only in patients, and the 828-bp allele was overrepresented in controls. There was no association with the 5-prime G/C alleles.

Yasutomo et al. (2001) described 2 patients with SLE who had a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2 of DNASE1, decreased DNASE1 activity, and an extremely high IgG titer against nucleosomal antigens. Noting that Dnase1-deficient mice develop an SLE-like syndrome, the authors concluded that their data were consistent with a direct connection between low DNASE1 activity and progression of SLE.

Kyogoku et al. (2002) found a SNP in FCGR2B, c.695T-C, that causes an ile232-to-thr (I232T) substitution in the transmembrane domain. In 193 Japanese patients with SLE and 303 healthy controls, the 232T/T genotype was significantly more frequent in patients. FCGR3A-176F/F was also significantly associated with SLE, and 2-locus analyses suggested that both FCGR2B and FCGR3A may contribute to susceptibility. The authors considered the previously reported FCGR3B association to be secondary to strong linkage disequilibrium with FCGR2B.

Kelly et al. (2002) reviewed known genetic associations with SLE at candidate loci, including HLA alleles, complement component genes, and Fc-gamma receptors. They noted that murine lupus models had suggested additional candidate genes and syntenic linkages. Johanneson et al. (2002) analyzed a known CD45 mutation in their SLE families, and the results did not support a role for it in the disease.

Prokunina et al. (2002) studied 2,510 individuals, including members of 5 independent family sets and unrelated patients, for SNPs in PDCD1, the strongest candidate gene in the SLEB2 locus previously identified in Nordic multicase families. An intronic PDCD1 SNP was associated with SLE in Europeans (12% of affected vs 5% of controls; P = 0.00001; relative risk 2.6) and in Mexicans (7% vs 2%; P = 0.0009; relative risk 3.5).

Russell et al. (2004) studied CRP and APCS in 2 cohorts totaling 586 UK simplex SLE families. Most families were European Caucasian, and the others were Afro-Caribbean, Indo-Asian, Oriental, or mixed. Family-based tests showed that the less common A allele of CRP 4 (rs1205), a SNP in the 3-prime flanking/untranslated region, was overtransmitted to probands (TRANSMIT P = 0.003 in all families; 143 vs 92 transmissions in 394 trios, P = 0.0008). CRP haplotype 2, which carries this allele, was associated with SLE. The allele was also overtransmitted to individuals with antinuclear antibodies (P = 0.002), but it showed no association with renal lupus or with other autoantibodies. No coding polymorphisms other than synonymous changes were found in CRP or APCS, and APCS polymorphisms were not significantly associated with SLE.

Li et al. (2004) found that patients with SLE carried a gain-of-function type of LTK polymorphism significantly more often than healthy controls. They suggested that it may form one genetic component of susceptibility to abnormal proliferation of self-reactive B cells.

Barreto et al. (2004) found that a dinucleotide repeat in the CTLA4 3-prime UTR was associated with SLE, with one allele conferring susceptibility and another conferring protection. They suggested that the repeat is in linkage disequilibrium with a true causative locus. The exon 1 +49 SNP was not associated with SLE in their population. However, in a meta-analysis of 7 published studies plus their own, carriers of the G allele had a significantly increased risk and carriers of the A allele a significantly decreased risk. In a meta-analysis of 14 studies, Lee et al. (2005) found that the CTLA4 exon 1 +49 GG genotype was associated with SLE (OR = 1.287; P = 0.011).

Su et al. (2004) identified 10 new SNPs in the FCGR2B promoter, which defined 2 functionally distinct proximal promoter haplotypes. In 600 Caucasians, the less frequent haplotype was associated with SLE (OR = 1.65; p = 0.0054), and the association held after adjustment for FCGR2A and FCGR3A polymorphisms (OR = 1.72; p = 0.0083). This haplotype increased reporter expression in B lymphoid and myeloid cell lines.

Kyogoku et al. (2004) genotyped 525 North American white patients with SLE and 1,961 white controls for the PTPN22 R620W polymorphism. The variant was associated with SLE (genotypic P = 0.00009), with minor (T) allele frequencies of 12.67% in cases and 8.64% in controls. One copy of the W620 allele increased risk (OR 1.37), and 2 copies more than doubled it (OR 4.37).

In 350 Korean patients with SLE and 330 controls, Shin et al. (2004) found no association between SLE risk and 6 DNASE1 SNPs. However, the exon 8 SNP +2373A-G (gln244-to-arg) was associated with anti-RNP and anti-dsDNA antibody production among patients. The exon 2 A/T mutation previously reported in 2 Japanese patients was not found in any Korean patient or control, and the authors concluded that this mutation is absent in Caucasian, African, and Asian populations.

Sigurdsson et al. (2005) analyzed 44 SNPs in 13 type I interferon pathway genes in 679 Swedish, Finnish, and Icelandic patients with SLE, 798 unaffected family members, and 438 unrelated controls. SNPs in TYK2 and IRF5 showed strong signals in joint linkage and association analysis (unadjusted P less than 10(-7)).

Blank et al. (2005) found that homozygosity for a G-to-C polymorphism at position -343 of the FCGR2B promoter (rs3219018) was associated with SLE in European Americans (OR = 11.1, P = 0.003).

Floto et al. (2005) showed that the SLE-associated FCGR2B T232 receptor was functionally impaired: it was excluded from sphingolipid rafts and could not inhibit activating receptors.

In a meta-analysis of 21 studies, Lee et al. (2006) found that the A/A genotype of the TNF-alpha promoter -308 A/G polymorphism was associated with SLE (overall OR, 3.2; P less than 0.001).

Aitman et al. (2006) found that low copy number of FCGR3B, an ortholog of rat Fcgr3, was associated with glomerulonephritis in patients with SLE.

Graham et al. (2006) replicated association of the IRF5 rs2004640 T allele with SLE in 4 independent case-control cohorts (P = 4.4 x 10(-16)) and by transmission disequilibrium testing (P = 0.0006). Haplotypes that carried a variant associated with elevated expression but lacked the exon 1B donor site did not confer risk. The authors concluded that a common IRF5 haplotype driving elevated expression of multiple IRF5 isoforms is an important risk factor for SLE.

In 1,416 individuals from 258 Caucasian and 142 Chinese lupus simplex families, Wu et al. (2007) found that a common CR2 haplotype of 3 SNPs (rs3813946, rs1048971, rs17615) was associated with lupus (P = 0.00001), conferring a 1.54-fold increase in risk.

Yang et al. (2007) studied C4 copy number in 1,241 European Americans. Patients with SLE had fewer copies of total C4 and of C4A than controls. Having only 2 copies of total C4 increased the risk of SLE (9.3% of patients vs 1.5% of controls; OR = 6.514), whereas 5 or more copies were protective (OR = 0.466). Zero copies (OR = 5.267) or 1 copy (OR = 1.613) of C4A was a risk factor, and 3 or more copies appeared protective. Family-based tests suggested that a haplotype with a single short C4B gene, in tight linkage disequilibrium with the TNFA -308A allele, was preferentially transmitted to patients.

Fanciulli et al. (2007) found a strong association between low FCGR3B copy number and SLE in 536 UK cases compared with UK 1958 birth cohort controls (P = 2.7 x 10(-8)). Individuals with fewer than 2 copies had an increased risk of SLE with glomerulonephritis (161 patients; OR = 2.43) and of SLE without known renal involvement (375 patients; OR = 2.21). Among patients with SLE, low copy number did not significantly increase the risk of glomerulonephritis. Zero copies of FCGR3B were found in 25 of 1,279 individuals with autoimmunity (2%), including 21 with SLE, compared with 1 of 862 controls (0.1%). The authors suggested that complete FCGR3B deficiency confers a very high risk of autoimmunity, particularly SLE.

Lee-Kirsch et al. (2007) identified monoallelic frameshift or missense mutations, and 1 3-prime UTR variant, in the TREX1 gene in 9 of 417 individuals with SLE. None were found in 1,712 controls (P = 4.1 x 10(-7)). Two mutant alleles altered the subcellular targeting of the protein.

Remmers et al. (2007) found that a STAT4 intron 3 haplotype marked by rs7574865 was strongly associated with SLE in 1,039 patients and 1,248 controls from 3 case series. The haplotype was present on 31% of patient chromosomes and 22% of control chromosomes (P = 1.87 x 10(-9); OR 1.55). Homozygosity for the risk allele more than doubled the risk of lupus. Because the same haplotype also increased the risk of rheumatoid arthritis, the authors suggested a shared pathway for the 2 diseases.

In 178 Japanese patients with SLE and 899 controls, Kamatani et al. (2008) found an association with rs3130342 in the 5-prime flanking region of TNXB (P = 9.3 x 10(-7); OR 3.11), which they replicated in 203 cases and 294 controls (P = 0.044). The association of C4 copy number with SLE was weaker and probably reflected linkage disequilibrium with this SNP. The rs3130342 association was independent of HLA-DRB1*1501.

Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008) genotyped SNPs across TNFSF4 in 416 European Caucasian UK and 263 Minnesota SLE parent-affected trios. They found a single overtransmitted upstream risk haplotype (UK Pu = 0.0104; Minnesota Pu = 3.00 x 10(-4); joint Pu = 1.86 x 10(-5); T/U 1.61) and an undertransmitted protective haplotype tagged by the rs844644 A allele. They replicated the association in 424 unrelated UK cases and 642 controls (rs2205960 OR 1.28; rs844644 OR 0.710). By conditional analysis, no single genotyped variant explained the association, with the possible exception of rs844644. SNPs and haplotypes in the receptor gene TNFRSF4 were not associated with SLE.

Hom et al. (2008) found that the SLE-associated promoter-region allele at the C8orf13-BLK locus was associated with reduced BLK and increased C8orf13 mRNA in B-cell lines.

International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) found 3 SNPs in or near ITGAM associated with SLE. The strongest was rs9888739 (P = 1.61 x 10(-23); OR = 1.62), and the association strengthened when combined with an independent study. They also found associations with KIAA1542 rs4963128 (OR = 0.78), which is in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs709266 in IRF7; with PXK rs6445975 (OR = 1.25); and with rs10798269 on 1q25.1 (OR = 0.82). The strongest association was in the HLA region, where the top SNPs reached P < 10(-50) and probably reflected the extended HLA-A1-B8-DR3 haplotype. They confirmed association at IRF5/TNPO3 and replicated associations at PTPN22, FCGR2A, and STAT4. Possible associations were also found at XKR6, LYN, ATG5, ICA1, BLK, and SCUBE1.

Kozyrev et al. (2008) found that the BANK1 branch point-site SNP rs17266594, in strong linkage disequilibrium with rs10516487, affected the expression of a full-length transcript and an exon 2-skipped transcript. A third associated BANK1 variant, rs3733197 (A383T), lies in the ankyrin domain.

Nath et al. (2008) identified an association between ITGAM and SLE in 3,818 individuals of European descent, which was strongest at the nonsynonymous SNP rs1143679 (P = 1.7 x 10(-17); OR = 1.78).

Oishi et al. (2008) found that rs3748079, in the promoter of ITPR3 on 6p21, was associated with SLE in 2 Japanese case-control samples (P = 1.78 x 10(-8); OR 1.88). It was also associated with rheumatoid arthritis and Graves disease. A SNP in NKX2.5 was also associated with SLE (OR 1.74), and individuals with risk genotypes at both loci had an OR of 5.77.

Willcocks et al. (2008) confirmed the association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE by comparing 171 UK Caucasian patients with 176 ethnically matched controls (P = 0.027). They found no association in 159 Hong Kong Chinese cases and 150 controls. In a female proband with SLE whose neutrophils had no FCGR3 mRNA, they found homozygosity for an FCGR3B-null allele (see Allelic Variants).

Graham et al. (2008) found that rs5029939 in TNFAIP3 was associated with SLE in their genomewide scan (P = 2.55 x 10(-8)). They replicated the association in 740 independent trios, giving a combined P of 2.89 x 10(-12) and an OR of 2.29. Variants in STAT4, BLK, and ITGAM also replicated. The rheumatoid arthritis risk variant rs6920220, about 185 kb upstream of TNFAIP3, was also associated with SLE. Haplotype and conditional analyses suggested 2 independent risk signals: one marked by rs6920220 and one by a haplotype carrying the minor alleles of rs10499197 and rs7749323. The association of the missense SNP rs2230926 was fully explained by these 2 SNPs.

Using genomewide data from 1,239 SLE cases and 1,629 controls of European ancestry, Musone et al. (2008) identified 3 independent SNPs in the TNFAIP3 region associated with SLE. The minor alleles of rs13192841 (OR 0.72) and rs6922466 (OR 0.76) were protective, whereas the minor allele of the coding SNP rs2230926 increased risk (OR 1.88). The 3 SNPs lay in different linkage disequilibrium blocks and remained independent in conditional analysis.

Orrú et al. (2009) found that the Q263 allele of the PTPN22 R263Q missense substitution, which reduces phosphatase activity, protected against SLE.

Sánchez et al. (2009) genotyped 9 IL18 SNPs in Spanish SLE cases and controls. They found an association with the rs360719 C allele, which was replicated in Italian and Argentinian cohorts (pooled OR = 1.37; combined P = 3.8E-07).

Gateva et al. (2009) identified a nonsynonymous UHRF1BP1 allele (R454Q; rs11755393, P = 2.22 x 10(-8)) as an SLE risk variant. It lies in a region of extended linkage disequilibrium that includes SNRPC.

Myouzen et al. (2010) found peak association with SLE at rs10761670 in the 5-prime flanking region of EGR2 (pooled OR = 1.23; P = 0.00023). The same SNP was associated with rheumatoid arthritis.

Willcocks et al. (2010) found that a SNP in FCGR2B that abrogates FcgammaRIIb function was strongly associated with SLE susceptibility in both Caucasians and Southeast Asians.

Niederer et al. (2010) found that low FCGR3B copy number (0 or 1 copies) was associated with SLE in combined UK and Swedish Caucasian cohorts (OR 1.57; P = 0.018) and in Hong Kong Chinese (OR 1.65; P = 4 x 10(-4)); in the Chinese cohort, the association was strongest in patients with lupus nephritis. Homozygosity for FCGR2B 232T was associated with SLE in 2 new Caucasian cohorts, and a meta-analysis of all Caucasian studies gave an OR of 2.26. FCGR3B copy number and FCGR2B I232T were in weak to moderate linkage disequilibrium but had independent effects on risk. FCGR3A copy number and FCGR3A V176F were not significantly associated with SLE.

Boteva et al. (2012) genotyped 1,028 SLE cases and 1,179 controls of northern and southern European ancestry for total C4, C4A, and C4B gene copy number and the loss-of-function C4 exon 29 CT indel. After accounting for HLA-DRB1 and MHC SNPs, genetically determined partial C4 deficiency was not an independent risk factor for SLE, and the C4A exon 29 CT insertion was not associated with SLE. The authors concluded that complete homozygous C4 deficiency is a strong genetic risk factor for SLE but that partial C4 deficiency does not independently predispose to it.

Manjarrez-Orduño et al. (2012) sequenced CSK in 24 European patients homozygous for CSK haplotype 'B' and genotyped 3,769 SLE cases and 3,404 controls of European ancestry. The A allele of the intronic SNP rs34933034 was associated with SLE (OR 1.32; P = 1.04 x 10(-9)). This allele occurred almost only on the B haplotype and explained the associations of B-haplotype tagging SNPs. They found no interaction with PTPN22 R620W (rs2476601).

Mueller et al. (2013) identified 2 highly paralogous 24.5-kb blocks in the FCGR2C/FCGR3B/FCGR2B locus. These blocks bound the regions in which nonallelic homologous recombination causes FCGR2C/FCGR3B copy number variation.

Zhao et al. (2017) resequenced NCF1 in 45 Chinese subjects and identified a missense variant, arg90-to-his (R90H; rs201802880), as the likely causal SLE variant at 7q11.23. It was associated with SLE in Asians (meta P = 3.1 x 10(-104), OR = 3.47), European Americans (OR = 2.61), and African Americans (OR = 2.02), and conditioning on it removed the GTF2IRD1-GTF2I signal. Having 1 copy of NCF1 increased risk, and having 3 or more copies was protective. R90H was also associated with primary Sjogren syndrome and with seropositive rheumatoid arthritis.

Farias et al. (2019) used targeted resequencing of 215 candidate genes in Swedish patients with SLE and found that a rare variant in intron 4 of MEF2D, rs200395694:G-T, was associated with SLE (p = 0.014). Twelve of 504 patients and 6 of 839 controls were heterozygous. The 3 carriers in the discovery cohort were 2 females and 1 male. All 3 had anti-Sm and anti-RNP antibodies, Raynaud phenomenon, arthritis, hematologic disorder, anti-dsDNA, and antinuclear antibodies. The authors considered MEF2D a peripheral gene for SLE with a modest association but an apparent functional effect.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) imputed C4A and C4B copy number into 6,748 European-ancestry SLE cases and 11,516 controls. SLE risk varied 7-fold across the 11 most common C4A/C4B combinations. Each copy of C4A was protective (OR 0.54), equivalent to 2.3 copies of C4B (OR 0.77). In an African American cohort (1,494 cases, 5,908 controls), joint analysis implicated C4A and C4B but not DRB1*03:01, which indicated that the MHC association with SLE long attributed to DRB1*03:01 arises from C4. A more modest, C4-independent MHC effect was tagged by rs2105898 and rs9271513.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Reveille et al. (1983) found DR2 and/or DR3 in 5 of 6 patients with familial SLE (83%) who had anti-Ro(SSA) antibodies, but the difference was not significant.

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found that photosensitivity was more common in C4A-deficient patients with SLE than in other patients (p less than 0.05), which resembled findings in complete classical pathway deficiencies. Other clinical features and autoantibody profiles did not differ.

Baechler et al. (2003) found that the interferon gene expression signature marked more severe SLE involving the kidneys, hematopoietic cells, and/or the central nervous system.

In the UK cohort studied by Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008), 45% of probands had features of antiphospholipid syndrome, 46% had features of Sjogren syndrome, and 23% had renal disease. TNFSF4 susceptibility variants did not bias the phenotype toward any of these.

Zhao et al. (2017) found that the NCF1 R90H risk allele was associated with earlier age at SLE onset in a dose-dependent manner in Korean and European American patients.

Farias et al. (2019) found that the MEF2D rs200395694 risk allele was associated with the triad of Raynaud phenomenon, anti-RNP antibodies, and anti-Sm antibodies (p = 0.00037). All 3 carriers among 140 Swedish patients had all 3 features, compared with 11 patients (8.5%) of the whole cohort.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) found that C4 alleles had stronger effects on SLE risk in men than in women (sex-by-C4 interaction p = 0.002). Common C4A/C4B combinations gave 14-fold variation in risk among men and 6-fold variation among women. The C4 alleles that protect against SLE were the same ones that increase schizophrenia risk.

### Pathogenesis

DeHoratius et al. (1975) found that, in SLE probands, lymphocytotoxic antibodies correlated with anti-poly A and anti-native DNA antibodies, whereas in household contacts they correlated with anti-poly A-poly U. The authors concluded that both an environmental agent, possibly a virus, and the genetic response are important in pathogenesis.

Yocum et al. (1975) noted that the unaffected twin of their discordant monozygotic pair had undergone castration at an early age, which suggested that ovarian hormones may play an important role in the development of SLE.

Arnett and Shulman (1976) found that identical twins developed SLE an average of 2 years apart. In sibs, ages at onset differed by an average of 9 years, but calendar dates of onset differed by only 3 years; the corresponding figures for parents and offspring were 20 and 8 years. They suggested that environmental influences help start the disease, especially in sibs.

Miller and Schwartz (1979) concluded that impaired suppressor-cell function cannot by itself explain SLE but that certain suppressor-cell abnormalities are genetic markers. They proposed that SLE develops only when at least 2 functionally distinct classes of genes are involved.

Solomon et al. (1983) raised mouse monoclonal anti-idiotypic antibodies against anti-native DNA antibodies purified from a patient with active SLE. One of these, 3I, recognized an idiotype outside the antigen-binding site and reacted with 8 of 9 sera with anti-native DNA activity. This suggested that anti-native DNA antibodies from unrelated patients share a common idiotype.

Steinberg et al. (1984) proposed that some predisposing genes produce a generalized immune abnormality and others lead to specific autoantibodies. In their model, environmental triggers such as infections increase autoantibody production by activating macrophages and T cells, which in turn stimulate B cells.

Diamond and Scharff (1984) found that U4, a somatic mutant of the S107 antiphosphocholine myeloma line, had a single glu-to-ala substitution at heavy chain residue 35. The change caused loss of phosphocholine binding and gain of reactivity with double-stranded DNA, protamine, and cardiolipin. They concluded that a single amino acid change can turn an antibacterial antibody into one resembling the autoantibodies of autoimmune disease.

Halpern et al. (1985) found that cationic 3I-reactive immunoglobulin bands correlated with the presence of anti-DNA antibodies, which suggested that immunoglobulin charge may help determine specificity for DNA.

Mackie et al. (1987) found prolonged, noncorrectable KPTTs in 4 healthy spouses of affected females and in 3 asymptomatic sibs. They concluded that these findings supported a role for transmissible agents or other environmental factors in lupus-like disorders.

In the family studied by Levcovitz et al. (1988), every unaffected adult relative with excess LMW-DNA had positive antinuclear antibodies except the grandmother, and none of the 5 children with excess LMW-DNA was ANA-positive. The authors suggested that excess LMW-DNA precedes ANAs and may predispose to the immunologic responses of SLE.

Sakane et al. (1989) found increased numbers of spontaneously immunoglobulin-secreting B cells in 22 of 29 blood relatives of SLE probands and in 4 of 5 unrelated household members. They concluded that environmental effects may also contribute to the familial occurrence of these B-cell abnormalities.

Wu et al. (1996) found that peripheral blood mononuclear cells from their patient with a FASL in-frame deletion showed decreased FasL activity, decreased activation-induced cell death, and increased T-cell proliferation after activation. This was the first report of defective FasL-mediated apoptosis linked to a human FASL mutation in SLE.

Mohan et al. (1999) proposed a 2-step epistatic model for nephrophilic autoantibodies in lupus. In this model, loci such as Sle1 break tolerance to chromatin, and full pathogenic maturation requires other loci, such as Sle3, as well as gender-based factors.

Lessard et al. (1999) found urinary nitroprocainamide in 7 of 13 CYP2D6 extensive metabolizers given procainamide but in none of 6 poor metabolizers, and in no subject taking the CYP2D6 inhibitor quinidine. They suggested that CYP2D6 is involved in the N-oxidation of procainamide, a pathway that produces a metabolite that may be involved in procainamide-induced lupus.

Linker-Israeli et al. (1999) found that B lymphoblastoid cells carrying SLE-associated IL6 3-prime minisatellite alleles secreted 3- to 4-fold more IL-6. Carriers had about 4-fold higher percentages of IL-6-positive monocytes, and IL-6 mRNA stability was increased in carrier patients.

Zhang et al. (2001) found significantly raised BLyS levels in the serum of patients with SLE. The BLyS in patient sera stimulated B-cell activation in vitro.

Solomou et al. (2001) found that unstimulated SLE T cells contained increased phosphorylated CREM, which bound the -180 site of the IL-2 promoter. After stimulation, SLE T cells showed less binding of phosphorylated CREB to this site than normal T cells. Increased p-CREM correlated with decreased IL-2 production, and a reporter driven by the -180 site was suppressed in SLE T cells. The authors concluded that transcriptional repression causes the decreased IL-2 production by SLE T cells.

DeGiorgio et al. (2001) found that a pentapeptide molecular mimic of dsDNA is present in the NMDA receptor subunits NR2a and NR2b. Anti-DNA antibodies that cross-reacted with NR2 caused neuronal apoptosis in vivo and in vitro. The cerebrospinal fluid of a patient with SLE contained such antibodies and caused neuronal apoptosis. The authors suggested that these antibodies may cause CNS abnormalities not due to thrombosis or vasculitis.

Blanco et al. (2001) found that SLE serum induced normal monocytes to differentiate into dendritic cells that could present antigens from dying cells to CD4-positive T cells. This activity correlated with disease activity and depended on interferon-alpha. The authors proposed that unchecked induction of dendritic cells by IFN-alpha may drive autoimmunity in SLE.

Leadbetter et al. (2002) showed that IgG2a-chromatin immune complexes activated rheumatoid factor-positive B cells through synergistic engagement of the antigen receptor and a MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor, implicating TLR9.

Prokunina et al. (2002) found that the SLE-associated PDCD1 allele altered a RUNX1 binding site in an intronic enhancer.

By gene expression profiling of peripheral blood mononuclear cells, Baechler et al. (2003) found that about half of patients with SLE had dysregulated expression of interferon pathway genes.

Russell et al. (2004) showed that CRP 2 (rs1800947) and CRP 4 independently influenced basal serum CRP levels in patients with SLE and their sibs, with a gene-dose effect for CRP 4. The allele associated with reduced basal CRP was the one associated with SLE. This supported the hypothesis that defective disposal of immunogenic nuclear material contributes to lupus.

Xu et al. (2004) found that activated T cells from patients with SLE resisted anergy and apoptosis by upregulating and sustaining COX-2 expression. COX-2 inhibition caused apoptosis of these cells by augmenting Fas signaling and decreasing c-FLIP.

Sigurdsson et al. (2005) concluded that their TYK2 and IRF5 findings supported an SLE mechanism involving key components of the type I interferon system.

Tao et al. (2005) found that NK T cells from patients with active SLE were highly sensitive to anti-CD95-induced apoptosis and were deficient in CD226 and survivin. CD226 preactivation upregulated survivin and rescued these cells, and the authors concluded that deficient CD226 and survivin expression underlies the reduced NK T cell number in SLE.

Blank et al. (2005) found that the FCGR2B -343C allele lowered promoter transcription and reduced AP1 binding. Activated B cells from -343 C/C patients had significantly less surface FcgammaRIIb.

Graham et al. (2006) found that the IRF5 rs2004640 T allele creates a donor splice site in an alternate exon 1 (exon 1B), allowing expression of several unique isoforms. They also identified a linked cis-acting variant associated with elevated IRF5 expression.

Kowal et al. (2006) found that lupus serum reactive with DNA and the NMDA receptor caused hippocampal neuron damage and memory impairment in mice after disruption of the blood-brain barrier. IgG eluted from the brain of a patient with SLE bound DNA and the NMDA receptor and caused neuronal apoptosis in mouse brain. In the brains of 4 other patients with neuropsychiatric lupus, IgG colocalized with anti-NMDA receptor antibodies.

Wu et al. (2007) found that the CR2 5-prime UTR SNP rs3813946 altered transcriptional activity.

Fanciulli et al. (2007) hypothesized that reduced FCGR3B-mediated clearance of immune complexes by neutrophils predisposes to disease in the glomeruli and at other sites. Willcocks et al. (2008) found that FCGR3B copy number correlated with FcgammaRIIIb surface expression, soluble serum FcgammaRIIIb, and neutrophil adherence to and uptake of immune complexes. Niederer et al. (2010) suggested that reduced FcgammaRIIIb expression could lead to increased immune complex deposition. Mueller et al. (2013) found ectopic FcgammaRIIb on natural killer cells in individuals with FCGR3B-deleted alleles. They concluded that the deletion places FCGR2C 5-prime regulatory sequences next to FCGR2B coding sequence, creating a chimeric gene.

Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008) showed that activated lymphoblastoid cell lines homozygous for the TNFSF4 risk haplotype had a 6.7-fold increase in TNFSF4 transcript compared with lines homozygous for the protective haplotype, with similar results in lymphocytes from 8 SLE probands. The T allele of rs1234317, which uniquely tags the risk haplotype, is predicted to destroy a binding site for the repressor E4BP4.

Balada et al. (2008) found that CD4+ T cells from 29 patients with SLE had lower DNA deoxymethylcytosine content than cells from 30 controls (0.802 vs 0.901, P = 0.007). Transcript levels of DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B did not differ, but at least one of these rose when low complement occurred together with lymphopenia, high anti-dsDNA titers, or high disease activity.

Kozyrev et al. (2008) suggested that BANK1 variants could contribute to sustained B cell-receptor signaling and B-cell hyperactivity. Nath et al. (2008) concluded that the ITGAM association implicates the alpha(M)beta2-integrin adhesion pathway. Oishi et al. (2008) found that NKX2.5 bound specifically to the nonsusceptible T allele at the ITPR3 rs3748079 site.

Musone et al. (2008) found that the A20 protein encoded by the TNFAIP3 rs2230926 risk allele (cys127) was modestly but consistently less effective at inhibiting TNF-induced NF-kappa-B activity, suggesting a hypomorphic A20.

Sthoeger et al. (2009) suggested that alpha-defensin secretion by activated neutrophils may activate the adaptive immune system in SLE.

Sánchez et al. (2009) found higher IL18 mRNA expression in carriers of the rs360719 C risk allele, which creates an OCT-1 binding site. Myouzen et al. (2010) found that rs10761670 genotype correlated with EGR2 expression and that 2 SNPs in complete linkage disequilibrium with it altered transcription factor binding.

Manjarrez-Orduño et al. (2012) found that the CSK rs34933034 A allele was associated with higher CSK transcript levels, increased inhibitory Lyn Tyr508 phosphorylation, enhanced calcium mobilization after BCR cross-linking, and higher plasma IgM. Newborns homozygous for the risk allele had twice the frequency of transitional B cells.

Kshirsagar et al. (2014) found enhanced Stat3 activity and higher frequencies of IL-17-producing cells among effector T cells from children with lupus nephritis. These Th17 cells had high Akt activity and enhanced migratory capacity, which Akt inhibition decreased, and rapamycin reduced Stat3 activation.

Zhao et al. (2017) suggested that NCF1 R90H might confer SLE risk by lowering NOX2-derived reactive oxygen species. However, the variant was not associated with intracellular ROS levels in neutrophils from controls.

Bialas et al. (2017) observed increased type I interferon signaling in postmortem hippocampal sections from patients with SLE. They suggested that interferon-driven, microglia-dependent synapse loss may explain the neurologic symptoms of CNS lupus.

Farias et al. (2019) found that the region containing MEF2D rs200395694 behaved as a cell-specific enhancer and that its 2 alleles bound different proteins. In minigene assays, the risk allele inhibited splicing of the alpha-2 isoform of MEF2D, and the authors speculated that this might reduce expression of alpha-2 targets such as DNASE1L3.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) found that cerebrospinal fluid from adult men contained on average 27% more C4 protein per C4 gene copy and 42% more C3 than that from women. They suggested that higher complement levels in men may explain both the stronger C4 effects in men and the greater SLE risk in women. They proposed that complement limits exposure of the adaptive immune system to ribonuclear self-antigens by speeding the clearance of cell debris.

### Diagnosis

Tan et al. (1982) revised the 1971 preliminary criteria for the classification of SLE. They added fluorescent antinuclear antibody and antibodies to native DNA and Sm antigen, aggregated some criteria involving the same organ systems, and dropped Raynaud phenomenon and alopecia. The revised criteria were 96% sensitive and 96% specific when tested with data from 18 clinics and performed better than the 1971 criteria.

Zhao et al. (2017) cautioned that short-read sequencing studies had not called NCF1 variants correctly because of 98% sequence identity with the pseudogenes NCF1B and NCF1C. They recommended NCF1-specific PCR and measurement of the deltaGT/GTGT ratio.

### Clinical Management

Batchelor et al. (1980) suggested guidelines for hydralazine therapy based on HLA-DR type and sex.

Gross et al. (2000) found that a soluble TACI-Ig fusion protein inhibited proteinuria and prolonged survival in NZBWF1 mice, and they suggested it as a promising treatment for human autoimmune disease. Zhang et al. (2001) proposed BLyS as a therapeutic target in systemic autoimmunity.

Xu et al. (2004) found that only some COX-2 inhibitors suppressed pathogenic anti-DNA autoantibody production by causing autoimmune T-cell apoptosis, an effect independent of PGE2.

McGaha et al. (2005) suggested that raising FcgammaRIIB levels on B cells may be an effective treatment for autoimmune diseases.

### Population Genetics

Siegel et al. (1970) compared epidemiologic data on SLE for New York City and Jefferson County, Alabama, from 1956 to 1965. In a community-based study, Fessel (1974) examined the incidence, prevalence, outcome, and first symptoms of SLE and found a high prevalence in black women.

Serdula and Rhoads (1979) found 168 cases of SLE, 107 of them definite, in civilian general hospitals on Oahu, Hawaii, for 1970 to 1975. Age-adjusted prevalence rates per 100,000 for definite cases were 5.8 in whites, 24.1 in Chinese, 19.9 in Filipinos, 20.4 in part-Hawaiians, and 18.2 in Japanese. Females made up an average of 90% of definite cases. Age-adjusted mortality rates per million were 3.04 for US whites, 8.82 for US nonwhites, 1.89 for Hawaii whites, and 14.46 for Hawaii nonwhites.

Batchelor et al. (1980) studied 26 patients with hydralazine-induced SLE, 25 of whom were slow acetylators; the female-to-male ratio was 4:1. HLA-DR4 was present in 73% of patients, compared with 33% of 113 healthy controls, 25% of 16 slow-acetylator hypertensive patients who took hydralazine for more than a year without developing SLE, and 25% of 20 patients with idiopathic SLE. Among slow acetylators, every woman with DR4 developed the disease. The only men who developed it carried DR2 and were taking 200 mg of hydralazine per day.

Reidenberg et al. (1980) determined acetylator phenotype in 21 New York City and 29 Israeli patients with idiopathic SLE: 30 were slow acetylators, 17 rapid, and 3 indeterminate. Combining these with published studies, they found 150 slow and 77 rapid acetylators worldwide, compared with an expected 122:105 (P < 0.001).

In contrast, Baer et al. (1986) found that the slow acetylator phenotype was about as frequent in 64 patients with idiopathic SLE (38%) as in 60 healthy volunteers (50%) and 52 non-SLE medical patients (44%), and they found no association with any SLE subgroup.

Molokhia et al. (2003) studied SLE cases and controls in Trinidad who reported no Indian or Chinese ancestry (52 cases and 107 controls). Mean west African admixture was 0.81 in cases and 0.74 in controls (P = 0.01). The risk ratio per unit change in admixture was 32.5, and it changed little after adjustment for socioeconomic status.

Russell et al. (2004) reported that the CRP 4 A allele had a frequency of about 0.3 in European and Asian-Indian populations and 0.14 in Afro-Caribbean families.

Lee et al. (2005) found that the CTLA4 +49 GG genotype and G allele were associated with SLE in Asians but not significantly in European-derived populations. Lee et al. (2006) found that the TNF-alpha -308 A/A genotype was associated with SLE in European-derived but not Asian-derived populations.

Yang et al. (2007) found that, among European Americans, gene copy number ranged from 2 to 6 for total C4, from 0 to 5 for C4A, and from 0 to 4 for C4B.

Nath et al. (2008) replicated the ITGAM rs1143679 association in 2 samples of African descent (overall meta-analysis P = 6.9 x 10(-22)).

Willcocks et al. (2010) found that the minor allele of the defunctioning FCGR2B SNP was more common in Southeast Asians and Africans than in Caucasians. Homozygosity for this allele protected against severe malaria in an East African population (OR = 0.56), which the authors suggested may contribute to the higher frequency of SLE in Africans and Southeast Asians. Niederer et al. (2010) found that low FCGR3B copy number was least common in Caucasians (7.4%) and most common in Kenyans, and that it was not associated with severe malaria or bacterial sepsis.

### Animal Model

Knight and Adams (1978) studied early severe lupus nephritis in (NZB x NZW) x NZW and (NZB x NZW) x NZB backcross mice. Their data indicated that NZB contributes 1 gene and NZW 2 genes or gene clusters to the renal disease of the F1 hybrid, and that 1 of the NZW genes is linked to H-2. All 3 genes must be dominant or codominant, because their effect was expressed in the heterozygous state.

Lahita et al. (1983) noted that the disease in their male patients with familial SLE was somewhat similar to that in BXSB mice. Steinberg et al. (1984) and Theofilopoulos and Dixon (1985) reviewed murine models of SLE. Steinberg et al. (1984) noted that the xid gene could eliminate most autoantibody-producing B cells in lupus mice and that an arachidonic acid analog was an effective treatment.

In an NZM/Aeg2410 x C57BL/6 backcross, Morel et al. (1994) identified 3 intervals on mouse chromosomes 1, 4, and 7 containing strong recessive glomerulonephritis susceptibility alleles. Heterozygosity at H-2 also correlated strongly with susceptibility, which was inherited as a threshold genetic liability.

In (NZB x NZW)F1 x NZW backcross mice, Vyse et al. (1996) found that one set of loci regulated IgG antibodies to dsDNA, ssDNA, histones, and chromatin, and that these loci overlapped with loci for anti-gp70 autoantibodies. The anti-gp70 loci showed stronger linkage with renal disease. A distal chromosome 4 locus, Nba1, was linked with nephritis but not with any measured autoantibody.

Mohan et al. (1999) found that B6 mice bicongenic for the NZM2410-derived loci Sle1 and Sle3 had splenomegaly, expanded activated B and CD4+ T cells, broad IgG autoantibody responses, and highly penetrant glomerulonephritis, particularly in females. Mice monocongenic for Sle1 lost tolerance to chromatin without developing disease, and Sle3-monocongenic mice had only mildly penetrant glomerulonephritis.

Gross et al. (2000) found that transgenic mice overexpressing zTNF4 (BLyS/BAFF) in lymphoid cells developed symptoms characteristic of SLE. Circulating zTNF4 was more abundant in NZBWF1 and MRL-lpr/lpr mice during the onset and progression of disease.

Napirei et al. (2000) found that Dnase1-deficient mice developed antinuclear antibodies, glomerular immune complex deposition, and full-blown glomerulonephritis in a Dnase1 dose-dependent manner. They suggested that lack or reduction of DNASE1 is a critical factor in the initiation of human SLE.

Sun et al. (2002) found that an agonistic anti-CD137 antibody blocked lymphadenopathy and autoimmune disease and prolonged survival in Fas-deficient MRL/lpr mice.

Nakamura et al. (2003) linked a region including IFN-alpha on NZB chromosome 4 to anxiety-like behavior in (NZB x NZW)F1 mice. These mice had elevated neuronal IFN-alpha and corticotropin-releasing hormone, and blockade of the mu-1 opioid receptor or the CRH receptor-1 partially overcame the behavior.

Li et al. (2004) identified Ltk as a candidate gene for aberrant activation of self-reactive CD5+ B1 cells in NZB mice. NZB mice carry a gain-of-function polymorphism in the LTK kinase domain near a PI3K p85-binding motif.

Xu et al. (2004) confirmed with COX-2 inhibitors and Cox-2-deficient mice that the COX-2/FLIP antiapoptosis program was used selectively by anergy-resistant lupus T cells.

Tournoy et al. (2004) found that PS1+/- PS2-/- mice developed, after about 6 months of age, an autoimmune disease resembling SLE. The disease included dermatitis, glomerulonephritis, keratitis, vasculitis, hypergammaglobulinemia with immune complex deposits, and high-titer nuclear autoantibodies, and it was accompanied by reduced gamma-secretase activity. PS1+/- PS2+/- mice remained healthy.

McGaha et al. (2005) showed that partially restoring FcgRIIB levels on B cells in lupus-prone mouse strains was sufficient to restore tolerance and prevent autoimmunity.

Barber et al. (2005) found that inhibitors of PI3K-gamma reduced glomerulonephritis and prolonged lifespan in MRL-lpr mice.

Huerta et al. (2006) found that mice immunized with a DNA peptide mimetope produced antibodies cross-reacting with DNA and the NMDA receptor. When the blood-brain barrier was opened with epinephrine, the mice lost neurons in the lateral amygdala and had a deficient response to fear conditioning.

By positional cloning in Wistar Kyoto rats, Aitman et al. (2006) identified loss of the rat-specific Fcgr3 paralog Fcgr3-rs as a determinant of macrophage overactivity and glomerulonephritis.

Talaei et al. (2015) found a promoter polymorphism in the NZB gene encoding the SLAM adapter EAT-2, within a lupus-associated chromosome 1 interval, that reduced EAT-2 in dendritic cells by about 70%. Silencing EAT-2 increased IL-12 production and Th1 differentiation. The authors concluded that EAT-2 negatively regulates dendritic cell cytokine production downstream of SLAM and that this polymorphism promotes lupus.

Bialas et al. (2017) reported behavioral phenotypes and synapse loss in lupus-prone mice, both of which were prevented by blocking type I interferon signaling. Type I interferon stimulated microglia to engulf neuronal and synaptic material.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
C4A, NULL ALLELE

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found homozygous C4A deficiency in 13 of 80 (16%) patients with SLE, who represented all diagnosed cases from a defined population in southern Sweden. Photosensitivity was more common in the C4A-deficient patients than in other patients with SLE (p less than 0.05). Other clinical features and autoantibody profiles did not differ.

**.0002 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FASLG, 84-BP DEL, EX4

Wu et al. (1996) screened 75 patients with SLE by SSCP analysis of the FasL extracellular domain. In 1 patient, who had lymphadenopathy, they identified a heterozygous 84-bp deletion within exon 4 of the FASLG gene, predicted to cause an in-frame deletion of 28 amino acids. The patient's peripheral blood mononuclear cells showed decreased FasL activity, decreased activation-induced cell death, and increased T-cell proliferation after activation.

**.0003 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
DNASE1, EX2, NONSENSE

Yasutomo et al. (2001) described 2 patients with SLE who had a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2 of the DNASE1 gene. Both had decreased DNASE1 activity and an extremely high IgG titer against nucleosomal antigens.

Shin et al. (2004) did not find the DNASE1 exon 2 A/T mutation, previously reported in 2 Japanese patients with SLE, in any of 350 Korean patients with SLE or 330 Korean controls.

**.0004 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FCGR2B, ILE232THR [c.695T>C; p.I232T]

Kyogoku et al. (2002) identified a c.695T-C SNP in the FCGR2B gene that results in an ile232-to-thr (I232T) substitution in the transmembrane domain. In 193 Japanese patients with SLE and 303 healthy controls, the 232T/T genotype was significantly more frequent in patients.

Floto et al. (2005) showed that the T232 receptor was excluded from sphingolipid rafts and was therefore unable to inhibit activating receptors.

Niederer et al. (2010) found that homozygosity for 232T (rs1050501) was associated with SLE in 2 new Caucasian cohorts from the UK and Sweden. A meta-analysis of all Caucasian studies gave an OR of 2.26 (95% CI 1.49-3.41).

**.0005 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
CRP, CRP4 G-A (rs1205)

Russell et al. (2004) studied 586 UK simplex SLE families and found that the less common A allele of CRP 4 (rs1205), a SNP in the 3-prime flanking/untranslated region of the CRP gene, was overtransmitted to probands (TRANSMIT P = 0.003; P = 0.0008 in 394 trios). The allele was also overtransmitted to individuals with antinuclear antibodies. CRP 4 influenced basal serum CRP levels with a gene-dose effect, and the allele associated with reduced basal CRP was the one associated with SLE.

**.0006 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
PTPN22, ARG620TRP [p.R620W; p.W620]

Kyogoku et al. (2004) genotyped 525 North American white patients with SLE and 1,961 white controls and found that the PTPN22 R620W polymorphism was associated with SLE (genotypic P = 0.00009). The minor (T) allele frequency was 12.67% in cases and 8.64% in controls. One copy of the W620 allele gave an OR of 1.37, and 2 copies gave an OR of 4.37.

International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) replicated the association at PTPN22 rs2476601 (P = 5.2 x 10(-6)). Orrú et al. (2009) described R620W as a gain-of-function risk variant, in contrast to the protective loss-of-function R263Q variant.

**.0007 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FCGR2B, -343G-C

Blank et al. (2005) found that homozygosity for a G-to-C polymorphism at position -343 of the FCGR2B promoter (rs3219018) was associated with SLE in European Americans (OR = 11.1, P = 0.003). The -343C allele reduced promoter transcription and AP1 binding. Activated B cells from -343 C/C patients had significantly reduced surface FcgammaRIIb.

**.0008 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FCGR3B, FCGR3B-NULL DELETION

Willcocks et al. (2008) identified a woman with SLE whose neutrophils lacked FCGR3 mRNA, although levels in her monocytes were normal. Her neutrophils had no surface FcgammaRIIIb and showed reduced uptake of and adherence to immune complexes. She was homozygous for an FCGR3B-null allele, a deletion that included FCGR3B, HSPA7, and FCGR2C but spared FCGR3A and FCGR2B. The null allele was inherited in a mendelian manner: her son, daughter, and one brother each carried a single FCGR3B copy, and her husband and another brother had 2 copies.

**.0009 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
TNFSF4, UPSTREAM RISK HAPLOTYPE

Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008) found that a single upstream TNFSF4 haplotype (GCTAATCATTTG), tagged by rs10912580, rs12039904, rs2205960, and rs1234317, was overtransmitted to affected offspring in 416 UK European Caucasian SLE trios (Pu = 0.0104) and in 263 Minnesota trios (Pu = 3.00 x 10(-4)). The association was replicated in 424 unrelated UK cases and 642 controls (rs2205960 OR 1.28). Activated lymphoblastoid cell lines homozygous for the risk haplotype showed a 6.7-fold increase in TNFSF4 transcript.

**.0010 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
TNFAIP3, PHE127CYS [p.F127C]

Musone et al. (2008) found that the minor allele of the TNFAIP3 coding SNP rs2230926 (phe127-to-cys) increased the risk of SLE (OR 1.88, P = 0.0016) in 1,239 cases and 1,629 controls of European ancestry. The cys127 A20 protein was as stable as the phe127 protein but was modestly and consistently less effective at inhibiting TNF-induced NF-kappa-B activity.

Graham et al. (2008) found that a haplotype carrying the minor allele of rs2230926 was not associated with SLE and that the association of rs2230926 was fully explained by rs10499197 and rs7749323. They concluded that rs2230926 may not be the only putative causal variant in the region.
