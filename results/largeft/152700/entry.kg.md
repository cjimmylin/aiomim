### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that susceptibility to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is associated with variation in multiple genes, including FASLG (134638) on chromosome 1q24.3, DNASE1 (125505) on chromosome 16p13.3, FCGR2B (604590), FCGR2A (146790), FCGR3A (146740), and FCGR3B (610665) on chromosome 1q23.3, C4A (120810) on chromosome 6p21.33, PTPN22 (600716) on chromosome 1p13.2, IRF5 (607218) on chromosome 7q32.1, STAT4 (600558) on chromosome 2q32.2-q32.3, ITGAM (120980) on chromosome 16p11.2, BANK1 (610292) on chromosome 4q24, PXK (611450) on chromosome 3p14.3, PHRF1 (611780) on chromosome 11p15.5, TNFAIP3 (191163) on chromosome 6q23.3, TNFSF4 (603594) on chromosome 1q25.1, CR2 (120650) on chromosome 1q32.2, CRP (123260) on chromosome 1q23.2, PDCD1 (600244) on chromosome 2q37.3, CTLA4 (123890) on chromosome 2q33.2, IL6 (147620) on chromosome 7p15.3, IL18 (600953) on chromosome 11q23.1, TNF (191160) on chromosome 6p21.33, TNXB (600985) on chromosome 6p21.33-p21.32, ITPR3 (147267) on chromosome 6p21.31, EGR2 (129010) on chromosome 10q21.3, CSK (124095) on chromosome 15q24.1, NCF1 (608512) on chromosome 7q11.23, and MEF2D (600663) on chromosome 1q22.

### Description

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem, antibody-mediated autoimmune disorder. Steinberg et al. (1984) noted that it occurs spontaneously in humans and mice and that genetic factors play an important predisposing role: some genes may produce a generalized immune abnormality, whereas others may lead to specific autoantibodies, and environmental triggers increase autoantibody production and augment expression of illness. Prokunina et al. (2002) stated that SLE is a complex autoimmune disease that affects 0.05% of the Western population, mostly women.

Schur (1995) reviewed the genetics of SLE, and Kotzin (1996) reviewed SLE.

### Clinical Features

Tuffanelli (1971) described lupus erythematosus panniculitis (lupus erythematosus profundus), a form of lupus erythematosus involving the subcutaneous fat.

Reed et al. (1972) described a hereditary inflammatory vasculitis with persistent nodules, which they regarded as a new, genetically determined entity probably related to lupus erythematosus.

Schaller (1972) described an illness resembling lupus erythematosus in mothers of boys with chronic granulomatous disease.

Kohler et al. (1974) reported identical twin boys who had both hereditary angioedema and 'familial' lupus erythematosus.

Yocum et al. (1975) studied a pair of monozygotic twins discordant for SLE. Both twins had biologically false-positive serologic tests for syphilis at an early age, but only one developed SLE. The twins did not differ in their immune responses to tetanus toxoid, keyhole limpet hemocyanin, or DNCB, in delayed sensitivity, or in antibody titers to viruses. The clinically unaffected twin had been castrated at an early age, and the authors suggested that ovarian hormones may play an important role in the development of SLE.

In familial SLE, Arnett and Shulman (1976) found that the disease began in each member of an identical twin pair within an average of 2 years of each other. Between sibs, the average difference in age at onset was 9 years, but the average difference in calendar date of onset was only 3 years; for parents and offspring, the corresponding figures were 20 and 8 years. The authors suggested that environmental influences play a role in starting SLE, especially in sibs. They found no real differences in the frequencies of clinical and laboratory features between familial and nonfamilial SLE and concluded that familial SLE is probably not a different disease entity.

Brustein et al. (1977) reported a woman with discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) whose child developed DLE lesions at 2 months of age; the original lesions persisted and new lesions kept appearing up to the time of the report, when the child was 28 months old. In a second child, then 11 months old, a probable lupus erythematosus rash developed at 1 week of age.

Horn et al. (1978) described a brother and sister with mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) and studied 18 additional family members spanning 3 generations; 8 relatives had symptoms or signs of MCTD without complete expression of the disease. Antibodies to ribonucleoprotein and high-titer antinuclear antibodies were found only in the affected sibs, whereas rheumatoid factor was positive in 9 of 17 relatives, with titers ranging from 1:160 to 1:2560.

Weinberg et al. (1980) studied a large pedigree ascertained through an individual with elliptocytosis, multiple lipomatosis, and a biologic false-positive serologic test for syphilis (BFPSTS), and tested 180 relatives. Two female members of the pedigree who had BFPSTS developed SLE, in agreement with the previously described association; the analysis did not lead to any conclusion about a causal relationship between the 2 traits.

Reveille et al. (1983) studied 8 families containing 2 or more members with SLE (22 patients) and 40 relatives. Among the 40 non-SLE relatives, 7 (18%) had other immune diseases (thyroid disease in 2, rheumatoid arthritis in 2, ITP in 2, Henoch-Schonlein purpura in 1), compared with 4 of the 22 SLE patients (3 with thyroid disease and 1 with PSS). Eleven non-SLE relatives (28%) had ANA, high-titer in 5, and 11 had antibodies to ssDNA; only SLE patients had anti-dsDNA, Ro(SSA), Sm, or nRNP antibodies.

Lahita et al. (1983) described several families in which SLE occurred mainly in males, affecting mostly the sons and the fathers. In some families, female members had other autoimmune diseases, such as idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, or symptoms suggestive of a lupus diathesis. The authors noted that the disease in these patients was somewhat similar to the disease in BXSB mice.

Stohl et al. (1985) described 3 black Jamaican patients with SLE whose T helper/inducer cells lacked the T4 epitope. All 3 patients had lymphadenopathy. The asymptomatic, otherwise healthy T4 epitope-deficient brother of one patient also had lymphadenopathy in the same distribution as his sister with SLE.

Mackie et al. (1987) studied 3 families in which more than one member had SLE or lupus-like disease. Of the 19 people studied, 11 had SLE or lupus-like disease; in 8 of these 11, the kaolin partial thromboplastin time (KPTT) was prolonged and there was other evidence of lupus anticoagulant. Only 5 of the 11 had high anticardiolipin antibody titers.

Sibley et al. (1993) described a family in which 3 members developed SLE complicated by ischemic vasculopathy (digital ischemia). The calendar years and patient ages at disease onset and the clinical courses were remarkably similar in all 3 patients, although their genotypes were not.

Garcia et al. (1996) studied autoantibody profiles in 222 African American patients with SLE, 94 with and 128 without lupus nephritis. Anti-dsDNA was the only antibody that differed significantly between the 2 groups (P less than 0.05). Positive anti-Ro/SS-A, anti-Sm, and anti-nRNP without anti-La/SS-B was seen in 14 (14.9%) patients with nephritis and 15 (11.7%) without it (P greater than 0.6). The authors concluded that African American SLE patients with lupus nephritis do not have a specific or distinctive autoantibody profile, but that their data confirmed the value of anti-dsDNA in these patients.

Stein et al. (2002) analyzed 372 affected individuals from 160 multiplex SLE pedigrees, 25 of which contained at least 1 affected male relative. Renal disease was significantly more frequent in female family members with an affected male relative than in those without (68% vs 43%; P = 0.002), a trend that persisted after stratification by race and was most pronounced in European Americans.

Quintero-Del-Rio et al. (2002) noted that renal disease occurs in 40 to 75% of SLE patients and contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality.

In 38 SLE multiplex pedigrees containing a patient with thrombocytopenia, Scofield et al. (2003) found that thrombocytopenia was associated with nephritis, serositis, neuropsychiatric involvement, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, anti-double-stranded DNA, and antiphospholipid antibody, and that SLE was more severe in families with a thrombocytopenic SLE patient whether or not thrombocytopenia in an individual patient was considered. They concluded that thrombocytopenia is a component of a severe familial form of SLE.

Kamat et al. (2003) reported the first case of identical triplets who all developed SLE, diagnosed in reverse birth order at ages 8, 9, and 11 years. Although genetically identical, each showed different clinical signs and symptoms, but all 3 had skin rash, fatigue, and biopsy-proven glomerulonephritis at different ages. Laboratory findings were similar, with positive antinuclear, anti-native DNA, and anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies and low complement levels.

In a combined sample of 372 individuals from 159 families plus 392 individuals from 181 new families, Xing et al. (2005) replicated the observation that SLE females with affected male relatives have a higher prevalence of renal disease than SLE females without affected male relatives, supporting the hypothesis that multiplex SLE families with at least 1 affected male ('male families') form a distinct subpopulation.

Bialas et al. (2017) noted that up to 75% of patients with SLE have neuropsychiatric symptoms, collectively called CNS lupus, ranging from anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment to seizures and, rarely, psychosis. CNS lupus typically presents at diagnosis or within the first year.

### Other Features

Larsen and Godal (1972) studied thyroid diseases and thyroid antibodies in families of patients with SLE. Among 27 unaffected first-degree relatives in 8 families with familial SLE, Arnett and Shulman (1976) found 2 sisters with thyroid disease, persistent leukopenia, and a raised sedimentation rate; their mother had SLE and Hashimoto thyroiditis, and their sib had SLE. The other 25 relatives were normal clinically, hematologically, biochemically, and serologically.

Beaucher et al. (1977) reported familial lupus erythematosus together with antibodies to DNA in household dogs.

Reidenberg et al. (1980) used dapsone or isoniazid to determine acetylator phenotype in 50 patients with idiopathic SLE, 21 from New York City and 29 from Israel. Thirty were slow acetylators, 17 were rapid acetylators, and 3 were indeterminate. Combined with published studies, the worldwide distribution in idiopathic SLE was 150 slow to 77 rapid acetylators, compared with an expected 122:105 (P less than 0.001). In contrast, Baer et al. (1986) found that the slow acetylator phenotype was about as common in 64 patients with idiopathic SLE (38%) as in 60 healthy volunteers (50%) and 52 non-SLE medical service patients (44%), and was not more frequent in SLE subgroups defined by demographic features or specific disease manifestations; they concluded that their results, like most previously published results, gave no evidence for an association between acetylator phenotype and idiopathic SLE.

Elcioglu and Hall (1998) described 2 sibs with chondrodysplasia punctata whose mother had SLE, described their radiologic features, and compared them with other forms of chondrodysplasia punctata, raising the question of phenocopy versus coincidence. Toriello (1998) also reported chondrodysplasia punctata in association with maternal SLE.

Austin-Ward et al. (1998) reported a newborn whose mother had SLE diagnosed before pregnancy. The child had clinical manifestations of neonatal lupus as well as chondrodysplasia punctata and other findings resembling the congenital anomalies associated with oral anticoagulant use, although there was no history of exposure. The authors speculated that the combined action of the different maternal autoantibodies may produce the whole spectrum of manifestations.

Kelly et al. (1999) reported a male infant whose neonatal radiographs showed stippled epiphyses (chondrodysplasia punctata). He then developed a typical lupus erythematosus rash, and his skin findings led to a diagnosis of SLE in his mother. Over 3 years of follow-up, the child had strikingly short stature, midface hypoplasia, anomalous digital development, slow resolution of the stippled epiphyses, and near normal cognitive development.

Kozlowski et al. (2004) reported 2 brothers from Cape Town with chondrodysplasia punctata, born in 1991 and 2000, the younger more severely affected. Their mother had longstanding disseminated lupus erythematosus and epilepsy and was treated with chloroquine and other drugs during both pregnancies. The parents were not consanguineous and the family history was unremarkable. Noting that 7 earlier cases of chondrodysplasia punctata linked to maternal lupus erythematosus had been reported, the authors concluded that maternal lupus erythematosus must be regarded as another cause of chondrodysplasia punctata.

### Biochemical Features

Pollak (1964) studied antinuclear antibodies in family members of patients with SLE.

DeHoratius et al. (1975) found anti-RNA antibodies in 82% of 28 SLE probands, 16% of 124 of their family members, and 5% of 76 control family members. Among SLE family members, the antibodies were found only in the 94 close household contacts of the probands, 21% of whom had them. Anti-native DNA antibodies were present in 68% of probands and 6% of close household contacts, compared with 1% in control families; the difference between household contacts and controls was not significant.

Using an in vitro suppressor-cell assay in 15 patients with SLE, 50 of their clinically healthy family members, and 41 normal persons, Miller and Schwartz (1979) found impaired suppressor-cell function in 11 of the 15 patients, with no correlation between disease activity and test results.

Exner et al. (1980) found a lupus anticoagulant in the plasma of 2 pairs of sibs using the kaolin clotting time mixing test and presented strong evidence for this inhibitor in a third sib pair. Mackie et al. (1987) found that anticardiolipin levels were raised in subjects with lupus anticoagulant, although the length of the KPTT did not correlate closely with the anticardiolipin titer.

Stohl et al. (1985) found that peripheral blood mononuclear cells from T4 epitope-deficient family members showed impaired B-cell differentiation after pokeweed mitogen stimulation compared with cells from T4 epitope-intermediate relatives. Helper/suppressor T-cell ratios, autoantibodies, mitogen proliferation, and mixed lymphocyte reactions did not correlate with T4 epitope phenotype. The authors suggested that SLE with the T4 epitope-deficient phenotype may be a distinct subset of SLE.

In familial SLE kindreds, Halpern et al. (1985) found anti-DNA antibodies by Millipore filter analysis in 4 of 8 SLE patients and in 2 of 19 family members without SLE; under conditions that dissociate immune complexes, anti-DNA antibodies were also found in 2 more patients and 2 more family members. On isoelectric focusing, cationic 3I-reactive bands (pH 7-8.5) correlated with the presence of anti-DNA antibodies, which the authors took to indicate that immunoglobulin charge may help determine specificity for DNA.

Levcovitz et al. (1988) found that every SLE patient they studied had 2 classes of newly synthesized DNA in sucrose density gradients of PHA-stimulated lymphocytes: a large-molecular-weight fraction that comigrated with control DNA, and an excess low-molecular-weight DNA (LMW-DNA) fraction not seen in control lymphocytes. The excess LMW-DNA did not depend on disease activity or drug therapy. In a 4-generation family in which 2 first cousins had SLE, excess lymphocyte LMW-DNA was present in both cousins, in their parents (who were sibs), in a common grandparent, in 4 of 9 sibs of one patient, and in 5 of 7 at-risk children. Every unaffected adult relative with the marker had positive antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) except the grandmother; none of the 5 children with excess LMW-DNA had positive ANAs.

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found that in SLE patients expressing both C4A and C4B isotypes, plasma C4B/C4A quotients stayed fairly stable regardless of disease activity, which argued against preferential breakdown of either isotype during complement activation in SLE.

Napirei et al. (2000) found, in agreement with earlier reports, that serum DNASE1 activities were lower in SLE patients than in normal subjects.

Zhang et al. (2001) found that increased B lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) in SLE patients was partially associated with higher levels of anti-dsDNA antibodies of the IgG, IgM, and IgA classes, but not with disease activity; they suggested that BLyS may be a useful marker for early activation of an autoimmune diathesis.

Sthoeger et al. (2009) found that the mean serum level of alpha-defensin human neutrophil peptide-1 (HNP-1) in SLE patients (11.07 +/- 13.92 ng/microl) was significantly higher than in healthy controls (0.12 +/- 0.07 ng/microl). Sixty percent of patients had very high serum levels, and 50% showed increased alpha-defensin gene expression in polymorphonuclear cells. High alpha-defensin levels correlated with disease activity but not with neutrophil count. Beta-defensin-2 (hBD-2) could not be detected by ELISA, and its mRNA levels were low and similar to those in controls.

### Inheritance

Familial occurrence of SLE was studied by Leonhardt (1964), Siegel et al. (1965), who compared families of patients with rheumatoid arthritis and SLE, Larsen (1972), in a proband material from central eastern Norway, and First (1973).

Block et al. (1975) studied 12 twin pairs in which one or both twins had SLE (7 definitely monozygotic, 3 definitely dizygotic) and compared them with 17 previously described twin pairs (12 definitely monozygotic). Four of 7 (57%) definitely monozygotic pairs were clinically concordant for SLE by ARA preliminary criteria, and concordance for antinuclear factor and hypergammaglobulinemia was 71% and 87%, respectively. All 3 dizygotic twin sets were discordant for clinical SLE, although one clinically well twin had marked serologic abnormalities. The authors suggested a strong genetic component in the pathogenesis of SLE, with nongenetic and environmental factors also contributing.

Arnett and Shulman (1976) studied 8 families with familial SLE. Combining these with 1 deceased pair and 31 families from the literature gave 8 pairs of identical twins, 1 pair of nonidentical twins, 16 sib combinations, and 15 parent-offspring combinations; 53 cases in 25 families had enough documentation for detailed analysis. Comparing 23 clinical and laboratory features, they found impressive concordance for disease expression between identical twins and between parents and offspring, but not between sibs or controls, supporting genetic influences on how SLE is expressed. They found 4 father-offspring pairs, which made it less likely that SLE is transmitted during the perinatal period.

Horn et al. (1978) found that the brother and sister with MCTD had an identical HLA genotype (11,12/2,12), which was also inherited by 3 of their sibs who had impressive rheumatic complaints.

Miller and Schwartz (1979) found abnormal suppressor-cell activity in 13 clinically healthy first-degree relatives of SLE patients, 12 of whom were women, with no correlation between assay results and lymphocytotoxic antibodies in relatives. They concluded that impaired suppressor-cell function cannot by itself explain SLE pathogenesis, that certain suppressor-cell abnormalities are genetic markers, and proposed that development of SLE requires participation of at least 2 functionally distinct classes of genes.

Exner et al. (1980) concluded that the lupus anticoagulant might often be an acquired coagulation defect that requires a genetic predisposition. Mackie et al. (1987), however, found a prolonged KPTT that did not correct on mixing in 4 healthy spouses of affected women and in 3 asymptomatic sibs, none of whom had a history of bleeding or thrombosis and all of whom had normal clotting factor levels; the authors concluded that these findings would support the involvement of transmissible agents or other environmental factors in lupus-like disorders.

In the pedigree studied by Weinberg et al. (1980), BFPSTS appeared to be inherited as a dominant trait, but the likelihood analysis was inconclusive.

Lahita et al. (1983) suggested that in families with mainly male SLE, the sons inherited the disease from their fathers.

Family studies by Stohl et al. (1985) indicated autosomal codominant inheritance of the T4 epitope phenotype, not linked to the HLA locus.

Halpern et al. (1985) tested sera from 27 members of 3 kindreds with familial SLE by radioimmunoassay for a cross-reactive idiotype on anti-DNA antibodies, detected with the antiidiotype 3I. High-titered reactivity was present in 6 of 8 SLE patients and 15 of 19 family members. Expression of this idiotype was significantly increased in relatives of SLE patients and usually occurred without anti-DNA activity.

In the family studied by Levcovitz et al. (1988), excess LMW-DNA occurred in both males and females and was passed from male to male; the authors concluded that excess LMW-DNA in stimulated lymphocytes was inherited as an autosomal dominant trait in this family.

Sakane et al. (1989) studied 34 clinically healthy family members of 6 SLE probands. Impaired phytohemagglutinin-induced IL-2 activity was found in 15 of 29 blood relatives but in none of 5 nonconsanguineous household members, leading the authors to conclude that impaired IL-2 activity strongly correlates with a genetic relationship. Abnormal spontaneous B-cell immunoglobulin secretion was found in 22 of 29 blood relatives, but also in 4 of 5 nonconsanguineous household members; the authors concluded that although the evidence favored a genetic basis for the B-cell abnormalities, environmental effects may also contribute.

Sibley et al. (1993) discussed the potential contributions of heredity and environment to the concordance of disease expression in their family with 3 affected members.

Gaffney et al. (1998) concluded that their genome scan data supported the hypothesis that multiple genes, including one in the HLA region, influence susceptibility to human SLE. Kelly et al. (2002) noted that SLE shows familial aggregation, with lambda(s) estimates of 10 to 20 and a monozygotic twin concordance rate above 20.

Stein et al. (2002) concluded that the increased prevalence of renal disease previously reported in men with SLE is largely a familial rather than a sex-based difference, at least in multiplex SLE families. A familial basis for differences in hematologic and immunologic manifestations was also suggested, whereas arthritis and dermatologic features appeared most influenced by sex.

Molokhia et al. (2003) concluded that their admixture results support an additive genetic model for the ethnic difference in SLE risk between west Africans and Europeans, rather than an environmental explanation or an 'overdominant' model.

The International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) found independent contributions from 2 HLA effects and 1 effect in each of 5 other regions; considered jointly, these SNPs predicted SLE with a C statistic of 0.67 and explained about 15% of the sibling risk ratio of 29, with no evidence of statistical interaction among them. Gateva et al. (2009) likewise found no evidence for nonadditive interactions among 26 confirmed SLE risk alleles; HLA-DRB1 (DR3), IRF5, and STAT4 each accounted for more than 1% of the genetic variance, and together the 26 loci explained an estimated 8% of the total genetic susceptibility to SLE.

### Mapping

Weinberg et al. (1980) typed 74 relatives in their pedigree for 7 blood group antigens and 58 relatives for 4 electrophoretic enzyme markers and found no linkage between the BFPSTS trait and any of the marker genes.

Fronek et al. (1986) found a similar distribution of T cell receptor beta chain gene RFLPs in SLE patients, their relatives, and normal controls, and suggested that the TCR beta chain genes are not coinherited with genes responsible for SLE. Wong et al. (1988) studied TCR alpha, beta, and gamma chain gene RFLPs in 5 families with multiple members affected by SLE, 14 unrelated SLE patients, and 14 normal controls; the distribution of RFLP patterns did not differ significantly among groups, and none of the 3 TCR genes cosegregated with disease in families with at least 2 affected members.

Gaffney et al. (1998) performed a genome-wide microsatellite screen in 105 SLE sib-pair families. The strongest evidence for linkage was near the HLA locus on 6p11-p21 (D6S257, lod = 3.90, P = 0.000011), with additional linkage at 16q13 (D16S415, lod = 3.64), 14q21-q23 (D14S276, lod = 2.81), and 20p12 (D20S186, lod = 2.62). Nine further regions had lod scores of 1.00 or greater: 1p36, 1p13, 1q42, 2p15, 2q21-q33, 3cent-q11, 4q28, 11p15, and 15q26.

Shai et al. (1999) performed a genome-wide screen with 350 markers in 188 lupus patients from 80 families with 2 or more affected relatives. Nonparametric multipoint analysis suggested predisposing loci on chromosomes 1 and 18, but no single locus showed overwhelming evidence for linkage, suggesting that the genetic etiology more likely results from several genes of moderate effect.

Lindqvist et al. (2000) performed genome scans in families with multiple SLE patients from Iceland and Sweden. In Icelandic families, 5 regions had lod scores greater than 2.0: 4p15-p13 (Z = 3.20), 9p22 (Z = 2.27), and 19q13 (Z = 2.06), homologous to the murine regions containing the lmb2, sle2, and sle3 loci, respectively; 19p13 (D19S247, Z = 2.58); and 2q37 (D2S125, Z = 2.06). In Swedish families, 2q11 (Z = 2.13) and 2q37 (D2S125, Z = 2.18) had lod scores above 2.0. Combining both sets gave a lod score of 4.24 at D2S125, which the authors concluded represents a new SLE susceptibility locus, hSLE1.

In a second genome screen of 82 SLE sib-pair families, Gaffney et al. (2000) found the best intervals at 7p22 (lod 2.87), 7q21 (lod 2.40), 10p13 (lod 2.24), and 7q36 (lod 2.15). In a combined analysis of 187 sib-pair families, they found significant linkage at 6p11-p21 (D6S426, lod 4.19) and 16q13 (D16S415, lod 3.85).

In a genome scan of 126 pedigrees multiplex for SLE, Gray-McGuire et al. (2000) identified linkage to chromosome 4p16-15.2 (P = 0.0003 and lod = 3.84), confirmed in European American families from an independent collection, and presented evidence of epistatic interaction between 4p16-15.2 and 5p15. Their data supported results of 3 independent studies for 9 regions previously reported to be linked to SLE.

Graham et al. (2002) genotyped a dense map of microsatellites across the HLA region in SLE families and identified 3 class II haplotypes showing transmission distortion, carrying DRB1*1501/DQB1*0602, DRB1*0801/DQB1*0402, and DRB1*0301/DQB1*0201. By visualizing ancestral recombinants, they narrowed the haplotypes containing DRB1*1501 and DRB1*0801 to a region of about 500 kb.

Quintero-Del-Rio et al. (2002) evaluated genome scan data in 75 SLE pedigrees with at least 1 patient meeting the ACR renal criterion. They found linkage at 10q22.3 in European Americans (lod 3.16; sib-pair P = 0.0000008), designated SLEN1, and at 2q34-35 in African Americans (sib-pair P = 0.000001), designated SLEN2. In 35 pedigrees with 2 or more SLE patients with renal disease, they found a lod score of 3.34 at 11p15.6 in African Americans (SLEN3).

Kelly et al. (2002) reviewed linkage studies of SLE multiplex pedigrees that had identified 60 susceptibility loci, 7 of which met the threshold for significant linkage: 1q22-23, 1q41, 2q37, 4p16, 6p21-11, 16q13, and 17p13. Linkage at 1q41, 4p16, and 6p21 had been independently confirmed.

Johanneson et al. (2002) genotyped 87 multicase SLE families from European and recently admixed populations for 62 microsatellite markers on chromosome 1 and found a significant 3-point lod score of 3.79 at 1q31, contributed by families from all populations; they concluded that this locus contains a major SLE susceptibility gene. Six regions (1p36, 1p21, 1q23, 1q25, 1q31, and 1q43) had lod scores of 1.50 or higher.

Prokunina et al. (2002) noted that they had previously reported a susceptibility locus, SLEB2, in Nordic multicase SLE families, and regarded PDCD1 as the strongest candidate gene within that locus.

Scofield et al. (2003) selected 38 of 184 SLE multiplex pedigrees that contained an SLE patient with thrombocytopenia and established linkage at 1q22-23 (maximum lod = 3.71) in these pedigrees and at 11p13 (maximum lod = 5.72) in the 13 African American pedigrees among them.

In a genome-wide scan of 64 SLE multiplex families with affected male members, Xing et al. (2005) found the largest signal (lod = 3.08) at 13q32 in 18 African American male families. IBD sharing suggested dominant inheritance, and multipoint model-based analysis gave a lod score of 3.13. Fine mapping localized the locus to a region tightly linked to D13S892; the authors suggested the susceptibility gene is specific to African Americans with a form of SLE in which males are at high risk.

In a genome search meta-analysis of 12 genome scans from 9 studies (605 families with 1,355 SLE-affected individuals), Lee and Nath (2005) found genome-wide significance at 6p22.3-6p21.1 (containing the HLA region) and 16p12.3-16q12.2, and 12 additional bins likely containing SLE susceptibility loci, including novel regions at 4q32.1-4q34.3 and 13q13.2-13q22.2.

In a targeted genome scan of 1q21-43 in 126 lupus multiplex families containing 151 affected sib pairs, Wu et al. (2007) found evidence of linkage at 1q32.2 (nonparametric linkage score 2.52, P = 0.006).

Hom et al. (2008) genotyped more than 500,000 SNPs in 1,311 North American patients of European descent with SLE and controls and replicated top loci in 793 Swedish patients and 857 controls. Variation upstream of BLK and C8orf13 on 8p23.1 was associated with SLE (rs13277113; OR, 1.39; P = 1 x 10(-10)), as were variants on 16p11.22 near ITGAM and ITGAX (rs11574637; OR, 1.33; P = 3 x 10(-11)). The BLK/C8orf13 risk allele was associated with lower BLK and higher C8orf13 mRNA levels in B-cell lines.

The International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) carried out a genome-wide association scan with 317,501 SNPs in 720 women of European ancestry with SLE and 2,337 controls, with replication in 1,846 affected women and 1,825 controls. Besides the HLA region and IRF5, they found replicated association at 16p11.2 (ITGAM), 11p15.5 (KIAA1542), 3p14.3 (PXK), and 1q25.1 (rs10798269; OR = 0.82; P = 1.11 x 10(-7)), the last lying within the 1q23-q25 linkage interval. The strongest association was in the HLA region at 6p21.3, where rs3131379 and rs1270942 reached P < 10(-50) and logistic regression identified 2 partially independent effects. Without requiring replication in every sample, possible associations were found in regions containing XKR6, LYN, ATG5, ICA1, BLK, and SCUBE1.

Graham et al. (2008) performed a genome-wide association study of 311,238 SNPs in 431 unrelated SLE cases and 2,155 controls. Three regions reached genome-wide significance: HLA, IRF5/TNPO3, and a new locus on 6q23 within TNFAIP3 (rs5029939; P = 2.55 x 10(-8); OR 2.28), which replicated in 740 independent trios (meta-analysis P = 2.89 x 10(-12)). STAT4 (rs3821236) and BLK (rs2618476) variants were also associated at genome-wide significance, and an ITGAM variant (rs11150610) showed replicated association.

In a genome-wide association study in a Chinese Han population (1,047 cases and 1,205 controls, with replication in 3,152 cases and 7,050 controls), Han et al. (2009) identified 9 new susceptibility loci, at ETS1, IKZF1, RASGRP3, SLC15A4, TNIP1, 7q11.23, 10q11.22, 11q23.3, and 16p11.2, and confirmed loci at BLK, IRF5, STAT4, TNFAIP3, TNFSF4, 6q21, and 22q11.21.

Gateva et al. (2009) followed up SNPs from 2,466 regions nominally associated in a genome-wide scan of 1,310 cases and 7,859 controls in independent US and Swedish samples and identified 5 new loci at genome-wide significance: TNIP1 (rs7708392; combined P = 3.8 x 10(-13); OR 1.27), distinct from psoriasis risk variants near TNIP1; the PRDM1-ATG5 intergenic region (rs6568431; P = 7.12 x 10(-10)), distinct from the ATG5 risk allele rs2245214; the JAZF1 promoter (rs849142; P = 1.54 x 10(-9)); UHRF1BP1, defined by the nonsynonymous allele R454Q (rs11755393; P = 2.22 x 10(-8)) in a region of extended LD including SNRPC; and IL10 (rs3024505; P = 3.95 x 10(-8); OR 1.19). Of 25 SNPs from 23 previously reported loci, 21 showed further evidence of association, and 9 loci reached genome-wide significance: HLA-DRB1 (DR3), IRF5, TNFAIP3, BLK, STAT4, ITGAM, PTPN22, PHRF1 (KIAA1542), and TNFSF4; no association was seen for LYN, SCUBE1, TLR5, or LY9. Twenty-one additional candidate loci had P < 1 x 10(-5), including IRF8, TAOK3 (N47S), LYST, and IL12RB2.

Zhao et al. (2017) genotyped SLE cases and controls of Chinese, European American, and African American ancestry with the Immunochip. In Chinese, the strongest association was at rs73366469 in the GTF2IRD1-GTF2I intergenic region on 7q11.23 (P = 3.8 x 10(-29), OR = 2.88), confirmed modestly in European Americans but not in African Americans.

Accounting for C4 alleles in European and African American cohorts, Kamitaki et al. (2020) identified a more modest effect independent of C4, tagged by rs2105898 and rs9271513, apparently involving noncoding variation in the HLA class II XL9 region associated with expression of many HLA class II genes.

### Heterogeneity

Batchelor et al. (1980) found that the distribution of HLA-DR antigens in hydralazine-induced SLE differed significantly from that in idiopathic SLE and concluded that the 2 syndromes are separate entities.

In their 8 SLE families, Reveille et al. (1983) found heterozygous C2 deficiency in only 1 of 7 kindreds studied, inherited by only 1 of 3 family members with SLE. HLA-DR2 and DR3 each occurred in 36% of SLE patients, not significantly different from non-SLE relatives or controls, and sib-pair analysis of 14 sets showed only random HLA distribution with SLE. The authors concluded that genetic factors other than HLA and complement deficiencies and/or environmental factors are necessary for expression of SLE.

Shai et al. (1999) found support for SLE susceptibility genes in the 1q44 and 1p36 regions only in Mexican American families and not in Caucasian families. Kelly et al. (2002) observed that SLE linkages usually dominate in one ethnicity or another. Restricting a genome scan meta-analysis to Caucasians, Lee and Nath (2005) found the strongest evidence for linkage at 6p21.1-6q15 and suggested that 3q22.1-3q25.33 may represent an ethnicity-specific linkage. Han et al. (2009) concluded that comparison of their Chinese Han GWAS with previous findings highlighted genetic heterogeneity in SLE susceptibility between Chinese Han and European populations.

Fanciulli et al. (2007) found association of low FCGR3B copy number with Wegener granulomatosis and microscopic polyangiitis but not with the organ-specific diseases Graves disease or Addison disease. In contrast, Willcocks et al. (2008) found that ANCA-associated systemic vasculitis was associated with high FCGR3B copy number (P = 10(-8)) in 556 UK patients, and that SLE patients had a significantly lower FCGR3B/CD36 ratio than patients with vasculitis.

Gateva et al. (2009) tested 42 variants from 35 loci previously associated with other autoimmune diseases and found suggestive association with SLE for IFIH1, CFB, CLEC16A, IL12B, and SH2B3, including an IFIH1 missense allele (rs1990760) and a protective CFB missense allele, R32Q, that remained significant after conditioning on HLA DR2 and DR3. Zhao et al. (2017) found that the NCF1 SLE risk variant was also associated with primary Sjogren syndrome in Chinese and European Americans and with seropositive rheumatoid arthritis in Koreans.

### Molecular Genetics

Fielder et al. (1983) studied families of 29 SLE patients and 42 normal subjects for HLA antigens and the complement polymorphisms C2, C4A, C4B, and Bf. Null alleles for C4A, C4B, or C2 were found in 24 of 29 (83%) patients compared with 18 of 42 (43%) controls. HLA-DR3 was present in 20 of 29 (69%) patients versus 7 of 39 (18%) controls, but because of strong linkage disequilibrium between DR3 and C4 null alleles, their relative contributions could not be distinguished. The authors suggested that the null alleles are relevant to genetic susceptibility, given the association of SLE with complement deficiencies. Using the empirical logistic method, Green et al. (1986) suggested that the association with C4 null alleles was primary and the DR3 association secondary.

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found homozygous C4A (120810) deficiency in 16% (13 of 80) of SLE patients ascertained from a defined population in southern Sweden.

Wu et al. (1996) screened the extracellular domain of FASL (FASLG; 134638) by SSCP in 75 patients with SLE and found a heterozygous 84-bp deletion within exon 4 in 1 patient with lymphadenopathy, predicted to cause an in-frame deletion of 28 amino acids. The patient's PBMCs had reduced FasL activity and activation-induced cell death. The authors suggested that FASL mutations are an uncommon cause of SLE.

Linker-Israeli et al. (1999) genotyped the IL6 (147620) 3-prime AT-rich minisatellite and 5-prime promoter in 146 SLE patients and 139 controls. The 3-prime allele distribution differed significantly in Caucasians (P = 0.001) and African Americans (P = 0.006); short alleles (792 bp or less) were seen exclusively in SLE patients. B lymphoblastoid cells from patients with SLE-associated alleles secreted 3- to 4-fold higher IL-6 levels, and IL-6 mRNA stability was enhanced. There was no association with the 5-prime G/C alleles.

Yasutomo et al. (2001) found a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2 of DNASE1 (125505) in 2 patients with SLE who had decreased DNASE1 activity and an extremely high IgG titer against nucleosomal antigens. Shin et al. (2004) did not find this mutation in 350 Korean SLE patients or 330 controls. Among 6 DNASE1 SNPs genotyped, none was associated with SLE risk, but the exon 8 Gln244Arg SNP was associated with anti-RNP and anti-dsDNA antibody production; Arg/Arg homozygosity was more frequent in patients with anti-RNP antibody than in those without (31.3% vs 14.4%; OR = 2.86).

Kyogoku et al. (2002) identified a c.695T-C SNP in FCGR2B (604590) causing an ile232-to-thr (I232T) substitution in the transmembrane domain; the 232T/T genotype was significantly increased in 193 Japanese SLE patients compared with 303 controls. In the same samples, FCGR3A (146740) 176F/F was significantly associated with SLE, and 2-locus analyses suggested that the previously reported FCGR3B association was secondary to linkage disequilibrium with FCGR2B. Floto et al. (2005) showed that FcgammaRIIb T232 could not inhibit activatory receptors because it was excluded from sphingolipid rafts.

Kelly et al. (2002) noted known genetic associations of SLE with HLA alleles, complement component genes, and Fc-gamma receptors. Johanneson et al. (2002) found that analysis of a known CD45 mutation did not support a role in SLE.

Within the SLEB2 locus, Prokunina et al. (2002) studied 2,510 individuals from 5 sets of families and unrelated patients and found that an intronic SNP in PDCD1 (600244) was associated with SLE in Europeans (12% of affected vs 5% of controls; relative risk 2.6) and Mexicans (7% vs 2%; relative risk 3.5). The SLE-associated allele altered a RUNX1 binding site in an intronic enhancer.

Russell et al. (2004) studied CRP (123260) in 586 UK simplex SLE families and found that patients were more likely to inherit the minor A allele of CRP 4 (rs1205), in the 3-prime flanking/untranslated region (P = 0.003 in all 586 families). The rare allele was also overtransmitted to ANA-positive individuals. Basal serum CRP levels were independently influenced by CRP 4 and by the synonymous CRP 2 SNP (rs1800947), and haplotypes carrying the CRP 4 minor allele were associated with lower basal CRP; the authors suggested that reduced basal CRP predisposes to SLE.

Li et al. (2004) found that SLE patients carried an LTK polymorphism of the same gain-of-function type as in NZB mice, at a significantly higher frequency than in controls.

Barreto et al. (2004) found that a 3-prime UTR dinucleotide repeat in CTLA4 (123890) was associated with SLE, but not the exon 1 +49 SNP in their own population; in a meta-analysis of 8 studies, G allele carriers at +49 had increased risk. Lee et al. (2005), in a meta-analysis of 14 studies, found that the +49 GG genotype was associated with SLE (OR 1.287), especially in Asians.

Su et al. (2004) identified 10 SNPs in the FCGR2B promoter defining 2 haplotypes; in 600 Caucasians, the less frequent haplotype was associated with SLE (OR = 1.65), independent of FCGR2A and FCGR3A polymorphisms, and increased reporter expression. Blank et al. (2005) found that homozygosity for a -343G-C promoter variant was associated with SLE in European Americans and reduced FcgammaRIIb expression.

Kyogoku et al. (2004) found association of the PTPN22 (600716) R620W polymorphism with SLE in 525 North American white patients and 1,961 controls; 1 copy of W620 increased risk (OR = 1.37) and 2 copies more than doubled it (OR = 4.37). Orru et al. (2009) found that the Q263 allele of PTPN22 R263Q, which reduced phosphatase activity, was associated with protection against SLE.

Sigurdsson et al. (2005) tested 44 SNPs in 13 type I interferon pathway genes in 679 Swedish, Finnish, and Icelandic patients, 798 unaffected family members, and 438 controls and found strong signals for TYK2 and IRF5 (607218) (P less than 10(-7)). Graham et al. (2006) replicated association of the IRF5 rs2004640 T allele in 4 case-control cohorts (P = 4.4 x 10(-16)) and by TDT, and showed that this allele creates a donor splice site in exon 1B; a common haplotype driving elevated expression of multiple isoforms conferred risk.

In a meta-analysis of 21 studies, Lee et al. (2006) found that the TNF (191160) -308 A/A genotype was associated with SLE (OR 3.2), especially in European-derived populations.

Aitman et al. (2006) found that low copy number of FCGR3B (610665) was associated with glomerulonephritis in SLE. Fanciulli et al. (2007) confirmed association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE with glomerulonephritis (P = 1.4 x 10(-8)) in 161 UK patients and also with SLE without known renal involvement in 375 cases (OR = 2.21 for fewer than 2 copies); zero copies were observed in 25 of 1,279 individuals with autoimmunity, including 21 with SLE, versus 1 of 862 controls.

Wu et al. (2007) analyzed 1,416 individuals from 258 Caucasian and 142 Chinese simplex families and found that a 3-SNP haplotype of CR2 (120650) (rs3813946, rs1048971, rs17615) was associated with SLE (P = 0.00001; 1.54-fold risk); rs3813946 in the 5-prime UTR altered transcriptional activity.

Yang et al. (2007) studied C4 copy number in 1,241 European Americans. Two copies of total C4 increased SLE risk (OR = 6.514), and 5 or more copies were protective (OR = 0.466). Zero copies (OR = 5.267) or 1 copy (OR = 1.613) of C4A were risk factors, and 3 or more copies appeared protective. A haplotype carrying a single short C4B gene, in tight LD with TNFA -308A, was preferentially transmitted to patients.

Lee-Kirsch et al. (2007) found monoallelic frameshift or missense mutations, plus one 3-prime UTR variant, in the TREX1 gene in 9 of 417 individuals with SLE and in none of 1,712 controls; 2 mutant alleles changed subcellular targeting of the protein.

Remmers et al. (2007) found that a haplotype marked by rs7574865 in intron 3 of STAT4 (600558) was associated with SLE in 1,039 patients and 1,248 controls (31% vs 22% of chromosomes; OR 1.55).

Kamatani et al. (2008) found association of TNXB (600985) 5-prime flanking SNP rs3130342 with SLE in 178 Japanese patients (OR 3.11), replicated in 203 cases; the C4 copy number association was weaker and probably reflected LD with this SNP.

Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008) found association of an upstream TNFSF4 (603594) risk haplotype with SLE in 416 UK trios, replicated in 263 Minnesota trios and in 424 UK cases; activated lymphoblastoid cells homozygous for the risk haplotype had a 6.7-fold increase in TNFSF4 transcript.

The SLEGEN et al. (2008) found 3 ITGAM (120980) SNPs associated with SLE (combined P = 2.02 x 10(-26) at rs9888739), KIAA1542 (PHRF1; 611780) rs4963128 (OR = 0.78), near IRF7, and PXK (611450) rs6445975 (OR = 1.25), and replicated associations in PTPN22, FCGR2A (146790), and STAT4. Nath et al. (2008) found the strongest ITGAM association at nonsynonymous rs1143679 in 3,818 Europeans (OR = 1.78), replicated in African-descent samples.

Kozyrev et al. (2008) identified association of BANK1 (610292) R61H with SLE (combined P = 3.7 x 10(-10); OR = 1.38) and associated variants rs17266594 and A383T.

Oishi et al. (2008) found that ITPR3 (147267) promoter SNP rs3748079 was associated with SLE in Japanese (OR 1.88); NKX2.5 bound only the nonsusceptible T allele, and the SNP was also associated with rheumatoid arthritis and Graves disease.

Willcocks et al. (2008) confirmed association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE in UK Caucasians but not in Hong Kong Chinese, and identified a woman with SLE homozygous-deficient for neutrophil FcgammaRIIIb whose deletion removed FCGR3B, HSPA7, and FCGR2C.

Graham et al. (2008) found 2 independent SLE signals near TNFAIP3 (191163) in 991 trio families, and Musone et al. (2008) found 3 independent signals in 1,239 European cases; the risk cys127 A20 protein was less effective at inhibiting NF-kappa-B.

Sanchez et al. (2009) found that the IL18 (600953) rs360719 C allele was associated with SLE in Spanish, Italian, and Argentinian cohorts (pooled OR = 1.37). Myouzen et al. (2010) found association of EGR2 (129010) rs10761670 with SLE (pooled OR = 1.23), correlated with increased EGR2 expression.

Willcocks et al. (2010) found that an FCGR2B SNP abrogating FcgammaRIIb function was associated with SLE in Caucasians and Southeast Asians. Niederer et al. (2010) found association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE in UK, Swedish, and Hong Kong Chinese cohorts (combined OR 1.62), independent of FCGR2B I232T, whose homozygosity was confirmed as associated in Caucasians (OR 2.26).

Boteva et al. (2012) genotyped 1,028 SLE cases and 1,179 controls from the UK and Spain and found that partial C4 deficiency states were not independent risk factors for SLE.

Manjarrez-Orduno et al. (2012) found association of CSK (124095) intronic rs34933034 A allele with SLE in 3,769 European cases (OR 1.32), with higher CSK expression in B cells.

Mueller et al. (2013) identified 2 paralogous 24.5-kb blocks defining the limits of FCGR2C/FCGR3B copy number variation and found that FCGR3B deletion creates a chimeric gene leading to ectopic FcgammaRIIb on NK cells.

Zhao et al. (2017) identified NCF1 (608512) p.Arg90His (rs201802880) as the likely causal 7q11.23 variant, associated in Asians (OR = 3.47), European Americans, and African Americans; 1 copy of NCF1 increased risk.

Farias et al. (2019) identified a rare intron 4 MEF2D (600663) variant associated with SLE in Swedish cohorts.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) imputed C4 copy number in 6,748 European SLE cases and 11,516 controls; each C4A copy was protective (OR 0.54), and in 1,494 African American cases C4A and C4B, but not DRB1*03:01, were implicated.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found that photosensitivity was more common in SLE patients with C4A deficiency than in other patients (p less than 0.05), with no differences in other clinical features or autoantibodies.

Baechler et al. (2003) found that an interferon gene expression signature marked more severe SLE involving the kidneys, hematopoietic cells, and/or the central nervous system.

Remmers et al. (2007) found that homozygosity for the STAT4 risk allele was associated with more than double the risk of lupus.

Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008) found that TNFSF4 risk-haplotype SNPs did not influence the lupus phenotype toward antiphospholipid syndrome, Sjogren syndrome, or lupus nephritis.

Zhao et al. (2017) found that the NCF1 p.Arg90His risk allele was associated with earlier age of SLE onset in a dose-dependent manner in Korean and European American patients.

Farias et al. (2019) found that all 3 Swedish carriers of the MEF2D rs200395694 risk allele had anti-Sm antibodies, anti-RNP antibodies, and Raynaud phenomenon (p = 0.00037).

Kamitaki et al. (2020) found that C4 alleles had stronger effects on SLE risk in men than in women (14-fold vs 6-fold variation).

### Pathogenesis

Lappat and Cawein (1968) carried out a family study of procainamide-induced SLE, asking whether a pharmacogenetic polymorphism might underlie susceptibility.

DeHoratius et al. (1975) found lymphocytotoxic antibodies in 57% of SLE family members and 68% of close household contacts; anti-RNA antibodies occurred in consanguineous but not nonconsanguineous household contacts, supporting roles for both an environmental agent, possibly a virus, and the genetic response.

Batchelor et al. (1980) found that 25 of 26 patients with hydralazine-induced SLE were slow acetylators, and HLA-DR4 frequency was 73%, significantly higher than in controls (33%), hydralazine-treated slow acetylators without SLE (25%), and idiopathic SLE (25%). There was a 4:1 female:male ratio; every slow-acetylator woman with DR4 developed SLE.

Solomon et al. (1983) raised a monoclonal anti-idiotypic antibody, 3I, against anti-native DNA antibodies from an SLE patient, which reacted with 8 of 9 sera containing anti-native DNA activity, suggesting a shared idiotype. Diamond and Scharff (1984) showed that a single amino acid substitution in the T15 heavy chain converted an antiphosphocholine antibody into one reactive with double-stranded DNA and cardiolipin.

Steinberg et al. (1984) proposed that infections activate macrophages and T cells that stimulate B cells, and that in the absence of normal control mechanisms, abnormal B-cell proliferation and differentiation trigger disease.

Levcovitz et al. (1988) concluded that excess LMW-DNA appears before ANAs in children of affected adults and may be a predisposing factor.

Gross et al. (2000) identified TACI and BCMA as receptors for zTNF4 (BLyS). Zhang et al. (2001) demonstrated elevated BLyS in SLE sera that stimulated B cell activation in vitro.

Solomou et al. (2001) found that SLE T cells contained increased phosphorylated CREM bound to the -180 site of the IL-2 promoter, and that transcriptional repression is responsible for decreased IL-2 production.

DeGiorgio et al. (2001) showed that a subset of anti-DNA antibodies cross-reacts with NMDA receptor subunits NR2a and NR2b and mediates neuronal apoptosis; CSF from an SLE patient contained such antibodies. Kowal et al. (2006) showed that such serum caused hippocampal damage and memory impairment in mice when the blood-brain barrier was breached, and found anti-NMDAR antibodies in brains of lupus patients.

Blanco et al. (2001) found that SLE serum induced monocytes to differentiate into dendritic cells in an IFN-alpha-dependent manner correlating with disease activity. Baechler et al. (2003) found dysregulated interferon pathway genes in about half of SLE patients.

Leadbetter et al. (2002) showed that chromatin-IgG complexes activate rheumatoid factor-positive B cells through dual engagement of the antigen receptor and a MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor, implicating TLR9.

Li et al. (2004) suggested that polymorphic LTK proteins up-regulate the PI3K pathway. Xu et al. (2004) showed that lupus T cells resist anergy and apoptosis by upregulating COX-2.

Tao et al. (2005) found deficient CD226 and survivin expression in NK T cells from active SLE, explaining their high apoptotic sensitivity.

Fanciulli et al. (2007) and Niederer et al. (2010) proposed that reduced FCGR3B expression impairs immune complex clearance by neutrophils.

Balada et al. (2008) found lower DNA deoxymethylcytosine content in CD4+ T cells from 29 SLE patients than in controls, with no difference in DNMT transcript levels.

Sthoeger et al. (2009) suggested that neutrophil alpha-defensin secretion may activate adaptive immunity in SLE.

Kshirsagar et al. (2014) found enhanced Stat3 and Akt activity and migratory capacity in Th17 cells from children with lupus nephritis.

Bialas et al. (2017) observed increased type I IFN signaling in postmortem hippocampal sections from SLE patients.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) found that men had more C4 and C3 protein than women per C4 gene copy, which may help explain the female predominance of SLE.

### Diagnosis

Tan et al. (1982) revised the 1971 criteria for classification of SLE, adding fluorescence antinuclear antibody and antibodies to native DNA and Sm antigen and dropping Raynaud phenomenon and alopecia. The revised criteria were 96% sensitive and 96% specific.

### Population Genetics

Siegel et al. (1970) compared SLE epidemiology in New York City and Jefferson County, Alabama, for 1956 to 1965. Fessel (1974) found a high prevalence of SLE in black women.

Serdula and Rhoads (1979) ascertained 168 SLE cases on Oahu, Hawaii; age-adjusted prevalence per 100,000 was 5.8 in whites, 24.1 in Chinese, 19.9 in Filipinos, 20.4 in part-Hawaiians, and 18.2 in Japanese, and about 90% of cases were female.

Molokhia et al. (2003) found mean west African admixture of 0.81 in SLE cases and 0.74 in controls in Trinidad (risk ratio 32.5 per unit admixture).

Willcocks et al. (2010) found that the defunctioning FCGR2B allele was more common in Southeast Asians and Africans and that homozygosity protected against severe malaria (OR = 0.56). Niederer et al. (2010) found that low FCGR3B copy number was least common in Caucasians (7.4%) and most common in Kenyans.

### Animal Model

Knight and Adams (1978) found that NZB contributes 1 gene and NZW 2 genes to lupus nephritis in (NZB x NZW) backcross mice, one NZW gene linked to H-2.

Morel et al. (1994) found 3 intervals on mouse chromosomes 1, 4, and 7 carrying recessive glomerulonephritis susceptibility alleles in NZM/Aeg2410 mice, inherited as a threshold liability. Mohan et al. (1999) found that Sle1 and Sle3 congenic loci together produced highly penetrant glomerulonephritis.

Napirei et al. (2000) found that Dnase1-deficient mice develop antinuclear antibodies and glomerulonephritis.
