### TEXT

This entry describes systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a genetically complex, multisystem autoimmune disease. Heterozygous mutations in the TREX1, DNASE1, and FASLG genes have been reported in rare patients. Variation in many genes has been associated with susceptibility to the disorder, and many susceptibility loci and genes have been identified by linkage and association studies.

### Description

Systemic lupus erythematosus is a multisystem, antibody-mediated autoimmune disorder that occurs spontaneously in humans and mice (Steinberg et al., 1984). It is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by antibodies to nuclear self-antigens, including antinuclear antibodies directed against naked DNA and entire nucleosomes. The resulting immune complexes are thought to accumulate in vessel walls, glomeruli, and joints, causing glomerulonephritis, arthritis, and general vasculitis (Napirei et al., 2000). Yasutomo et al. (2001) noted that SLE causes progressive glomerulonephritis, arthritis, and an erythematoid rash. Many of the autoantibodies recognize nucleic acids and nucleic acid-binding proteins, which activate Toll-like receptors and lead to production of type I interferon (Gateva et al., 2009). The disease is clinically diverse but is unified by autoantibody production and deposition of immune complexes in organs including the joints, skin, and kidneys. The resulting inflammation causes tissue damage with features ranging from skin rashes and cytopenias to organ-threatening glomerulonephritis (Niederer et al., 2010).

Gray-McGuire et al. (2000) described SLE as a complex autoimmune disorder involving at least hormonal, environmental, and genetic factors. The International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) described SLE as an autoimmune inflammatory disease characterized by antinuclear autoantibodies, complement and interferon activation, and tissue destruction. It affects mainly women, at a 9:1 ratio, particularly during the childbearing years, and has strong genetic and environmental components. SLE is among the most heritable of the common autoimmune diseases, with a sib risk ratio of approximately 30 (Gateva et al., 2009). Kamitaki et al. (2020) cited a heritability of 66% and noted that SLE affects 9 times more women than men.

### Clinical Features

Tuffanelli (1971) reviewed lupus erythematosus panniculitis (profundus).

Kohler et al. (1974) reported hereditary angioedema and 'familial' lupus erythematosus in identical twin boys.

Brustein et al. (1977) described the child of a woman with discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE). The child developed DLE lesions at the age of 2 months. These lesions persisted and new lesions kept appearing up to age 28 months. In a second child, now 11 months old, a probable lupus erythematosus rash developed at 1 week of age.

Stohl et al. (1985) identified 3 black Jamaicans with SLE whose T helper/inducer cells lacked the T4 epitope. All 3 had lymphadenopathy. The asymptomatic, otherwise healthy, T4 epitope-deficient brother of one patient also had lymphadenopathy in the same distribution as his affected sister. Family studies pointed to autosomal codominant inheritance of the T4 epitope phenotype, not linked to the HLA locus. Cultured cells from T4 epitope-deficient family members showed impaired B-cell differentiation after pokeweed mitogen stimulation. The authors suggested that SLE with the T4 epitope-deficient phenotype may represent a distinct subset of SLE.

Sibley et al. (1993) described a family in which 3 members developed SLE complicated by ischemic vasculopathy with digital ischemia. The calendar years of onset, ages at onset, and clinical courses were remarkably similar in all 3 patients, although their genotypes were not.

Garcia et al. (1996) studied the autoantibody profile in 222 African American patients with SLE, 94 with lupus nephritis and 128 without. Only anti-dsDNA antibodies differed significantly between the 2 groups. The authors concluded that African American SLE patients with lupus nephritis do not have a specific or distinctive autoantibody profile.

Kamat et al. (2003) reported identical triplets who were diagnosed with SLE in reverse birth order at ages 8, 9, and 11 years. Each sib had different clinical signs and symptoms, but all 3 had skin rash, fatigue, and biopsy-proven glomerulonephritis at different ages. Laboratory findings were similar in the 3 sibs: positive antinuclear antibodies, anti-native DNA, and anti-double-stranded DNA, as well as low complement levels.

Bialas et al. (2017) noted that up to 75% of patients with SLE have neuropsychiatric symptoms, collectively called central nervous system (CNS) lupus. These range from anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment to seizures and, rarely, psychosis. CNS lupus typically presents at diagnosis or within the first year.

Farias et al. (2019) collected clinical data for 140 Swedish patients with SLE. Among them, 65% had malar rash, 31% discoid rash, 69% photosensitivity, 29% oral ulcers, 72% arthritis, 36% serositis, 24% renal disorder, 5% neurologic disorder, and 62% hematologic disorder. Antinuclear antibodies were present in 98% and anti-dsDNA antibodies in 59%. Raynaud phenomenon was reported in 50% of patients with available data.

### Other Features

Reed et al. (1972) described hereditary inflammatory vasculitis with persistent nodules, which they considered a genetically determined new entity probably related to lupus erythematosus. Schaller (1972) reported an illness resembling lupus erythematosus in mothers of boys with chronic granulomatous disease. Larsen and Godal (1972) studied thyroid diseases and thyroid antibodies in families of patients with SLE.

Beaucher et al. (1977) studied antibodies to DNA in household dogs in the setting of familial lupus erythematosus.

Horn et al. (1978) diagnosed mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) in a brother and sister and studied 18 further family members spanning 3 generations. Eight relatives had symptoms or signs of MCTD without complete expression of the disease. The affected sibs had the identical HLA genotype 11,12/2,12, which was also inherited by 3 of their sibs, who had impressive rheumatic complaints.

Exner et al. (1980) detected a lupus anticoagulant in 2 pairs of sibs and presented strong evidence for it in a third sib pair. They suggested that the lupus anticoagulant may often be an acquired coagulation defect that requires genetic predisposition. Mackie et al. (1987) studied 19 persons from 3 families with more than 1 member affected by SLE or lupus-like disease. Eight of the 11 affected persons had a prolonged KPTT and other evidence of lupus anticoagulant. Prolonged noncorrectable KPTTs were also found in 4 healthy spouses of affected females and in 3 asymptomatic sibs. The authors considered these findings consistent with transmissible agents or other environmental factors being involved in lupus-like disorders.

Weinberg et al. (1980) studied a large pedigree with elliptocytosis, multiple lipomatosis, and biologic false-positive serologic test for syphilis (BFPSTS). BFPSTS appeared to be dominant, but the analysis was inconclusive. Two female pedigree members with BFPSTS developed SLE.

Drug-induced lupus: Lappat and Cawein (1968) carried out a familial study of procainamide-induced SLE and raised the question of pharmacogenetic polymorphism. Batchelor et al. (1980) studied 26 patients with hydralazine-induced SLE, 25 of whom were slow acetylators. HLA-DR4 was significantly more frequent in this group (73%) than in healthy subjects (33%), in slow-acetylator hypertensive patients treated with hydralazine without developing SLE (25%), and in patients with idiopathic SLE (25%). The female-to-male ratio was 4:1. Among slow acetylators treated with hydralazine, all women with DR4 developed SLE, and the only affected men had DR2 and were receiving 200 mg hydralazine per day. The DR antigen distribution differed significantly between hydralazine-induced and idiopathic SLE, which supported the view that the 2 syndromes are separate entities. The authors suggested guidelines for hydralazine therapy.

Lessard et al. (1997) showed that CYP2D6 is the major human cytochrome P450 isozyme forming N-hydroxyprocainamide, a reactive metabolite of procainamide that may be responsible for lupus erythematosus in treated patients. In vivo, Lessard et al. (1999) found urinary nitroprocainamide in 7 of 13 extensive CYP2D6 metabolizers but in none of 6 poor metabolizers. It was undetectable in all individuals during concomitant quinidine administration.

Chondrodysplasia punctata and maternal lupus: Elcioglu and Hall (1998) described 2 sibs with chondrodysplasia punctata whose mother had SLE. Austin-Ward et al. (1998) reported a newborn whose mother had SLE diagnosed before pregnancy. The infant had neonatal lupus together with chondrodysplasia punctata and other findings resembling the anomalies associated with oral anticoagulants, although there was no history of exposure. Toriello (1998) also discussed chondrodysplasia punctata and maternal SLE.

Kelly et al. (1999) reported a male infant with stippled epiphyses on neonatal radiographs who developed a typical lupus erythematosus rash, which led to the diagnosis of SLE in his mother. Over 3 years of follow-up, the child had strikingly short stature, midface hypoplasia, anomalous digital development, slow resolution of the stippled epiphyses, and near-normal cognitive development. Kozlowski et al. (2004) described 2 brothers with chondrodysplasia punctata whose mother had longstanding disseminated lupus erythematosus and epilepsy, treated during both pregnancies with chloroquine and other agents. They noted 7 previous reports of this association and concluded that maternal lupus erythematosus must be regarded as a causative factor in chondrodysplasia punctata.

### Biochemical Features

Reidenberg et al. (1980) determined acetylator phenotypes in 21 patients from New York City and 29 from Israel with idiopathic SLE: 30 were slow, 17 rapid, and 3 indeterminate. Pooled with published data, the distribution was 150 slow to 77 rapid, against an expected 122:105 (P less than 0.001). In contrast, Baer et al. (1986) found similar frequencies of the slow acetylator phenotype in 64 patients with idiopathic SLE (38%), 60 healthy volunteers (50%), and 52 non-SLE medical patients (44%). They concluded that there was no evidence for an association between acetylator phenotype and idiopathic SLE.

Levcovitz et al. (1988) found that PHA-stimulated lymphocytes from all patients with SLE contained an excess low-molecular-weight DNA (LMW-DNA) fraction, which was not found in control lymphocytes. This fraction was independent of disease activity and drug therapy. In a 4-generation family in which 2 first cousins had SLE, excess LMW-DNA was present in both affected cousins, in their sib parents, in a common grandparent, in 4 of 9 sibs of one patient, and in 5 of 7 at-risk children. Both sexes were affected, and male-to-male transmission occurred, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance of the trait. All unaffected adult carriers except the grandmother had positive antinuclear antibodies, whereas none of the 5 children with the marker did. The authors suggested that excess LMW-DNA precedes the appearance of ANAs and may predispose to the immunologic responses of SLE.

Sthoeger et al. (2009) measured defensins in sera from patients with SLE and from healthy controls. Human beta-defensin-2 could not be detected by ELISA, and its mRNA levels in patients were low and similar to those in controls. By contrast, the mean alpha-defensin level in the sera of all SLE patients (11.07 +/- 13.92 ng/microl) was significantly higher than in controls (0.12 +/- 0.07 ng/microl). Sixty percent of patients had very high serum levels (18.5 +/- 13.36 ng/microl), and 50% showed elevated gene expression in polymorphonuclear cells. High alpha-defensin levels correlated with disease activity but not with neutrophil count.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) found that, among adults aged 20 to 50 years, C4 and C3 protein levels in cerebrospinal fluid and plasma were higher in men than in women. They suggested that this sex difference in complement levels may help explain the greater risk of SLE in women.

### Inheritance

Pollak (1964) studied antinuclear antibodies in families of patients with SLE, and Leonhardt (1964) carried out family studies in SLE. Siegel et al. (1965) performed a comparative family study of rheumatoid arthritis and SLE. Larsen (1972) reported family studies of a proband series from central eastern Norway, and First (1973) described familial SLE.

Block et al. (1975) studied 12 twin pairs (7 definitely monozygotic, 3 definitely dizygotic) in which one or both twins had SLE. Four of the 7 (57%) monozygotic pairs were clinically concordant, and concordance for antinuclear factor and hypergammaglobulinemia was 71% and 87%, respectively. All 3 dizygotic pairs were discordant, although one clinically well twin had marked serologic abnormalities. The authors concluded that there is a strong genetic component in the pathogenesis of SLE. Yocum et al. (1975) studied monozygotic twins discordant for SLE. Both twins had biologically false-positive serology at an early age. The clinically unaffected twin had been castrated at an early age, suggesting that ovarian hormones may play an important role in the development of SLE.

Arnett and Shulman (1976) studied 8 families with familial SLE and analyzed 53 cases from 25 families, including literature cases. There was impressive concordance for disease expression between identical twins and between parents and offspring, but not between sibs. Familial and nonfamilial SLE did not differ in clinical and laboratory features. The finding of 4 father-offspring pairs made perinatal transmission less likely. In sibs, the average difference in age at onset was 9 years, but the average difference in actual date of onset was only 3 years; the corresponding figures for parents and offspring were 20 and 8 years. These data suggested environmental influences in the initiation of SLE.

Lahita et al. (1983) presented several families in which SLE predominated in males, mainly in sons and fathers, and suggested that the sons had inherited the disease from their fathers. Females in some of these families had other autoimmune diseases, such as idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura. The authors noted some similarity to the disease in BXSB mice.

Talal (1979) discussed SLE, autoimmunity, sex, and inheritance, and Hughes and Batchelor (1983) discussed the genetics of SLE. The genetics of SLE was reviewed by Schur (1995), and the genetic analysis of autoimmune disease by Vyse and Todd (1996). Kotzin (1996) reviewed SLE.

Shai et al. (1999) cited a familial aggregation (lambda-s) of 20 for SLE. Gray-McGuire et al. (2000) noted a 2 to 3% sib recurrence rate and monozygotic twin concordance greater than 20%. Kelly et al. (2002) cited lambda-s estimates of 10 to 20 and a monozygotic twin concordance rate greater than 20%. The SLEGEN consortium (International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al., 2008) estimated a concordance rate of about 30% in monozygotic twins, 10 times that in dizygotic twins (about 3%), and a sib relative risk ratio (lambda-s) of 29.

### Mapping

Fronek et al. (1986) found a similar distribution of restriction fragment length polymorphisms of the T-cell receptor beta chain in SLE patients, their relatives, and normal controls. Wong et al. (1988) studied 5 families with multiple affected members, 14 unrelated SLE patients, and 14 normal controls. They found no cosegregation of the T-cell receptor alpha, beta, or gamma chain genes with SLE.

Gaffney et al. (1998) performed a genomewide microsatellite screen in 105 SLE sib-pair families. The strongest evidence for linkage was near the HLA locus at 6p11-p21 (D6S257, lod = 3.90) and at 16q13 (D16S415, lod = 3.64), 14q21-q23 (D14S276, lod = 2.81), and 20p12 (D20S186, lod = 2.62). Nine other regions had lod scores of 1.00 or greater.

Shai et al. (1999) screened 188 patients from 80 families with 2 or more affected relatives using 350 markers. They found evidence for predisposing loci on chromosomes 1 and 18 but no single locus with overwhelming evidence of linkage. Support for loci at 1q44 and 1p36 was found only in Mexican American families and not in Caucasian families.

Lindqvist et al. (2000) performed genome scans in multiplex SLE families from Iceland and Sweden. In the Icelandic families, lod scores greater than 2.0 were found at 4p15-p13 (Z = 3.20), 9p22 (Z = 2.27), 19q13 (Z = 2.06), 19p13 (Z = 2.58), and 2q37 (Z = 2.06). The first 3 regions are homologous to the murine regions containing the lmb2, sle2, and sle3 loci. In the Swedish families, scores above 2.0 were found at 2q11 (Z = 2.13) and 2q37 (Z = 2.18). Combining both family sets gave a lod score of 4.24 at D2S125 on 2q37, which the authors designated hSLE1.

Gaffney et al. (2000) reported a second genome screen in 82 sib-pair families. The best intervals were 7p22 (lod = 2.87), 7q21 (2.40), 10p13 (2.24), and 7q36 (2.15). In a combined analysis of 187 sib-pair families, markers at 6p11-p21 (D6S426, lod = 4.19) and 16q13 (D16S415, lod = 3.85) met criteria for significant linkage.

Gray-McGuire et al. (2000) scanned 126 pedigrees multiplex for SLE and identified linkage to 4p16-p15.2 (lod = 3.84). They also found evidence for an epistatic interaction between 4p16-p15.2 and 5p15 in European American families. The 4p16-p15.2 linkage was confirmed in an independent collection of European American pedigrees.

Quintero-Del-Rio et al. (2002) stratified pedigrees by the ACR renal criterion. In European American pedigrees, they found linkage at 10q22.3 (screening lod = 3.16; sib-pair P = 0.0000008), which they designated SLEN1. In African American pedigrees, they found linkage at 2q34-q35 (P = 0.000001; SLEN2). In 35 pedigrees with 2 or more patients with renal disease, they obtained a lod score of 3.34 at 11p15.6 in African Americans (SLEN3).

Kelly et al. (2002) reviewed 60 SLE susceptibility loci identified in linkage studies. Seven met the threshold for significant linkage: 1q22-q23, 1q41, 2q37, 4p16, 6p21-p11, 16q13, and 17p13. These linkages usually dominated in one ethnicity or another.

Johanneson et al. (2002) genotyped 87 multicase families from European and admixed American populations for 62 chromosome 1 markers. They identified 6 regions with lod scores of 1.50 or greater (1p36, 1p21, 1q23, 1q25, 1q31, and 1q43). The locus at 1q31 gave a significant 3-point lod score of 3.79, with contributions from families of all populations. Analysis of a known mutation in the CD45 gene did not support a role for this mutation in disease.

Scofield et al. (2003) selected 38 pedigrees that included an SLE patient with thrombocytopenia from 184 multiplex SLE pedigrees. They established linkage at 1q22-q23 (lod = 3.71) in these 38 pedigrees and at 11p13 (lod = 5.72) in the 13 African American pedigrees.

Xing et al. (2005) performed a genome scan in 64 multiplex families with at least 1 affected male. They found the largest signal at 13q32 (lod = 3.08) in 18 African American families. Model-based analysis gave a lod score of 3.13 under a dominant model with a disease allele frequency of 0.0004 and penetrance of 0.5. Fine mapping localized the locus to a region tightly linked to D13S892.

Lee and Nath (2005) performed a genome scan meta-analysis of 12 genome scans from 9 studies, comprising 605 families with 1,355 affected individuals. Regions at 6p22.3-p21.1, which contains the HLA region, and 16p12.3-q12.2 met genome-wide significance. They also identified novel regions at 4q32.1-q34.3 and 13q13.2-q22.2, as well as a possible ethnicity-specific linkage at 3q22.1-q25.33.

### Heterogeneity

In a genome-wide association study (GWAS) in a Chinese Han population, Han et al. (2009) compared their findings with earlier GWAS and highlighted genetic heterogeneity of SLE susceptibility between Chinese Han and European populations.

### Molecular Genetics

Complement and HLA: Fielder et al. (1983) studied the families of 29 patients with SLE and 42 normal subjects. They found null alleles for C4A, C4B, or C2 in 24 of 29 (83%) patients, compared with 18 of 42 (43%) controls. HLA-DR3 was present in 20 (69%) patients and in 7 of 39 (18%) controls, and there was strong linkage disequilibrium between DR3 and the C4A and C4B null alleles. Analyzing these data, Green et al. (1986) suggested that the association with C4 null alleles is primary and the DR3 association secondary.

Reveille et al. (1983) examined 8 families with 22 SLE patients and 40 relatives. Heterozygous C2 deficiency, found in 1 kindred on an A25, B18, DR7 haplotype, was inherited by only 1 of 3 affected family members. DR2 and DR3 each occurred in 36% of patients, which did not differ significantly from non-SLE relatives or local controls. Sib-pair analysis showed only random distribution with SLE. The authors concluded that genetic factors other than HLA and complement deficiencies, and/or environmental factors, are necessary for expression of SLE in these families.

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found homozygous C4A deficiency in 13 of 80 (16%) SLE patients, representing all diagnosed cases from a defined population in southern Sweden.

Graham et al. (2002) used a dense microsatellite map across the HLA region in families with SLE. They identified 3 class II haplotypes with transmission distortion, containing DRB1*1501/DQB1*0602, DRB1*0801/DQB1*0402, and DRB1*0301/DQB1*0201, respectively. Ancestral recombinants narrowed the DRB1*1501 and DRB1*0801 haplotypes to an approximately 500-kb region.

Yang et al. (2007) studied C4 gene copy number in 1,241 European Americans. Gene copy number ranged from 2 to 6 for total C4, from 0 to 5 for C4A, and from 0 to 4 for C4B. Only 2 copies of total C4 was a risk factor (patients 9.3%, unrelated controls 1.5%; OR = 6.514; P = 0.00002), whereas 5 or more copies was protective (5.79% vs 12%; OR = 0.466). Zero copies (OR = 5.267) and 1 copy (OR = 1.613) of C4A were risk factors, and 3 or more copies of C4A appeared protective (OR = 0.574). A haplotype with a single short C4B gene, in tight linkage disequilibrium with the TNFA -308A allele, was preferentially transmitted to patients.

In a Japanese case-control study, Kamatani et al. (2008) found that SNP rs3130342 in the 5-prime flanking region of TNXB was associated with SLE (P = 0.000000930, OR = 3.11). The association was replicated in 203 cases and 294 controls (P = 0.0440, OR = 1.52). The C4 copy number association was weaker and likely reflected linkage disequilibrium with this SNP, and the TNXB association was independent of HLA-DRB1*1501.

FASLG: Wu et al. (1996) used SSCP analysis to screen the FASLG extracellular domain in 75 patients with SLE. In 1 patient with lymphadenopathy they found a heterozygous 84-bp deletion within exon 4, predicting a 28-amino acid in-frame deletion. The patient's PBMCs showed decreased FasL activity, decreased activation-induced cell death, and increased T-cell proliferation after activation. The authors suggested that FASLG mutations are an uncommon cause of SLE.

IL6: Linker-Israeli et al. (1999) found that the distribution of AT-rich minisatellite alleles in the 3-prime flanking region of IL6 differed significantly between 146 SLE patients and 139 controls, in both Caucasians and African Americans. Short alleles (792 bp or less) were seen only in SLE patients, and the 828-bp allele was overrepresented in controls. B lymphoblastoid cells from patients with SLE-associated alleles secreted 3- to 4-fold more IL-6.

DNASE1: Yasutomo et al. (2001) described 2 patients with SLE who had a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2 of the DNASE1 gene, decreased DNASE1 activity, and an extremely high IgG titer against nucleosomal antigens. Shin et al. (2004) did not find this exon 2 mutation in 350 Korean SLE patients or 330 controls. Combined with previous reports, they took this as strong evidence that the mutation is not present in Caucasian, African, or Asian populations. Shin et al. (2004) also found no association of DNASE1 polymorphisms with SLE risk. However, the nonsynonymous SNP +2373A-G (gln244-to-arg) was associated with anti-RNP and anti-dsDNA antibody production among patients: the Arg/Arg genotype occurred in 31.3% of anti-RNP-positive patients versus 14.4% of anti-RNP-negative patients (P = 0.0006, OR = 2.86).

FCGR2B: In 193 Japanese SLE patients and 303 healthy controls, Kyogoku et al. (2002) found that the 232T/T genotype of an FCGR2B polymorphism (c.695T-C; ile232-to-thr, I232T) in the transmembrane domain was significantly increased in patients. FCGR3A-176F/F was also associated. The previously reported FCGR3B association appeared secondary to linkage disequilibrium with FCGR2B. Floto et al. (2005) showed that FcgammaRIIb T232 is functionally impaired: it is excluded from sphingolipid rafts and cannot inhibit activatory receptors.

Su et al. (2004) identified 10 novel SNPs in the FCGR2B promoter and 2 functionally distinct proximal promoter haplotypes. The less frequent haplotype increased reporter gene expression in B lymphoid and myeloid cell lines. In a case-control study of 600 Caucasians, it was associated with SLE (OR = 1.65; P = 0.0054), and the association persisted after adjustment for FCGR2A and FCGR3A (OR = 1.72; P = 0.0083). Blank et al. (2005) found that homozygosity for the FCGR2B promoter polymorphism -343G-C (rs3219018) was associated with SLE in European Americans (OR = 11.1, P = 0.003). The C allele decreased promoter transcription and AP1 binding, and surface FcgammaRIIb was reduced on activated B cells from -343 C/C patients.

PDCD1: Within the SLEB2 susceptibility locus previously reported in Nordic multicase families, Prokunina et al. (2002) analyzed 2,510 individuals for SNPs in PDCD1. One intronic SNP was associated with SLE in Europeans (12% of patients vs 5% of controls; P = 0.00001; relative risk 2.6) and in Mexicans (7% vs 2%; P = 0.0009; relative risk 3.5). The associated allele alters a RUNX1 binding site in an intronic enhancer.

CRP: Russell et al. (2004) studied CRP and APCS polymorphisms in 586 UK simplex SLE families. The less common A allele of SNP CRP 4 (rs1205), in the 3-prime flanking region of CRP, was associated with SLE (P = 0.006 in all families; P = 0.0008 in 394 trios) and with antinuclear autoantibody production. CRP 4 and the synonymous SNP CRP 2 independently influenced basal CRP levels. Inheritance of the minor alleles at both SNPs predicted a basal CRP of 0.9 mg/l, compared with 3.4 mg/l for the common alleles. The authors suggested that reduced basal CRP expression predisposes to SLE.

LTK: Li et al. (2004) found that SLE patients had a gain-of-function LTK polymorphism, similar to one in the kinase domain of NZB mice, at a significantly higher frequency than healthy controls.

CTLA4: Barreto et al. (2004) found a significant association of a 3-prime UTR dinucleotide repeat in CTLA4 with SLE, but no association with the exon 1 +49 SNP in their population. In a meta-analysis of 8 studies, they found increased risk for carriers of the G allele and decreased risk for carriers of the A allele. In a meta-analysis of 14 studies, Lee et al. (2005) found the exon 1 +49 GG genotype to be associated with SLE (OR = 1.287). The association was significant in Asians (OR = 1.293) but not in European-derived populations (OR = 1.268).

PTPN22: Kyogoku et al. (2004) genotyped 525 North American white SLE patients and 1,961 white controls for the PTPN22 R620W polymorphism. The T allele (W620) was associated with SLE (genotypic P = 0.00009), with frequencies of 12.67% in cases and 8.64% in controls. One copy increased risk (OR = 1.37), and 2 copies more than doubled it (OR = 4.37).

IRF5 and TYK2: Sigurdsson et al. (2005) analyzed 44 SNPs in 13 type I interferon pathway genes in 679 Swedish, Finnish, and Icelandic SLE patients, 798 unaffected family members, and 438 controls. SNPs in TYK2 and IRF5 showed strong signals of joint linkage and association (unadjusted P less than 10(-7)). Graham et al. (2006) replicated the association of the IRF5 rs2004640 T allele with SLE in 4 case-control cohorts (P = 4.4 x 10(-16)) and by TDT (P = 0.0006). The T allele creates a 5-prime donor splice site in an alternate exon 1 that allows expression of several unique IRF5 isoforms. An independent cis-acting variant was associated with elevated IRF5 expression. Haplotypes carrying the elevated-expression variant but lacking the exon 1B donor site did not confer risk.

TNF: In a meta-analysis of 21 studies, Lee et al. (2006) found that the TNF promoter -308 A/A genotype was associated with SLE (OR = 3.2), particularly in European-derived populations (OR = 4.0). No association was found in Asian-derived populations.

FCGR3B: Aitman et al. (2006) found that low FCGR3B copy number was associated with glomerulonephritis in SLE. Fanciulli et al. (2007) confirmed this association in 161 UK SLE patients with glomerulonephritis and 312 controls (P = 1.4 x 10(-8); OR = 2.43 for fewer than 2 copies). They also found an association in 375 SLE patients without known renal involvement (P = 1.4 x 10(-5); OR = 2.21), and in all 536 SLE cases together (P = 2.7 x 10(-8)). Zero copies of FCGR3B were observed in 25 of 1,279 individuals with autoimmunity, including 21 with SLE, compared with 1 of 862 controls. There was no association with Graves disease or Addison disease.

Willcocks et al. (2008) confirmed the association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE in 171 Caucasian patients and 176 controls (P = 0.027). They did not find it in a Chinese cohort from Hong Kong (159 cases, 150 controls). They studied a family whose proband had SLE and complete FCGR3B deficiency, with Mendelian inheritance of the null allele. Across this family and in the normal population, FCGR3B copy number correlated with neutrophil surface FcgammaRIIIb expression, neutrophil adherence to and uptake of immune complexes, and soluble FcgammaRIIIb. In contrast, ANCA-associated systemic vasculitis was associated with high FCGR3B copy number.

TREX1: Lee-Kirsch et al. (2007) found monoallelic frameshift or missense mutations, and one 3-prime UTR variant, of TREX1 in 9 of 417 individuals with SLE. These changes were absent in 1,712 controls (P = 4.1 x 10(-7)). Two mutant TREX1 alleles altered subcellular targeting.

CR2: Wu et al. (2007) found evidence of linkage at 1q32.2 (nonparametric linkage score 2.52, P = 0.006) in 126 lupus multiplex families with 151 affected sib pairs. In 1,416 individuals from 258 Caucasian and 142 Chinese simplex families, a common 3-SNP CR2 haplotype (rs3813946, rs1048971, rs17615) was associated with SLE (P = 0.00001), with a 1.54-fold increased risk. SNP rs3813946, in the 5-prime UTR, altered transcriptional activity.

STAT4: Remmers et al. (2007) found that a SNP haplotype in the third intron of STAT4, marked by rs7574865, was strongly associated with SLE in 1,039 patients and 1,248 controls from 3 series. The haplotype was present on 31% of patient chromosomes and 22% of control chromosomes (P = 1.87 x 10(-9); OR = 1.55). Homozygosity for the risk allele more than doubled the risk of lupus. The same haplotype was associated with rheumatoid arthritis.

TNFSF4: Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008) studied UK SLE trios and replicated their findings in 263 Minnesota trios and in a UK case-control set of 424 cases and 642 controls. They showed that the region upstream of TNFSF4 contains a single risk haplotype for SLE. In the combined analysis, the strongest associations were rs12039904 (P = 1.91 x 10(-6)), which tags the overtransmitted haplotype, and rs844644 (P = 6.08 x 10(-7)), which tags an undertransmitted haplotype. Lymphoblastoid cells homozygous for the risk haplotype had a 6.7-fold increase in TNFSF4 transcript and more cells with surface TNFSF4. No association was found for TNFRSF4.

Genomewide association studies: Hom et al. (2008) genotyped more than 500,000 SNPs in 1,311 SLE cases and 1,783 controls of European descent and replicated their findings in 793 cases and 857 controls from Sweden. A variant upstream of BLK and C8orf13 on 8p23.1 (rs13277113; OR = 1.39; P = 1 x 10(-10)) was associated with reduced BLK expression and increased C8orf13 expression. Variants near ITGAM and ITGAX on 16p11.22 were also associated (rs11574637; OR = 1.33; P = 3 x 10(-11)).

The International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al. (2008) performed a genomewide association scan with 317,501 SNPs in 720 women of European ancestry with SLE and 2,337 controls, with replication in 1,846 affected women and 1,825 controls. Besides the HLA region (strongest SNP rs3131379; P = 1.7 x 10(-52); OR = 2.36) and IRF5, they found replicated associations at ITGAM on 16p11.2, KIAA1542 on 11p15.5, PXK on 3p14.3, and rs10798269 on 1q25.1. SNP rs4963128 in KIAA1542 lies 23 kb from IRF7. They also confirmed associations at FCGR2A, PTPN22, and STAT4. The independently associated SNPs explained about 15% of the sib risk ratio.

Kozyrev et al. (2008) identified an association between SLE and a nonsynonymous SNP in BANK1 (rs10516487; arg61-to-his, R61H), replicated in 4 independent case-control sets (combined P = 3.7 x 10(-10); OR = 1.38). A branch point-site SNP in strong linkage disequilibrium, rs17266594, affected expression of a full-length transcript and an alternatively spliced transcript lacking exon 2, which encodes a protein without a putative IP3R-binding domain. A third associated variant was in the ankyrin domain (rs3733197; A383T).

Nath et al. (2008) found an association between ITGAM and SLE in 3,818 individuals of European descent, strongest at the nonsynonymous SNP rs1143679 (P = 1.7 x 10(-17); OR = 1.78). The association was replicated in 2 samples of African descent (overall meta-analysis P = 6.9 x 10(-22)).

In 2 Japanese case-control samples, Oishi et al. (2008) found that SNP rs3748079 in the ITPR3 promoter was associated with SLE (P = 0.0000000178; OR = 1.88). NKX2.5 bound specifically to the nonsusceptible T allele, and an NKX2.5 SNP was also associated with SLE (P = 0.0037; OR = 1.74). Individuals with risk genotypes at both loci had an odds ratio of 5.77.

Graham et al. (2008) performed a genomewide association scan in 431 SLE cases and 2,155 controls with replication in 740 trios. They found an association with rs5029939 in TNFAIP3 on 6q23 (meta-analysis P = 2.89 x 10(-12); OR = 2.29). In 991 trio families, the minor allele of rs6920220, previously associated with rheumatoid arthritis, was also associated with SLE (P = 8.92 x 10(-5)). Conditional and haplotype analyses suggested 2 independent signals near TNFAIP3.

Musone et al. (2008) analyzed 158 SNPs in the TNFAIP3 region on chromosome 6 in 1,239 SLE cases and 1,629 controls of European ancestry, using data from a GWAS. Conditional analysis identified 3 independently associated SNPs: rs13192841, rs2230926, and rs6922466. In allelic tests, rs13192841 had the smallest P value (5.4 x 10(-8); OR 1.4), and rs2230926 had the highest odds ratio (2.0; P = 3.0 x 10(-4)). In multivariate logistic regression, the minor alleles of rs13192841 (OR 0.72) and rs6922466 (OR 0.76) were protective, and the minor allele of rs2230926 increased risk (OR 1.88). rs2230926 encodes a phe127-to-cys (F127C) substitution in the A20 protein. The Cys127 protein was modestly but consistently less effective at inhibiting TNF-induced NF-kappa-B activity.

Orrú et al. (2009) reported that an R263Q substitution within the catalytic domain of the lymphoid tyrosine phosphatase LYP, encoded by PTPN22, reduced phosphatase activity. High-resolution structural analysis revealed the molecular basis for this loss of function. The Q263 variant conferred protection against SLE.

Sánchez et al. (2009) genotyped 9 SNPs in the IL18 gene in Spanish SLE cases and controls. The SNP rs360719 remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Its C allele remained associated in replication cohorts from Italy and Argentina and in meta-analysis (pooled OR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.21-1.54, combined P = 3.8E-07). Individuals carrying the C risk allele had significantly increased relative expression of IL18 mRNA in PBMCs, and the polymorphism created a binding site for the transcription factor OCT-1.

Han et al. (2009) performed a GWAS of SLE in a Chinese Han population, genotyping 1,047 cases and 1,205 controls. They replicated 78 SNPs in 2 additional cohorts totaling 3,152 cases and 7,050 controls. They identified 9 new susceptibility loci: ETS1, IKZF1, RASGRP3, SLC15A4, TNIP1, 7q11.23, 10q11.22, 11q23.3, and 16p11.2. They also confirmed 7 previously reported loci: BLK, IRF5, STAT4, TNFAIP3, TNFSF4, 6q21, and 22q11.21.

Gateva et al. (2009) selected SNPs from 2,466 regions with nominal evidence of association in a GWAS and genotyped them in an independent sample of 1,963 cases and 4,329 controls from the United States and Sweden. They identified 5 new SLE susceptibility loci at genome-wide significance: TNIP1 (rs7708392; OR 1.27), PRDM1 (rs6568431; OR 1.20), JAZF1 (rs849142; OR 1.20), UHRF1BP1 (OR 1.17), and IL10 (rs3024505; OR 1.19). The UHRF1BP1 locus was defined by a nonsynonymous allele, R454Q (rs11755393). Twenty-one additional candidate loci reached P values of 1 x 10(-5) or lower. A screen of alleles previously associated with other autoimmune diseases suggested 5 further loci (P less than 1 x 10(-3)): IFIH1, CFB, CLEC16A, IL12B, and SH2B3. In the combined dataset, previously reported variants at HLA-DRB1, IRF5, TNFAIP3, BLK, STAT4, ITGAM, PTPN22, PHRF1, and TNFSF4 reached genome-wide significance. Together, the 26 SLE risk loci explained an estimated 8% of total genetic susceptibility to SLE.

Myouzen et al. (2010) found that rs10761670, an SNP in the 5-prime flanking region of the EGR2 gene, correlated positively with EGR2 expression. In a case-control study of 3 SLE cohorts genotyped for 14 tag SNPs in the EGR2 region, rs10761670 showed the peak association with SLE (pooled OR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.10-1.37, P = 0.00023). The same SNP was associated with rheumatoid arthritis. Two SNPs in complete linkage disequilibrium with rs10761670, rs1412554 and rs1509957, affected transcription factor binding and transcriptional activity in vitro.

Willcocks et al. (2010) reported that an SNP in FCGR2B that abrogates receptor function was strongly associated with susceptibility to SLE in both Caucasians and Southeast Asians. The minor allele was more common in Southeast Asians and Africans than in Caucasians.

Niederer et al. (2010) used a combined paralogue ratio test to determine FCGR gene copy number (CN). Low FCGR3B CN was associated with SLE in Caucasians (OR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.08-2.27, P = 0.018), and the association was replicated in Hong Kong Chinese (OR = 1.65, 95% CI 1.25-2.18, P = 4 x 10(-4)). FCGR3B CN was not associated with ANCA-associated vasculitis, malaria, or bacterial infection. Analysis of 2 new Caucasian SLE cohorts from the UK and Sweden further supported an association between homozygosity for the FcgammaRIIB-232T allele (I232T, rs1050501) and SLE. Meta-analysis of Caucasian studies gave an OR of 2.26 (95% CI 1.49-3.41). Although FCGR3B CNV and FCGR2B I232T were in linkage disequilibrium, logistic regression showed that each contributed independently to SLE risk.

Boteva et al. (2012) genotyped 2,207 subjects of northern and southern European ancestry (1,028 SLE cases and 1,179 controls) from UK and Spanish populations. They measured total C4, C4A, and C4B gene copy numbers and typed the loss-of-function C4 exon 29 CT indel. Using multiple logistic regression to account for HLA-DRB1 and MHC-region SNP associations, they concluded that genetically determined partial C4 deficiency states were not independent risk factors for SLE in these populations. The C4A exon 29 CT insertion was not associated with SLE in either cohort.

Manjarrez-Orduño et al. (2012) sequenced CSK in 24 SLE patients of European ancestry who were homozygous for a CSK haplotype nominally associated with SLE. They then genotyped 3,769 SLE cases and 3,404 controls of European ancestry and found an association of the A allele of the intronic polymorphism rs34933034 with SLE (OR 1.32, P = 1.04 x 10(-9)). Conditional analysis indicated that associations of other haplotype-tagging SNPs were secondary to rs34933034. In healthy individuals, the risk allele was associated with several B-cell changes: increased CSK expression in naive B cells, increased inhibitory phosphorylation of Lyn at Tyr508, enhanced calcium mobilization after B-cell receptor cross-linking, and higher plasma IgM. In cord blood, newborns homozygous for the risk allele had double the frequency of transitional B cells, owing to an expansion of late transitional cells.

Zhao et al. (2017) studied a strong SLE-associated signal in the GTF2IRD1-GTF2I region at 7q11.23 detected by ImmunoChip genotyping. Using NCF1-specific PCR and Sanger sequencing in 45 Chinese subjects, they identified a missense variant in NCF1, g.74779296G-A, encoding p.Arg90His, as the putative causal variant. The variant was associated with SLE in Asians (OR = 3.47, Pmeta = 3.1 x 10(-104)), European Americans (OR = 2.61), and African Americans (OR = 2.02). It was also associated with primary Sjogren syndrome and rheumatoid arthritis. In addition, 1 copy of NCF1 was associated with increased SLE risk, whereas 3 or more copies were protective.

Farias et al. (2019) performed targeted resequencing of coding and conserved regulatory regions of 215 candidate genes in Swedish SLE patients. They identified a rare variant, rs200395694 G-T, in intron 4 of the MEF2D gene. In 504 SLE patients and 839 healthy controls, the variant was present in 12 heterozygous patients and 6 heterozygous controls (p = 0.014). Among the patients, the variant was associated with a triad of Raynaud phenomenon, anti-RNP antibodies, and anti-Sm antibodies (p = 0.00037). EMSA showed differential protein binding of the 2 alleles, and reporter assays showed allele-specific, cell-type-dependent enhancer activity. In minigene assays, the risk allele inhibited splicing of the alternative alpha-2 isoform of MEF2D.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) imputed C4 alleles from SNP data, using a reference panel built from whole-genome sequence data, in 6,748 SLE cases and 11,516 controls of European ancestry. Individuals with the 11 most common combinations of C4A and C4B gene copy number showed 7-fold variation in relative risk of SLE. Each copy of C4A was protective (OR 0.54), and C4B was more modestly protective (OR 0.77). In an African American cohort (1,494 cases and 5,908 controls), in which C4 alleles showed little linkage disequilibrium with HLA alleles, joint analysis implicated C4A (p less than 10(-14)) and C4B (p less than 10(-5)) but not DRB1*0301 (p = 0.29). C4 alleles had stronger effects in men than in women: common C4A/C4B combinations generated 14-fold variation in SLE risk among men versus 6-fold among women. These findings contrast with the conclusion of Boteva et al. (2012) that partial C4 deficiency states are not independent risk factors for SLE.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found that photosensitivity was more common in SLE patients with homozygous C4A deficiency than in other SLE patients (p less than 0.05). Otherwise, clinical features and autoantibody profiles were similar in the 2 groups.

Stein et al. (2002) analyzed 372 affected individuals from 160 multiplex SLE pedigrees. Renal disease was significantly more frequent in female patients with an affected male relative than in those without (68% vs 43%; P = 0.002). The authors concluded that the higher prevalence of renal disease previously reported in men with SLE is, in large part, familial rather than sex-based. Xing et al. (2005) replicated this finding after adding 392 individuals from 181 new families.

Scofield et al. (2003) found that thrombocytopenia in SLE was associated with nephritis, serositis, neuropsychiatric involvement, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, anti-dsDNA, and antiphospholipid antibody. SLE was more severe in families with a thrombocytopenic patient, consistent with thrombocytopenia being a component of a severe familial form of SLE.

Baechler et al. (2003) found dysregulated expression of interferon-pathway genes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of about half of the SLE patients studied. This signature marked more severe disease involving the kidneys, hematopoietic cells, and/or the central nervous system.

Niederer et al. (2010) found that the association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE in Hong Kong Chinese was strongest in the subgroup of patients with lupus nephritis.

Zhao et al. (2017) found that the NCF1 allele encoding p.Arg90His was dose-dependently associated with earlier age of onset in Korean and European American patients with SLE.

Farias et al. (2019) found that all 3 patients in their clinical cohort who carried the MEF2D rs200395694 variant were positive for anti-Sm antibodies, anti-RNP antibodies, and Raynaud phenomenon, compared with 11 patients (8.5%) overall.

### Pathogenesis

DeHoratius et al. (1975) found anti-RNA antibodies in 82% of 28 SLE probands, 16% of 124 family members, and 5% of 76 control family members. In SLE families, these antibodies were found only in the 94 close household contacts of the probands (21%), and they were present in consanguineous but not nonconsanguineous household contacts. Anti-native DNA antibodies were present in 68% of probands, compared with 6% of household contacts and 1% of controls. The authors concluded that both an environmental agent, possibly a virus, and the genetic response are important in SLE.

Miller and Schwartz (1979) used an in vitro suppressor-cell assay to study 15 patients, 50 clinically healthy family members, and 41 normal persons. Suppressor-cell function was impaired in 11 patients and in 13 first-degree relatives, 12 of whom were women. They proposed that the development of SLE requires at least 2 functionally distinct classes of genes.

Solomon et al. (1983) produced a mouse monoclonal anti-idiotypic antibody, 3I, against anti-native DNA antibodies from an SLE patient. Eight of 9 sera with anti-native DNA activity reacted with 3I, suggesting that anti-native DNA antibodies from unrelated SLE patients share a common idiotype. Halpern et al. (1985) found high-titered 3I reactivity in 6 of 8 SLE patients and 15 of 19 family members from 3 kindreds with familial SLE. In contrast, anti-DNA antibodies were found in 4 of 8 patients and 2 of 19 family members, indicating that the idiotype usually occurs in relatives without anti-DNA activity.

Diamond and Scharff (1984) showed that a somatic mutant of the T15 antiphosphocholine antibody, in which alanine replaced glutamic acid at residue 35 of the heavy chain, lost phosphocholine binding. The mutant antibody acquired reactivity with double-stranded DNA, protamine, and cardiolipin, resembling the autoantibodies seen in autoimmune disease.

Sakane et al. (1989) studied 34 clinically healthy family members of 6 SLE probands. Impaired IL-2 activity was found in 15 of 29 consanguineous relatives and in none of 5 nonconsanguineous household members. Abnormal B-cell assays were found in 22 of 29 consanguineous relatives and in 4 of 5 nonconsanguineous household members. The authors concluded that impaired IL-2 activity correlates with genetic relationship, whereas environmental effects may also contribute to the B-cell abnormalities.

Napirei et al. (2000) found serum DNASE1 activity to be lower in SLE patients than in normal subjects. Zhang et al. (2001) found significantly elevated B lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) in sera of SLE patients. This BLyS could stimulate B-cell activation in vitro, and its elevation was partially associated with higher anti-dsDNA levels but not with disease activity.

Solomou et al. (2001) showed that nuclear extracts from SLE T cells contained increased phosphorylated CREM bound to the -180 site of the IL2 promoter. Increased p-CREM correlated with decreased IL-2 production, indicating that transcriptional repression is responsible for the decreased IL-2 production by SLE T cells.

Blanco et al. (2001) found that serum from SLE patients induced normal monocytes to differentiate into dendritic cells capable of capturing antigens from dying cells. This capacity correlated with disease activity and depended on interferon-alpha.

DeGiorgio et al. (2001) showed that a subset of lupus anti-DNA antibodies cross-reacts with the NR2a and NR2b subunits of the NMDA receptor and mediates apoptotic death of neurons. The cerebrospinal fluid of an SLE patient contained such antibodies and mediated neuronal death. Kowal et al. (2006) showed that serum from lupus patients with reactivity to DNA and the NMDA receptor elicited hippocampal neuron damage and memory impairment in mice whose blood-brain barrier had been compromised. IgG eluted from a patient's brain bound DNA and the NMDA receptor and caused neuronal apoptosis. Brains of 4 other patients with neuropsychiatric lupus showed endogenous IgG colocalizing with anti-NMDA receptor antibodies.

Leadbetter et al. (2002) showed that effective activation of rheumatoid factor-positive B cells by IgG2a-chromatin immune complexes requires synergistic engagement of the antigen receptor and a MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor; inhibitor studies implicated TLR9.

Xu et al. (2004) showed that activated T cells of lupus patients resist anergy and apoptosis by upregulating and sustaining COX-2 expression. COX-2 inhibition caused apoptosis of these cells by augmenting Fas signaling and decreasing c-FLIP. They noted that the COX-2 gene lies in a lupus-susceptibility region on chromosome 1.

Tao et al. (2005) found that NK T cells from patients with active SLE were highly sensitive to anti-CD95-induced apoptosis. They attributed this to deficient expression of CD226 and survivin.

Balada et al. (2008) found a significantly lower deoxymethylcytosine content in CD4+ T cells from 29 SLE patients than in 30 controls. Transcript levels of DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B did not differ between patients and controls.

Guiducci et al. (2010) found that the interferon (IFN) signature in the blood of SLE patients was not affected by oral glucocorticoid treatment. Intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy normalized the signature, which correlated with a reduction in blood plasmacytoid dendritic cells (PDCs); this effect was transient, with the signature returning to prepulse levels by day 8. Stimulation of PDCs through TLR7 and TLR9 by nucleic acid-containing immune complexes or synthetic ligands activated the NF-kappa-B pathway and protected PDCs from glucocorticoid-induced death. Glucocorticoids did not affect NF-kappa-B activation in PDCs. A bifunctional TLR7 and TLR9 inhibitor restored glucocorticoid sensitivity of PDCs in vitro.

Mueller et al. (2013) mapped allelic and paralogous sequence variation across the FCGR2/FCGR3 locus. They identified 2 highly paralogous 24.5-kb blocks that define the limits of the regions in which nonallelic homologous recombination leads to FCGR2C/FCGR3B copy number variation. Individuals with FCGR3B-deleted alleles showed ectopic presence of Fc-gamma-RIIb on natural killer cells. The authors concluded that FCGR3B deletion juxtaposes the 5-prime regulatory sequences of FCGR2C with the coding sequence of FCGR2B, creating a chimeric gene. They proposed this as an explanation for the SLE risk associated with reduced FCGR3B copy number.

Kshirsagar et al. (2014) found enhanced Stat3 activity in CD4+CD45RA-Foxp3- and Foxp3(low) effector T cells from children with lupus nephritis, and this correlated with increased frequencies of IL17-producing cells. RORC and IL17 mRNA levels were significantly higher in PBMCs from these children than in controls. mTOR inhibition by rapamycin reduced both Stat3 activation and the frequency of IL17-producing T cells. Th17 cells from children with lupus nephritis had high Akt activity and enhanced migratory capacity, and inhibition of Akt signaling significantly decreased their migration.

Bialas et al. (2017) observed increased type I IFN signaling in postmortem hippocampal brain sections from patients with SLE.

### Diagnosis

Tan et al. (1982) published the 1982 revised criteria for the classification of SLE. These incorporated fluorescence antinuclear antibody and antibodies to native DNA and Sm antigen, and omitted Raynaud phenomenon and alopecia because of low sensitivity and specificity. The revised criteria were 96% sensitive and 96% specific.

### Clinical Management

Guiducci et al. (2010) found that blocking TLR7 and TLR9 enhanced the glucocorticoid sensitivity of PDCs in vitro and in lupus-prone mice. They suggested that inhibitors of TLR7 and TLR9 signaling could be effective corticosteroid-sparing drugs in SLE.

### Population Genetics

Siegel et al. (1970) compared epidemiologic data on SLE for New York City and Jefferson County, Alabama, for 1956 to 1965. Fessel (1974) studied the incidence, prevalence, and outcome of SLE in the community and noted a high prevalence in black women.

Serdula and Rhoads (1979) ascertained 168 SLE cases, 107 of them definite, in civilian general hospitals on Oahu, Hawaii, for 1970 to 1975. Age-adjusted prevalence rates per 100,000 for definite cases were 5.8 in whites, 24.1 in Chinese, 19.9 in Filipinos, 20.4 in part-Hawaiians, and 18.2 in Japanese. Females accounted for an average of 90% of definite cases.

Prokunina et al. (2002) noted that SLE affects 0.05% of the Western population, predominantly women. The SLEGEN consortium (International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al., 2008) cited an estimated prevalence of 31 per 100,000 women in populations of European ancestry, 50 to 75% lower than in other populations. Graham et al. (2008) cited prevalence estimates of 40 to 400 per 100,000, with higher rates and more severe complications in persons of Hispanic or African ancestry.

Molokhia et al. (2003) studied 124 SLE cases and 219 matched controls in Trinidad. In the admixture analysis, restricted to 52 cases and 107 controls without Indian or Chinese ancestry, mean west African admixture was 0.81 in cases and 0.74 in controls (P = 0.01). The risk ratio associated with unit change in admixture was 32.5, and adjustment for socioeconomic status changed it only slightly. These results supported an additive genetic model for the ethnic difference in SLE risk.

Willcocks et al. (2010) found that homozygosity for the minor allele of a function-abrogating FCGR2B SNP was associated with substantial protection against severe malaria in an East African population (odds ratio = 0.56; P = 7.1 x 10(-5)). They suggested that this protective effect may contribute to the higher frequency of the SNP, and hence of SLE, in Africans and Southeast Asians.

Niederer et al. (2010) found significant differences in FCGR3B and FCGR3A copy number profiles among Caucasian, East Asian, and Kenyan populations. Low FCGR3B copy number (0 or 1 copies) was least common in Caucasians, intermediate in Chinese and Vietnamese, and highest in Kenyans.

### Animal Model

Knight and Adams (1978) studied lupus nephritis in (NZB x NZW) backcross mice. They concluded that the NZB strain contributes 1 gene and the NZW strain 2 genes, or clusters of closely linked genes, to the renal disorder of the F1 hybrid. One NZW gene was linked to the H-2 complex, and all 3 genes were dominant or codominant.

Steinberg et al. (1984) reviewed insights from animal models. They noted that most autoantibody-producing B cells can be eliminated from lupus mice by the xid gene and that an arachidonic acid analog is an effective treatment for murine lupus. Theofilopoulos and Dixon (1985) reviewed murine models of SLE.

In a backcross of the NZM/Aeg2410 strain with C57BL/6, Morel et al. (1994) identified 3 intervals on chromosomes 1, 4, and 7 containing strong recessive glomerulonephritis susceptibility alleles. Heterozygosity at H-2 also correlated strongly with susceptibility. Vyse et al. (1996) found that, in (NZB x NZW)F1 x NZW backcross mice, loci linked to anti-gp70 autoantibodies showed the strongest linkage with renal disease. A distal chromosome 4 locus, Nba1, was linked with nephritis but not with any autoantibody measured. Mohan et al. (1999) showed that B6 mice bicongenic for Sle1 and Sle3 developed splenomegaly, activated B and CD4+ T cells, a variegated IgG autoantibody response, and highly penetrant glomerulonephritis, particularly in females. This supported a 2-step epistatic model.

Gross et al. (2000) found that transgenic mice overexpressing zTNF4 (BLyS) in lymphoid cells developed symptoms characteristic of SLE. Circulating zTNF4 was more abundant in NZBWF1 and MRL-lpr/lpr mice during disease. Treatment of NZBWF1 mice with soluble TACI-Ig inhibited proteinuria and prolonged survival.

Napirei et al. (2000) found that Dnase1-deficient mice showed antinuclear antibodies, deposition of immune complexes in glomeruli, and full-blown glomerulonephritis in a Dnase1 dose-dependent manner.

Sun et al. (2002) showed that treatment with an agonistic monoclonal antibody to CD137 blocked lymphadenopathy and spontaneous autoimmune disease in Fas-deficient MRL/lpr mice and prolonged survival. Barber et al. (2005) found that inhibition of PI3K-gamma reduced glomerulonephritis and prolonged lifespan in MRL-lpr mice.

Nakamura et al. (2003) found that a region including interferon-alpha on NZB chromosome 4 was linked to anxiety-like behavior in (NZB x NZW)F1 mice, which had elevated neuronal interferon-alpha. Li et al. (2004) identified a gain-of-function polymorphism in the Ltk kinase domain of NZB mice as a candidate for aberrant B1-cell activation.

Tournoy et al. (2004) found that most PS1+/- PS2-/- mice developed an autoimmune disease from about 6 months of age. The disease was characterized by dermatitis, glomerulonephritis, keratitis, and vasculitis, with hypergammaglobulinemia, immune complex deposits, and high-titer nuclear autoantibodies.

McGaha et al. (2005) showed that partial restoration of FcgammaRIIB levels on B cells in lupus-prone mouse strains was sufficient to restore tolerance and prevent autoimmunity.

Huerta et al. (2006) found that mice immunized with a DNA peptide mimetope and given epinephrine to open the blood-brain barrier lost neurons in the lateral amygdala. These mice developed a deficient response to fear conditioning.

Aitman et al. (2006) identified loss of the rat-specific Fcgr3 paralog Fcgr3-rs as a determinant of macrophage overactivity and glomerulonephritis in Wistar Kyoto rats.

Guiducci et al. (2010) found that PDCs, conventional dendritic cells, and B cells were significantly more resistant to glucocorticoid-induced death in the lupus-prone mouse strains (NZB x NZW)F1 and TLR7.Tg.6 than in normal strains. Blocking TLR7 and TLR9 in vivo enhanced the glucocorticoid sensitivity of these cells.

Talaei et al. (2015) showed that the promoter region of the NZB mouse gene encoding the SLAM adapter molecule EAT-2 is polymorphic, resulting in an approximately 70% reduction of EAT-2 in dendritic cells. Silencing EAT-2 in dendritic cells lacking this polymorphism increased IL12 production and enhanced Th1 differentiation in T cell-dendritic cell cocultures. SLAM coengagement blocked CD40-induced activation of p38 MAPK and JNK in dendritic cells, and this block was reversed in dendritic cells carrying the NZB EAT-2 allele. The authors concluded that this polymorphism promotes the development of lupus.

Bialas et al. (2017) reported behavioral phenotypes and synapse loss in lupus-prone mice that were prevented by blocking type I IFN signaling. Type I IFN stimulated microglia to become reactive and engulf neuronal and synaptic material.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS**  
FASLG, 84-BP DEL, EX4 [84-bp deletion in exon 4; 28-amino acid in-frame deletion]

Wu et al. (1996) screened DNA from 75 patients with SLE by SSCP analysis of the FASLG extracellular domain. In 1 patient with lymphadenopathy, they identified a heterozygous 84-bp deletion within exon 4, predicting a 28-amino acid in-frame deletion. The patient's PBMCs showed decreased FasL activity, decreased activation-induced cell death, and increased T-cell proliferation after activation.

**.0002 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FCGR2B, ILE232THR [c.695T>C; I232T; rs1050501]

Kyogoku et al. (2002) identified a c.695T-C SNP in FCGR2B, causing an ile232-to-thr (I232T) substitution in the transmembrane domain. The 232T/T genotype was significantly more frequent in 193 Japanese SLE patients than in 303 healthy individuals.

Floto et al. (2005) showed that FcgammaRIIb T232 is functionally impaired. It is excluded from sphingolipid rafts and is therefore unable to inhibit activatory receptors.

Willcocks et al. (2010) found that an SNP in FCGR2B that abrogates receptor function was strongly associated with SLE susceptibility in Caucasians and Southeast Asians. Homozygosity for the minor allele was associated with protection against severe malaria in an East African population (odds ratio = 0.56).

Niederer et al. (2010) described this variant as an ile232-to-thr (I232T; rs1050501) substitution in the transmembrane domain of the inhibitory receptor FcgammaRIIB, which disrupts its inhibitory function. In 2 new Caucasian cohorts from the UK and Sweden, they found further evidence that homozygosity for the 232T allele was associated with SLE; meta-analysis of Caucasian studies gave an OR of 2.26. The association was independent of low FCGR3B copy number, despite linkage disequilibrium between the 2 variants.

**.0003 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
PTPN22, ARG620TRP [R620W; rs2476601]

Kyogoku et al. (2004) genotyped 525 North American white SLE patients and 1,961 white controls for the PTPN22 R620W polymorphism. The minor T allele (W620) was associated with SLE (genotypic P = 0.00009), with frequencies of 12.67% in cases and 8.64% in controls. One copy of the allele gave an odds ratio of 1.37, and 2 copies an odds ratio of 4.37.

In their genomewide association data, the SLEGEN consortium (International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al., 2008) replicated the association of PTPN22 rs2476601 with SLE (P = 5.2 x 10(-6); OR = 1.53).

**.0004 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FCGR2B, -343G-C [-343G>C; rs3219018]

Blank et al. (2005) found that homozygosity for a G-to-C substitution at position -343 of the FCGR2B promoter (rs3219018) was associated with SLE in European Americans (OR = 11.1, P = 0.003). The C allele decreased promoter transcription and binding of the AP1 transcription complex. Surface expression of FcgammaRIIb was significantly reduced on activated B cells from -343 C/C SLE patients.

**.0005 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
BANK1, ARG61HIS [R61H; rs10516487]

In a genomewide scan using 85,042 SNPs, Kozyrev et al. (2008) identified an association between SLE and the nonsynonymous BANK1 SNP rs10516487 (R61H). The association was replicated in 4 independent case-control sets (combined P = 3.7 x 10(-10); OR = 1.38). A branch point-site SNP in strong linkage disequilibrium with it, rs17266594, affected expression of a full-length transcript and of an alternatively spliced transcript lacking exon 2.

**.0006 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
TNFAIP3, PHE127CYS [rs2230926; Phe127Cys]

Musone et al. (2008) found that the minor allele of rs2230926, a nonsynonymous SNP in TNFAIP3, was associated with increased risk of SLE in 1,239 cases and 1,629 controls of European ancestry. The OR was 2.0 in allelic testing and 1.88 in multivariate logistic regression. The SNP results in a phe127-to-cys (F127C) substitution in the A20 protein. The Cys127 protein was comparably stable to the Phe127 protein but was modestly and consistently less effective at inhibiting TNF-induced NF-kappa-B activity.

**.0007 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, PROTECTION AGAINST**  
PTPN22, ARG263GLN [R263Q]

Orrú et al. (2009) found that an arg263-to-gln (R263Q) substitution within the catalytic domain of LYP, encoded by PTPN22, reduced phosphatase activity. High-resolution structural analysis revealed the molecular basis for this loss of function. The Q263 variant conferred protection against SLE.

**.0008 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
NCF1, ARG90HIS [g.74779296G>A; p.Arg90His; rs201802880]

Zhao et al. (2017) identified a g.74779296G-A transition in the NCF1 gene, resulting in an arg90-to-his (R90H) substitution, by NCF1-specific PCR and Sanger sequencing in Chinese subjects. They proposed it as the likely causal variant underlying an SLE association signal in the GTF2IRD1-GTF2I region at 7q11.23. The variant was associated with SLE in Chinese and Korean (Asian meta-analysis OR = 3.47, P = 3.1 x 10(-104)), European American (OR = 2.61), and African American (OR = 2.02) cohorts. Conditioning on R90H eliminated the association signals at p.Ser99Gly and in the GTF2IRD1-GTF2I region. Arg90 lies in a phosphoinositide-binding pocket of the PX domain of p47phox. The substitution was predicted to be deleterious, and the authors cited in vitro studies indicating that changing Arg90 to histidine reduces ROS production. The risk allele was dose-dependently associated with earlier age of onset in Korean and European American patients.
