### TEXT

This entry describes systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a genetically complex, multisystem autoimmune disorder in which susceptibility is influenced by multiple genetic loci, including the HLA region and many non-HLA genes, together with hormonal and environmental factors. Rare heterozygous mutations in the DNASE1, TREX1 and FASLG genes have been reported in individual patients with SLE.

### Description

Systemic lupus erythematosus is a multisystem autoimmune inflammatory disease characterized by antinuclear autoantibodies, complement and interferon activation, and tissue destruction (International Consortium for Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Genetics (SLEGEN) et al., 2008). Antibodies are directed against naked DNA and whole nucleosomes, and the resulting immune complexes are thought to accumulate in vessel walls, glomeruli and joints, causing glomerulonephritis, arthritis and general vasculitis (Napirei et al., 2000). SLE predominantly affects women, at a ratio of about 9:1, particularly during the childbearing years. It has strong genetic and environmental components: concordance is about 30% in monozygotic twins and about 3% in dizygotic twins, and the sibling relative risk ratio is high (SLEGEN et al., 2008).

### Clinical Features

Brustein et al. (1977) described a child of a woman with discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) who developed DLE lesions at the age of 2 months. These lesions persisted and new ones were still developing at 28 months. In a second child, a probable lupus erythematosus rash developed at 1 week of age.

Lahita et al. (1983) presented several families in which SLE predominated in males, mainly affecting sons and fathers. Females in some of these families had other autoimmune diseases, such as idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, or symptoms suggestive of a lupus diathesis. The authors suggested that in these families the sons had inherited the disease from their fathers, and they noted that the disease resembled that of BXSB mice.

Stohl et al. (1985) identified 3 black Jamaicans with SLE whose T helper/inducer cells lacked the T4 epitope. All 3 had lymphadenopathy. The asymptomatic, otherwise healthy, T4 epitope-deficient brother of one patient had lymphadenopathy in the same distribution as his sister with SLE. Family studies pointed to autosomal codominant inheritance of the T4 epitope phenotype, not linked to HLA. Cells from T4 epitope-deficient family members showed impaired B-cell differentiation after pokeweed mitogen stimulation.

Sibley et al. (1993) described a family in which 3 members developed SLE complicated by ischemic vasculopathy (digital ischemia). The calendar years of onset, the ages at onset and the clinical courses were remarkably similar in all 3 patients, although their genotypes were not.

García et al. (1996) studied the autoantibody profile of 222 African American patients with SLE, 94 with and 128 without lupus nephritis. Only anti-dsDNA differed significantly between the groups, and the authors concluded that African American patients with lupus nephritis do not have a distinctive autoantibody profile.

Stein et al. (2002) analyzed 372 affected individuals from 160 multiplex SLE pedigrees, 25 of which contained at least 1 affected male. Renal disease was significantly more common in female family members who had an affected male relative than in those who did not (68% vs 43%). Arthritis and dermatologic features appeared to be influenced mostly by sex. The authors concluded that the increased prevalence of renal disease previously reported in men with SLE is largely a familial rather than a sex-based difference, at least in multiplex families.

Scofield et al. (2003) selected 38 pedigrees containing an SLE patient with thrombocytopenia (platelets less than 100,000/microL) from a collection of 184 multiplex SLE pedigrees. Thrombocytopenia was associated with nephritis, serositis, neuropsychiatric involvement, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, anti-dsDNA and antiphospholipid antibody. SLE was more severe in families with a thrombocytopenic patient, and the authors suggested that thrombocytopenia marks a severe familial form of SLE.

Kamat et al. (2003) reported the first identical triplets with SLE. They were diagnosed in reverse birth order at ages 8, 9 and 11 years. Each child had different clinical signs and symptoms, but all 3 had skin rash, fatigue and biopsy-proven glomerulonephritis. All were positive for antinuclear, anti-native DNA and anti-dsDNA antibodies and had low complement levels.

Bialas et al. (2017) noted that up to 75% of patients with SLE have neuropsychiatric symptoms, ranging from anxiety, depression and cognitive impairment to seizures and, rarely, psychosis. CNS lupus typically presents at diagnosis or within the first year.

### Other Features

Drug-induced lupus. Lappat and Cawein (1968) reported a familial study of procainamide-induced SLE that raised the question of a pharmacogenetic polymorphism. Batchelor et al. (1980) studied 26 patients with SLE induced by hydralazine; 25 of the 26 were slow acetylators. HLA-DR4 was present in 73% of these patients, compared with 33% of healthy subjects, 25% of slow-acetylator hypertensive patients treated with hydralazine who did not develop SLE, and 25% of patients with idiopathic SLE. The female-to-male ratio was 4:1. Among slow acetylators treated with hydralazine, all women with DR4 developed hydralazine-induced SLE. The distribution of DR antigens differed significantly between hydralazine-induced and idiopathic SLE, supporting the view that they are separate entities.

Reidenberg et al. (1980) determined acetylator phenotypes in 21 patients from New York City and 29 from Israel with idiopathic SLE: 30 were slow acetylators, 17 rapid and 3 indeterminate. Combined with published studies, the worldwide distribution was 150 slow to 77 rapid, compared with an expected 122:105. In contrast, Baer et al. (1986) found similar frequencies of the slow acetylator phenotype in 64 patients with idiopathic SLE (38%), 60 healthy volunteers (50%) and 52 non-SLE medical patients (44%), and found no evidence for an association.

Lessard et al. (1997) showed in vitro that CYP2D6 is the major human cytochrome P450 isozyme forming N-hydroxyprocainamide, a reactive procainamide metabolite that may be responsible for procainamide-induced lupus. In vivo, Lessard et al. (1999) gave procainamide to 19 healthy individuals: 13 extensive and 6 poor CYP2D6 metabolizers. Urinary nitroprocainamide was measurable in 7 of the 13 extensive metabolizers but in none of the poor metabolizers.

Maternal lupus and chondrodysplasia punctata. Elçioglu and Hall (1998) described 2 sibs with chondrodysplasia punctata whose mother had SLE. Austin-Ward et al. (1998) reported a newborn whose mother had been diagnosed with SLE before pregnancy. The infant had neonatal lupus, chondrodysplasia punctata and other findings resembling the anomalies associated with oral anticoagulants, without any exposure to them. Kelly et al. (1999) reported a male infant with stippled epiphyses on neonatal radiographs who developed a typical lupus rash, which led to the diagnosis of SLE in his mother. Over 3 years of follow-up he had strikingly short stature, midface hypoplasia, anomalous digital development, slow resolution of the stippled epiphyses and near-normal cognitive development.

Kozlowski et al. (2004) reported 2 brothers with chondrodysplasia punctata, born in Cape Town in 1991 and 2000; the younger was more severely affected. Their mother had longstanding disseminated lupus erythematosus and epilepsy and had been treated with chloroquine and other agents during both pregnancies. The parents were nonconsanguineous. The authors counted 7 earlier reports of chondrodysplasia punctata with maternal lupus erythematosus and concluded that maternal lupus is a causative factor.

Lupus anticoagulant. Exner et al. (1980) detected a lupus anticoagulant in 2 pairs of sibs and found strong evidence for it in a third sib pair. They suggested that the lupus anticoagulant may be an acquired coagulation defect requiring a genetic predisposition. Mackie et al. (1987) studied 3 families with more than 1 member affected by SLE or lupus-like disease. Of 19 persons, 11 had SLE or lupus-like disease, and 8 of these 11 had a prolonged KPTT and other evidence of lupus anticoagulant. Four healthy spouses of affected females and 3 asymptomatic sibs also had prolonged, noncorrectable KPTTs. The authors considered this to support a role for transmissible agents or other environmental factors.

In a large pedigree segregating elliptocytosis, multiple lipomatosis and a biologic false-positive serologic test for syphilis (BFPSTS), Weinberg et al. (1980) found that 2 female members with BFPSTS developed SLE.

Schaller (1972) reported an illness resembling lupus erythematosus in mothers of boys with chronic granulomatous disease. Kohler et al. (1974) reported hereditary angioedema with 'familial' lupus erythematosus in identical twin boys.

### Biochemical Features

Levcovitz et al. (1988) found that PHA-stimulated lymphocytes from all patients with SLE made 2 classes of newly synthesized DNA: a large-molecular-weight fraction comigrating with control DNA, and an excess low-molecular-weight DNA (LMW-DNA) fraction not found in control lymphocytes. The excess LMW-DNA was independent of disease activity and drug therapy.

Napirei et al. (2000) found that serum DNASE1 activity was lower in patients with SLE than in normal subjects. Zhang et al. (2001) found significantly elevated B lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) in SLE patients. The elevation was partially associated with higher levels of anti-dsDNA antibodies but not with disease activity.

Balada et al. (2008) found that CD4+ T cells from 29 SLE patients had a significantly lower deoxymethylcytosine content than those from 30 controls. Transcript levels of DNMT1, DNMT3A and DNMT3B did not differ between patients and controls. Sthoeger et al. (2009) found that the mean serum level of alpha-defensin (HNP-1) was significantly higher in SLE patients than in controls and correlated with disease activity.

### Inheritance

Block et al. (1975) studied 12 twin pairs in which 1 or both twins had SLE. Four of 7 definitely monozygotic pairs (57%) were clinically concordant, whereas all 3 dizygotic sets were discordant. Concordance for antinuclear factor was 71% and for hypergammaglobulinemia 87%. Comparison with other first-degree relatives suggested a strong genetic component. Yocum et al. (1975) described monozygotic twins discordant for SLE. Both had biologically false-positive serology at an early age; the unaffected twin had undergone castration at an early age, suggesting a role for ovarian hormones.

Arnett and Shulman (1976) analyzed 53 cases in 25 families, combining their own 8 families with cases from the literature. Concordance of disease expression was impressive between identical twins and between parents and offspring, but not between sibs. Familial SLE did not appear to differ from nonfamilial SLE. The finding of 4 father-offspring pairs made perinatal transmission less likely. Timing of onset suggested environmental influences on initiation, especially in sibs.

Levcovitz et al. (1988) studied a 4-generation family in which 2 first cousins had SLE. Excess lymphocyte LMW-DNA was present in the affected cousins, their sib parents, a common grandparent, 4 of 9 sibs of one patient, and 5 of 7 at-risk children. Both sexes were affected and male-to-male transmission was observed, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance of the trait. All unaffected adults with the marker, except the grandmother, had positive antinuclear antibodies, whereas none of the 5 children with the marker did. The authors suggested that excess LMW-DNA precedes the appearance of antinuclear antibodies.

Shai et al. (1999) cited a sibling recurrence risk ratio (lambda-s) of 20. In their genome screen of multiplex families, no single locus showed overwhelming evidence for linkage, suggesting no 'major' susceptibility genes and instead the action of several genes of moderate effect. Gray-McGuire et al. (2000) cited a sibling recurrence rate of 2 to 3% and monozygotic twin concordance greater than 20%. Kamitaki et al. (2020) stated that SLE risk is 66% heritable.

### Mapping

HLA region. Fielder et al. (1983) studied the families of 29 SLE patients and 42 normal subjects. HLA-DR3 was present in 69% of patients and 18% of controls, and was in strong linkage disequilibrium with null alleles of C4A and C4B. Green et al. (1986) applied the empirical logistic method to these associations. Their analysis suggested that the association with C4 null alleles is primary and that the DR3 association is likely secondary. In contrast, Reveille et al. (1983) studied 8 families with 2 or more members with SLE and found that HLA haplotypes segregated randomly with SLE in sib-pair analysis. They concluded that genetic factors other than HLA and complement deficiencies, and/or environmental factors, are needed for disease expression.

Fronek et al. (1986) and Wong et al. (1988) found no evidence of linkage between SLE and the T-cell receptor genes. Wong et al. (1988) studied 5 multiplex families, 14 unrelated patients and 14 controls, and found that the TCR alpha, beta and gamma genes did not cosegregate with the disease.

Gaffney et al. (1998) performed a genomewide microsatellite screen in 105 SLE sib-pair families. The strongest evidence for linkage was near HLA at 6p11-p21 (lod = 3.90), followed by 16q13 (lod = 3.64), 14q21-q23 (lod = 2.81) and 20p12 (lod = 2.62). In a second cohort of 82 sib-pair families, Gaffney et al. (2000) found the best intervals at 7p22, 7q21, 10p13 and 7q36. In the combined 187 families, markers at 6p11-p21 (D6S426, lod = 4.19) and 16q13 (D16S415, lod = 3.85) met criteria for significant linkage.

Shai et al. (1999) screened 188 lupus patients from 80 multiplex families and found evidence for predisposing loci on chromosomes 1 and 18. Support for loci at 1q44 and 1p36 came only from Mexican American families, not from Caucasian families.

Lindqvist et al. (2000) performed genome scans in multicase families from Iceland and Sweden. In the Icelandic families, regions at 4p15-p13, 9p22, 19q13, 19p13 and 2q37 had lod scores greater than 2.0. Combining both family sets gave a lod score of 4.24 at D2S125, favoring linkage to 2q37. They designated this new locus hSLE1.

Gray-McGuire et al. (2000) scanned 126 multiplex pedigrees and identified linkage to 4p16-p15.2 (lod = 3.84). They also found evidence of epistasis between 4p16-p15.2 and 5p15 in European American families, and confirmed the 4p16-p15.2 linkage in an independent European American pedigree collection.

Quintero-Del-Rio et al. (2002) stratified pedigrees by the ACR renal criterion. In European American pedigrees, sib-pair regression gave P = 0.0000008 at 10q22.3 (SLEN1). In African American pedigrees it gave P = 0.000001 at 2q34-q35 (SLEN2), and a lod score of 3.34 at 11p15.6 (SLEN3) in 35 pedigrees with 2 or more patients with renal disease.

Kelly et al. (2002) reviewed linkage studies in SLE multiplex pedigrees, which had identified 60 susceptibility loci in man. Seven of these met or exceeded the threshold for significant linkage: 1q22-q23, 1q41, 2q37, 4p16, 6p21-p11, 16q13 and 17p13. These linkages usually dominated in one ethnicity or another.

Johanneson et al. (2002) genotyped 87 multicase families from European populations (Iceland, Sweden, England, Norway, Italy and Greece) and admixed populations (Mexico, Colombia and the US) with 62 chromosome 1 markers. The locus at 1q31 gave a significant 3-point lod score of 3.79, with contributions from families of all populations. Analysis of a known mutation in the CD45 gene did not support a role for that mutation in the disease.

Scofield et al. (2003) analyzed 38 pedigrees containing an SLE patient with thrombocytopenia and established linkage at 1q22-q23 (maximum lod = 3.71). In the 13 African American pedigrees, linkage was at 11p13 (maximum lod = 5.72).

Xing et al. (2005) performed a genomewide scan in 64 multiplex families with at least 1 affected male. The largest signal (lod = 3.08) was at 13q32 in 18 African American families with affected males. Identity-by-descent sharing suggested dominant inheritance, and fine mapping localized the locus to a region tightly linked to D13S892.

Lee and Nath (2005) performed a genome scan meta-analysis of 12 genome scans from 9 studies, comprising 605 families with 1,355 affected individuals. Two regions met genomewide significance: 6p22.3-p21.1, which contains HLA, and 16p12.3-q12.2.

In a targeted genome scan of 1q21-q43, Wu et al. (2007) found evidence of linkage at 1q32.2 in 126 multiplex families containing 151 affected sib pairs (nonparametric linkage score 2.52).

### Heterogeneity

SLEGEN et al. (2008) noted that rare monogenic forms of lupus occur with mutations in TREX1 or with complete deficiency of complement C1q, C2 or C4. Boteva et al. (2012) likewise noted that complete C4 deficiency confers a strong genetic risk for SLE, but they found that partial C4 deficiency states were not independent risk factors (see MOLECULAR GENETICS).

### Molecular Genetics

Complement C4. Fielder et al. (1983) found null alleles of C4A, C4B or C2 in 24 of 29 (83%) SLE patients, compared with 18 of 42 (43%) normal controls. The authors suggested that these null alleles are relevant to genetic susceptibility.

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found homozygous C4A deficiency in 13 of 80 (16%) patients with SLE, representing all diagnosed cases from a defined population in southern Sweden.

FASLG. Wu et al. (1996) screened DNA from 75 SLE patients by SSCP analysis of the FASLG extracellular domain. One patient, who had lymphadenopathy, was heterozygous for an 84-bp deletion within exon 4, predicted to cause a 28-amino acid in-frame deletion. The patient's PBMCs showed decreased FasL activity, decreased activation-induced cell death and increased T-cell proliferation after activation. The authors suggested that FASLG mutations are an uncommon cause of SLE.

IL6. Linker-Israeli et al. (1999) found that the distribution of alleles of an AT-rich minisatellite in the 3-prime flanking region of IL6 differed significantly between 146 SLE patients and 139 controls, in both Caucasians and African Americans. Short alleles (792 bp or less) were seen only in patients. B lymphoblastoid cells from patients with SLE-associated alleles secreted 3- to 4-fold more IL6.

DNASE1. Yasutomo et al. (2001) described 2 SLE patients with a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2 of DNASE1. Both had decreased DNASE1 activity and extremely high IgG titers against nucleosomal antigens.

FCGR2B. Kyogoku et al. (2002) identified a c.695T-C SNP in FCGR2B encoding an ile232-to-thr (I232T) substitution in the transmembrane domain. Among 193 Japanese SLE patients and 303 healthy individuals, the 232T/T genotype was significantly more frequent in patients. FCGR3A-176F/F was also associated. Two-locus analysis suggested that both FCGR2B and FCGR3A may contribute, and that the previously reported FCGR3B association was secondary to linkage disequilibrium with FCGR2B.

PDCD1. Within the SLEB2 locus that they had previously reported in Nordic multicase families, Prokunina et al. (2002) analyzed PDCD1 in 2,510 individuals. An intronic SNP was associated with SLE in Europeans (12% of affected individuals vs 5% of controls; relative risk 2.6) and in Mexicans (7% vs 2%; relative risk 3.5). The associated allele alters a RUNX1 binding site in an intronic enhancer.

CRP. Russell et al. (2004) studied the pentraxin genes CRP and APCS at 1q23.2 in 586 UK simplex SLE families. The minor allele of the CRP 4 SNP in the 3-prime region of CRP was associated with SLE, most strongly in 394 trios (143 transmissions vs 92 nontransmissions; P = 0.0008), and with antinuclear antibody production. CRP 4 and CRP 2 independently influenced basal serum CRP levels, and the allele associated with reduced basal CRP was the one associated with SLE.

LTK. In addition to a gain-of-function Ltk polymorphism in NZB mice, Li et al. (2004) found that SLE patients carried a similar LTK polymorphism significantly more often than healthy controls.

CTLA4. Barreto et al. (2004) found an association between SLE and a dinucleotide repeat in the 3-prime UTR of CTLA4, but not the exon 1 +49 SNP. In a meta-analysis of 7 previous studies and their own, carriers of the G allele at +49 had a significantly increased risk. Lee et al. (2005) performed a meta-analysis of 14 studies. The exon 1 +49 GG genotype was significantly associated with SLE (OR = 1.287), at least in Asians; the effect in European-derived populations was of similar magnitude but not significant.

Su et al. (2004) identified 10 novel SNPs in the FCGR2B promoter and 2 functionally distinct proximal promoter haplotypes. The less frequent haplotype increased reporter expression and was associated with SLE in 600 Caucasians (OR = 1.65), independently of FCGR2A and FCGR3A polymorphisms.

PTPN22. Kyogoku et al. (2004) genotyped 525 North American white SLE patients and 1,961 white controls for the PTPN22 R620W polymorphism. The minor (T) allele frequency was 12.67% in cases and 8.64% in controls. One copy of the W620 allele increased risk (OR = 1.37), and 2 copies more than doubled it (OR = 4.37).

Shin et al. (2004) studied DNASE1 polymorphisms in 350 Korean SLE patients and 330 controls and found no association with SLE risk. The exon 2 mutation reported in 2 Japanese patients was absent from all of these patients and controls, and the authors concluded, with earlier reports, that it is not present in Caucasian, African or Asian populations (for the association of DNASE1 gln244-to-arg with autoantibodies, see GENOTYPE/PHENOTYPE CORRELATIONS).

TYK2 and IRF5. Sigurdsson et al. (2005) analyzed 44 SNPs in 13 type I interferon pathway genes in 679 Swedish, Finnish and Icelandic patients, 798 unaffected family members and 438 unrelated controls. SNPs in TYK2 and IRF5 gave strong signals in joint linkage and association analysis (unadjusted P less than 10(-7)).

Blank et al. (2005) found that homozygosity for a G-to-C substitution at position -343 of the FCGR2B promoter (rs3219018) was associated with SLE in European Americans (OR = 11.1). The C allele decreased promoter transcription and AP1 binding, and surface FcgammaRIIb expression was reduced on activated B cells from -343 C/C patients.

Floto et al. (2005) showed that the SLE-associated FcgammaRIIb T232 variant cannot inhibit activatory receptors because it is excluded from sphingolipid rafts.

TNF. Lee et al. (2006) performed a meta-analysis of 21 studies of the TNF -308 A/G promoter polymorphism. The A/A genotype was associated with SLE overall (OR = 3.2) and in European-derived populations (OR = 4.0), but not in Asian-derived populations.

FCGR3B copy number. Aitman et al. (2006) found that low copy number of FCGR3B was associated with glomerulonephritis in SLE.

IRF5. Graham et al. (2006) replicated the association of the IRF5 rs2004640 T allele with SLE in 4 independent case-control cohorts (P = 4.4 x 10(-16)) and by transmission disequilibrium testing. The T allele creates a 5-prime donor splice site in an alternate exon 1, allowing expression of several unique IRF5 isoforms. An independent cis-acting variant was associated with elevated IRF5 expression. Haplotypes carrying the elevated-expression variant but lacking the exon 1B donor site did not confer risk.

CR2. Wu et al. (2007) analyzed 1,416 individuals from 258 Caucasian and 142 Chinese lupus simplex families. A common 3-SNP CR2 haplotype (rs3813946, rs1048971, rs17615) was associated with lupus, with a 1.54-fold increased risk. SNP rs3813946 in the 5-prime UTR altered transcriptional activity.

Yang et al. (2007) studied C4 gene copy number in 1,241 European Americans. Having only 2 copies of total C4 increased SLE risk (9.3% of patients vs 1.5% of controls; OR = 6.514), whereas 5 or more copies were protective. Zero copies and 1 copy of C4A were risk factors, and 3 or more copies of C4A appeared protective.

Fanciulli et al. (2007) confirmed the association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE with glomerulonephritis in 161 UK patients and 312 controls. They also found a new association in 375 unrelated SLE patients without known renal involvement (OR = 2.21 for fewer than 2 copies). Across all 536 SLE cases, P = 2.7 x 10(-8). Zero copies of FCGR3B were found in 25 of 1,279 individuals with autoimmunity, including 21 with SLE, but in only 1 of 862 controls. Low copy number was also associated with Wegener granulomatosis and microscopic polyangiitis, but not with Graves disease or Addison disease.

TREX1. Lee-Kirsch et al. (2007) found monoallelic frameshift or missense mutations, and one 3-prime UTR variant, of TREX1 in 9 of 417 individuals with SLE; none were found in 1,712 controls. Two mutant alleles altered subcellular targeting.

STAT4. Remmers et al. (2007) found that a SNP haplotype in intron 3 of STAT4, marked by rs7574865, was associated with SLE in 1,039 patients and 1,248 controls from 3 series. It was present on 31% of patient chromosomes and 22% of control chromosomes (OR = 1.55). Homozygosity for the risk allele more than doubled the risk of lupus. The same haplotype was associated with rheumatoid arthritis.

TNXB. In a Japanese case-control study (178 patients, 899 controls), Kamatani et al. (2008) found an association between SLE and rs3130342 in the 5-prime flanking region of TNXB (OR = 3.11), which was replicated in 203 cases and 294 controls. The C4 copy number association was weaker and the TNXB association was independent of HLA-DRB1*1501.

TNFSF4. Cunninghame Graham et al. (2008) studied UK nuclear families and 263 Minnesota trios and found a single risk haplotype in the upstream region of TNFSF4. The association was replicated in 424 UK cases and 642 controls. Lymphoblastoid cell lines homozygous for the overtransmitted haplotype had 6.7-fold more TNFSF4 transcript than those homozygous for the undertransmitted haplotype. No association was found with TNFRSF4.

BLK and ITGAM. Hom et al. (2008) genotyped more than 500,000 SNPs in 1,311 SLE cases and 1,783 controls of European descent and replicated top loci in 793 cases and 857 controls from Sweden. Variation upstream of BLK and C8orf13 (rs13277113; OR = 1.39) was associated with SLE and with altered mRNA levels in B-cell lines. Variants near ITGAM and ITGAX on 16p11.22 (rs11574637; OR = 1.33) were also associated.

SLEGEN et al. (2008) performed a genomewide association study using 317,501 SNPs in 720 women of European ancestry with SLE and 2,337 controls. Associated SNPs were genotyped in 1,846 affected women and 1,825 controls. Besides HLA and IRF5, replicated associations were found at 16p11.2 (ITGAM), 11p15.5 (KIAA1542), 3p14.3 (PXK) and 1q25.1 (rs10798269), with evidence for association at FCGR2A, PTPN22 and STAT4.

BANK1. In a genome scan of 85,042 SNPs, Kozyrev et al. (2008) identified an association between SLE and a nonsynonymous substitution (rs10516487, R61H) in BANK1, which was replicated in 4 case-control sets (combined OR = 1.38). A branch-point SNP (rs17266594) in strong linkage disequilibrium with it influenced expression of a full-length isoform and an isoform lacking exon 2. A third associated variant (rs3733197, A383T) lay in the ankyrin domain.

Nath et al. (2008) studied 3,818 individuals of European descent. The strongest ITGAM association was at the nonsynonymous SNP rs1143679 (OR = 1.78), and it was replicated in 2 samples of African descent.

ITPR3. Oishi et al. (2008) found that a promoter SNP (rs3748079) in ITPR3 was associated with SLE in 2 Japanese case-control samples (OR = 1.88). NKX2.5 bound specifically to the nonsusceptible T allele, and an NKX2.5 SNP was also associated with SLE.

Willcocks et al. (2008) confirmed that low FCGR3B copy number was associated with SLE in 171 Caucasian patients compared with 176 controls. No association was found in a Hong Kong Chinese cohort of 159 cases and 150 controls. In an FcgammaRIIIb-deficient family and in the normal population, FCGR3B copy number correlated with protein expression, soluble FcgammaRIIIb, and neutrophil adherence to and uptake of immune complexes.

TNFAIP3. Graham et al. (2008) performed a genomewide association study of 431 SLE cases and 2,155 controls and found an association with rs5029939 in TNFAIP3. The association was replicated in 740 trios (meta-analysis P = 2.89 x 10(-12); OR = 2.29). They found evidence for 2 independent signals, one of them marked by the rheumatoid arthritis-associated SNP rs6920220.

Musone et al. (2008) analyzed 1,239 SLE cases and 1,629 controls of European ancestry and identified 3 independent SNPs in the TNFAIP3 region: rs13192841, rs2230926 and rs6922466. The minor alleles of rs13192841 and rs6922466 were protective. The minor allele of the coding SNP rs2230926, which results in a phe127-to-cys substitution in A20, increased risk (OR = 1.88). The Cys127 protein was modestly but consistently less effective than Phe127 at inhibiting TNF-induced NF-kappa-B activity.

Orru et al. (2009) found that the PTPN22 missense substitution R263Q in the catalytic domain of LYP reduces phosphatase activity, and that the Q263 variant protected against SLE.

IL18. Sanchez et al. (2009) found that the C allele of IL18 SNP rs360719 was associated with SLE in Spanish, Italian and Argentinian cohorts (pooled OR = 1.37). The risk allele was associated with increased IL18 mRNA expression and created a binding site for OCT-1.

In a genomewide association study of a Chinese Han population (1,047 cases and 1,205 controls, with replication in 3,152 cases and 7,050 controls), Han et al. (2009) identified 9 new susceptibility loci: ETS1, IKZF1, RASGRP3, SLC15A4, TNIP1, 7q11.23, 10q11.22, 11q23.3 and 16p11.2. They confirmed 7 previously reported loci and highlighted genetic heterogeneity between Chinese Han and European populations.

Gateva et al. (2009) genotyped SNPs from 2,466 regions in an independent sample of 1,963 cases and 4,329 controls and identified 5 new loci: TNIP1 (OR = 1.27), PRDM1 (OR = 1.20), JAZF1 (OR = 1.20), UHRF1BP1 (OR = 1.17) and IL10 (OR = 1.19).

EGR2. Myouzen et al. (2010) found that SNP rs10761670 in the 5-prime flanking region of EGR2 correlated positively with EGR2 expression and was associated with SLE in 3 cohorts (pooled OR = 1.23).

Niederer et al. (2010) used paralog ratio tests to measure FCGR copy number. They supported the association of low FCGR3B copy number with SLE in Caucasians (OR = 1.57) and replicated it in Chinese (OR = 1.65). Despite linkage disequilibrium between FCGR3B copy number and FCGR2B I232T, both reduced FCGR3B copy number and homozygosity for FCGR2B 232T were individually strongly associated with SLE.

Boteva et al. (2012) genotyped 2,207 subjects of northern and southern European ancestry (1,028 SLE cases and 1,179 controls) for total C4, C4A and C4B copy number and for the C4 exon 29 CT indel. They found that genetically determined partial C4 deficiency was not an independent risk factor for SLE in UK and Spanish populations.

CSK. Manjarrez-Orduno et al. (2012) genotyped 3,769 cases and 3,404 controls of European ancestry and found that the A allele of the intronic CSK variant rs34933034 was associated with SLE (OR = 1.32). The risk allele was associated with increased CSK expression, increased inhibitory phosphorylation of Lyn, increased BCR-mediated activation of mature B cells and higher plasma IgM. The fraction of transitional B cells was doubled in cord blood from carriers.

Mueller et al. (2013) mapped the FCGR2C/FCGR3B/FCGR2B locus in detail. They found that FCGR3B deletion juxtaposes the 5-prime regulatory sequences of FCGR2C with the coding sequence of FCGR2B. This creates a chimeric gene that leads to ectopic FcgammaRIIb on natural killer cells, which they proposed as an explanation for the SLE risk associated with reduced FCGR3B copy number.

NCF1. Zhao et al. (2017) traced a strong SLE association signal in the GTF2IRD1-GTF2I region at 7q11.23 to a missense variant in NCF1, arg90-to-his (R90H; rs201802880). It was associated with SLE in Asians (OR = 3.47), European Americans (OR = 2.61) and African Americans (OR = 2.02). It was also associated with primary Sjogren syndrome and with rheumatoid arthritis in Koreans. One copy of NCF1 was associated with increased SLE risk, and 3 or more copies were protective.

MEF2D. Farias et al. (2019) performed targeted resequencing of 215 candidate genes and identified a rare variant, rs200395694:G-T, in intron 4 of MEF2D. It was associated with SLE in Swedish cohorts: 12 heterozygotes among 504 patients versus 6 among 839 controls.

Kamitaki et al. (2020) imputed C4 alleles in 6,748 SLE cases and 11,516 controls of European ancestry. Common combinations of C4A and C4B copy number produced 7-fold variation in SLE risk. Each copy of C4A was protective (OR = 0.54), equivalent to 2.3 copies of C4B (OR = 0.77). In 1,494 African American cases and 5,908 controls, where C4 and HLA alleles are in much weaker linkage disequilibrium, joint analysis implicated C4A and C4B but not DRB1*0301.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Sturfelt et al. (1990) found photosensitivity more often in C4A-deficient SLE patients than in others; clinical features and autoantibodies were otherwise similar.

Shin et al. (2004) found that the DNASE1 exon 8 SNP +2373A-G (gln244-to-arg; Q244R) was associated with production of anti-RNP and anti-dsDNA antibodies among Korean SLE patients. The Arg/Arg genotype occurred in 31.3% of patients with anti-RNP antibody and in 14.4% of those without.

Zhao et al. (2017) found that the NCF1 R90H allele was dose-dependently associated with earlier age at onset in Korean and European American patients.

Farias et al. (2019) found that the MEF2D variant rs200395694 was associated with a triad of Raynaud phenomenon, anti-U1-RNP antibodies and anti-Sm antibodies among patients (p = 0.00037).

Kamitaki et al. (2020) found that C4 alleles acted more strongly in men than in women. Common combinations of C4A and C4B produced 14-fold variation in SLE risk among men and 6-fold variation among women. C4 and C3 proteins were present at higher levels in the cerebrospinal fluid and plasma of men than of women aged 20 to 50 years.

### Pathogenesis

DeHoratius et al. (1975) found anti-RNA antibodies in 82% of 28 SLE probands, 16% of 124 family members and 5% of 76 control family members. Among close household contacts, anti-RNA antibodies were found in consanguineous but not in nonconsanguineous contacts. Lymphocytotoxic antibodies were present in 57% of SLE family members. The authors suggested roles for both an environmental agent, possibly a virus, and the genetic response.

Miller and Schwartz (1979) found impaired suppressor-cell function in 11 of 15 SLE patients and in 13 first-degree relatives, 12 of them women. They proposed that SLE requires at least 2 functionally distinct classes of genes. Sakane et al. (1989) studied 34 healthy family members of 6 SLE probands. Impaired IL2 activity was found in 15 of 29 consanguineous relatives and in none of 5 nonconsanguineous household members, whereas B-cell assay abnormalities were found in 22 of 29 consanguineous relatives and in 4 of 5 nonconsanguineous household relatives.

Solomon et al. (1983) produced a mouse monoclonal anti-idiotypic antibody, 3I, against antinative DNA antibodies from an SLE patient. Eight of 9 sera with antinative DNA activity reacted with 3I, suggesting a shared idiotype among unrelated patients. Halpern et al. (1985) examined 27 members of 3 kindreds with familial SLE and found high-titered 3I reactivity in 6 of 8 SLE patients and 15 of 19 family members. Expression of the idiotype was significantly increased in relatives and usually occurred without anti-DNA activity.

Diamond and Scharff (1984) showed that a single amino acid substitution (alanine for glutamic acid at residue 35) in the T15 heavy chain of an antiphosphocholine antibody abolished phosphocholine binding. The mutant antibody gained reactivity with dsDNA, protamine and cardiolipin, resembling autoantibodies of autoimmune disease.

Solomou et al. (2001) found that nuclear extracts from SLE T cells express increased phosphorylated CREM, which binds the -180 site of the IL2 promoter. This transcriptional repression accounted for the decreased IL2 production by SLE T cells.

Blanco et al. (2001) found that serum from SLE patients induced normal monocytes to differentiate into dendritic cells. This capacity correlated with disease activity and depended on IFN-alpha. Baechler et al. (2003) used global gene expression profiling of peripheral blood mononuclear cells and found that about half of SLE patients had dysregulated expression of IFN pathway genes. This signature marked more severe disease involving the kidneys, hematopoietic cells and/or the central nervous system.

DeGiorgio et al. (2001) showed that a subset of anti-DNA antibodies cross-react with the NMDA receptor subunits NR2a and NR2b and mediate apoptotic neuronal death. The cerebrospinal fluid of an SLE patient contained such antibodies. Kowal et al. (2006) showed that serum from lupus patients reactive with DNA and NMDA receptor caused hippocampal neuron damage and memory impairment in mice whose blood-brain barrier had been compromised with lipopolysaccharide. IgG eluted from a patient's brain bound DNA and NMDA receptor and caused neuronal apoptosis when injected into mouse brains.

Xu et al. (2004) found that activated T cells from lupus patients resist anergy and apoptosis by markedly upregulating and sustaining COX-2 expression. Tao et al. (2005) found that NK T cells from patients with active SLE are highly sensitive to anti-CD95-induced apoptosis because of deficient expression of CD226 and survivin.

Kshirsagar et al. (2014) found enhanced Stat3 activity in effector T cells from children with lupus nephritis, correlating with increased IL17-producing cells. Th17 cells from these children had high Akt activity and enhanced migratory capacity.

### Diagnosis

Tan et al. (1982) revised the 1971 preliminary criteria for the classification of SLE. The 1982 criteria included fluorescent antinuclear antibody and antibodies to native DNA and Sm antigen, and excluded Raynaud phenomenon and alopecia. The revised criteria were 96% sensitive and 96% specific when tested against SLE and control patient data from 18 clinics.

### Clinical Management

Guiducci et al. (2010) showed that stimulation of plasmacytoid dendritic cells through TLR7 and TLR9 by nucleic acid-containing immune complexes activates the NF-kappa-B pathway essential for their survival. This could account for the reduced ability of glucocorticoids to inhibit the IFN pathway in SLE patients and in 2 lupus-prone mouse strains. They suggested that inhibitors of TLR7 and TLR9 signaling could act as corticosteroid-sparing drugs.

### Population Genetics

Fessel (1974) reported a community study of SLE describing a high prevalence in black women. Serdula and Rhoads (1979) ascertained 168 SLE cases on Oahu, Hawaii, from 1970 to 1975, of which 107 were considered definite. Age-adjusted prevalence per 100,000 was 5.8 in whites, 24.1 in Chinese, 19.9 in Filipinos, 20.4 in part-Hawaiians and 18.2 in Japanese. About 90% of definite cases were female.

Molokhia et al. (2003) studied 52 cases and 107 controls in Trinidad who reported no Indian or Chinese ancestry. Mean west African admixture was 0.81 in cases and 0.74 in controls. The results supported an additive genetic model for the ethnic difference in SLE risk between west Africans and Europeans, rather than an environmental explanation.

SLEGEN et al. (2008) estimated the prevalence of SLE at 31 per 100,000 women in populations of European ancestry, 50 to 75% lower than in other populations.

Willcocks et al. (2010) showed that an FCGR2B SNP that abrogates receptor function was strongly associated with SLE in both Caucasians and Southeast Asians. Its minor allele was more common in Southeast Asians and Africans, and homozygosity for the minor allele was associated with protection against severe malaria in an East African population (OR = 0.56). They suggested that this protection may explain the higher frequency of the allele, and hence of SLE, in Africans and Southeast Asians.

### Animal Model

Knight and Adams (1978) studied backcrosses of (NZB x NZW)F1 mice and concluded that the NZB strain contributes 1 gene and the NZW strain 2 genes (or clusters of closely linked genes) to the renal disease of the F1 hybrid. One of the NZW genes was linked to the H-2 complex, and all 3 genes act as dominant or codominant genes.

Steinberg et al. (1984) reviewed insights from murine models. They noted that most autoantibody-producing B cells can be eliminated from lupus mice by the xid gene, and that an analog of arachidonic acid is an effective treatment for murine lupus.

Morel et al. (1994) analyzed a backcross of the lupus-prone NZM/Aeg2410 strain with C57BL/6. They identified intervals on chromosomes 1, 4 and 7 containing strong recessive glomerulonephritis susceptibility alleles; heterozygosity at H-2 also correlated with susceptibility. Vyse et al. (1996) found in (NZB x NZW)F1 x NZW backcross mice that loci linked with anti-gp70 autoantibodies showed the strongest linkage with renal disease. A distal chromosome 4 locus, Nba1, was linked with nephritis but not with any autoantibody measured.

Mohan et al. (1999) found that mice congenic for Sle1 lose tolerance to chromatin without developing pathogenic autoantibodies. Mice bicongenic for Sle1 and Sle3 developed splenomegaly, a broad IgG autoantibody response and highly penetrant glomerulonephritis, particularly in females, supporting a 2-step epistatic model.

Gross et al. (2000) found that transgenic mice overexpressing zTNF4 (BLyS) in lymphoid cells develop symptoms characteristic of SLE. Treatment of NZBWF1 mice with soluble TACI-Ig inhibited proteinuria and prolonged survival.

Napirei et al. (2000) generated Dnase1-deficient mice. They developed antinuclear antibodies, glomerular immune complex deposition and full-blown glomerulonephritis in a Dnase1-dose-dependent manner.

Leadbetter et al. (2002) showed that activation of rheumatoid factor-positive B cells by IgG2a-chromatin immune complexes requires synergistic engagement of the antigen receptor and a MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor, with inhibitor studies implicating TLR9.

Sun et al. (2002) found that an agonistic monoclonal antibody to CD137 blocked lymphadenopathy and spontaneous autoimmune disease in Fas-deficient MRL/lpr mice and prolonged their survival.

Nakamura et al. (2003) found that a region including the IFN-alpha genes on NZB chromosome 4 was significantly linked to anxiety-like behavior in (NZB x NZW)F1 mice, and that neuronal IFN-alpha levels were elevated in these mice.

Li et al. (2004) identified a gain-of-function polymorphism in the kinase domain of Ltk in NZB mice, near a binding motif for the p85 subunit of PI3K.

Tournoy et al. (2004) found that most PS1+/- PS2-/- mice, which have partial loss of presenilins, developed an autoimmune disease from about 6 months of age. Features resembling human SLE included dermatitis, glomerulonephritis, keratitis, vasculitis, hypergammaglobulinemia and high-titer nuclear autoantibodies.

McGaha et al. (2005) showed that partial restoration of inhibitory FcgammaRIIB levels on B cells in lupus-prone mouse strains was sufficient to restore tolerance and prevent autoimmunity. Barber et al. (2005) found that inhibitors of PI3K-gamma reduced glomerulonephritis and prolonged lifespan in MRL-lpr mice.

Aitman et al. (2006) identified loss of the rat-specific Fcgr3 paralog Fcgr3-rs as a determinant of macrophage overactivity and glomerulonephritis in Wistar Kyoto rats.

Huerta et al. (2006) found that mice immunized with a peptide mimetope of DNA, then given epinephrine to open the blood-brain barrier, lost neurons in the lateral amygdala and showed a deficient response in fear-conditioning paradigms.

Talaei et al. (2015) found that a promoter polymorphism in the NZB gene encoding the SLAM adapter EAT-2 causes about a 70% reduction of EAT-2 in dendritic cells, leading to increased IL12 production and promoting lupus.

Bialas et al. (2017) reported behavioral phenotypes and synapse loss in lupus-prone mice that were prevented by blocking type I IFN signaling. Type I IFN stimulated microglia to engulf neuronal and synaptic material.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FASLG, 84-BP DEL, EX4 [84-bp deletion in exon 4 (28-amino acid in-frame deletion)]

Wu et al. (1996) screened 75 SLE patients by SSCP analysis. In 1 patient with SLE and lymphadenopathy they identified a heterozygous 84-bp deletion within exon 4 of FASLG, predicted to cause a 28-amino acid in-frame deletion. The patient's PBMCs showed decreased FasL activity, decreased activation-induced cell death and increased T-cell proliferation after activation.

**.0002 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
DNASE1, NONSENSE MUTATION, EXON 2 [heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2]

Yasutomo et al. (2001) described 2 SLE patients with a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 2 of DNASE1, decreased DNASE1 activity and extremely high IgG titers against nucleosomal antigens.

Shin et al. (2004) did not find this exon 2 mutation in 350 Korean SLE patients or 330 controls.

**.0003 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FCGR2B, ILE232THR [c.695T>C; p.Ile232Thr]

Kyogoku et al. (2002) identified a c.695T-C SNP in FCGR2B resulting in an ile232-to-thr (I232T) substitution in the transmembrane domain. The 232T/T genotype was significantly more frequent in 193 Japanese SLE patients than in 303 healthy individuals.

Floto et al. (2005) showed that the T232 receptor cannot inhibit activatory receptors because it is excluded from sphingolipid rafts.

Niederer et al. (2010) found that homozygosity for FCGR2B 232T was strongly associated with SLE independently of reduced FCGR3B copy number, despite linkage disequilibrium between the two.

**.0004 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
PTPN22, ARG620TRP [R620W]

Kyogoku et al. (2004) genotyped 525 North American white SLE patients and 1,961 white controls. The PTPN22 R620W (T) allele was associated with SLE (allele frequency 12.67% in cases vs 8.64% in controls). One copy of W620 gave an OR of 1.37 and 2 copies an OR of 4.37.

**.0005 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, AUTOANTIBODY PRODUCTION IN**  
DNASE1, GLN244ARG [+2373A>G; Gln244Arg]

Shin et al. (2004) studied 350 Korean SLE patients and 330 controls. The DNASE1 exon 8 SNP +2373A-G (gln244-to-arg; Q244R) was not associated with SLE risk, but it was associated with production of anti-RNP and anti-dsDNA antibodies among patients. Arg/Arg homozygosity was found in 31.3% of anti-RNP-positive patients and 14.4% of anti-RNP-negative patients.

**.0006 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
FCGR2B, -343G-C (rs3219018) [-343G>C; rs3219018]

Blank et al. (2005) found that homozygosity for the -343C allele of the FCGR2B promoter was associated with SLE in European Americans (OR = 11.1). The C allele decreased transcription and AP1 binding, and surface FcgammaRIIb expression was reduced on activated B cells from C/C patients.

**.0007 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
IRF5, rs2004640 [rs2004640]

Graham et al. (2006) replicated the association of the IRF5 rs2004640 T allele with SLE in 4 independent case-control cohorts and by family-based TDT. The T allele creates a 5-prime donor splice site in an alternate exon 1.

**.0008 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
STAT4, rs7574865 [rs7574865]

Remmers et al. (2007) found that the STAT4 intron 3 haplotype marked by rs7574865 was present on 31% of chromosomes from SLE patients and 22% from controls (OR = 1.55). Homozygosity for the risk allele more than doubled the risk of lupus.

**.0009 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
BANK1, ARG61HIS [rs10516487; R61H]

Kozyrev et al. (2008) identified an association between SLE and the BANK1 nonsynonymous substitution R61H (rs10516487), replicated in 4 independent case-control sets (combined OR = 1.38).

**.0010 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
ITGAM, rs1143679 [rs1143679]

Nath et al. (2008) found the strongest ITGAM association with SLE at the nonsynonymous SNP rs1143679 (OR = 1.78) among 3,818 individuals of European descent, and replicated it in 2 samples of African descent.

**.0011 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
TNFAIP3, PHE127CYS [rs2230926; Phe127Cys]

Musone et al. (2008) found that the minor allele of rs2230926, which results in a phe127-to-cys substitution in A20, was independently associated with increased SLE risk (OR = 1.88). The Cys127 protein was modestly but consistently less effective at inhibiting TNF-induced NF-kappa-B activity.

In haplotype and conditional analyses, Graham et al. (2008) found that the association at rs2230926 could be completely explained by associations at nearby SNPs, suggesting that it may not be the only putative causal variant.

**.0012 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
CSK, rs34933034 [rs34933034]

Manjarrez-Orduno et al. (2012) found that the A allele of the intronic CSK variant rs34933034 was associated with SLE in 3,769 cases and 3,404 controls of European ancestry (OR = 1.32). The risk allele was associated with increased CSK expression and augmented inhibitory phosphorylation of Lyn.

**.0013 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
NCF1, ARG90HIS [g.74779296G>A; p.Arg90His; rs201802880]

Zhao et al. (2017) identified the NCF1 missense variant arg90-to-his (R90H) as the likely causal variant underlying the SLE association signal at 7q11.23. It was associated with SLE in Asians (OR = 3.47), European Americans (OR = 2.61) and African Americans (OR = 2.02). Arg90 lies in a phosphoinositide-binding pocket of the PX domain, and in vitro mutation studies show that changing it to histidine reduces ROS production.

**.0014 SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
MEF2D, rs200395694 [rs200395694:G>T]

Farias et al. (2019) identified a rare variant, rs200395694:G-T, in intron 4 of MEF2D in heterozygous state in 12 of 504 Swedish SLE patients and 6 of 839 controls. It was associated with a triad of Raynaud phenomenon, anti-RNP and anti-Sm antibodies. The region has properties of a cell-specific enhancer that is differentially affected by the 2 alleles, and the risk allele inhibited splicing of an alternative tissue-specific isoform.
