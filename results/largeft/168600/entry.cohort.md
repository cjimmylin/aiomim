### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that susceptibility to late-onset Parkinson disease (PD) is conferred by variation in several genes. These include heterozygous mutation in the glucocerebrosidase gene (GBA1; 606463) on chromosome 1q22 and common variation in the MAPT gene (157140) on chromosome 17q21.31, the HLA-DRA gene (142860) on chromosome 6p21.32, and the MAOB gene (309860) on chromosome Xp11.3. A variant in the GLUD2 gene (300144) on chromosome Xq24 has been reported to modify age at onset.

### Description

Polymeropoulos et al. (1996) noted that Parkinson disease is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer disease and affects about 1% of the population over age 50. Polymeropoulos et al. (1997) described PD as a common neurodegenerative disorder with a lifetime incidence of approximately 2% and a documented pattern of familial aggregation. Lees et al. (2009) described PD as a common progressive bradykinetic disorder that can be accurately diagnosed. It is characterized by severe loss of nigral cells in the pars compacta and by aggregated alpha-synuclein that builds up in specific brainstem, spinal cord, and cortical regions.

### Clinical Features

Kissel and Andre (1976) reported monozygotic twin sisters who developed Parkinson disease and anosmia at age 39. The disease was kept under control and regressed with L-dopa. The authors noted that 2 families with the same association had been reported previously, and suggested that a genetically determined anomaly of dopamine metabolism was probably responsible for the association of PD and anosmia.

In a twin study, Duvoisin et al. (1981) found that cigarette smoking appeared less frequent in probands with PD than in their unaffected monozygotic co-twins (11.9 vs 16.1 pack-years). They also found evidence of premorbid personality differences dating back to late adolescence or early adult years. They took this to point to a prodromal onset of the disease that early.

Barbeau and Pourcher (1982) carried out a pilot study of 32 patients whose parkinsonism began before age 40 years and identified 3 subgroups: postencephalitic, onset and course with predominant tremor, and onset and course with akinesia and rigidity. Essential tremor was highly prevalent in the families of patients with tremor-onset early parkinsonism. Other cases of PD were frequent in the families of patients with akineto-rigid onset. Familial grey hair, hypertension, diabetes, and thyroid disease appeared more often than expected.

Tune et al. (1982) reported a family with familial manic-depressive illness and familial Parkinson disease.

Markopoulou et al. (1995) described a Greek-American kindred in which 16 of the 98 individuals in 6 generations, spanning 3 successive generations, had developed parkinsonism. Onset ranged from 31 to 71 years. The clinical presentation consisted of asymmetric rigidity, resting tremor, bradykinesia, and postural instability, and symptoms responded to levodopa. The disease appeared to be inherited in an autosomal dominant manner.

Plante-Bordeneuve et al. (1995) ascertained 14 PD families including 110 first-degree and 40 second-degree relatives, in which 31 individuals (17 females and 14 males) were affected. They compared these 31 patients with 31 age-matched patients with sporadic PD and found no statistical difference in selected clinical parameters. In the familial cases, clinical parameters and disease course varied within and between families.

Bonifati et al. (1995) studied 22 nonconsecutive PD families, each with at least 2 living and personally examined cases (52 PD cases in total). Anticipation of onset age in new generations was observed in some families. Familial cases had an earlier age at onset than sporadic cases from the same consecutive series, and onset age was the only way in which familial and sporadic PD differed.

Racette et al. (2002) examined 113 members of an Old-Order Amish pedigree. They classified 67 as normal, 17 as clinically definite PD, 6 as clinically probable PD, and 23 as clinically possible PD. Mean age at onset in the clinically definite subjects was 56.7 years. The phenotype was typical of idiopathic PD, including rest tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, and response to levodopa. Dementia occurred in 6 of the clinically definite subjects. Many subjects had levodopa-related motor complications, including wearing off and dopa-induced dyskinesias.

Neudorfer et al. (1996) described 6 patients with type I Gaucher disease, which had been considered non-neuronopathic, who developed an atypical parkinsonian syndrome. The hallmark of the syndrome was a relatively severe clinical course: neurologic signs appeared early, in the fourth to sixth decade of life, progressed aggressively, and were refractory to conventional anti-Parkinson therapy.

Tayebi et al. (2001) described a woman with mild Gaucher disease and impaired horizontal saccadic eye movements. She developed a tremor at age 42, followed by rapid deterioration of her gait. A pallidotomy at age 47 years was unsuccessful, and her motor and cognitive decline continued despite enzyme replacement therapy.

Varkonyi et al. (2003) reported 4 adult patients with both Gaucher disease and parkinsonism. Parkinsonian manifestations began relatively early, and the disease was rapidly progressive and refractory to therapy.

Bembi et al. (2003) described 4 patients (including 2 sibs) with type 1 Gaucher disease who presented with signs of typical PD: asymmetric onset of rigidity, resting tremor, bradykinesia, and a favorable response to Parkinson therapies. The authors noted that the parkinsonism previously reported with type 1 Gaucher disease had been characterized mainly by bilateral akinetic-rigid signs and a poor response to levodopa.

In a study of 276 outpatients with PD, Paleacu et al. (2005) found hallucinations in 32%. On stepwise logistic regression, the risk factors for hallucinations were a lower Mini-Mental State Examination score (p less than 0.0001) and a positive family history of dementia (p = 0.0005).

Voon et al. (2007) compared 21 patients with idiopathic PD who developed pathologic gambling after starting dopaminergic medication with a consecutive sample of 42 patients with idiopathic PD without compulsive behaviors. Patients with pathologic gambling had a younger age at PD onset and higher novelty seeking. They also more often had medication-induced hypomania or mania, impaired planning, or a personal or immediate family history of alcohol use disorders.

Constantinescu et al. (2007) found 5 cases of malignant melanoma in the DATATOP clinical trial cohort of patients with early PD. Published values for a standard healthy population predicted 1.5 cases. The standardized event ratio was 3.3 (95% CI, 1.1-7.8).

In a cross-sectional survey, Ferreira et al. (2007) found neoplastic or preneoplastic skin lesions in 35 of 150 PD patients (23.3%), compared with 20 of 146 controls (13.7%) (OR, 1.92). Twenty-nine PD patients (19%) had actinic keratosis, and basal cell carcinoma was diagnosed in 4 (3%). The difference from controls was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for gender, because males were overrepresented in the PD group. The authors described the results as nonconclusive but in agreement with earlier studies that had suggested an increased risk of skin cancer in PD.

In the PAGE study, Dong et al. (2014) found a diagnosis of melanoma in 18 PD cases (2.2%) and 26 controls (1.6%), a modest but nonsignificant positive association (OR, 1.32; 95% CI, 0.71-2.44). Hair, eye, and skin color were not associated with PD risk.

Tell-Marti et al. (2015) noted that epidemiologic studies had reported that PD and melanoma occur together, and that common MC1R variants, which determine skin and hair color, are associated with melanoma.

### Biochemical Features

Barbeau et al. (1985) studied 4-hydroxylation of debrisoquine in 40 patients with PD and 40 normal controls. Significantly more parkinsonian patients than controls had partially or totally defective 4-hydroxylation. Poor metabolizers tended to have had an earlier onset of disease.

Botella-Lopez et al. (2006) found that the 180-kDa Reelin band was increased in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with several neurodegenerative disorders, including PD, frontotemporal dementia, progressive supranuclear palsy, and Alzheimer disease. CSF levels of the 180-kDa band correlated positively with Tau protein.

### Inheritance

Spellman (1962) reported familial cases of parkinsonism and presented evidence of a dominant trait in one patient's family.

Kondo et al. (1973) carried out a genetic analysis of Parkinson disease and reported evidence of a multifactorial etiology.

Young et al. (1977) evaluated affected ancestral second-degree relatives in 12 PD families. Affected relatives were found on both sides of the family more often than expected for autosomal dominant inheritance. The authors concluded that most cases of PD have a multifactorial cause, although dominant inheritance in some families was not entirely ruled out.

Duvoisin et al. (1981) found zero concordance for PD in the first 12 monozygotic twin pairs examined in an ongoing twin study. They concluded that these preliminary findings suggested that genetic factors do not play a major role in the etiology of PD.

Barbeau and Pourcher (1982) found familial cases in 46% of patients whose parkinsonism began before age 40 years. This was higher than the 10 to 15% seen in general PD populations.

Ward et al. (1983) studied 43 monozygotic (MZ) and 19 dizygotic (DZ) twin pairs in which an index case had definite PD and found only 1 MZ pair definitely concordant. When pairs with questionable clinical features were included, 4 of 48 MZ and 1 of 19 DZ pairs were concordant. The frequency of PD in MZ cotwins was similar to that expected in an age- and sex-matched unrelated control group. Although they could not identify a single environmental agent, the authors concluded that the major factors in the etiology of PD are nongenetic.

Calne et al. (1987) reported 6 families with PD in which symptoms tended to begin at about the same time regardless of the patient's age. The mean difference in time of onset between generations was 4.6 years, whereas the mean difference in age at onset between children and parents was 25.2 years. The authors took this as suggestive of an environmental rather than a genetic cause. They found support for this view in the lack of correlation between occurrence of the disease and degree of consanguinity.

Duvoisin (1987) reviewed the genetics of PD and concluded that the best available data did not support a role of heredity in its etiology. Case-control studies had not shown a significantly higher prevalence of PD among first-degree relatives of index cases than among controls. Concordance in monozygotic twins was unexpectedly low and not significantly different from that in dizygotic twins. He noted, however, that a nonhereditary, possibly congenital genetic defect was not excluded.

Lazzarini et al. (1994) used survival analysis on first-degree relatives of consecutively seen PD probands. Sibs of probands with an affected parent had a significantly higher cumulative risk than sibs of probands without one, which the authors took to suggest significant familial aggregation in a subset of randomly ascertained families. In 80 multicase families, age-adjusted segregation ratios approached 0.5, and affected parents and affected sibs occurred in similar proportions. These findings, together with the distribution of ancestral secondary cases, were compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance with reduced penetrance in a subset of PD.

In the Greek-American kindred with autosomal dominant, levodopa-responsive parkinsonism, Markopoulou et al. (1995) observed anticipation. Affected members of the third generation had onset at ages 50 to 71 years, those of the fourth generation at 40 to 55 years, and the one affected member of the fifth generation at age 31 years.

Payami et al. (1995) studied age at onset and family history in 137 probands with idiopathic PD. Probands with an affected parent, aunt, or uncle (n = 21) had a significantly earlier onset (47.7 +/- 8.8 years) than probands with an affected sib only (n = 11; 60.3 +/- 12.9 years) or with no affected sib, parent, aunt, or uncle (n = 105; 59.2 +/- 11.4 years). Onset was on average 17 years earlier in the proband generation than in the parental generation. Within families, calendar years of onset varied too much to suggest a common point of exposure. The authors concluded that their data were most compatible with genetic anticipation, which could suggest involvement of an unstable trinucleotide repeat.

Plante-Bordeneuve et al. (1995) found that transmission in their 14 PD families was compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance. A total segregation ratio of 0.25 suggested incomplete penetrance, which increased with age from 0 below age 30 to 0.43 over age 70. In 8 multigeneration kindreds, age at onset was earlier in children than in their parents (mean difference, 26 years) but was similar within a generation. Although ascertainment bias could not be excluded, the authors suggested that the results were compatible with anticipation.

In their 22 multicase PD families, Bonifati et al. (1995) found that crude segregation ratios were similar for parents and sibs, that lifetime cumulative risk in sibs approached 0.4, and that ancestral relatives were all unilaterally distributed. They concluded that autosomal dominant inheritance with strongly age-related penetrance was most likely in familial PD.

Zareparsi et al. (1998) performed complex segregation analyses on the kindreds of 136 PD patients randomly ascertained from a clinic population. They rejected a nontransmissible environmental factor, no major gene (sporadic), and all mendelian modes of inheritance (dominant, recessive, additive, decreasing) (P less than 0.001). They concluded that the familial clustering was best explained by a rare familial factor transmitted in a non-mendelian fashion that influenced age at onset rather than susceptibility. If confirmed, they noted, this would matter for gene mapping studies looking for mendelian susceptibility genes.

Montgomery et al. (1999) found that among sons and daughters of PD patients who had abnormal scores on a PD test battery, the affected parent was much more often the father.

Tanner et al. (1999) screened 19,842 white male twins from the National Academy of Sciences/National Research Council World War II Veteran Twins Registry and identified 193 twins with PD. Pairwise concordance was similar in 71 MZ and 90 DZ pairs (0.155 vs 0.111; relative risk, 1.39). The authors concluded that genetic factors do not play a major role in typical PD and that no genetic component is evident when disease begins after age 50 years. However, in 16 pairs in which at least 1 twin was diagnosed at or before age 50, MZ concordance was 1.0 (4 pairs) and DZ concordance was 0.167 (relative risk, 6.0). This suggested that genetic factors are important when PD begins at or before age 50 years.

Sveinbjornsdottir et al. (2000) confirmed the diagnosis of PD in 772 living and deceased patients diagnosed in Iceland over the previous 50 years. Using a genealogic database covering 610,920 Icelanders over 11 centuries, they found that the patients, including a subgroup of 560 with late-onset disease (onset after age 50 years), were significantly more related to each other than matched controls, and that this relatedness reached beyond the nuclear family. In relatives of patients with late-onset PD, the risk ratio was 6.7 for sibs, 3.2 for offspring, and 2.7 for nephews and nieces. The authors concluded that late-onset PD has both a genetic and an environmental component.

Vaughan et al. (2001) suggested that nigral degeneration with Lewy body formation, and the clinical picture of PD that results, may be a final common pathway of a multifactorial disease process involving both environmental and genetic factors. They cited low heritability in the general population, the rarity of large families, and genetic heterogeneity.

In the Old-Order Amish kindred, Racette et al. (2002) found that the mean kinship coefficient among subjects with PD or PD by history (0.036) was higher than among age-matched normal Amish controls (0.016; p = 0.007). They took this as evidence that PD is inherited in this family.

Maher et al. (2002) performed segregation analysis on the nuclear families of 948 consecutively ascertained PD index cases from 3 US centers (60.4% male; mean age at onset, 57.7 years). Affected fathers were reported in 4.7%, mothers in 6.6%, brothers in 2.9%, and sisters in 3.2%. The analysis supported a rare major mendelian gene for PD in both an age-of-onset model and a susceptibility model. The age-of-onset model pointed to a gene that affects age-dependent penetrance by influencing age at onset rather than susceptibility. The authors could not tell whether the 2 analyses modeled the same gene or different genes. They suggested that reduced penetrance through gene-environment interactions might explain the low concordance in monozygotic twins.

Marder et al. (2003) compared first-degree relatives of 221 PD probands with onset at age 50 or younger and of 266 probands with onset after age 50 with relatives of 409 control probands. The risk of PD was similarly raised in relatives of early-onset (RR, 2.9) and late-onset (RR, 2.7) probands. In late-onset families, both sibs (RR, 3.6) and parents (RR, 2.5) were at higher risk. In early-onset families, only sibs had a markedly higher risk (RR, 7.9). The authors concluded that this pattern was consistent with an autosomal recessive contribution to early-onset but not late-onset PD.

Wirdefeldt et al. (2004) screened 33,780 twins in 14,082 pairs from the Swedish Twin Registry and identified 247 twins with possible PD. Only 2 pairs, both female dizygotic, were concordant. Concordance remained low in all zygosity groups when twins with suspected parkinsonism or another movement disorder were also counted as affected. The best-fitting model contained only environmental components of variance. The authors concluded that environmental factors are most important in the etiology of PD and that genetic effects are notably weak compared with other complex diseases.

Sidransky et al. (2009) concluded that GBA mutations are likely a susceptibility factor rather than a mendelian cause of PD and are among the most common risk factors for PD identified to date.

Using data from the NeuroGenetics Research Consortium (NGRC), Hamza et al. (2010) estimated that the heritability of PD fell from 0.6 to 0.4 after known pathogenic and susceptibility loci were excluded, but remained significant. They took this to suggest additional unidentified genes. Risk alleles at SNCA, MAPT, GAK, and HLA had cumulative effects: carriers of 6 or more risk alleles had about 5-fold higher risk than carriers of 0 or 1 (OR, 4.95).

### Mapping

Polymeropoulos et al. (1996) found linkage of PD to markers on chromosome 4q21-q23 in a large kindred with autosomal dominant PD (maximum lod score of 6.00 for marker D4S2380).

Nussbaum and Polymeropoulos (1997) reviewed that candidate gene linkage approaches in PD families had not been rewarding. However, a genomewide scan in a large family with PD with diffuse Lewy bodies had mapped PD to 4q21-q23, a region containing alpha-synuclein, and a missense mutation in that gene had been identified in 4 independent PD families.

Scott et al. (2001) performed a complete genomic screen with 344 markers in 174 families with multiple individuals diagnosed with idiopathic PD, ascertained through 13 clinic populations in the continental United States and Australia. Of 870 family members studied, 378 were diagnosed with PD. Overall and in families with late-onset PD, significant evidence for linkage was found on chromosomes 17q (MLOD = 2.28; lod = 2.62), 8p (MLOD = 2.01; lod = 2.22), and 5q (MLOD = 2.39; lod = 1.50). Linkage to chromosome 9q (MLOD = 1.52; lod = 2.59) was found in families with both levodopa-responsive and levodopa-nonresponsive patients. In families with at least 1 individual with onset before age 40, there was linkage to the parkin region on chromosome 6 (MLOD = 5.07; lod = 5.47). The authors concluded that parkin is important in early-onset PD and that multiple genetic factors may be important in idiopathic late-onset PD.

Martin et al. (2001) noted that they had previously observed linkage of idiopathic PD to chromosome 17q21, a region containing the tau gene.

Li et al. (2002) carried out a genomic screen for age at onset in 174 PD families. They found heritabilities of 40 to 60% and significant evidence of linkage of age at onset to chromosome 1p (lod = 3.41). Age-at-onset linkage on chromosomes 6 and 10 was found independently in both Alzheimer disease and PD families. A unified analysis gave a single peak on 10q between D10S1239 and D10S1237 (maximum lod = 2.62), suggesting a common gene affecting age at onset in both diseases. Li et al. (2003) later noted this linkage to a 15-cM region on chromosome 10q.

Pankratz et al. (2002) performed a genome screen with multipoint nonparametric linkage analysis in 160 multiplex PD families without parkin mutations. In 96 sib pairs (90 families) with verified PD, the highest lod scores were on chromosome X (lod = 2.1) and chromosome 2 (lod = 1.9). When all examined individuals were counted as affected (170 sib pairs), the scores rose to 2.7 for chromosome X and 2.5 for chromosome 2. Evidence of linkage was also found on chromosomes 4, 5, and 13 (lod scores greater than 1.5).

Pankratz et al. (2003) carried out a genomewide nonparametric linkage analysis in 754 affected individuals (425 sib pairs) from 362 multiplex PD families. In 216 sib pairs with verified PD, the maximum lod score was 3.4 on chromosome 2. Including all 425 affected sib pairs gave a lod score of 3.1 on the X chromosome. In 85 families with a very strong family history, they found linkage to chromosome 2 (lod = 4.9) and a lod score of 2.4 on chromosome 14. In 277 families without a strong family history, they found linkage to chromosome 10 (lod = 2.4) and the X chromosome (lod = 3.2).

Maraganore et al. (2005) performed a 2-tiered whole-genome association study. They genotyped 198,345 SNPs in 443 sib pairs discordant for PD, then tested 1,793 associated SNPs in 332 matched case-unrelated control pairs. Eleven SNPs were associated with PD (P less than 0.01) in both tiers with the same direction of effect. A SNP within the SEMA5A gene had the lowest combined P value (P = 7.62 x 10(-6)), and a second SNP tagged the PARK11 late-onset PD susceptibility locus (P = 1.70 x 10(-5)). Among 941 additional borderline SNPs that tested a priori hypotheses, the 2 SNPs with the lowest combined P values (9.07 x 10(-6) and 2.96 x 10(-5)) tagged the PARK10 locus. The authors noted that independent replication was needed.

Pankratz et al. (2009) performed a genomewide association study in 857 familial PD cases negative for LRRK2 G2019S and 867 controls. No result met genomewide significance. The strongest association was with SNPs in the GAK/DGKQ region on chromosome 4p (additive p = 3.4 x 10(-6); OR = 1.69). They also found association with the regions containing SNCA (rs356229; p = 5.5 x 10(-5); OR = 1.35) and MAPT (recessive p = 2.0 x 10(-5); OR = 0.56). A meta-analysis with the case-control GWAS of Fung et al. improved the p values for GAK/DGKQ (2.5 x 10(-7)) and MAPT, and also for PIK3CD, FIGN, and LY75/PLA2R1.

Gao et al. (2009) performed a genomewide linkage screen of 5,824 SNPs in 278 families of European, non-Hispanic descent with PD. They found significant evidence for linkage on chromosome 18q11 (maximum lod = 4.1) and suggestive evidence on chromosome 3q25 (maximum lod = 2.5).

In a genomewide association study and 2 replication studies totaling 2,011 Japanese PD cases and 18,381 controls, Satake et al. (2009) identified a new susceptibility locus on 1q32 (P = 1.52 x 10(-12)), which they designated PARK16. They also identified a second new risk locus at BST1 on 4p15 (P = 3.94 x 10(-9)). Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) replicated the 1q32 PARK16 locus in individuals of European ancestry (rs823128; OR = 0.66; combined P = 7.29 x 10(-8)). The locus spans SNORA72, NUCKS1, RAB7L1, SLC41A1, and PM20D1. They did not replicate the BST1 signal, possibly because the associated SNPs have a low minor allele frequency in Caucasians.

In a genomewide association study of 2,000 PD patients of European origin and 1,986 controls from the NGRC, Hamza et al. (2010) found a new association with the HLA region on chromosome 6p21.3, peaking at rs3129882 in intron 1 of HLA-DRA (P = 2.9 x 10(-8)). The association replicated in 2 datasets (meta-analysis OR, 1.26; P = 1.9 x 10(-10)). They confirmed associations with SNCA (P = 3.4 x 10(-11)) and MAPT (P = 1.3 x 10(-6)). Combining their data with those of Pankratz et al. replicated an association with GAK on 4p16 (rs11248051; OR, 1.46; P = 3.2 x 10(-9)). They did not replicate PARK16 and found only weak support for BST1 and common LRRK2 variants.

### Heterogeneity

Sandy et al. (1996) suggested that patients with young-onset PD may differ from those with older onset in their risk factors. Genotyping studies in older-onset patients had reported modest increases in risk with the CYP2D6 B and A alleles, but they found no such association in young-onset patients.

Vaughan et al. (2001) noted that 2 genes (SNCA and parkin) and 2 further loci had been implicated in familial PD. UCHL1 mutations had also been described in familial PD, although the evidence for a role of UCHL1 was less clear.

In the Old-Order Amish PD kindred, Racette et al. (2002) found no mutations in known PD genes, and no haplotype in previously linked PD regions cosegregated with disease. They suggested that a novel gene or genes might account in part for the increased prevalence.

Among 203 PD sib pairs, Pankratz et al. (2002) found parkin mutations in 22 families, which they left out of their genome screen to reduce genetic heterogeneity.

Gao et al. (2009) found that their linkage results were strongest in families that had not previously been screened for linkage. Simulation studies suggested that the findings were likely due to locus heterogeneity rather than random statistical error.

Lees et al. (2009) noted that age is the main known risk factor for PD. They added that susceptibility genes, including alpha-synuclein, LRRK2, and GBA, have shown genetic predisposition to be another important causal factor.

Satake et al. (2009) found that PARK16, SNCA, and LRRK2 were risk loci in both Japanese and European populations, while BST1 and MAPT showed differences between populations. They suggested that population differences contribute to genetic heterogeneity in PD. Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) likewise proposed population-specific genetic heterogeneity: the MAPT association was absent in the Asian cohort, while the SNCA association replicated across populations.

In a Spanish case-control sample, Mata et al. (2011) found no association with PD for SNPs at PARK16 (rs823156, rs947211), PARK17 (GAK rs11248051), or PARK18 (HLA-DRA rs3129882), overall or in any age stratum (p values 0.09 to 0.88). For PARK18 the study had 99% power at the originally reported OR. The authors suggested that population structure, power, or population-specific environmental interactions might explain the discordance.

### Molecular Genetics

Polymeropoulos et al. (1997) identified a mutation in the alpha-synuclein gene (SNCA) in a large Italian kindred, in which a PD susceptibility gene had been mapped to chromosome 4q. They also found it in 3 unrelated families of Greek origin with autosomal dominant inheritance of PD.

Kurth et al. (1993) identified a single-stranded conformational polymorphism in a Hae III-digested MAOB PCR product. The frequency of allele 1 was 0.62 in 46 PD patients versus 0.45 in 121 controls, and allele 1 was associated with a relative risk for PD of 2.03 (p less than 0.02). There were no significant differences in allele frequencies for an MAOA polymorphism. The authors suggested that an inherited MAOB variant may be involved in a genetic predisposition to PD. Ho et al. (1995) identified an A/G single-stranded conformational polymorphism in MAOB, 36 bases upstream from the intron 13-exon 14 boundary. In an allelic association study, they found no statistically significant association of this polymorphism with PD, contrary to the results of a previous study.

In a case-control study of 224 Taiwanese PD patients and 197 matched controls, Wu et al. (2001) found that the MAOB intron 13 G genotype (G in men and G/G in women) was associated with a 2.07-fold increased relative risk of PD. The COMT Val158Met polymorphism alone showed no correlation with risk. However, patients carrying both the COMT L and MAOB G genotypes showed a significant synergistic enhancement of risk. The authors cautioned that the combined genotypes were rare and that confirmation in a larger sample was needed.

Bordet et al. (1994) used PCR with XbaI RFLP analysis to look for CYP2D6 mutations linked to the debrisoquine/sparteine poor-metabolizer phenotype. They studied 105 Caucasian patients with idiopathic PD and 15 with diffuse Lewy body disease. The rate of genotypically defined poor metabolizers and the frequency of the D6-B mutation did not differ significantly between the groups. In a case-control study of 108 patients with young-onset PD (age 51 years or younger) and 236 controls, Sandy et al. (1996) found no significant differences in CYP2D6 allele frequencies. Carrying 1 or more nonfunctional alleles (A, B, D, and D2) was not associated with increased risk (OR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.41-1.13).

Parboosingh et al. (1995) used single-strand conformation analysis to screen the Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase, Mn superoxide dismutase, and catalase genes in 107 unrelated patients with familial or sporadic PD. They identified a gly-to-asp substitution in exon 9 of the catalase gene in 1 patient, who also had decreased red blood cell catalase activity. They considered this variant most likely silent and not the cause of PD in that family. No mutations were found in the other patients, and the authors concluded that PD is not caused by mutations in these 3 genes.

Bandmann et al. (1996) found a normal sequence of the H5 pore region of KCNJ7, the human homolog of the weaver mouse gene, in familial and sporadic PD. They concluded that nigral cell loss probably has a different cause in PD and in weaver mice.

Martin et al. (2001) studied 1,056 individuals from 235 families (426 affected and 579 unaffected members) with idiopathic PD. They found significant family-based association with PD for 3 of 5 tau (MAPT) SNPs tested: SNP 3 (P = 0.03), SNP 9i (P = 0.04), and SNP 11 (P = 0.04). Haplotype analysis gave stronger evidence, with a positive association for one haplotype (P = 0.009) and a negative association for another (P = 0.007). The authors concluded that combining linkage with positional association analysis implicated tau as a susceptibility gene for idiopathic PD.

Mattila et al. (2002) reported an association between PD and a polymorphism at position -511 of the IL1B gene in Finnish patients.

Levecque et al. (2003) carried out a community-based case-control study of 209 PD patients and 488 matched controls of European, mostly French, ancestry. They found association of PD with a polymorphism in exon 29 of NOS1 (OR for T allele carriers = 1.53, P = 0.02) and a protective association with a polymorphism in exon 22 of NOS2 (OR for AA carriers = 0.50, P = 0.01). A NOS1 exon 18 polymorphism showed no association. The NOS1 polymorphisms interacted significantly with cigarette smoking, and the authors concluded that nNOS and iNOS may act as modifier genes in PD.

van der Walt et al. (2003) genotyped 10 SNPs defining European mtDNA haplogroups in 609 white PD patients and 340 white controls. Compared with haplogroup H carriers, carriers of haplogroups J (OR, 0.55; P = 0.02) and K (OR, 0.52; P = 0.02) had a significantly lower risk of PD. SNP 10398G, which defines J and K and causes a thr-to-ala change in the ND3 subunit of complex I, was strongly associated with protection (OR, 0.53; P = 0.0001), and the effect seemed stronger in women. SNP 9055A in ATP6 was protective in women (OR, 0.45; P = 0.03).

In 99 Chinese PD patients and 126 controls from Hong Kong, Chan et al. (2003) found that the NAT2 slow acetylator genotype was significantly more frequent in patients than in controls (68.7% vs 28.6%; adjusted OR, 5.53; 95% CI, 3.08-9.92). Smoking did not modify the association.

Marx et al. (2003) screened the synphilin-1 gene in 328 German patients with familial or sporadic PD. In 2 apparently sporadic patients they identified a c.1861C-T transition resulting in an arg621-to-cys (R621C) substitution, which was not present in 702 chromosomes from healthy German controls.

Pankratz et al. (2003) performed a genome screen in 23 families in which PD patients had a mutation in only 1 allele of the parkin gene. They found evidence of linkage to chromosome 10 (lod = 2.3) and concluded that their findings supported the importance of gene-by-gene interactions in PD susceptibility.

Li et al. (2003) used a 'genomic convergence' approach to choose candidate genes, combining the 10q linkage region with hippocampal expression differences in Alzheimer disease. In 1,362 samples from their PD dataset (635 patients), they found significant age-at-onset associations for GSTO1 (P = 0.007) and the neighboring GSTO2 (P = 0.005), but none for SCD, NDUFB8, or PRSS11.

Varkonyi et al. (2003) found no mutations in the parkin or alpha-synuclein genes in their 4 patients with Gaucher disease and parkinsonism, each of whom had a different Gaucher genotype. Four of the alleles carried the N370S mutation in GBA1. The authors suggested that the co-occurrence points to a shared pathway, a modifier, or another genetic cause.

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) screened 99 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with idiopathic PD for 6 common GBA mutations. Thirty-one patients (31.3%) had 1 or 2 mutant alleles: 23 were heterozygous for N370S, 4 heterozygous for 84GG, 3 homozygous for N370S, and 1 heterozygous for R496H. By comparison, 4.1% of 74 Ashkenazi patients with Alzheimer disease and 6.2% of 1,543 healthy Ashkenazi controls were carriers. Patients with PD had significantly greater odds of being Gaucher disease carriers than patients with Alzheimer disease (OR, 10.8) or controls (OR, 7.0). The authors suggested that heterozygosity for a GBA mutation may predispose Ashkenazi Jews to PD.

Kwok et al. (2005) identified 2 functional SNPs in GSK3B: a promoter SNP (rs334558) whose T allele had greater transcriptional activity in vitro, and an intronic SNP (rs6438552) whose T allele altered splicing. The rs6438552 T allele was more frequent in PD brains than in control brains, with a corresponding increase in exon 9+11-skipped transcripts and in Tau phosphorylation. After stratification by MAPT haplotype, a GSK3B haplotype carrying both T alleles was associated with PD risk: OR 1.64 (p = 0.007) in H1/H2 plus H2/H2 individuals and 0.68 (p less than 0.001) in H1/H1 individuals. The authors suggested that GSK3B polymorphisms interact with Tau haplotypes to modify PD risk.

Toft et al. (2006) screened 311 Norwegian PD patients and 474 controls for the GBA mutations N370S and L444P. A mutant allele was found in 7 patients (2.3%) and 8 controls (1.7%) (p = 0.58), and the authors concluded that the study did not indicate increased susceptibility to PD in GBA mutation carriers in Norway.

Tan et al. (2007) studied GBA mutations and the risk of PD in Chinese patients.

In a case-control study, Tan et al. (2007) found no association between PD and the CYP1A2 -163C-A polymorphism (rs762551) (P = 0.08), and no interaction between caffeine and CYP1A2 genotype (P = 0.956). They concluded that the protective association with caffeine held in both fast and slow caffeine metabolizers.

In a case-control study of 721 patients with PD, 57 with dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and 554 controls of European ancestry from the Seattle area, Mata et al. (2008) screened for the GBA mutations N370S and L444P. Heterozygote frequency was significantly higher in PD (2.9%; P less than 0.001) and DLB (3.5%; P = 0.045) than in controls (0.4%).

Gan-Or et al. (2008) screened 420 Israeli Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients, 333 elderly controls, and 3,805 young controls for 8 GBA mutations. Two were associated with mild Gaucher disease (N370S, R496H) and 6 with severe Gaucher disease (84GG, IVS2+1, V394L, D409H, L444P, RecTL). GBA carrier frequency was 17.9% in patients, compared with 4.2% in elderly and 6.35% in young controls. More than one-third of the patients carried a mutation in GBA or LRRK2.

Gutti et al. (2008) stressed that PD cohorts need appropriate genotyping strategies to detect GBA mutations.

Neumann et al. (2009) screened GBA in 790 British PD patients and 257 age- and ethnicity-matched controls. Mutations were found in 33 patients (4.18%) and 3 controls (1.17%) (p = 0.01; OR, 3.7; 95% CI, 1.12-12.14). Fourteen different mutations were identified, including 3 novel ones: K7E, D443N, and G193E.

In a multicenter study from 16 centers, Sidransky et al. (2009) analyzed GBA in 5,691 PD patients (780 Ashkenazi Jews) and 4,898 controls (387 Ashkenazi Jews). The Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio for any GBA mutation in patients versus controls was 5.43 across centers. The combined odds ratios were 3.96 for N370S and 6.73 for L444P. Seventeen patients (15 Ashkenazi) carried 2 GBA mutations: N370S/N370S in 14, N370S/R496M in 2, and N370S/V394L in 1. The common variants E326K and T369M, which are not pathogenic in Gaucher disease, were not significantly associated with PD.

Gao et al. (2009) genotyped the MC1R Arg151Cys polymorphism in a nested case-control study (272 cases and 1,185 controls). Compared with Arg/Arg, the Cys/Cys genotype, which was associated with red hair, carried a higher PD risk (adjusted RR, 3.15; 95% CI, 1.06-9.37).

Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) carried out a 2-stage genomewide association study in individuals of European ancestry: 1,713 cases and 3,978 controls, followed by replication in 3,361 cases and 4,573 controls. They found strong association at SNCA (rs2736990; OR = 1.23; P = 2.24 x 10(-16)) and at the MAPT locus (rs393152; OR = 0.77; P = 1.95 x 10(-16)). There was supporting evidence for a signal 0.17 Mb upstream of LRRK2 (rs1491923; OR = 1.14; combined P = 1.55 x 10(-5)), but it did not pass their multiple-testing threshold, and the neighboring gene SLC2A13 could not be ruled out. Most associated SNCA SNPs clustered in the 3-prime linkage disequilibrium block. The REP1 risk allele was in linkage disequilibrium with the 3-prime risk alleles and was not independently associated.

In Japanese patients, Satake et al. (2009) found strong associations with PD at SNCA on 4q22 (P = 7.35 x 10(-17)) and LRRK2 on 12q12 (P = 2.72 x 10(-8)). Both genes are involved in autosomal dominant forms of parkinsonism, and the authors concluded that these loci also play a part in typical PD.

Pankratz et al. (2009) noted that GAK was a promising candidate because it had been shown to be differentially expressed (1.56-fold) in the substantia nigra pars compacta of PD patients. Minor alleles of SNPs tagging the MAPT H2 haplotype were associated with reduced risk.

Elstner et al. (2009) found that SNP rs2010795 in intron 8 of PDXK was associated with increased PD risk in 676 German patients (p = 0.00032; OR, 1.319). The association replicated in British and Italian cohorts, giving p = 1.18 x 10(-7) (OR, 1.304) in a combined analysis of 1,232 patients and 2,802 controls. A second PDXK SNP, rs2070535 in the 3-prime UTR, was associated with PD in the German cohort. Vilarino-Guell et al. (2010) reported an association between PDXK and PD, whereas Guella et al. (2010) found no association of rs2010795 with PD in an Italian population.

Plaitakis et al. (2010) found that a rare c.1492T-G variant in the X-linked GLUD2 gene, resulting in a ser445-to-ala (S445A) substitution in the regulatory domain of hGDH2, interacted significantly with age at onset of PD in Caucasian populations. Hemizygous male carriers developed PD 6 to 13 years earlier than subjects with other genotypes in 2 independent Greek PD groups and 1 North American PD cohort.

Tan et al. (2010) analyzed 13 SNPs at GWAS-identified PD loci in 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects. They found association with PARK16, PARK8, and PARK1 but not with BST1. PARK16 SNPs were associated with decreased risk and PARK1 and PARK8 SNPs with increased risk. In a pooled analysis with 2 Japanese cohorts (1,366 patients and 16,669 controls), association with all 4 loci, including BST1, was robust and of similar strength in Chinese and Japanese subjects.

Hamza et al. (2010) identified association of late-onset sporadic PD with rs3129882 in intron 1 of HLA-DRA in a GWAS of 2,000 Caucasian patients and 1,986 controls. The association replicated in 2 further datasets.

Alcalay et al. (2010) studied 953 patients with early-onset PD (onset at age 50 or younger) from 13 movement disorders centers. Mutations were found in 158 (16.6%). Sixty-four (6.7%) carried PRKN mutations: 33 were heterozygotes and 27 were homozygotes or compound heterozygotes. Thirty-five (3.6%) had LRRK2 G2019S, 1 carried LRRK2 G2385R, and 64 (6.7%) had GBA mutations (18 L444P heterozygotes, 38 N370S heterozygotes, and 2 N370S homozygotes). One patient had a single A104T mutation in DJ1. No carriers of the SNCA or PINK1 mutations tested were found. Seven patients carried mutations in 2 genes, in combinations of LRRK2 G2019S, GBA L444P or N370S, and heterozygous PRKN mutations, with onset between 31 and 49 years.

In 1,445 PD patients and 1,161 controls from northern Spain, Mata et al. (2011) found significant association with SNPs in MAPT (rs1800547; OR, 0.79; p = 3.1 x 10(-4)) and SNCA (rs356219; OR, 1.23; p = 5.5 x 10(-4)). In analyses stratified by age, the association was significant only in the late-onset group. The A allele of rs1800547, a surrogate marker for the H1 haplotype, was associated with PD under a dominant model (OR, 1.23; p = 1.1 x 10(-2)).

In the PAGE study of 808 non-Hispanic white PD cases and 1,623 controls, Dong et al. (2014) tested 11 SNPs previously linked to pigmentation or melanoma. After Bonferroni correction, only rs4911414 at ASIP was associated with PD (p = 0.001), and this was not replicated in the larger IPDGC dataset (P = 0.43). None of 360 pigmentation or melanoma SNPs tested in IPDGC reached significance, and MC1R rs1805007 was not significantly associated (OR, 1.06; P = 0.37).

Tell-Marti et al. (2015) sequenced the entire MC1R gene in 870 Spanish PD patients and 736 controls and found that the p.R160W variant (rs1805008) was marginally associated with PD (OR, 2.10; gender- and age-adjusted p = 0.009; Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.063).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found that Ashkenazi PD patients who carried GBA mutations had an earlier age at onset than noncarriers (mean, 60.0 vs 64.2 years; P = 0.04).

Gan-Or et al. (2008) found that GBA mutations associated with severe Gaucher disease (84GG, IVS2+1, V394L, D409H, L444P, RecTL) increased PD risk 13.6-fold, while mild mutations (N370S, R496H) increased it 2.2-fold. Mean ages at onset were 55.7 years in severe mutation carriers and 57.9 years in mild mutation carriers, compared with 60.7 years in patients without known GBA or LRRK2 mutations. Severe mutations made up 29% of GBA carriers among patients but 7% among young controls. An earlier age at onset was also observed in carriers of LRRK2 G2019S. Gan-Or et al. (2009) reported that PD patients with severe GBA mutations differed in phenotype from those with mild mutations.

Among British PD patients with GBA mutations, Neumann et al. (2009) found an early onset of disease, hallucinations in 45% (14/31), and symptoms of cognitive decline or dementia in 48% (15/31).

Compared with noncarriers, PD patients with GBA mutations in the study of Sidransky et al. (2009) presented about 4 years earlier (mean age at onset, 54.9 vs 58.8 years). Carriers were more likely to have an affected first- or second-degree relative (24.0% vs 17.8%). They less often had asymmetric onset, bradykinesia, rest tremor, and rigidity, and more often reported cognitive changes (26.3% vs 19.0%).

In 600 Ashkenazi PD patients, Gan-Or et al. (2010) found that, compared with noncarriers, more LRRK2 G2019S carriers reported rigidity (p = 0.007) and balance disturbances (p = 0.008) as initial symptoms. More GBA mutation carriers reported bradykinesia (p = 0.021). The authors suggested that LRRK2 and GBA mutations have distinct effects on the initial symptoms of PD.

Plaitakis et al. (2010) found no effect on age at onset in female PD patients heterozygous for the GLUD2 S445A variant. They suggested that modification of the overactive variant enzyme by estrogens might explain this.

Tan et al. (2010) observed a trend toward a stronger protective effect of PARK16 SNPs in sporadic than in familial PD, and in older than in younger subjects.

Hamza et al. (2010) found that the HLA association was uniform across strata of family history, age at onset, sex, and environmental exposure. It was particularly strong for sporadic PD (P = 5.5 x 10(-10)), late-onset PD (P = 2.4 x 10(-8)), and men. There was no evidence of interaction with smoking, coffee, NSAIDs, SNCA, or MAPT.

In early-onset PD, Alcalay et al. (2010) found that GBA L444P was associated with a higher UPDRS-III motor score after adjustment for covariates. LRRK2 G2019S carriers more often had the postural instability and gait difficulty motor phenotype. The authors cautioned that larger studies were needed to tell whether the L444P difference was a true clinical phenotype or a result of multiple comparisons.

### Pathogenesis

Calne and Langston (1983) suggested that in most cases of PD, the cause may be an environmental factor, possibly toxic, superimposed on a background of slow, sustained neuronal loss due to advancing age.

Langston et al. (1983) described 4 persons who developed marked parkinsonism after intravenous use of an illicit drug. Analysis of the substance injected by 2 of them showed mainly 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,5,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP), with trace amounts of the meperidine analog MPPP. They proposed that MPTP selectively damages cells in the substantia nigra.

Barbeau et al. (1985) postulated that PD results from environmental factors acting on genetically susceptible individuals against a background of normal aging. They noted that many potentially neurotoxic xenobiotics are detoxified by hepatic cytochrome P450. Calne et al. (1987) concluded that their family findings fit the hypothesis that some cases of PD result from early, subclinical environmental damage followed by age-related neuronal loss.

Di Monte (1991) reviewed evidence that impaired mitochondrial function may underlie neuronal death in PD. MPP+, the toxic metabolite of MPTP, likely blocks mitochondrial electron flow at complex I, and complex I activity is deficient in brain and other tissues of parkinsonian patients. Because 7 complex I subunit genes are encoded by mtDNA, Di Monte (1991) suggested that abnormal mtDNA might play a role. Muqit et al. (2006) stated that impaired mitochondrial function is thought to play a major role in PD pathogenesis. They added that recent genetic advances suggest mitochondrial dysfunction may be the primary defect.

Hughes et al. (1992) found Lewy bodies in the cerebral cortex of all 76 patients with nigral Lewy bodies whose clinical diagnosis had been idiopathic PD.

Markopoulou et al. (1995) argued that the inheritance pattern in their Greek-American kindred, and the occurrence of parkinsonism in successive generations living in Greece and the United States, challenged environmental factors as the primary cause of parkinsonism in this family.

Nussbaum and Polymeropoulos (1997) reviewed that the common idiopathic form of PD has a pathologic correlate: Lewy bodies distributed diffusely throughout the substantia nigra, hypothalamus, hippocampus, autonomic ganglia, and olfactory tracts. They suggested that at least some familial PD with diffuse Lewy bodies results from an abnormal alpha-synuclein protein that interferes with normal protein degradation, leading to inclusions and neuronal death.

Clarke et al. (2000) found that neuronal death followed exponential kinetics in several neurodegeneration models and in Parkinson and Huntington diseases. These kinetics argued against the cumulative damage hypothesis. The authors proposed a 'one-hit' model in which mutation imposes a mutant steady state on the neuron and a single random event initiates cell death.

Tayebi et al. (2001) suggested that gene changes associated with a new rearrangement, arising from a crossover between the metaxin gene and its pseudogene, could contribute to the atypical parkinsonian phenotype of their patient with Gaucher disease.

Auluck et al. (2002) found that Lewy bodies in human postmortem tissue immunostained for molecular chaperones and suggested that chaperones may play a role in the progression of PD.

In a review, Warner and Schapira (2003) concluded that most cases of PD are likely caused by a complex interplay between genetic and environmental factors.

Teismann et al. (2003) found that COX-2, the rate-limiting enzyme in prostaglandin E2 synthesis, was upregulated in brain dopaminergic neurons of PD patients and MPTP-treated mice. In the mice, COX-2 was induced after MPTP administration through a JNK/c-Jun-dependent mechanism.

In SH-SY5Y cells under proteasomal inhibition, Marx et al. (2003) found that synphilin-1 with the R621C mutation formed significantly fewer cytoplasmic inclusions than wildtype protein. Cells expressing the mutant were also more susceptible to staurosporine-induced cell death. The authors suggested that inclusion formation may be beneficial and that reduced ability to form inclusions may sensitize neurons to stress.

Li et al. (2003) noted that GSTO1 may be involved in posttranslational modification of interleukin-1-beta, which they described as provocative given that inflammation may play a role in PD. Maraganore et al. (2005) noted that the SEMA5A protein plays an important role in neurogenesis and neuronal apoptosis, which they considered consistent with existing hypotheses about PD pathogenesis.

Fujita et al. (2006) used SPECT with [123I]5-iodo-A-85380 to image nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in 10 nondemented PD patients and 15 age-matched healthy controls. The patients showed a widespread significant decrease (about 10%) in total distribution volume in cortical and subcortical regions, with no significant change in delivery (K1).

Cooper et al. (2006) found that the earliest defect after alpha-synuclein expression in yeast was a block in endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-to-Golgi vesicular trafficking. In a genomewide screen, proteins acting at this step formed the largest class of toxicity modifiers, among them the Rab GTPase Ypt1p, which associated with cytoplasmic alpha-synuclein inclusions.

Chan et al. (2007) suggested that substantia nigra pars compacta dopaminergic neurons are vulnerable in PD because they rely unusually heavily on L-type Cav1.3 calcium channels to drive rhythmic pacemaking. This reliance increases with age and renders the neurons vulnerable to stressors. Juvenile neurons use pacemaking mechanisms common to neurons not affected in PD.

Outeiro et al. (2007) found that a potent inhibitor of sirtuin 2 (SIRT2), or SIRT2 knockdown by siRNA, rescued alpha-synuclein toxicity and changed the shape of inclusions in a cellular model of PD.

Gao et al. (2009) suggested that pigmentation may play a role in PD and might partly explain why PD and melanoma occur together more often than expected, rather than the association being an adverse effect of levodopa. In a separate study, Gao et al. (2009) concluded that melanoma and PD share common genetic components.

Neumann et al. (2009) found widespread, abundant alpha-synuclein pathology (Braak stage 5-6) in all 17 GBA mutation carriers examined. Diffuse neocortical Lewy body-type pathology tended to occur more often in GBA carriers than in matched PD controls without GBA mutations.

Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) found that the MAPT risk alleles were in linkage disequilibrium with the H1 haplotype. In 133 human frontal cortex samples, the risk alleles were associated with higher MAPT expression and lower LRRC37A expression. Risk SNPs at SNCA, LRRK2, and PARK16 showed no association with expression of nearby genes.

Elstner et al. (2009) performed whole-genome expression profiling of laser-microdissected substantia nigra pars compacta neurons from 8 PD patients and 9 controls. MTND2, PDXK, and SRGAP3 were upregulated and TRAPPC4 was downregulated. mtDNA-encoded respiratory chain genes were upregulated, whereas 13 nuclear genes encoding respiratory chain subunits were downregulated. They noted that PDXK makes pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate, a cofactor of dopa decarboxylase.

Plaitakis et al. (2010) found that the S445A variant hGDH2 enzyme had enhanced basal activity, resisted GTP inhibition, and was markedly sensitive to modification by estrogens. They concluded that this gain-of-function variant hastens PD onset in hemizygous subjects, probably by damaging nigral cells through enhanced glutamate oxidative dehydrogenation.

Using PET with the serotonin 2A receptor ligand setoperone F 18, Ballanger et al. (2010) compared 7 nondemented PD patients with visual hallucinations and 7 age-matched PD patients without hallucinations. The patients with hallucinations had higher receptor binding in the ventral visual pathway (bilateral inferooccipital gyrus, right fusiform gyrus, inferotemporal cortex) and in the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, medial orbitofrontal cortex, and insula.

Hamza et al. (2010) noted that rs3129882 had been identified as a cis-acting regulatory variant whose genotype correlated with expression of HLA-DRA, HLA-DQA2, and HLA-DRB5. They suggested that the HLA association supports a role for neuroinflammation in PD.

Berman et al. (2011) found that EAAC1 was expressed more highly on dopaminergic neurons than on other neurons in mouse and normal human midbrain. Surviving dopaminergic neurons in postmortem PD brains also expressed EAAC1.

Minones-Moyano et al. (2011) found decreased expression of miR-34b and miR-34c in several PD brain areas at motor stages (Braak stages 4 and 5). Misregulation was also present at premotor stages (Braak 1-3) in untreated cases. Depleting miR-34b/c in SH-SY5Y dopaminergic cells reduced viability, altered mitochondrial function, increased oxidative stress, and reduced DJ1 and Parkin expression. DJ1 and Parkin were also reduced in PD brains with strong miR-34b/c downregulation.

Chung et al. (2013) generated cortical neurons from iPS cells of a female member of the Contursi kindred, who carried the SNCA A53T mutation, and compared them with mutation-corrected controls. A53T neurons showed increased nitric oxide and nitrotyrosine and early, progressive accumulation of ER forms of glucocerebrosidase and nicastrin. Neurons from a male patient of the Iowa kindred with SNCA triplication, aggressive dementia, and parkinsonism closely phenocopied these defects. Postmortem frontal cortex from another A53T kindred member showed nitrotyrosine staining and increased ER-form glucocerebrosidase. Sporadic PD cortex showed the same trend. In yeast, raising or lowering nitric oxide production altered alpha-synuclein toxicity.

Tardiff et al. (2013) showed that the compound NAB acts through the E3 ubiquitin ligase Rsp5 (the yeast homolog of NEDD4) to promote endosomal transport. Alpha-synuclein impaired Rsp5-dependent endosomal trafficking and bulk endocytosis, and NAB2 restored these processes.

In an expression QTL study of purified immune cells from 461 healthy individuals, Raj et al. (2014) found that PD and Alzheimer disease susceptibility alleles were enriched for monocyte-specific cis-eQTL effects. They suggested that the inflammatory component of PD susceptibility may be driven more strongly by the myeloid compartment.

Sulzer et al. (2017) found that PBMCs from PD patients responded to 2 antigenic regions of alpha-synuclein, near the N terminus (Y39 region, aa31-46) and near the C terminus (aa116-140, requiring S129 phosphorylation). Responses were mainly from IL-5-secreting CD4+ T cells and were significantly greater than in controls. The Y39 epitope bound the PD-associated alleles DRB1*15:01 and DRB5*01:01 with high affinity. About 40% of PD patients had responses to alpha-synuclein epitopes, and class I-restricted responses to the 9-mer aa37-45, which binds HLA-A*11:01, were seen in 5 of 19 patients and none of 12 controls. The authors suggested that these responses may explain the association of PD with specific MHC alleles.

Mittal et al. (2017) screened 1,126 compounds for effects on SNCA expression and found that beta-2-adrenoreceptor (beta2AR) agonists lowered SNCA mRNA and protein. Beta2AR knockout, silencing, or the antagonist propranolol increased SNCA expression. Beta2AR regulated SNCA transcription through H3K27 acetylation at the SNCA promoter and enhancers. In iPSC-derived neuronal precursors from a patient with SNCA triplication, clenbuterol reduced SNCA, lowered mitochondrial superoxide production, and increased viability under rotenone.

Burbulla et al. (2017) found that iPSC-derived dopaminergic neurons from 2 patients with idiopathic PD showed decreased basal mitochondrial respiration and accumulation of oxidized dopamine, followed later by decreased lysosomal glucocerebrosidase activity. These changes appeared later (day 150-180) than in neurons with homozygous DJ-1 mutations. They proposed a cascade in which mitochondrial oxidant stress leads to oxidized dopamine. Dopamine quinones then modify the glucocerebrosidase catalytic site, causing lysosomal dysfunction and alpha-synuclein accumulation. Similar increases in oxidized dopamine were seen in neurons with parkin, PINK1, or LRRK2 mutations or alpha-synuclein triplication.

### Diagnosis

Hughes et al. (1992) examined the brains of 100 patients diagnosed prospectively by consultant neurologists as having idiopathic PD. Only 76 had nigral Lewy bodies. The other 24 had diagnoses that included progressive supranuclear palsy, multiple system atrophy, Alzheimer disease, Alzheimer-type pathology, and basal ganglia vascular disease. Applying the recommended diagnostic criteria retrospectively raised diagnostic accuracy to 82%. The authors concluded that their findings called into question the concept of PD as a single distinct morbid entity.

Montgomery et al. (1999) applied a PD test battery covering motor function, olfaction, and mood to 80 asymptomatic first-degree relatives of PD patients and 100 normal controls. Abnormal scores occurred significantly more often in relatives (22.5%) than in controls (9%). The authors suggested that the battery may detect the asymptomatic carrier state or risk for PD.

Sidransky et al. (2009) concluded that all GBA exons need to be sequenced. In non-Ashkenazi cases, as many as 46% of mutant alleles could be missed by screening only for N370S and L444P. In addition, 22 of 43 subjects with L444P carried recombinant alleles that included pseudogene sequence.

### Clinical Management

In a phase 1 safety trial, Gill et al. (2003) infused GDNF directly into the putamen of 5 PD patients. After 1 year there were no serious clinical side effects. The off-medication UPDRS motor subscore improved by 39%, and the activities of daily living subscore by 61%. Medication-induced dyskinesias fell by 64% and were not seen off medication. After 18 months, [18F]dopamine PET showed a significant 28% increase in putamen dopamine storage. One catheter had to be repositioned, and MRI changes resolved after the GDNF concentration was lowered.

Teismann et al. (2003) suggested that COX-2 inhibitors may be therapies for PD, because of their safety record and ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier. Muqit et al. (2006) suggested that drugs targeting the mitochondria may be the best hope for disease-modifying therapy.

Voon et al. (2007) found that novelty seeking, a personal or family history of alcohol use disorders, and younger age at PD onset together predicted pathologic gambling with 83.7% accuracy. They concluded that patients with these features may have a greater risk of pathologic gambling with dopamine agonists.

Constantinescu et al. (2007) found no association between levodopa therapy and melanoma incidence in the DATATOP cohort. Ferreira et al. (2007) recommended that future epidemiologic studies screen PD patients for actinic keratosis, given its frequency in PD and its associated risk of melanoma.

Lees et al. (2009) stated that dopamine replacement therapy considerably reduces motor handicap in PD. Treating associated depression, pain, constipation, and nocturnal difficulties can improve quality of life.

Ballanger et al. (2010) proposed that selective serotonin 2A receptor antagonists be studied as treatment for visual hallucinations and psychosis in PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) concluded that patients with early-onset PD who are of Jewish or Hispanic ancestry, have onset at age 30 or younger, or have an affected first-degree relative may benefit from genetic counseling. Because the penetrance of these mutations is unknown, they saw little or no role for predictive or prenatal testing.

In a randomized double-blind crossover trial in 33 PD patients homozygous for COMT Val158Met alleles (17 Val/Val, 16 Met/Met), Corvol et al. (2011) found that adding 200 mg entacapone to an acute levodopa challenge increased the best ON time more in Val/Val than in Met/Met patients (39 +/- 10 vs 9 +/- 9 minutes; p = 0.04). The levodopa area under the curve also rose more in Val/Val patients (+62% vs +34%), and their red blood cell COMT activity was inhibited more.

Berman et al. (2011) found that long-term oral N-acetylcysteine reduced dopaminergic neuron loss, nitrotyrosine immunoreactivity, and pole test deficits in EAAC1-null mice. They suggested that glutathione repletion be evaluated clinically in PD.

Chung et al. (2013) found that the small molecule NAB2, acting through the Rsp5/Nedd4 ubiquitin ligase pathway, decreased nitric oxide levels and corrected ER accumulation of nicastrin and glucocerebrosidase in A53T patient neurons. Tardiff et al. (2013) found that NAB reversed alpha-synuclein toxicity or pathology in C. elegans dopaminergic neurons, rat midbrain cultures expressing A53T alpha-synuclein, and iPSC-derived cortical neurons from PD patients with A53T or SNCA triplication.

In DJ-1 mutant human dopaminergic neurons, Burbulla et al. (2017) found that long-term treatment with mito-TEMPO or N-acetyl cysteine reduced oxidized dopamine and improved lysosomal function. Isradipine or FK506 also reduced oxidized dopamine.

### Population Genetics

In a hospital-based survey of age-specific prevalence in 551 patients from Helsinki and Vancouver, Teravainen et al. (1986) concluded that PD may be starting earlier than it did previously.

In a door-to-door survey of 982 people older than 65 years in 2 rural Bavarian villages, Trenkwalder et al. (1995) found a PD prevalence of 0.71% (95% CI, 0.19-1.23%), with 5 affected men and 2 affected women. Other causes of parkinsonism were also found, and 50% of parkinsonism cases had not been diagnosed before the survey.

Bonifati et al. (1995) found a positive family history of PD in 24% of 100 consecutive PD cases versus 6% of spouse controls. When isolated tremor was included, the figures were 43% versus 9%.

Zareparsi et al. (1998) stated that nearly 20% of PD is familial and that only a small subset of familial cases shows autosomal dominant transmission.

Tanner et al. (1999) estimated a concordance-adjusted PD prevalence of 8.67 per 1,000 among white male World War II veteran twins.

Martin et al. (2001) found substantial linkage disequilibrium (P less than 0.001) among 4 of the 5 tau SNPs tested.

Racette et al. (2002) calculated a minimum PD prevalence of 552 per 100,000 among persons 40 years and older in an Old-Order Amish community, higher than in the general population.

In a door-to-door survey in central Singapore of people aged 50 years and older, Tan et al. (2004) identified 46 people with PD. The prevalence, age-adjusted to the 1970 US census, was 0.30% and was the same in Chinese (0.33%), Malays (0.29%), and Indians (0.28%). The authors concluded that environmental factors may matter more than racially determined genetic factors.

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) identified 95 Gaucher disease carriers (6.2%) among 1,543 healthy Ashkenazi Jews: 92 were heterozygous for N370S and 3 for 84GG.

In a case-control study of 418 PD patients and 468 matched controls, Tan et al. (2007) found a significant inverse association between caffeine intake and PD (P = 2.01 x 10(-5)). Compared with low drinkers, the odds ratio was 0.71 for moderate and 0.47 for high drinkers.

Mata et al. (2008) concluded that GBA mutations had a large effect on susceptibility to Lewy body disorders at the individual level. The population-attributable risk in individuals of European ancestry, however, was modest at approximately 3%. Neumann et al. (2009) concluded that GBA mutations occur in British PD patients at a higher frequency than mutations in any other known PD gene.

Sidransky et al. (2009) found N370S or L444P in 15.3% of Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients versus 3.4% of controls, and in 3.2% of non-Ashkenazi patients versus 0.6% of controls. N370S was not found in any Asian patients or controls. When GBA was fully sequenced in non-Ashkenazi patients, 6.9% carried a mutation.

In the Health Professionals Follow-up Study and the Nurses' Health Study, Gao et al. (2009) found that PD risk rose as natural hair color became lighter. Pooled relative risks were 1.0, 1.40, 1.61, and 1.93 for black, brown, blond, and red hair, respectively. The association was strongest for onset before age 70 years. In the same cohorts, a family history of melanoma in a first-degree relative was associated with higher PD risk (RR, 1.85; p = 0.004), whereas a family history of colorectal, lung, prostate, or breast cancer was not.

Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) estimated the combined population attributable risk of the identified loci at about 25%, cautioning that this value should be interpreted with care.

Hamza et al. (2010) found significant population substructure among European Americans. In controls, the frequency of the rs3129882 risk allele ranged from 0.36 in Washington to 0.46 in New York. Across Europe it followed a gradient, low in Northern Europeans and high in Southern Europeans, particularly Italians.

In early-onset PD, Alcalay et al. (2010) found mutations more often in patients with onset at age 30 or younger (40.6% vs 14.6%), in those of Jewish ancestry (32.4% vs 13.7%), and in those with a first-degree family history of PD (23.9% vs 15.1%). Hispanic patients more often carried PRKN mutations (15.6% vs 5.9%).

Sulzer et al. (2017) found the HLA class II alleles DRB1*15:01/DRB5*01:01 in about one-third of 67 PD patients, compared with about 15% of 36 controls.

Following the entire Norwegian population for 11 years, Mittal et al. (2017) found that use of the beta2AR agonist salbutamol was associated with reduced PD risk (rate ratio, 0.66), with a dose-response relationship. Use of the antagonist propranolol was associated with increased risk (rate ratio, 2.20).

### Animal Model

Bandmann et al. (1996) noted that homozygous weaver mice, which carry a homozygous mutation in the H5 pore region of mGirk2, show progressive postnatal loss of mesencephalic dopaminergic cells and had been proposed as an animal model of PD.

In Drosophila, Auluck et al. (2002) found that directed expression of Hsp70 prevented the dopaminergic neuronal loss associated with alpha-synuclein expression, and that interfering with endogenous chaperone activity accelerated alpha-synuclein toxicity.

In MPTP mice, Teismann et al. (2003) showed that COX-2 inhibition protected against dopaminergic neurodegeneration by preventing formation of dopamine-quinone, not by reducing inflammation.

Scherzer et al. (2003) profiled gene expression in transgenic Drosophila expressing human alpha-synuclein and identified 51 signature transcripts associated with alpha-synuclein expression. Specific changes were present already at the presymptomatic stage and were absent in tau transgenic flies.

Cooper et al. (2006) showed that increased expression of Rab1 protected against alpha-synuclein-induced dopaminergic neuron loss in animal models of PD.

Sotnikova et al. (2006) developed dopamine-deficient dopamine transporter knockout (DDD) mice, in which acute inhibition of tyrosine hydroxylase eliminated brain dopamine for up to 16 hours. The mice showed severe akinesia, rigidity, tremor, and ptosis.

Kreitzer and Malenka (2007) found that endocannabinoid-mediated long-term depression in indirect-pathway striatal neurons was absent in models of PD. It was rescued by a D2 receptor agonist or by inhibitors of endocannabinoid degradation, and these drugs given together in vivo reduced parkinsonian motor deficits.

Chan et al. (2007) found that blocking Cav1.3 channels in adult mouse substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons caused reversion to juvenile pacemaking. This 'rejuvenation' protected the neurons in in vitro and in vivo models of PD.

Outeiro et al. (2007) found that SIRT2 inhibitors protected against dopaminergic cell death in vitro and in a Drosophila model of PD.

Berman et al. (2011) found that EAAC1-null mice showed age-dependent loss of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons, with no difference at 3 months but a 41% decrease by 12 months. The loss was accompanied by increased nitrotyrosine, nitrosylated alpha-synuclein, and microglial activation. Striatal dopamine was only nonsignificantly reduced, and motor deficits were seen only on the pole test at 12 months.

In a screen of yeast expressing toxic levels of alpha-synuclein, Tardiff et al. (2013) identified an N-aryl benzimidazole (NAB) that reversed alpha-synuclein phenotypes without lowering alpha-synuclein levels.

Mittal et al. (2017) found that clenbuterol crossed the blood-brain barrier and lowered alpha-synuclein mRNA and protein in the substantia nigra of wildtype mice. It also prevented loss of tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons in MPTP-treated mice.

Burbulla et al. (2017) found that DJ-1 knockout mice had little oxidized dopamine and no nigral neuron loss. Overexpression of human A53T alpha-synuclein or chronic L-DOPA feeding increased oxidized dopamine and reduced glucocerebrosidase activity in these mice, and L-DOPA also caused dopaminergic neuron loss.

### History

Duvoisin (1987) noted that the idea of a hereditary role in PD arose from positive family histories, reports of kindreds with parkinsonism, and the first systematic family study by Mjones. He argued that closer study showed these familial clusters reflected other disorders, notably olivopontocerebellar atrophy and other forms of multiple system atrophy.

Nussbaum and Polymeropoulos (1997) noted that hereditary influences in PD had been discounted because of a high monozygotic twin discordance rate in studies later shown to be inadequate and inconclusive. Interest in hereditary factors revived when a positive family history became apparent as a major risk factor.

Stien (2005) wrote about the portrayal of parkinsonism in the works of Shakespeare, and Zhang et al. (2006) reviewed early descriptions of PD in ancient China.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, LEU444PRO [p.L444P]

Tayebi et al. (2001) described a woman with mild Gaucher disease and impaired horizontal saccadic eye movements. She developed a tremor at age 42, followed by rapid deterioration of gait and progressive parkinsonism. Sequencing of the GBA1 gene identified the L444P and D409H mutations. Southern blot analysis showed an additional 17-kb band on the maternal allele, which PCR and sequencing showed to be a duplication including the glucocerebrosidase pseudogene, the metaxin gene, and a pseudometaxin/metaxin fusion.

Toft et al. (2006) screened 311 Norwegian PD patients and 474 controls for N370S and L444P. A mutant allele was found in 7 patients (2.3%) and 8 controls (1.7%) (p = 0.58).

Mata et al. (2008) found that the combined heterozygote frequency for N370S and L444P was 2.9% in 721 PD patients of European ancestry, compared with 0.4% in 554 controls (P less than 0.001).

Gan-Or et al. (2008) included L444P among the GBA mutations associated with severe Gaucher disease. In Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients, this group of severe mutations increased PD risk 13.6-fold.

In a multicenter study, Sidransky et al. (2009) found a combined odds ratio of 6.73 for L444P in PD patients versus controls. Twenty-two of 43 subjects with L444P carried recombinant alleles that included pseudogene sequence.

Among 953 patients with early-onset PD, Alcalay et al. (2010) identified 18 L444P heterozygotes. L444P was associated with a higher UPDRS-III motor score. Three further patients carried L444P together with LRRK2 G2019S, a heterozygous PRKN exon 2 deletion, or a heterozygous PRKN R275W mutation, with onset at 31 to 34 years.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ASP409HIS [p.D409H]

In a woman with mild Gaucher disease and parkinsonism beginning with tremor at age 42, Tayebi et al. (2001) identified the D409H mutation in the GBA1 gene together with L444P. The maternal allele also carried a duplication including the glucocerebrosidase pseudogene, the metaxin gene, and a pseudometaxin/metaxin fusion.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) included D409H among the GBA mutations associated with severe Gaucher disease. In Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients, this group of mutations increased PD risk 13.6-fold.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ASN370SER [p.N370S]

Varkonyi et al. (2003) reported 4 adult patients with Gaucher disease and rapidly progressive, therapy-refractory parkinsonism of relatively early onset. Each had a different Gaucher genotype, and 4 of the alleles carried the N370S mutation in GBA1.

Among 99 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with idiopathic PD, Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 23 who were heterozygous and 3 who were homozygous for N370S. Ninety-two of 1,543 healthy Ashkenazi controls were heterozygous for N370S.

Mata et al. (2008) found that the combined heterozygote frequency for N370S and L444P was 2.9% in PD patients versus 0.4% in controls.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) found that the mild Gaucher disease mutations N370S and R496H together increased PD risk 2.2-fold in Ashkenazi Jews.

Sidransky et al. (2009) found a combined odds ratio of 3.96 for N370S in PD patients versus controls. Fourteen patients were N370S homozygotes, and N370S was not found in any Asian patients or controls.

Among 953 patients with early-onset PD, Alcalay et al. (2010) identified 38 N370S heterozygotes and 2 N370S homozygotes. Three further patients carried N370S together with LRRK2 G2019S or a heterozygous PRKN D53E mutation.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, 84GG

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 4 heterozygotes for the GBA1 84GG mutation among 99 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with idiopathic PD, and 3 among 1,543 healthy Ashkenazi controls.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) included 84GG, a frameshift mutation, among the GBA mutations associated with severe Gaucher disease. This group of mutations increased PD risk 13.6-fold in Ashkenazi Jews.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ARG496HIS [p.R496H]

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 1 Ashkenazi Jewish patient with idiopathic PD who was heterozygous for the R496H mutation in GBA1.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) classified R496H with N370S as mutations associated with mild Gaucher disease. Together these increased PD risk 2.2-fold in Ashkenazi Jews.

**.0006 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, VAL394LEU [p.V394L]

Gan-Or et al. (2008) included V394L among the GBA mutations associated with severe Gaucher disease. This group of mutations increased PD risk 13.6-fold in Ashkenazi Jews.

In a multicenter study of PD patients, Sidransky et al. (2009) found 1 patient with the GBA1 genotype N370S/V394L.

**.0007 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ARG496MET [p.R496M]

In a multicenter study of PD patients, Sidransky et al. (2009) found 2 patients with the GBA1 genotype N370S/R496M.

**.0008 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, AGE AT ONSET OF, MODIFIER OF**  
GLUD2, SER445ALA [c.1492T>G; p.S445A]

Plaitakis et al. (2010) identified a rare c.1492T-G variant in the X-linked GLUD2 gene, resulting in a ser445-to-ala (S445A) substitution in the regulatory domain of hGDH2. Hemizygous male carriers developed PD 6 to 13 years earlier than subjects with other genotypes in 2 independent Greek PD groups and 1 North American PD cohort. Heterozygous females showed no effect on age at onset. The variant enzyme had enhanced basal activity, resisted GTP inhibition, and was markedly sensitive to modification by estrogens. The authors concluded that this gain-of-function variant hastens PD onset, probably through enhanced glutamate oxidative dehydrogenation that damages nigral cells.
