### TEXT

A number sign (#) is used with this entry because of evidence that susceptibility to late-onset Parkinson disease (PD) is conferred by variation in several genes, including heterozygous mutation in the GBA1 gene (606463) on chromosome 1q22, variation in the SNCA gene (163890) on chromosome 4q22.1, variation at the MAPT locus (157140) on chromosome 17q21.31, and variation in the LRRK2 gene (609007) on chromosome 12q12. Hemizygosity for a variant in the X-linked GLUD2 gene (300144) on chromosome Xq24 has been reported to modify age at onset.

### Description

Parkinson disease (PD) is a common progressive neurodegenerative disorder. It is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer disease, affecting approximately 1% of the population over age 50, and significant familial aggregation has been documented (Polymeropoulos et al., 1996). The lifetime incidence is approximately 2% (Polymeropoulos et al., 1997). Its pathologic hallmarks are massive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the pars compacta of the substantia nigra and Lewy bodies (Vaughan et al., 2001). Lewy bodies are eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions distributed diffusely in the substantia nigra, hypothalamus, hippocampus, autonomic ganglia and olfactory tracts (Nussbaum and Polymeropoulos, 1997). Lees et al. (2009) described PD as a progressive bradykinetic disorder characterized by severe loss of nigral cells and by accumulation of aggregated alpha-synuclein in specific brainstem, spinal cord and cortical regions, and stated that age is the main known risk factor. Vaughan et al. (2001) suggested that nigral degeneration with Lewy body formation may be a final common pathway of a multifactorial disease process in which both environmental and genetic factors have a role.

### Clinical Features

Kissel and Andre (1976) reported monozygotic twin sisters who both developed Parkinson disease and anosmia at age 39 years. The disease was controlled and regressed with L-dopa treatment. The authors noted that 2 families with the same association had been reported previously.

In 12 monozygotic twin pairs discordant for PD, Duvoisin et al. (1981) noted that one unaffected co-twin had essential tremor, another had cerebrovascular disease, and a third was an alcoholic. Probands appeared to smoke less than co-twins (11.9 vs 16.1 pack-years). Differences in premorbid personality between probands and co-twins dated back to late adolescence or early adulthood, which the authors suggested pointed to a prodromal onset of the disease as early as late adolescence or early adult life.

Barbeau and Pourcher (1982) studied 32 patients whose PD began before age 40 and identified 3 subgroups: postencephalitic, onset and course with predominant tremor, and onset and course with akinesia and rigidity.

Tune et al. (1982) reported a case of familial manic-depressive illness occurring together with familial Parkinson disease.

Hughes et al. (1992) found that in all 76 patients with clinically diagnosed PD who had nigral Lewy bodies, Lewy bodies were also present in the cerebral cortex. The authors suggested that these observations called into question the concept of PD as a single distinct morbid entity.

Markopoulou et al. (1995) reported a Greek-American kindred in which 16 of 98 individuals across 6 generations developed levodopa-responsive parkinsonism in 3 successive generations. Affected members were examined in both Greece and the United States. The presentation consisted of asymmetric rigidity, resting tremor, bradykinesia, and postural instability.

Plante-Bordeneuve et al. (1995) studied 14 families with clinically typical PD. Among 110 first-degree and 40 second-degree relatives, 31 individuals were affected (17 females and 14 males). Compared with 31 age-matched patients with sporadic PD, the familial cases showed no statistical difference in the selected clinical parameters, and clinical parameters and disease course varied within and between families, as in sporadic cases.

Bonifati et al. (1995) found that 9 of their consecutive PD cases had at least one living affected relative, giving 20 familial PD cases. Compared with sporadic cases from the same consecutive series, the familial cases had an earlier age at onset, and the authors concluded that familial and sporadic PD differed only in onset age. They observed anticipation of age at onset in newer generations of some families.

Neudorfer et al. (1996) described 6 patients with type I Gaucher disease, previously considered non-neuronopathic, who developed parkinsonian symptoms. The atypical parkinsonian syndrome had a relatively severe course, with early appearance of neurologic signs in the fourth to sixth decade of life, aggressive progression, and refractoriness to conventional anti-Parkinson therapy.

Montgomery et al. (1999) applied a PD test battery, including tests of motor function, olfaction, and mood, to 80 asymptomatic first-degree relatives of PD patients and 100 normal controls. Of the relatives, 22.5% scored in the abnormal range, compared with 9% of controls, a statistically significant difference; relatives with abnormal scores differed on all 3 components of the battery from relatives with normal scores. The authors suggested that the battery may detect the asymptomatic carrier state or risk for PD.

Tayebi et al. (2001) described a woman with mild Gaucher disease and impaired horizontal saccadic eye movements who developed a tremor at age 42 years, after which her gait deteriorated rapidly. A pallidotomy at age 47 was unsuccessful, and her motor and cognitive decline continued despite enzyme replacement therapy.

Racette et al. (2002) described a large, multi-incident Old-Order Amish pedigree with PD. Of 113 examined members, 17 had clinically definite, 6 clinically probable, and 23 clinically possible PD. Mean age at onset in the clinically definite subjects was 56.7 years. The phenotype was typical of idiopathic PD, with rest tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, and response to levodopa. Dementia occurred in 6 of the clinically definite subjects, and many had levodopa-related motor complications, including wearing off and dyskinesias.

Among 948 PD index cases from 3 US centers studied by Maher et al. (2002), 60.4% were male and 39.6% female, with a mean age at onset of 57.7 years. Affected relatives included fathers (4.7%), mothers (6.6%), brothers (2.9%), and sisters (3.2%); although most index cases were male, modestly more of the reported affected relatives were female.

Varkonyi et al. (2003) reported 4 adult patients with type 1 Gaucher disease and parkinsonism whose parkinsonian signs began relatively early, progressed quickly, and did not respond to therapy.

Bembi et al. (2003) described 4 patients with type 1 Gaucher disease, 2 of whom were sibs, who had typical PD with asymmetric onset of rigidity, resting tremor, bradykinesia, and a good response to antiparkinsonian therapy. The authors contrasted this with earlier reports, in which PD with Gaucher disease mainly showed bilateral akinetic-rigid signs and a poor response to levodopa.

Paleacu et al. (2005) found hallucinations in 32% of 276 outpatients with PD. In stepwise logistic regression, risk factors for hallucinations were Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score (p less than 0.0001) and a positive family history of dementia (p = 0.0005).

Using SPECT with the ligand [123I]5-IA, Fujita et al. (2006) imaged nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in 10 nondemented PD patients and 15 age-matched healthy controls. The patients had a widespread, significant decrease of about 10% in total distribution volume in both cortical and subcortical regions, with no significant change in delivery (K1).

Neumann et al. (2009) found that British PD patients carrying GBA mutations had early disease onset, hallucinations in 45% (14 of 31), and symptoms of cognitive decline or dementia in 48% (15 of 31). Neuropathologic examination of all 17 GBA mutation carriers studied showed widespread and abundant alpha-synuclein pathology, Braak stage 5-6, with McKeith limbic or diffuse neocortical Lewy body-type pathology; diffuse neocortical Lewy body-type pathology tended to be more frequent in carriers than in matched PD controls without GBA mutations.

### Other Features

Barbeau and Pourcher (1982) reported that familial grey hair, hypertension, diabetes, and thyroid disease appeared more often than expected in the families of patients with early-onset PD.

Constantinescu et al. (2007) found 5 cases of malignant melanoma in the DATATOP clinical trial cohort of patients with early PD, whereas 1.5 cases would be expected in a standard healthy population (standardized event ratio 3.3; 95% CI, 1.1-7.8). They found no association between levodopa therapy and melanoma incidence.

In a cross-sectional survey in which a movement disorder specialist and a dermatologist examined 150 PD patients and 146 age-matched controls, Ferreira et al. (2007) found neoplastic or preneoplastic skin lesions in 35 PD patients (23.3%) and 20 controls (13.7%) (OR 1.92; 95% CI 1.05-3.51). The difference was no longer significant after adjustment for sex, because the PD group had more males. Twenty-nine PD patients (19%) had actinic keratosis and 4 (3%) had basal cell carcinoma. The authors considered their results nonconclusive but in agreement with previous studies suggesting an increased risk of skin cancer in PD.

Gao et al. (2009) examined natural hair color in early adulthood and PD risk among 38,641 men (Health Professionals Follow-up Study) and 93,661 women (Nurses' Health Study) in the US, documenting 539 PD cases over 16 to 22 years of follow-up. PD risk increased with decreasing darkness of hair color: after adjustment for age, smoking, ethnicity and other covariates, pooled relative risks were 1, 1.40, 1.61, and 1.93 (95% CI 1.08-3.43) for black, brown, blond, and red hair, respectively. The association was particularly strong for onset before age 70 years (RR for red vs black hair 3.83) but not significant for onset at 70 years or older.

In a prospective study of 157,036 men and women in the same 2 US cohorts, Gao et al. (2009) identified 616 incident PD cases during 14 to 20 years of follow-up. A family history of melanoma in a first-degree relative was associated with a higher risk of PD (multivariate RR 1.85; 95% CI 1.2-2.8; p = 0.004), whereas a family history of colorectal, lung, prostate, or breast cancer was not. The authors concluded that melanoma and PD share common genetic components.

In contrast, in the PAGE study of non-Hispanic whites, Dong et al. (2014) found first primary melanoma in 18 PD cases (2.2%) and 26 controls (1.6%), a nonsignificant odds ratio of 1.32 (95% CI 0.71-2.44); hair, eye, and skin color were not significantly associated with PD risk.

Tell-Marti et al. (2015) noted that epidemiologic studies had reported co-occurrence of PD and melanoma, which prompted their study of MC1R, a gene whose common variants are associated with melanoma, as a modifier of PD risk.

### Biochemical Features

Botella-Lopez et al. (2006) found that the 180-kDa Reelin band in cerebrospinal fluid was increased in all the neurodegenerative disorders they studied, including Parkinson disease, frontotemporal dementia, progressive supranuclear palsy, and Alzheimer disease, and that its level correlated positively with CSF Tau protein.

### Inheritance

The transmission of PD has been debated. Several family studies were compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance with reduced, age-related penetrance in a subset of families (Lazzarini et al., 1994; Bonifati et al., 1995; Plante-Bordeneuve et al., 1995). Twin studies found little evidence for a genetic contribution to typical late-onset PD (Tanner et al., 1999; Wirdefeldt et al., 2004). Genealogic and family studies, however, showed that late-onset PD has a genetic component in addition to environmental factors (Sveinbjornsdottir et al., 2000; Marder et al., 2003).

Spellman (1962) reported familial cases of parkinsonism and presented evidence of a dominant trait in a patient's family.

Kondo et al. (1973) performed a genetic analysis of PD and reported evidence of a multifactorial etiology.

Young et al. (1977) examined the position of affected ancestral second-degree relatives in the pedigrees of 12 families with PD. Affected relatives were on both sides of the family more often than autosomal dominant inheritance would predict. The authors concluded that this pattern suggested a multifactorial cause for most cases, but did not entirely rule out dominant inheritance in some families.

Duvoisin et al. (1981) found zero concordance for PD in the first 12 monozygotic twin pairs examined in an ongoing twin study and suggested that genetic factors do not play a major role in the etiology of PD.

Barbeau and Pourcher (1982) found familial cases in 46% of patients with PD beginning before age 40, compared with 10 to 15% in general Parkinson populations. Patients with tremor onset often had relatives with essential tremor, whereas patients with akineto-rigid onset often had other relatives with PD.

Ward et al. (1983) studied 43 monozygotic (MZ) and 19 dizygotic (DZ) twin pairs in which the index case had definite PD. Only 1 MZ pair was definitely concordant; when pairs with questionable features were included, 4 of 48 MZ pairs and 1 of 19 DZ pairs were concordant. The frequency of PD in MZ cotwins was similar to that expected in an age- and sex-matched unrelated control group, and the authors concluded that the major etiologic factors are nongenetic.

Calne et al. (1987) reported 6 families with PD in which symptoms tended to begin at about the same calendar time regardless of patient age: across generations the mean difference in time of onset was 4.6 years, whereas the mean difference in age at onset between children and parents was 25.2 years. The authors interpreted this pattern as suggesting an environmental rather than a genetic cause.

In a review, Duvoisin (1987) noted that reported familial clusters of parkinsonism, on closer study, reflected other disorders, notably olivopontocerebellar atrophy and other forms of multiple system atrophy, and that case-control studies had not found a significantly higher prevalence of PD among first-degree relatives than in controls. The MZ twin concordance rate had been unexpectedly low and not significantly different from that in DZ twins or from the prevalence among first-degree relatives. He concluded that the best available data did not support a role of heredity in PD, although a nonhereditary, possibly congenital genetic defect was not excluded.

Lazzarini et al. (1994) studied relatives of PD patients seen consecutively over 1 year. Sibs of probands with an affected parent had a significantly higher cumulative risk of PD than sibs of probands without an affected parent. In 80 multicase families, each with 2 or more affected individuals, age-adjusted segregation ratios approached 0.5 and the proportions of affected parents and sibs were similar. Together with the distribution of affected ancestors, these findings were compatible with autosomal dominant inheritance with reduced penetrance in a subset of PD.

Markopoulou et al. (1995) concluded that parkinsonism in their Greek-American kindred appeared to be inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, and argued that this, together with the development of parkinsonism in successive generations living on 2 continents, challenged environmental factors as the main cause in this kindred. They also reported anticipation: affected members developed symptoms at ages 50 to 71 years in the third generation, 40 to 55 years in the fourth generation, and 31 years in the fifth generation.

Payami et al. (1995) studied 137 probands with idiopathic PD. The 21 probands with an affected parent, aunt, or uncle had a significantly earlier age at onset (47.7 +/- 8.8 years) than the 11 probands with only an affected sib (60.3 +/- 12.9 years) and the 105 probands with no affected sib, parent, aunt, or uncle (59.2 +/- 11.4 years) (p = 0.0001). Age at onset was on average 17 years earlier in the proband generation than in the parental generation (p = 0.0001). Because intrafamily variation in calendar year of onset was too great to suggest a common environmental exposure, they concluded that the data were most compatible with genetic anticipation, which could suggest an unstable trinucleotide repeat.

Plante-Bordeneuve et al. (1995) found that transmission in 14 PD families was compatible with an autosomal dominant model; a total segregation ratio of 0.25 suggested incomplete penetrance, which rose with age from 0 below age 30 to 0.43 above age 70. In 8 multigeneration kindreds, age at onset was earlier in children than in their parents (mean difference 26 +/- 4.6 years, p = 0.01) but similar within a generation (mean difference 4.7 +/- 5.7 years). Although ascertainment bias could not be excluded, the authors suggested that these results were compatible with anticipation.

Bonifati et al. (1995) found a positive family history of PD in 24% of 100 consecutive PD cases and 6% of spouse controls (p less than 0.001); when isolated tremor in relatives was also counted, the figures were 43% and 9% (p less than 0.001). In 22 nonconsecutive families, each with at least 2 living, personally examined PD cases (52 cases in total), crude segregation ratios were similar for parents and sibs, lifetime cumulative risk approached 0.4 in sibs and tended to be comparable in parents at later ages, and ancestral relatives were all unilaterally distributed. The authors concluded that autosomal dominant inheritance with strongly age-related penetrance was most likely.

Zareparsi et al. (1998) performed complex segregation analyses on the kindreds of 136 PD patients randomly ascertained from a clinic population. They rejected the hypotheses of a nontransmissible environmental factor, of no major gene or type, and of all Mendelian models (dominant, recessive, additive, decreasing) (P less than 0.001), and concluded that familial clustering was best explained by a rare familial factor transmitted in a non-Mendelian fashion that influences age at onset.

Montgomery et al. (1999) found that among sons and daughters of PD patients with abnormal PD test battery scores, the affected parent was much more often the father.

Tanner et al. (1999) screened 19,842 white male twins in the National Academy of Sciences/National Research Council World War II Veteran Twins Registry and identified 193 twins with PD. In 71 MZ and 90 DZ pairs with complete diagnoses, pairwise concordance was similar (0.155 MZ vs 0.111 DZ; relative risk 1.39; 95% CI 0.63-3.1). However, in 16 pairs in which at least 1 twin was diagnosed at or before age 50 years, MZ concordance was 1.0 (4 pairs) and DZ concordance was 0.167 (relative risk 6.0; 95% CI 1.69-21.26). The authors concluded that genetic factors do not play a major role in typical PD and that no genetic component is evident when the disease begins after age 50 years, but that genetic factors appear important when the disease begins at or before age 50.

Sveinbjornsdottir et al. (2000) confirmed the diagnosis of PD in 772 living and deceased Icelandic patients diagnosed over the previous 50 years. Using a computerized genealogy database covering 610,920 people in Iceland over 11 centuries, they found that patients, including a subgroup of 560 with late-onset disease (onset after age 50 years), were significantly more related to each other than matched controls, with relatedness extending beyond the nuclear family. The risk ratio for PD among relatives of patients with late-onset disease was 6.7 (95% CI 4.3-9.6) for sibs, 3.2 (95% CI 1.2-7.8) for offspring, and 2.7 (95% CI 1.6-3.9) for nephews and nieces. They concluded that late-onset PD has a genetic as well as an environmental component.

In the Old-Order Amish kindred, Racette et al. (2002) found that the mean kinship coefficient in subjects with PD or PD by history (0.036) was significantly higher (p = 0.007) than in age-matched normal Amish controls (0.016), which they took as evidence that PD is inherited in this family.

Maher et al. (2002) performed segregation analysis on the nuclear families of 948 consecutively ascertained PD index cases from 3 US centers (University of Virginia, UMDNJ-Robert Wood Johnson, and Boston University). The analyses supported a rare major Mendelian gene in both age-of-onset and susceptibility models; the age-of-onset model suggested a gene influencing age-dependent penetrance rather than susceptibility, and it was not evident whether the 2 analyses modeled the same gene.

Marder et al. (2003) estimated PD risk in first-degree relatives of 221 probands with onset at age 50 years or younger and 266 probands with onset after age 50, compared with relatives of 409 control probands. Risk was similarly increased among relatives of early-onset (RR 2.9; 95% CI 1.6-5.0) and late-onset (RR 2.7; 95% CI 1.6-4.4) probands. In late-onset families both sibs (RR 3.6; 95% CI 1.3-10.3) and parents (RR 2.5; 95% CI 1.4-4.6) were at increased risk, whereas in early-onset families sibs (RR 7.9) but not parents (RR 1.7, not significant) were at increased risk. The authors concluded that this pattern was consistent with an autosomal recessive contribution to early-onset but not late-onset PD, and that the specific genes and mode of inheritance may differ.

Wirdefeldt et al. (2004) screened 33,780 twins in 14,082 same-sex and opposite-sex pairs from the Swedish Twin Registry by telephone interview and linkage to the Swedish Inpatient Discharge Register, identifying 247 twins with possible PD and 517 with suspected parkinsonism or movement disorder. There were only 2 concordant pairs for possible PD, both female and dizygotic, and concordance remained low when the definition of affected was broadened. The best-fitting structural equation model included only environmental components of variance, with a slightly larger familial component in women. The authors concluded that genetic effects in PD are notably less important than in other complex diseases.

Using NeuroGenetics Research Consortium data, Hamza et al. (2010) estimated that the heritability of PD declined from 0.6 to 0.4 after excluding known pathogenic and susceptibility loci but remained significant, suggesting that additional genes exist.

### Mapping

In a large kindred with autosomal dominant PD, Polymeropoulos et al. (1996) found linkage to markers on chromosome 4q21-q23, with a maximum lod score of 6.00 for marker D4S2380. Polymeropoulos et al. (1997) noted that this susceptibility gene in a large Italian kindred had been mapped to the long arm of chromosome 4.

Scott et al. (2001) performed a genomic screen with 344 markers in 174 families with multiple individuals with idiopathic PD, ascertained through 13 clinic populations in the continental United States and Australia (870 family members: 378 affected, 379 unaffected, 113 unclear). They found evidence for linkage to chromosomes 17q (MLOD = 2.28; LOD = 2.62), 8p (MLOD = 2.01; LOD = 2.22), and 5q (MLOD = 2.39; LOD = 1.50) overall and in families with late-onset PD, and to chromosome 9q (MLOD = 1.52; LOD = 2.59) in families with both levodopa-responsive and levodopa-nonresponsive patients. Martin et al. (2001) noted that the 17q21 linkage region contains the tau gene.

In a genomic screen for age at onset in 174 PD families and 449 Alzheimer disease families, Li et al. (2002) estimated the heritability of age at onset at 40 to 60% in both data sets. In PD they found significant linkage of age at onset to chromosome 1p (lod = 3.41). Linkage on chromosomes 6 and 10 was found independently in both data sets, and a unified analysis identified a single peak on 10q between D10S1239 and D10S1237 (maximum lod 2.62), suggesting a common gene affecting age at onset in both diseases. Li et al. (2003) used 'genomic convergence' to prioritize candidate genes in this 15-cM region on 10q and found 4 genes with significantly different hippocampal expression in Alzheimer disease: stearoyl-CoA desaturase, NDUFB8, PRSS11, and GSTO1.

Pankratz et al. (2002) studied 203 neurologically assessed PD sib pairs, excluded 22 families with parkin mutations, and performed a genome screen in 160 multiplex families. With verified PD only (96 sib pairs from 90 families), the highest lod scores were on chromosome X (2.1) and chromosome 2 (1.9); with all examined individuals counted as affected (170 sib pairs from 160 families), they rose to 2.7 and 2.5, respectively. Lod scores above 1.5 were also found on chromosomes 4, 5, and 13.

Pankratz et al. (2003) performed genome-wide nonparametric linkage analysis in 754 affected individuals comprising 425 sib pairs from 362 multiplex PD families. With verified PD only (216 sib pairs), the maximum lod score was 3.4 on chromosome 2; with all affected individuals, a lod score of 3.1 was obtained on the X chromosome. In 85 families with a very strong family history, there was strong evidence of linkage to chromosome 2 (lod = 4.9) and a lod of 2.4 on chromosome 14; in the 277 other families, linkage was detected to chromosomes 10 (lod = 2.4) and X (lod = 3.2).

In a 2-tiered whole-genome association study, Maraganore et al. (2005) genotyped 198,345 SNPs in 443 sib pairs discordant for PD, then 1,793 PD-associated SNPs in 332 matched case-unrelated control pairs. Eleven SNPs were associated with PD (P less than 0.01) in both tiers with the same direction of effect; the lowest combined P value (P = 7.62 x 10(-6)) was for a SNP within the SEMA5A gene, and a second top SNP tagged the PARK11 late-onset susceptibility locus (P = 1.70 x 10(-5)). In tier 2b, the 2 SNPs with the lowest combined P values (P = 9.07 x 10(-6) and P = 2.96 x 10(-5)) tagged the PARK10 locus. The authors stated that independent replication was needed.

In a genomewide association study of 857 familial PD cases and 867 controls, mostly Caucasian and non-Hispanic, Pankratz et al. (2009) found no result meeting genomewide significance. The strongest association was with SNPs in the GAK/DGKQ region on chromosome 4p (rs11248060; p = 3.4 x 10(-6), OR = 1.69), improving to p = 2.5 x 10(-7) on meta-analysis with a previous GWAS. They suggested GAK as a promising candidate because it had been found among genes differentially expressed in PD substantia nigra pars compacta (1.56-fold change); SNPs in TMEM175, between GAK and DGKQ, showed no suggestive association.

In a genome-wide linkage screen of 5,824 SNPs in 278 families of European, non-Hispanic descent, Gao et al. (2009) found significant linkage on chromosome 18q11 (maximum lod = 4.1) and suggestive linkage on 3q25 (maximum lod = 2.5).

In a GWAS and 2 replication studies in a total of 2,011 Japanese PD patients and 18,381 controls, Satake et al. (2009) identified a new susceptibility locus on chromosome 1q32 (P = 1.52 x 10(-12)), designated PARK16, and BST1 on 4p15 as a second new risk locus (P = 3.94 x 10(-9)). Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) replicated the PARK16 locus in Europeans (rs823128, OR = 0.66, combined P = 7.29 x 10(-8); replication cohort of 2,816 cases and 3,401 controls); the locus spans SNORA72, NUCKS1, RAB7L1, SLC41A1, and PM20D1. They failed to replicate the BST1 signal, possibly owing to low minor allele frequency in Caucasians.

In a GWAS of 2,000 Caucasian PD patients and 1,986 controls from the NeuroGenetics Research Consortium, Hamza et al. (2010) found a novel association with the HLA region on chromosome 6p21.3, peaking at rs3129882 in intron 1 of HLA-DRA (P = 2.9 x 10(-8)); the association replicated in 2 independent datasets (meta-analysis OR 1.26, P = 1.9 x 10(-10)).

### Heterogeneity

In the same elderly Bavarian population in which they assessed PD prevalence, Trenkwalder et al. (1995) found other causes of parkinsonism, including drug-induced parkinsonism (4 of 982), vascular parkinsonism (2 of 982), multiple system atrophy (3 of 982), Fahr disease (1 of 982), and normal-pressure hydrocephalus (4 of 982); 50% of parkinsonism cases were newly diagnosed.

Sandy et al. (1996) noted that, in contrast with studies in older-onset PD reporting modestly increased risk with CYP2D6 B and A alleles, CYP2D6 alleles were not associated with young-onset PD, and suggested that young-onset and older-onset PD may differ in risk factors.

Vaughan et al. (2001) noted that 2 genes (SNCA and parkin) and 2 further loci had been implicated in familial PD, although large families are rare, heritability in the general population is low, and the disorder is genetically heterogeneous; evidence for a role of UCHL1 was less clear.

In their genomic screen, Scott et al. (2001) found significant linkage to the parkin region on chromosome 6 (MLOD = 5.07; LOD = 5.47) in families with at least 1 individual with onset before age 40 years, and concluded that parkin is important in early-onset PD while multiple genetic factors may be important in late-onset PD.

Pankratz et al. (2002) identified parkin mutations in 22 of their sib-pair families and excluded them from the genome screen to reduce heterogeneity. Pankratz et al. (2003) found linkage to chromosome 10 (lod = 2.3) in 23 families with patients carrying a mutation in only 1 parkin allele, and concluded that gene-by-gene interactions are important in PD susceptibility.

In the Amish kindred, Racette et al. (2002) found no mutations in genes with known PD-causing mutations, no haplotype cosegregating with the disease in any previously linked region, and no increased homozygosity for markers in these regions among definite cases, suggesting that a novel gene or genes may be involved.

Gao et al. (2009) found that their 18q11 and 3q25 linkage results were strongest in families not previously screened for linkage, and simulations suggested that this was likely due to locus heterogeneity rather than random error.

Lees et al. (2009) noted that susceptibility genes, including alpha-synuclein, LRRK2 and GBA, have shown that genetic predisposition is an important causal factor in PD. Neumann et al. (2009) suggested that GBA mutations are the most common genetic factor identified for PD to date, being found in British patients at a higher frequency than any other known PD gene.

Comparing Japanese and European GWAS results, Satake et al. (2009) found that PARK16, SNCA and LRRK2 were shared risk loci whereas BST1 and MAPT showed population differences. Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) likewise noted that the MAPT association was absent in the Japanese cohort whereas SNCA association replicated across populations, suggesting population-specific genetic heterogeneity.

Mata et al. (2011) did not replicate the associations of PD with PARK16 (rs823156 near SLC41A1, p = 0.09; rs947211, p = 0.50), PARK17 (GAK rs11248051, p = 0.60), PARK18 (HLA-DRA rs3129882, OR 1.01, p = 0.88), or a chromosome 8 intergenic SNP among 1,445 Spanish patients and 1,161 controls, in the overall sample or any age stratum. For PARK18, their sample had 99% power at the originally reported OR of 1.31. They suggested population structure, insufficient power (particularly for PARK16), or population-specific environmental interactions as explanations.

### Molecular Genetics

Using PCR and SSCP analysis after HaeIII digestion, Kurth et al. (1993) found a polymorphism in the MAOB gene (309860). Allele 1 and allele 2 frequencies were 0.45 and 0.55 in 121 controls, compared with 0.62 and 0.38 in 46 PD patients; allele 1 was associated with a 2.03-fold relative risk (CI 1.44-2.61; p less than 0.02). A MAOA polymorphism showed no difference.

Bordet et al. (1994) analyzed CYP2D6 (124030) poor-metabolizer mutations by PCR and XbaI RFLP analysis in 105 Caucasian patients with idiopathic PD and 15 with diffuse Lewy body disease. The rate of poor metabolizers and the frequency of the D6-B mutation did not differ significantly between the groups, and they found no relationship between CYP2D6 impairment and the spread of neuropathologic lesions.

Parboosingh et al. (1995) screened 107 unrelated familial and sporadic PD patients for mutations in SOD1, SOD2, and catalase (CAT; 115500) by single-strand conformation analysis and found no causative mutations. A glycine-to-aspartic acid substitution in exon 9 of CAT was found in 1 patient with familial PD who had decreased red cell catalase activity, but the authors concluded that it was most likely silent and not the cause of PD in this family.

Ho et al. (1995) found that an SSCP in MAOB was an A or G located 36 bases upstream of the intron 13-exon 14 boundary, and found no significant association between this polymorphism and PD, in contrast with a previous study.

Comparing CYP2D6 genotypes in 108 patients with young-onset (age 51 years or younger) PD and 236 controls, Sandy et al. (1996) found no significant differences in allele frequencies; the B allele frequency was 0.14 in cases and 0.20 in controls (p = 0.10), and neither carriage of B alleles (OR 0.58; 95% CI 0.33-1.00) nor of nonfunctional alleles (OR 0.68; 95% CI 0.41-1.13) was associated with increased risk.

Because weaver mice show progressive postnatal loss of mesencephalic dopaminergic cells, Bandmann et al. (1996) sequenced the H5 pore region of KCNJ7, the human homolog of the weaver gene, in familial and sporadic PD and found it normal in every case.

Polymeropoulos et al. (1997) identified a mutation in the alpha-synuclein gene (SNCA) in the large Italian kindred with linkage to 4q and in 3 unrelated families of Greek origin with autosomal dominant PD.

In a case-control study of 224 Taiwanese PD patients and 197 matched controls genotyped by PCR-RFLP, Wu et al. (2001) found that the MAOB intron 13 G genotype (G in men and G/G in women) was associated with a 2.07-fold increased relative risk of PD. The COMT (116790) Val158Met polymorphism alone was not associated with risk, but risk was synergistically increased in carriers of both the COMT L (low-activity) and MAOB G genotypes; the authors noted that these combined genotypes were rare and the finding needed confirmation.

In a patient with mild Gaucher disease and parkinsonism, Tayebi et al. (2001) found 2 GBA1 mutations, L444P and D409H, as well as a duplication on the maternal allele including the glucocerebrosidase pseudogene, the metaxin gene, and a pseudometaxin/metaxin fusion, which they suggested could contribute to the atypical phenotype.

Martin et al. (2001) tested 5 SNPs in the tau gene (MAPT) using family-based association tests in 1,056 individuals (426 affected) from 235 families from the United States and Australia. Three SNPs showed significant association (SNP 3, P = 0.03; SNP 9i, P = 0.04; SNP 11, P = 0.04), and haplotype analysis showed one haplotype positively (P = 0.009) and another negatively (P = 0.007) associated, with substantial linkage disequilibrium among SNPs. They concluded that tau is a susceptibility gene for idiopathic PD.

Mattila et al. (2002) reported an association between a polymorphism at position -511 in the IL1B gene (147720) and PD in Finnish patients.

In a community-based study of 209 PD patients and 488 controls of European (mostly French) ancestry enrolled through the French agricultural workers' health insurance organization, Levecque et al. (2003) found associations with a polymorphism in exon 22 of NOS2 (163730) (OR for AA carriers 0.50, 95% CI 0.29-0.86, P = 0.01) and an exon 29 polymorphism of NOS1 (163731) (OR for T allele carriers 1.53, 95% CI 1.08-2.16, P = 0.02), but not an exon 18 NOS1 polymorphism (OR 1.20, P = 0.30). The NOS1 polymorphisms interacted significantly with current or past smoking (exon 18, P = 0.05; exon 29, P = 0.04), and the authors proposed NOS1 and NOS2 as modifier genes in PD.

Varkonyi et al. (2003) found that each of 4 patients with Gaucher disease and parkinsonism had a different Gaucher genotype, with 4 alleles carrying the N370S mutation; no mutations were found in parkin or SNCA.

Van der Walt et al. (2003) genotyped 10 SNPs defining European mtDNA haplogroups in 609 white PD patients and 340 white controls. Haplogroups J (OR 0.55; 95% CI 0.34-0.91; P = 0.02) and K (OR 0.52; 95% CI 0.30-0.90; P = 0.02) had a lower risk of PD than haplogroup H. The 10398G SNP in MT-ND3 (516002), which defines haplogroups J and K and causes a threonine-to-alanine substitution in the ND3 subunit of complex I, was strongly associated with lower risk (OR 0.53; 95% CI 0.39-0.73; P = 0.0001), with a stronger effect in women. The 9055A SNP in MT-ATP6 (516060) was protective in women (OR 0.45; 95% CI 0.22-0.93; P = 0.03).

Chan et al. (2003) genotyped the M1, M2, and M3 slow acetylator polymorphisms of NAT2 (612182) by PCR-RFLP in 99 Hong Kong Chinese PD patients and 126 controls. The slow acetylator genotype was more frequent in patients (68.7% vs 28.6%; adjusted OR 5.53, 95% CI 3.08-9.92), and smoking did not change the association.

Marx et al. (2003) screened the synphilin-1 gene (SNCAIP) in 328 German patients with familial or sporadic PD and identified a heterozygous arg621-to-cys (R621C) mutation in 2 apparently sporadic patients; it was absent from 702 control chromosomes. In SH-SY5Y cells under proteasomal inhibition, C621 synphilin-1 formed fewer cytoplasmic inclusions than wildtype, and expressing cells were more susceptible to staurosporine-induced death.

In age-at-onset association studies of 635 PD patients (1,362 samples) and 1,773 Alzheimer disease patients (2,814 samples), Li et al. (2003) found significant association for GSTO1 (P = 0.007) and the neighboring GSTO2 (P = 0.005), but not for 3 other candidate genes.

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) screened 99 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with idiopathic PD, 74 with Alzheimer disease, and 1,543 Ashkenazi controls for the 6 most common Ashkenazi GBA1 mutations. Thirty-one PD patients (31.3%) had 1 or 2 mutant alleles: 23 were heterozygous for N370S, 4 heterozygous for 84GG, 3 homozygous for N370S, and 1 heterozygous for R496H, compared with 4.1% of Alzheimer patients and 6.2% of controls.

Kwok et al. (2005) identified 2 functional SNPs in GSK3B (605004). The T allele of promoter SNP rs334558 had greater transcriptional activity in vitro, and the intronic SNP rs6438552 regulated splice acceptor choice, its T allele being associated with higher levels of transcripts lacking exons 9 and 11, which correlated with increased tau phosphorylation. The rs6438552 T allele was more frequent in PD than in control brains, which also had more of this transcript and more tau phosphorylation. A gene-gene interaction was found between GSK3B rs334558 T/T and MAPT H1/H1 (p = 0.009); a GSK3B haplotype carrying T at both SNPs had an OR of 1.64 in H1/H2 and H2/H2 individuals (p = 0.007) and 0.68 in H1/H1 individuals (p less than 0.001).

Toft et al. (2006) screened 311 Norwegian PD patients and 474 controls for GBA1 N370S and L444P. Seven patients (2.3%) and 8 controls (1.7%) carried a mutant allele (p = 0.58), and the authors found no indication of increased susceptibility in Norway.

Tan et al. (2007) studied GBA mutations and PD risk in Chinese patients. In a separate study of 418 PD patients and 468 matched controls, Tan et al. (2007) found no association between the CYP1A2 (124060) -163C-A polymorphism (rs762551), which makes carriers fast caffeine metabolizers, and PD (P = 0.08).

In 721 PD patients, 57 patients with dementia with Lewy bodies, and 554 controls from the Seattle area, Mata et al. (2008) found that heterozygosity for GBA1 N370S or L444P was more frequent in PD (2.9%; P less than 0.001) and DLB (3.5%; P = 0.045) than in controls (0.4%), and estimated a modest (approximately 3%) population-attributable risk in individuals of European ancestry. Gutti et al. (2008) emphasized the need for appropriate genotyping strategies for GBA mutations in PD cohorts.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) screened 420 Israeli Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients, 333 elderly controls, and 3,805 young controls for 8 GBA1 mutations, 2 associated with mild Gaucher disease (N370S, R496H) and 6 with severe Gaucher disease (84GG, IVS2+1, V394L, D409H, L444P, RecTL). Carrier frequency was 17.9% in PD patients, 4.2% in elderly controls, and 6.35% in young controls. Patients carrying LRRK2 G2019S had earlier onset, and more than one-third of patients carried a mutation in GBA1 or LRRK2.

Pankratz et al. (2009) found association between familial PD and the SNCA region (rs356229 minor allele, additive p = 5.5 x 10(-5), OR = 1.35), with associated SNPs in intron 4 and the 3-prime region; rs356229 showed modest LD with rs356219, previously associated with SNCA mRNA levels. They also found association with the C17orf69/MAPT region under a recessive model (rs1724425; p = 2.0 x 10(-5), OR = 0.56; meta-analysis p = 9.8 x 10(-6)), with minor alleles tagging the protective H2 haplotype also featuring in the additive meta-analysis.

In a case-control study nested in 2 US cohorts (272 PD cases and 1,185 controls), Gao et al. (2009) found that homozygosity for the loss-of-function MC1R (155555) Arg151Cys polymorphism, strongly associated with red hair, was associated with increased PD risk relative to Arg/Arg (adjusted RR 3.15; 95% CI 1.06-9.37); heterozygotes had RR 1.23.

Neumann et al. (2009) screened GBA1 in 790 British PD patients and 257 matched controls and found mutations in 33 patients (4.18%) and 3 controls (1.17%) (P = 0.01; OR 3.7; 95% CI 1.12-12.14). Fourteen different mutations were identified, including 3 previously undescribed ones, K7E, D443N, and G193E.

Sidransky et al. (2009) pooled GBA1 genotype data from 16 centers on 4 continents, comprising 5,691 PD patients (780 Ashkenazi Jews) and 4,898 controls (387 Ashkenazi Jews). Every center found more mutations in patients than controls; the Mantel-Haenszel OR for any GBA mutation was 5.43 (95% CI 3.89-7.57) after excluding the Tokyo and Norway centers. Screening only for N370S and L444P, a mutation was present in 15.3% of Ashkenazi patients vs 3.4% of Ashkenazi controls and 3.2% of non-Ashkenazi patients vs 0.6% of controls (OR 3.96 for N370S and 6.73 for L444P); N370S was not found in any Asian subject. With 6 to 8 mutations screened, 19.6% of Ashkenazi patients were carriers (OR 6.48), and full sequencing showed carriers in 6.9% of non-Ashkenazi patients (OR 6.51); 54% of 117 mutations identified were neither N370S nor L444P, and 22 of 43 subjects with L444P carried recombinant alleles including pseudogene sequence. Seventeen patients carried 2 mutations. The nonpathogenic variants E326K and T369M were not significantly associated with PD. The authors concluded that GBA mutations are likely a susceptibility factor rather than a Mendelian cause of PD and among the most common risk factors identified to date.

In a 2-stage GWAS in individuals of European ancestry from the US, Germany, and Britain (1,713 cases and 3,978 controls in stage I, replication in 3,361 cases and 4,573 controls), Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) observed strong association signals at SNCA (rs2736990, OR = 1.23, combined P = 2.24 x 10(-16)) and at MAPT (rs393152, OR = 0.77, P = 1.95 x 10(-16)), demonstrating a role for common variants in typical PD. Three of 4 associated SNCA SNPs lay in the 3-prime LD block; the REP1 risk allele was in LD with 3-prime risk alleles and its association was not independent, and SNCA risk SNPs differed from those associated with multiple system atrophy. MAPT risk alleles were in LD with the H1 haplotype, and in 133 frontal cortex samples the rs393152 risk allele was associated with higher MAPT and lower LRRC37A expression. They also found supporting evidence for LRRK2: rs1491923, 0.17 Mb upstream, gave a combined OR of 1.14 (P = 1.55 x 10(-5)).

Satake et al. (2009) found strong associations between PD and SNCA (P = 7.35 x 10(-17)) and LRRK2 (P = 2.72 x 10(-8)) in Japanese individuals, and concluded that autosomal dominant parkinsonism loci are involved in typical PD.

Elstner et al. (2009) found association between idiopathic PD and an intron 8 SNP in PDXK (179020), rs2010795, in 676 German patients and 972 controls (p = 3.2 x 10(-4); OR 1.319), replicated in British and Italian cohorts (combined 1,232 patients and 2,802 controls, p = 1.18 x 10(-7); OR 1.304); a 3-prime UTR SNP, rs2070535, was also associated in the German cohort (p = 6.4 x 10(-4); OR 0.783). Vilarino-Guell et al. (2010) also studied PDXK in PD, and Guella et al. (2010) found no association of rs2010795 with PD in an Italian population.

Plaitakis et al. (2010) found that a rare T1492G variant in the X-linked GLUD2 gene, encoding the brain glutamate dehydrogenase hGDH2 and resulting in a ser445-to-ala substitution in its regulatory domain, interacted significantly with age at onset. Hemizygous individuals developed PD 6 to 13 years earlier than those with other genotypes in 2 Greek PD groups and 1 North American cohort; heterozygous females did not show this effect.

In 2 case-control cohorts totaling 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects, Tan et al. (2010) found that PARK16, PARK8, and PARK1, but not BST1, were associated with PD, with PARK16 SNPs associated with decreased risk and PARK1 and PARK8 SNPs with increased risk. Pooled with 2 Japanese cohorts (1,366 patients and 16,669 controls), there was robust association with all 4 loci, of similar strength in Chinese and Japanese.

Hamza et al. (2010) confirmed associations with SNCA (rs356220, P = 3.4 x 10(-11)) and MAPT (rs199533, P = 1.3 x 10(-6)) and replicated association with GAK (602052) on 4p16 (rs11248051; combined with Pankratz et al. data OR 1.46, P = 3.2 x 10(-9)). They did not replicate PARK16.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 patients with early-onset PD (onset at 50 years or younger) from 13 centers, without regard to family history, for mutations in SNCA, PRKN, PINK1, DJ1, LRRK2, and GBA1. Mutations were found in 158 (16.6%): 64 in PRKN, 35 LRRK2 G2019S, 64 in GBA1, and 1 in DJ1. GBA1 carriers included 18 L444P heterozygotes, 38 N370S heterozygotes, and 2 N370S homozygotes. They identified 31 LRRK2 G2019S carriers (30 heterozygotes and 1 homozygote) and 1 G2385R carrier. Thirty-three patients were PRKN heterozygotes and 27 carried 2 PRKN mutations. Seven patients carried mutations in more than 1 gene, none of whom reported PD in a first-degree relative.

In 1,445 PD patients and 1,161 controls from northern Spain, Mata et al. (2011) found association with SNCA rs356219 (OR 1.23; p = 5.5 x 10(-4)) and MAPT rs1800547 (OR 0.79; p = 3.1 x 10(-4)), significant only in the late-onset group; the rs1800547 A allele, a surrogate for the H1 haplotype, was associated under a dominant model (OR 1.23; p = 1.1 x 10(-2)).

Dong et al. (2014) tested pigmentation and melanoma GWAS SNPs in the PAGE study (808 cases, 1,623 controls) and the IPDGC dataset (5,333 cases, 12,019 controls). ASIP (600201) rs4911414 was associated with PD in PAGE (p = 0.001) but not in IPDGC (p = 0.43), none of 360 SNPs was associated after correction, and MC1R rs1805007 was not associated in IPDGC (OR 1.06, P = 0.37).

Sequencing MC1R in 870 Spanish PD patients and 736 controls, Tell-Marti et al. (2015) found that the melanoma risk variant R160W (rs1805008) was marginally associated with PD (OR 2.10, adjusted p = 0.009, Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.063).

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found that Ashkenazi PD patients carrying GBA1 mutations had a younger age at onset than noncarriers (mean 60.0 vs 64.2 years; P = 0.04).

Gan-Or et al. (2008) found that 29% of GBA1 carriers among PD patients carried a severe Gaucher mutation, compared with 7% of carriers among young controls. Severe mutations increased PD risk 13.6-fold and mild mutations 2.2-fold, and mean age at onset was 55.7 years with severe mutations, 57.9 years with mild mutations, and 60.7 years in patients without known GBA1 or LRRK2 mutations. Gan-Or et al. (2009) reported a differential phenotype in patients with severe versus mild GBA1 mutations.

Sidransky et al. (2009) found that GBA1 mutation carriers had an earlier mean age at onset than noncarriers (54.9 vs 58.8 years; p less than 0.001), more often reported a first- or second-degree relative with PD (24.0% vs 17.8%), had significantly less asymmetric onset, bradykinesia, resting tremor and rigidity, and more cognitive changes (26.3% vs 19.0%).

Among 600 Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients, Gan-Or et al. (2010) found that LRRK2 G2019S carriers more often reported muscle stiffness (p = 0.007) and balance disturbances (p = 0.008) as initial symptoms, whereas GBA1 carriers more often reported slowness (p = 0.021).

Tan et al. (2010) observed a trend toward a stronger protective effect of PARK16 SNPs in sporadic than in familial PD, and in older than in younger subjects.

Hamza et al. (2010) found that the HLA-DRA rs3129882 association was uniform across strata and particularly strong for sporadic PD (P = 5.5 x 10(-10)), late-onset PD (P = 2.4 x 10(-8)), and men (P = 1.1 x 10(-7)), with no interaction with smoking, coffee, NSAIDs, SNCA, or MAPT. Compared with carriers of 0 or 1 risk allele at SNCA, MAPT, GAK and HLA, PD risk was doubled with 4 risk alleles (OR 2.49) and 5-fold higher with 6 or more (OR 4.95).

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that GBA1 L444P carriers had higher (worse) UPDRS-III motor scores than noncarriers after adjustment, and that LRRK2 G2019S carriers more often had the postural instability and gait difficulty motor phenotype (96%). PRKN carriers had an earlier age at onset.

### Pathogenesis

Kissel and Andre (1976) suggested that a genetically determined anomaly of dopamine metabolism was probably responsible for PD with anosmia in their monozygotic twins.

Calne and Langston (1983) suggested that in most cases the cause of PD may be an environmental, possibly toxic, factor superimposed on slow, sustained age-related neuronal loss. Langston et al. (1983) described 4 persons who developed marked parkinsonism after intravenous use of an illicit drug containing mainly MPTP, and proposed that MPTP selectively damages cells in the substantia nigra.

Barbeau et al. (1985) found that significantly more of 40 PD patients than 40 controls had partially or totally defective debrisoquine 4-hydroxylation, and that poor metabolizers tended to have earlier onset. They postulated that PD results from environmental factors acting on genetically susceptible individuals against a background of normal aging. Calne et al. (1987) found no correlation between disease and degree of consanguinity in their families and suggested that some cases result from early subclinical environmental damage followed by age-related neuronal loss.

Di Monte (1991) reviewed evidence for impaired mitochondrial function in PD, including the action of the MPTP metabolite MPP+ at complex I and reduced complex I activity in patients, and suggested that abnormal mtDNA, which encodes 7 complex I subunits, might play a role. Muqit et al. (2006) noted that genetic advances suggested mitochondrial dysfunction may be the primary defect.

Nussbaum and Polymeropoulos (1997) suggested that at least some familial PD with diffuse Lewy bodies results from an abnormal protein that interferes with normal protein degradation, leading to inclusions and neuronal death, and proposed shared mechanisms with Alzheimer disease mutations.

Clarke et al. (2000) found that neuronal death followed exponential kinetics in several models and in PD and Huntington disease, arguing against cumulative damage and in favor of a 'one-hit' model in which a single random event initiates cell death.

Auluck et al. (2002) found that Lewy bodies in human brain immunostained for molecular chaperones. Maher et al. (2002) suggested that gene-environment interactions with reduced penetrance may explain the low MZ twin concordance for PD. Warner and Schapira (2003) concluded that most cases probably result from interplay between genetic and environmental factors.

Teismann et al. (2003) found that COX-2 was upregulated in brain dopaminergic neurons of PD patients and MPTP-treated mice and concluded that it has a critical role in the pathogenesis and selectivity of neurodegeneration.

Cooper et al. (2006) found that the earliest defect following alpha-synuclein expression in yeast was a block in ER-to-Golgi trafficking, and that the largest class of toxicity modifiers acted at this step, including the Rab GTPase Ypt1p. Chan et al. (2007) suggested that substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons rely unusually on L-type Cav1.3 calcium channels for pacemaking, increasingly with age, making them vulnerable to stressors.

Tan et al. (2007) found that caffeine intake was inversely associated with PD (P = 2.01 x 10(-5); OR 0.47 for high vs low drinkers), with no interaction with CYP1A2 genotype.

Sidransky et al. (2009) noted that both gain-of-function (protein aggregation or lysosomal dysfunction) and loss-of-function (ceramide fluctuation) mechanisms had been postulated for the link between GBA1 and PD.

By expression profiling of laser-microdissected nigral dopaminergic neurons from 8 PD patients and 9 controls, Elstner et al. (2009) found MTND2, PDXK and SRGAP3 upregulated and TRAPPC4 downregulated; mtDNA-encoded respiratory chain genes were upregulated and 13 nuclear-encoded ones downregulated. They suggested that PDXK, which produces pyridoxal 5-prime-phosphate, a cofactor of dopa decarboxylase, might influence PD risk.

Plaitakis et al. (2010) showed that the Ser445Ala hGDH2 enzyme had increased basal activity resistant to GTP inhibition but sensitive to estrogens, and concluded that this gain-of-function variant hastens onset in hemizygous subjects, probably through increased glutamate oxidative dehydrogenation, with estrogen modification possibly explaining the lack of effect in heterozygous females.

Using PET with setoperone F 18, Ballanger et al. (2010) found increased serotonin 2A receptor binding in the ventral visual pathway and prefrontal, orbitofrontal, and insular cortex of 7 nondemented PD patients with visual hallucinations compared with 7 without.

Hamza et al. (2010) noted that rs3129882 is a cis-regulatory variant correlated with expression of HLA-DRA, HLA-DQA2 and HLA-DRB5, supporting a role for neuroinflammation and immunity. Raj et al. (2014), studying T cells and monocytes from 461 healthy individuals, found PD and Alzheimer disease susceptibility alleles enriched for monocyte-specific cis-eQTL effects, suggesting that the myeloid compartment drives the inflammatory component of susceptibility.

Berman et al. (2011) found increased EAAC1 expression in dopaminergic neurons of mouse and human midbrain, maintained in surviving neurons of 4 PD brains, and suggested a contributory role for glutathione depletion in dopaminergic neuronal death.

Minones-Moyano et al. (2011) found decreased miR-34b and miR-34c in PD brain areas at Braak stages 4 and 5 and also at premotor stages 1-3. In SH-SY5Y cells, their depletion reduced viability, altered mitochondrial function, caused oxidative stress, lowered ATP, and reduced DJ1 and Parkin expression.

Chung et al. (2013) found that iPS cell-derived cortical neurons from a Contursi kindred patient with SNCA A53T, compared with isogenic corrected controls, showed nitrosative stress, ER accumulation of the ERAD substrates GCase and nicastrin, and ER stress, with similar findings in postmortem A53T cortex. Neurons from an Iowa kindred patient with SNCA triplication phenocopied these defects, and sporadic PD cortex showed the same trend of ER GCase accumulation. Tardiff et al. (2013) showed that the compound NAB acts through the Rsp5/Nedd4 ubiquitin ligase to restore endosomal trafficking impaired by alpha-synuclein.

Sulzer et al. (2017) studied 67 PD patients and 36 controls and found HLA DRB1*15:01/DRB5*01:01 in about one-third of patients vs about 15% of controls. Alpha-synuclein peptides from 2 regions, near the N terminus (Y39) and the C terminus (phosphorylated S129), elicited mainly IL-5 responses in about 40% of patients, largely from CD4+ T cells. DRB1*15:01 and DRB5*01:01 bound the Y39 epitope with high affinity, and DRB1*15:01, DRB5*01:01, DQB1*03:04 and A*11:01 together accounted for every Y39 responder. Five of 19 patients but no controls responded to short peptides presented by MHC class I, including a 9-mer binding HLA-A*11:01.

Mittal et al. (2017) found that beta-2-adrenoreceptor (ADRB2) agonists lowered SNCA mRNA and protein in neuroblastoma cells and rat neurons, that Adrb2 knockout or antagonism increased SNCA expression, and that regulation occurred through H3K27 acetylation at the SNCA promoter and intron 4 enhancers. In iPSC-derived cells from a patient with SNCA triplication, clenbuterol reduced SNCA expression and rotenone-induced mitochondrial superoxide and increased viability.

### Diagnosis

Hughes et al. (1992) examined the brains of 100 patients diagnosed prospectively by neurologists with idiopathic PD. Seventy-six had nigral Lewy bodies; the 24 without had progressive supranuclear palsy, multiple system atrophy, Alzheimer disease, Alzheimer-type pathology, or basal ganglia vascular disease. Retrospective use of recommended criteria improved diagnostic accuracy to 82%.

### Clinical Management

In a phase 1 trial, Gill et al. (2003) infused GDNF directly into the putamen of 5 PD patients. After 1 year there were no serious clinical side effects; off-medication UPDRS motor score improved by 39% and activities of daily living by 61%, medication-induced dyskinesias fell by 64%, and putamen [18F]dopamine storage increased by 28% at 18 months.

Teismann et al. (2003) suggested COX-2 inhibitors as potential PD therapies.

Voon et al. (2007) compared 21 PD patients who developed pathological gambling on medication with 42 PD patients without compulsive behaviors. Younger onset, novelty seeking, and personal or family history of alcohol use disorders predicted pathological gambling with 83.7% accuracy.

Lees et al. (2009) stated that dopamine replacement therapy considerably reduces motor handicap and that treatment of depression, pain, constipation and nighttime problems can improve quality of life.

In a randomized crossover trial in 33 PD patients homozygous for COMT Val158Met (17 Val/Val, 16 Met/Met), Corvol et al. (2011) found that entacapone increased best ON time (39 +/- 10 vs 9 +/- 9 minutes, p = 0.04) and levodopa AUC (+62% vs +34%, p = 0.01) more in Val/Val patients.

Chung et al. (2013) found that NAB2, which activates the Rsp5/Nedd4 pathway, or NEDD4 overexpression lowered nitric oxide and restored post-ER forms of nicastrin and GCase in A53T patient neurons.

### Population Genetics

Kessler (1978) reviewed PD from an epidemiologic perspective. In a hospital-based survey of 551 patients from Helsinki and Vancouver, Teravainen et al. (1986) concluded that PD may be starting earlier than previously.

In a door-to-door survey of 982 individuals older than 65 years in 2 rural Bavarian villages, Trenkwalder et al. (1995) found a PD prevalence of 0.71% (95% CI 0.19-1.23%), with male predominance (5 men, 2 women).

Tanner et al. (1999) found a concordance-adjusted PD prevalence of 8.67 per 1,000 among 19,842 white male twin veterans.

Racette et al. (2002) calculated a minimum PD prevalence of 552 per 100,000 among people aged 40 years and older in the index Old-Order Amish community, higher than in the general population.

In a door-to-door survey of 15,000 people aged 50 years and older in central Singapore, Tan et al. (2004) identified 46 people with PD, 16 newly diagnosed; age-adjusted prevalence was 0.30% and was the same in Chinese (0.33%), Malays (0.29%), and Indians (0.28%), comparable to Western countries.

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found that Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients had greater odds of being GBA1 mutation carriers than Alzheimer patients (OR 10.8) or controls (OR 7.0).

Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) estimated the combined population attributable risk of the identified loci at approximately 25%. Hamza et al. (2010) found that the rs3129882 risk allele frequency in controls varied by region (0.36 in Washington to 0.46 in New York), being low in Northern Europeans and high in Southern Europeans, particularly Italians.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found that mutations were more common in early-onset patients with onset at age 30 or younger (40.6% vs 14.6%), with Jewish ancestry (32.4% vs 13.7%), and with a first-degree family history (23.9% vs 15.1%), the last association being absent in Jewish patients.

In the whole Norwegian population over 11 years, Mittal et al. (2017) found that salbutamol use was associated with reduced PD risk (rate ratio 0.66) and propranolol use with increased risk (rate ratio 2.20).

### Animal Model

Auluck et al. (2002) found that directed expression of Hsp70 prevented alpha-synuclein-associated dopaminergic neuron loss in Drosophila, whereas interfering with endogenous chaperone activity accelerated toxicity.

In MPTP-treated mice, Teismann et al. (2003) showed that COX-2 induction was JNK/c-Jun-dependent and that targeting COX-2 protected against neurodegeneration by preventing dopamine-quinone formation.

Scherzer et al. (2003) profiled gene expression in alpha-synuclein transgenic flies and found 51 signature transcripts tightly associated with alpha-synuclein expression, with changes already at the presymptomatic stage; these were normal in tau transgenic flies.

Cooper et al. (2006) showed that elevated Rab1 expression protected against alpha-synuclein-induced dopaminergic neuron loss in animal models.

Kreitzer and Malenka (2007) found that endocannabinoid-mediated LTD at indirect-pathway striatal synapses required D2 receptor activation, was absent in PD models, and was restored by a D2 agonist or endocannabinoid degradation inhibitors, which together reduced parkinsonian motor deficits in vivo.

Chan et al. (2007) found that blocking Cav1.3 channels in adult mouse nigral dopaminergic neurons restored juvenile pacemaking and protected them in mouse models of PD.

Outeiro et al. (2007) found that pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of SIRT2 rescued alpha-synuclein toxicity in cells and protected dopaminergic neurons in a Drosophila model.

Berman et al. (2011) found that EAAC1-null mice had age-dependent loss of nigral dopaminergic neurons (41 +/- 10% by 12 months), with increased nitrotyrosine, nitrosylated alpha-synuclein, and microglial activation; oral N-acetylcysteine reduced neuronal loss and pole test deficits.

Chung et al. (2013) found that alpha-synuclein caused nitrosative stress in yeast and that Hrd1 and its human homolog SYVN1 rescued toxicity in rat neurons. Tardiff et al. (2013) found that NAB reversed alpha-synuclein phenotypes in yeast, C. elegans, rat neurons expressing A53T, and iPS cell-derived neurons from patients with A53T or SNCA triplication.

Mittal et al. (2017) found that clenbuterol lowered nigral alpha-synuclein in mice and prevented MPTP-induced loss of nigral neurons.

### History

Duvoisin (1987) noted that the idea of a hereditary role in PD came from family histories and kindreds, strengthened by Mjones's systematic family study about 50 years earlier. Nussbaum and Polymeropoulos (1997) reviewed how hereditary influences had been discounted because of high MZ twin discordance, until those studies were shown to be inadequate and family history was recognized as a major risk factor.

Stien (2005) discussed descriptions of parkinsonism in Shakespeare, and Zhang et al. (2006) reviewed early descriptions of PD in ancient China.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, LEU444PRO [p.L444P]

In a woman with mild Gaucher disease and parkinsonism, Tayebi et al. (2001) found 2 GBA1 mutations, L444P and D409H, and a duplication including the glucocerebrosidase pseudogene on the maternal allele.

Toft et al. (2006) found N370S or L444P in 2.3% of 311 Norwegian PD patients and 1.7% of 474 controls (p = 0.58).

Mata et al. (2008) found N370S or L444P heterozygosity in 2.9% of PD patients and 0.4% of controls from Seattle (P less than 0.001).

Gan-Or et al. (2008) classified L444P among the severe Gaucher mutations screened in 420 Ashkenazi PD patients.

Sidransky et al. (2009) found a combined Mantel-Haenszel OR of 6.73 for L444P in their multicenter study; 22 of 43 subjects with L444P carried recombinant alleles including pseudogene sequence.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found 18 L444P heterozygotes among 953 early-onset PD patients; carriers had worse UPDRS-III motor scores than noncarriers.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ASP409HIS [p.D409H]

Tayebi et al. (2001) found D409H together with L444P in a woman with mild Gaucher disease and parkinsonism.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) included D409H among the severe Gaucher mutations screened in Ashkenazi PD patients.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ASN370SER [p.N370S; p.R496M; p.V394L]

Varkonyi et al. (2003) found N370S in 4 alleles among 4 patients with Gaucher disease and parkinsonism.

Among 99 Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients, Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 23 heterozygous and 3 homozygous for N370S.

Toft et al. (2006) found no increased frequency of N370S or L444P in Norwegian PD patients.

Mata et al. (2008) found N370S or L444P heterozygosity in 2.9% of PD patients vs 0.4% of controls.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) classified N370S as a mild Gaucher mutation in their Ashkenazi PD study.

Sidransky et al. (2009) found a combined OR of 3.96 for N370S (5.62 in Ashkenazi Jews), absent in Asians; 14 patients were homozygous and 3 compound heterozygous for N370S with R496M or V394L.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found 38 N370S heterozygotes and 2 homozygotes among 953 early-onset PD patients; carriers were more likely to be Jewish.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, REDUCED RISK OF**  
MT-ND3, 10398G

Van der Walt et al. (2003) found that the mtDNA 10398G SNP, which defines haplogroups J and K and changes threonine to alanine in ND3, was associated with lower PD risk in 609 white patients vs 340 controls (OR 0.53; P = 0.0001), more strongly in women (OR 0.43).

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
SNCAIP, ARG621CYS [c.1861C>T; p.R621C]

Marx et al. (2003) identified a heterozygous 1861C-T transition resulting in an arg621-to-cys (R621C) substitution in 2 apparently sporadic German PD patients; it was absent in 702 control chromosomes. Mutant synphilin-1 formed fewer inclusions under proteasomal inhibition, and expressing cells were more susceptible to staurosporine-induced death.

**.0006 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, 84GG

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 4 of 99 Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients heterozygous for 84GG.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) classified 84GG as a severe Gaucher mutation in their Ashkenazi PD study.

**.0007 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ARG496HIS [p.R496H]

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 1 of 99 Ashkenazi Jewish PD patients heterozygous for R496H.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) classified R496H as a mild Gaucher mutation.

**.0008 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
LRRK2, GLY2019SER [p.G2019S]

Gan-Or et al. (2008) found that Ashkenazi PD patients carrying G2019S had earlier onset.

Gan-Or et al. (2010) found that G2019S carriers among 600 Ashkenazi patients more often reported rigidity and balance disturbance as initial symptoms.

Alcalay et al. (2010) found 31 G2019S carriers (30 heterozygotes, 1 homozygote) among 953 early-onset patients, 96% with the postural instability and gait difficulty phenotype.

**.0009 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, LYS7GLU [p.K7E]

Neumann et al. (2009) identified K7E as a previously undescribed GBA1 mutation among 790 British PD patients.

**.0010 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, ASP443ASN [p.D443N]

Neumann et al. (2009) identified D443N as a previously undescribed GBA1 mutation among 790 British PD patients.

**.0011 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA1, GLY193GLU [p.G193E]

Neumann et al. (2009) identified G193E as a previously undescribed GBA1 mutation among 790 British PD patients.

**.0012 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, MODIFIER OF AGE AT ONSET**  
GLUD2, SER445ALA [c.1492T>G; p.S445A]

Plaitakis et al. (2010) found that hemizygous carriers of a 1492T-G transition, resulting in a ser445-to-ala (S445A) substitution, developed PD 6 to 13 years earlier in 2 Greek groups and 1 North American cohort; heterozygous females showed no effect. The variant enzyme had increased basal activity resistant to GTP inhibition and sensitive to estrogens.

**.0013 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
SNCA, ALA53THR [c.53A>T; p.A53T]

Chung et al. (2013) found nitrosative stress, ERAD substrate accumulation, and ER stress in iPS cell-derived neurons from a Contursi kindred patient carrying A53T, compared with isogenic corrected controls.

Tardiff et al. (2013) found protein trafficking defects and nitrosative stress in neurons from A53T patients that were reversed by NAB.
