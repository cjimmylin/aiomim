### TEXT

This entry describes late-onset Parkinson disease (PD), a common neurodegenerative disorder thought to result from interacting genetic and environmental factors. The papers associate susceptibility with heterozygous mutations in the GBA gene, in which mutations cause Gaucher disease. They also associate it with common variation at several loci, including SNCA, MAPT, and the HLA region. A causal gene for typical late-onset PD has not been established.

### Description

Parkinson disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer disease, affecting approximately 1% of the population over age 50 years (Polymeropoulos et al., 1996). Clinically it is characterized by bradykinesia, resting tremor, muscular rigidity, and postural instability, with a clinically significant response to levodopa (Pankratz et al., 2002). Pathologically it is characterized by severe nigral cell loss in the pars compacta and by accumulation of aggregated alpha-synuclein in specific brainstem, spinal cord, and cortical regions (Lees et al., 2009).

Warner and Schapira (2003) concluded that most cases probably result from a complex interplay between genetic and environmental influences. Lees et al. (2009) noted that age is the main known risk factor and that susceptibility genes, including alpha-synuclein, LRRK2, and GBA, have shown genetic predisposition to be another important causal factor.

### Clinical Features

Kissel and Andre (1976) reported monozygotic twin sisters who developed Parkinson disease and anosmia at age 39 years. The disease was controlled and regressed with L-dopa. The authors noted that 2 families with the same association had been reported previously.

Barbeau and Pourcher (1982) studied 32 patients whose parkinsonism began before age 40 years. They identified 3 subgroups: postencephalitic, tremor-predominant, and akinetic-rigid. In this early-onset group, 46% of cases were familial, compared with 10 to 15% in the general Parkinson population. Patients with tremor onset had a high prevalence of essential tremor in their families, whereas those with akinetic-rigid onset had a high familial incidence of PD.

Markopoulou et al. (1995) described a Greek American kindred with levodopa-responsive parkinsonism. Of 98 individuals in 6 generations, 16 in 3 successive generations were affected. The presentation consisted of asymmetric rigidity, resting tremor, bradykinesia, and postural instability, and inheritance appeared to be autosomal dominant. The kindred showed anticipation: affected members developed symptoms at ages 50 to 71 years in the third generation, 40 to 55 years in the fourth, and 31 years in the fifth.

Neudorfer et al. (1996) presented 6 patients with type I Gaucher disease who had parkinsonian symptoms. The syndrome was atypical, with a relatively severe course: neurologic signs appeared early, in the fourth to sixth decade of life, progressed aggressively, and were refractory to conventional anti-Parkinson therapy.

Montgomery et al. (1999) gave a PD test battery of motor function, olfaction, and mood tests to 80 asymptomatic first-degree relatives of PD patients and 100 normal controls. Abnormal scores were found in 22.5% of relatives and 9% of controls. Sons and daughters with abnormal scores were more likely to have an affected father.

Tayebi et al. (2001) described a patient with mild Gaucher disease and impaired horizontal saccadic eye movements. She developed a tremor at age 42 years, followed by rapid deterioration of her gait, and a pallidotomy at age 47 was unsuccessful. Her motor and cognitive deterioration progressed despite enzyme replacement therapy. GBA sequencing identified the L444P and D409H mutations, and the maternal allele also carried a duplication that included the glucocerebrosidase pseudogene and the metaxin gene.

Racette et al. (2002) examined 113 members of a large Old Order Amish pedigree. They classified 67 as normal, 17 as clinically definite PD, 6 as clinically probable PD, and 23 as clinically possible PD. Mean age at onset in the clinically definite subjects was 56.7 years. The phenotype was typical of idiopathic PD, with rest tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, and response to levodopa. Six of the clinically definite subjects had dementia, and many had levodopa-related motor complications, including wearing off and dopa-induced dyskinesias. The mean kinship coefficient was higher in subjects with PD than in age-matched Amish controls (0.036 vs 0.016). Sequence analysis found no mutations in known PD genes.

Varkonyi et al. (2003) reported 4 adults with Gaucher disease and parkinsonism. Parkinsonism began relatively early, progressed rapidly, and was refractory to therapy. Each patient had a different Gaucher genotype, although 4 alleles carried the N370S mutation. No mutations were found in the parkin or alpha-synuclein genes.

Bembi et al. (2003) described 4 patients with type 1 Gaucher disease, including 2 sibs, who had signs of typical PD: asymmetric onset of rigidity, resting tremor, bradykinesia, and a favorable response to Parkinson therapies.

Paleacu et al. (2005) studied 276 outpatients with PD and found hallucinations in 32%. Risk factors for hallucinations were the Mini-Mental State Examination score and a positive family history of dementia.

Voon et al. (2007) compared 21 patients with idiopathic PD who developed pathologic gambling after starting medication with 42 PD patients without compulsive behaviors. Patients who gambled had a younger age at PD onset and higher novelty seeking. They also more often had medication-induced hypomania or mania, impaired planning, or a personal or immediate family history of alcohol use disorders.

Ballanger et al. (2010) performed PET with the serotonin 2A receptor ligand setoperone F 18 in 7 nondemented PD patients with visual hallucinations and 7 age-matched PD patients without them. Patients with hallucinations had increased serotonin 2A receptor binding in the ventral visual pathway and in the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, medial orbitofrontal cortex, and insula.

### Other Features

Constantinescu et al. (2007) identified 5 cases of malignant melanoma in the DATATOP trial cohort of early PD, against 1.5 expected (standardized event ratio, 3.3). Melanoma incidence was not associated with levodopa therapy.

Ferreira et al. (2007) found neoplastic or preneoplastic skin lesions in 35 of 150 PD patients (23.3%) and 20 of 146 controls (13.7%). The difference lost statistical significance after adjustment for gender. Twenty-nine PD patients (19%) had actinic keratosis, and 4 had basal cell carcinoma.

In 2 large US cohorts, the Health Professionals Follow-up Study and the Nurses' Health Study, Gao et al. (2009) found that PD risk rose as natural hair color became lighter. Pooled relative risks were 1.40, 1.61, and 1.93 for brown, blond, and red hair compared with black hair. In a nested case-control study, individuals with the MC1R Cys/Cys genotype at the Arg151Cys polymorphism, which is associated with red hair, had a higher PD risk than those with the Arg/Arg genotype (adjusted RR, 3.15).

In a separate prospective analysis of 157,036 participants in the same 2 cohorts, Gao et al. (2009) identified 616 incident PD cases. A family history of melanoma in a first-degree relative was associated with a higher risk of PD (multivariate relative risk, 1.85). A family history of colorectal, lung, prostate, or breast cancer was not significantly associated.

Dong et al. (2014) examined pigmentation and melanoma GWAS SNPs in 808 PD cases and 1,623 controls from the PAGE study and in 5,333 cases and 12,019 controls from the IPDGC, all non-Hispanic whites. In PAGE, only rs4911414 at the ASIP gene was associated with PD after Bonferroni correction, and none of the 360 SNPs examined in IPDGC was associated after correction for multiple comparisons. Melanoma had been diagnosed in 18 PD cases (2.2%) and 26 controls (1.6%) (odds ratio, 1.3).

Tell-Marti et al. (2015) sequenced the MC1R gene in 870 PD patients and 736 controls from Spain. The melanoma risk variant p.R160W (rs1805008) was marginally associated with PD (odds ratio, 2.10; p = 0.009; Bonferroni-corrected p = 0.063).

### Biochemical Features

Barbeau et al. (1985) studied debrisoquine 4-hydroxylation in 40 patients with PD and 40 controls. Significantly more patients than controls had partially or totally defective hydroxylation, and poor metabolizers tended to have earlier disease onset.

Botella-Lopez et al. (2006) found that the 180-kD band of reelin was increased in the cerebrospinal fluid in all the neurodegenerative disorders they analyzed, including Parkinson disease.

Using SPECT, Fujita et al. (2006) imaged nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in 10 nondemented PD patients and 15 age-matched healthy subjects. Total distribution volume was decreased by about 10% in both cortical and subcortical regions in the patients.

### Inheritance

The role of heredity in PD has been debated. Young et al. (1977) analyzed ancestral second-degree relatives with PD in 12 families. Affected relatives were bilaterally distributed more often than autosomal dominant inheritance would predict, which suggested a multifactorial etiology for most cases. Dominant inheritance in some families was not entirely excluded.

Duvoisin et al. (1981) found zero concordance for PD in the first 12 monozygotic twin pairs of an ongoing twin study. They concluded that genetic factors do not play a major role in PD.

Ward et al. (1983) studied 43 monozygotic and 19 dizygotic twin pairs in which the index case had definite PD. Only 1 monozygotic pair was definitely concordant. When pairs with questionable features were included, 4 of 48 monozygotic and 1 of 19 dizygotic pairs were concordant. The authors concluded that the major etiologic factors are nongenetic.

Calne et al. (1987) reported 6 families with PD in which onset in different generations tended to occur at about the same calendar time. The mean difference in calendar time of onset between generations was 4.6 years, whereas the mean difference in age at onset between children and parents was 25.2 years. They interpreted this pattern as suggesting an environmental rather than genetic cause.

In a review, Duvoisin (1987) concluded that the best available data did not support a role for heredity in PD. He noted that familial aggregations of parkinsonism had reflected other disorders, such as olivopontocerebellar atrophy and multiple system atrophy.

Lazzarini et al. (1994) analyzed 80 multicase families with 2 or more affected members. Age-adjusted segregation ratios approached 0.5, the proportions of affected parents and sibs were similar, and the distribution of ancestral secondary cases was consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with reduced penetrance in a subset of PD.

Payami et al. (1995) studied 137 probands with idiopathic PD. The 21 probands with an affected parent, aunt, or uncle had a younger age at onset (47.7 +/- 8.8 years) than probands with only an affected sib (60.3 +/- 12.9 years) or with no affected relatives (59.2 +/- 11.4 years). Onset was on average 17 years earlier in the proband generation than in the parental generation, which the authors interpreted as most compatible with genetic anticipation.

Plante-Bordeneuve et al. (1995) ascertained 14 families with 110 first-degree and 40 second-degree relatives, of whom 31 (17 females and 14 males) were affected. Familial cases did not differ clinically from 31 age-matched sporadic cases. Transmission was compatible with an autosomal dominant model. The total segregation ratio of 0.25 suggested incomplete penetrance, which rose from 0 below age 30 years to 0.43 over age 70 years. In 8 multigeneration kindreds, onset was earlier in children than in parents (mean difference, 26 +/- 4.6 years).

Bonifati et al. (1995) found a positive family history of PD in 24% of 100 consecutive PD patients and in 6% of spouse controls. Familial cases had an earlier age at onset than sporadic cases. In 22 multicase families with 52 cases, lifetime cumulative risk in sibs approached 0.4 and all ancestral relatives were unilaterally distributed. The authors concluded that autosomal dominant inheritance with strongly age-related penetrance was most likely in familial PD.

Zareparsi et al. (1998) performed complex segregation analysis in kindreds of 136 randomly ascertained PD patients. The hypotheses of a nontransmissible environmental factor, of no major gene, and of all Mendelian modes of inheritance were rejected. Familial clustering was best explained by a rare familial factor that is transmitted in a non-Mendelian fashion and influences age at onset.

Tanner et al. (1999) screened 19,842 white male twins from the World War II Veteran Twins Registry and identified 193 twins with PD. Pairwise concordance was similar in monozygotic and dizygotic pairs (0.155 vs 0.111). However, in pairs in which at least 1 twin was diagnosed at or before age 50 years, monozygotic concordance was 1.0 (4 pairs) and dizygotic concordance was 0.167. The authors concluded that genetic factors are important when PD begins at or before age 50 years but not when it begins later.

Sveinbjornsdottir et al. (2000) confirmed PD in 772 patients in Iceland and used a genealogic database covering 11 centuries. Patients, including a subgroup of 560 with onset after age 50 years, were more closely related to each other than matched controls were, and the relatedness extended beyond the nuclear family. For relatives of patients with late-onset PD, the risk ratio was 6.7 for sibs, 3.2 for offspring, and 2.7 for nephews and nieces. The authors concluded that late-onset PD has a genetic as well as an environmental component.

Maher et al. (2002) performed segregation analysis in the nuclear families of 948 consecutively ascertained PD index cases with a mean age at onset of 57.7 years. The analysis supported a rare major Mendelian gene in both age-of-onset and susceptibility models.

Marder et al. (2003) compared first-degree relatives of 221 probands with onset at or before age 50 years and 266 probands with later onset against relatives of 409 controls. Risk of PD was similarly increased in relatives of early-onset probands (risk ratio, 2.9) and late-onset probands (2.7). In early-onset families, sibs were at markedly increased risk (risk ratio, 7.9) but parents were not. In late-onset families, both sibs (3.6) and parents (2.5) were at increased risk. The authors concluded that this pattern was consistent with an autosomal recessive contribution to early-onset but not late-onset PD.

Wirdefeldt et al. (2004) screened 33,780 twins in the Swedish Twin Registry. Among twins with possible PD there were only 2 concordant pairs, both female dizygotic, and the best-fitting model included only environmental components of variance.

Using NeuroGenetics Research Consortium data, Hamza et al. (2010) estimated that the heritability of PD fell from 0.6 to 0.4 after known pathogenic and susceptibility loci were excluded. Because it remained significant, they concluded that additional unidentified genes exist.

### Mapping

In a large kindred with autosomal dominant PD, Polymeropoulos et al. (1996) found linkage to markers on chromosome 4q21-q23, with a maximum lod score of 6.00 at marker D4S2380.

Scott et al. (2001) performed a complete genomic screen with 344 markers in 174 families with multiple members affected by idiopathic PD, studying 870 family members, 378 of them with PD. There was significant linkage to chromosome 6 in the parkin gene (MLOD = 5.07; LOD = 5.47) in families with at least 1 member with onset before age 40 years. Linkage was also detected to chromosomes 17q, 8p, and 5q, both overall and in late-onset families, and to 9q in families with both levodopa-responsive and nonresponsive patients. The authors suggested that multiple genetic factors may contribute to idiopathic late-onset PD.

Li et al. (2002) performed a genomic screen for age at onset in 174 PD families and 449 Alzheimer disease families. Heritabilities of 40 to 60% were found in both datasets. For PD, there was significant linkage of age at onset to chromosome 1p (lod = 3.41). Combined analysis of both diseases identified a single peak on chromosome 10q between D10S1239 and D10S1237 (maximum lod score, 2.62).

Pankratz et al. (2002) studied 203 sib pairs with PD and excluded the 22 families with parkin mutations. In the remaining 160 multiplex families, analysis using all examined individuals gave lod scores of 2.7 on chromosome X and 2.5 on chromosome 2. There was also evidence of linkage to chromosomes 4, 5, and 13 (lod scores above 1.5).

In 362 multiplex families comprising 754 affected individuals (425 sib pairs), Pankratz et al. (2003) obtained a maximum lod score of 3.4 on chromosome 2 under a stringent diagnostic model and 3.1 on the X chromosome when all affected individuals were included. In 85 families with a very strong family history, the lod score was 4.9 for chromosome 2 and 2.4 for chromosome 14. Subset analyses also gave lod scores of 2.3 to 2.4 on chromosome 10.

In a genomewide linkage screen of 5,824 SNPs in 278 families of European, non-Hispanic descent, Gao et al. (2009) found significant linkage on chromosome 18q11 (MLOD = 4.1) and suggestive linkage on chromosome 3q25 (MLOD = 2.5).

### Heterogeneity

Polymeropoulos et al. (1997) identified a mutation in the alpha-synuclein gene (SNCA) in the Italian kindred linked to chromosome 4q and in 3 unrelated families of Greek origin with autosomal dominant PD.

Vaughan et al. (2001) reviewed the genetics of parkinsonism. They noted that 2 genes, SNCA and parkin, and 2 additional loci had been implicated in familial PD, and that the evidence for a role of UCHL1 mutations was less clear. Pankratz et al. (2009) noted that mutations in 5 genes (PARK2, PARK7, PINK1, SNCA, and LRRK2) had been found in fewer than 5% of patients with PD.

Alcalay et al. (2010) screened 953 individuals with PD onset before age 51 years for mutations in SNCA, PRKN, PINK1, DJ1, LRRK2, and GBA. Mutations were found in 158 (16.6%): 64 (6.7%) in PRKN, 35 (3.6%) with LRRK2 G2019S, 64 (6.7%) in GBA, and 1 (0.2%) in DJ1. Carriers were more frequent among patients with onset at age 30 years or younger (40.6% vs 14.6%), patients of Jewish ancestry (32.4% vs 13.7%), and patients with a first-degree family history of PD (23.9% vs 15.1%).

### Molecular Genetics

Kurth et al. (1993) identified a single-stranded conformational polymorphism in the MAOB gene. Allele frequencies were 0.62 and 0.38 in 46 patients with PD and 0.45 and 0.55 in 121 controls, and MAOB allele 1 was associated with a relative risk of 2.03.

Ho et al. (1995) showed that the MAOB polymorphism was an A or G located 36 bases upstream of the intron 13-exon 14 boundary. Unlike the previous study, they found no significant association between this polymorphism and PD.

Bordet et al. (1994) genotyped CYP2D6 poor-metabolizer mutations in 105 Caucasian patients with idiopathic PD and 15 with diffuse Lewy body disease. The rate of genotypically defined poor metabolizers and the frequency of the D6-B mutation did not differ between the 2 groups.

Parboosingh et al. (1995) screened 107 unrelated familial and sporadic PD patients for mutations in the genes for copper/zinc superoxide dismutase, manganese superoxide dismutase, and catalase, and found none. A gly-to-asp substitution in exon 9 of the catalase gene in 1 patient was considered most likely a silent mutation.

Sandy et al. (1996) found no significant differences in CYP2D6 allele frequencies between 108 patients with young-onset PD (onset at or before age 51 years) and 236 controls.

Bandmann et al. (1996) sequenced the H5 pore region of hiGIRK2, the human homolog of the weaver mouse gene, in familial and sporadic PD and found it normal in all cases.

Martin et al. (2001) tested 5 SNPs in the tau gene (MAPT), which lies in a region of chromosome 17q21 linked to idiopathic PD, in 1,056 individuals from 235 families. Three SNPs were significantly associated with PD. Haplotype analysis showed a positive association with one haplotype (P = 0.009) and a negative association with another (P = 0.007).

In a Taiwanese case-control study of 224 patients and 197 controls, Wu et al. (2001) found that the MAOB intron 13 G genotype was associated with a 2.07-fold increased risk of PD. COMT alone showed no correlation with risk, but the association was synergistically enhanced in patients who carried both the COMT L and MAOB G genotypes.

In a French community-based study of 209 patients and 488 controls, Levecque et al. (2003) found associations of PD with polymorphisms in exon 22 of iNOS (OR for AA carriers, 0.50) and exon 29 of nNOS (OR for T-allele carriers, 1.53). The nNOS polymorphisms interacted significantly with cigarette smoking.

Van der Walt et al. (2003) genotyped 10 SNPs defining European mtDNA haplogroups in 609 white PD patients and 340 controls. Haplogroups J (OR, 0.55) and K (OR, 0.52) were associated with decreased risk compared with haplogroup H. The 10398G SNP, which causes a thr-to-ala change in ND3, was strongly associated with this protective effect (OR, 0.53), and the effect was stronger in women.

Chan et al. (2003) found the NAT2 slow acetylator genotype in 68.7% of 99 Chinese PD patients in Hong Kong and 28.6% of 126 controls (adjusted OR, 5.53).

Marx et al. (2003) analyzed the synphilin-1 gene (SNCAIP) in 328 German familial and sporadic PD patients. In 2 apparently sporadic patients they identified a C-to-T transition at coding position 1861, causing an arg621-to-cys (R621C) substitution, which was absent from 702 control chromosomes. In SH-SY5Y cells, the C621 mutant formed significantly fewer inclusions under proteasomal inhibition and made cells more susceptible to staurosporine-induced death.

Li et al. (2003) followed up the chromosome 10q age-at-onset linkage region using a 'genomic convergence' approach. They found significant associations with age at onset for GSTO1 (P = 0.007) and the adjacent GSTO2 (P = 0.005) in Alzheimer disease and PD samples.

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) screened 99 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with idiopathic PD, 74 Ashkenazi patients with Alzheimer disease, and 1,543 healthy Ashkenazi Jews for the 6 GBA mutations most common in this population. Thirty-one PD patients (31.3%) had 1 or 2 mutant alleles: 23 were heterozygous for N370S, 4 were heterozygous for 84GG, 3 were homozygous for N370S, and 1 was heterozygous for R496H. PD patients had significantly greater odds of carrying a Gaucher mutation than controls (OR, 7.0) or patients with Alzheimer disease (OR, 10.8). Carriers had an earlier mean age at onset than noncarriers (60.0 vs 64.2 years).

Maraganore et al. (2005) performed a 2-tiered whole-genome association study, genotyping 198,345 SNPs in 443 sib pairs discordant for PD and then 1,793 associated SNPs in 332 matched case-control pairs. A SNP in SEMA5A had the lowest combined P value (7.62 x 10(-6)). Other SNPs tagged the PARK11 and PARK10 late-onset susceptibility loci.

Kwok et al. (2005) identified 2 functional SNPs in GSK3B: a promoter SNP (rs334558) that affects transcriptional strength and an intronic SNP (rs6438552) that affects splicing. They found gene-gene interaction between the rs334558 T/T genotype and the MAPT H1/H1 haplotype, and a GSK3B haplotype was associated with disease risk after stratification by tau haplotype.

Toft et al. (2006) screened 311 Norwegian PD patients and 474 controls for the GBA N370S and L444P mutations. Seven patients (2.3%) and 8 controls (1.7%) carried a mutant allele, a nonsignificant difference.

In 418 PD patients and 468 matched controls, Tan et al. (2007) found no association between the CYP1A2 -163C-A variant (rs762551) and PD. Higher caffeine intake was associated with reduced risk, with no interaction between caffeine intake and CYP1A2 genotype.

Mata et al. (2008) studied 721 PD patients, 57 patients with dementia with Lewy bodies, and 554 controls from Seattle. Heterozygotes for GBA N370S or L444P were significantly more frequent in PD (2.9%) and in dementia with Lewy bodies (3.5%) than in controls (0.4%).

Pankratz et al. (2009) performed a GWAS in 857 familial PD cases and 867 controls. No result reached genomewide significance. The strongest association was with SNPs in the GAK/DGKQ region on chromosome 4, and consistent evidence of association was seen at SNCA and MAPT.

Neumann et al. (2009) screened the GBA gene in 790 British PD patients and 257 controls. Mutations were found in 33 patients (4.18%) and 3 controls (1.17%) (OR, 3.7). Fourteen different mutations were identified, 3 of them (K7E, D443N, and G193E) not previously described.

Sidransky et al. (2009) combined data from 16 centers on 5,691 PD patients (780 Ashkenazi Jews) and 4,898 controls (387 Ashkenazi Jews). N370S or L444P was found in 15% of Ashkenazi patients vs 3% of controls, and in 3% of non-Ashkenazi patients vs less than 1% of controls. Full sequencing in non-Ashkenazi patients showed that screening for these 2 mutations alone could miss up to 46% of mutant alleles. The odds ratio for any GBA mutation was 5.43. Seventeen patients carried 2 GBA mutations. The authors concluded that GBA mutations are likely to be a susceptibility factor rather than a Mendelian cause of PD.

Simon-Sanchez et al. (2009) performed a GWAS in 1,713 PD patients of European ancestry and 3,978 controls, with replication in 3,361 cases and 4,573 controls. They found strong association signals at SNCA (rs2736990; OR, 1.23) and at the MAPT locus (rs393152; OR, 0.77). They also replicated the PARK16 locus on 1q32 and gave supporting evidence that variation around LRRK2 modulates risk. The MAPT risk alleles were in linkage disequilibrium with the H1 haplotype and were associated with increased MAPT expression in brain. The association with MAPT was absent in the Japanese cohort, suggesting population-specific genetic heterogeneity.

In a GWAS and 2 replication studies totaling 2,011 cases and 18,381 controls from Japan, Satake et al. (2009) identified a new susceptibility locus on 1q32, designated PARK16, and BST1 on 4p15 as a second new risk locus. They also found strong associations at SNCA and LRRK2.

Elstner et al. (2009) profiled whole-genome expression in laser-microdissected substantia nigra neurons from PD patients and controls. An intronic PDXK variant, rs2010795, was associated with PD in a German cohort and the association was confirmed in British and Italian cohorts (combined OR, 1.3; p = 1.2 x 10(-7)).

Plaitakis et al. (2010) reported that a rare T1492G variant in the X-linked GLUD2 gene, causing a ser445-to-ala substitution, was associated with earlier onset of PD in hemizygous men (see ALLELIC VARIANTS).

In 2 case-control cohorts totaling 1,349 ethnic Chinese subjects, Tan et al. (2010) found association of PD with the PARK16, PARK8, and PARK1 loci but not BST1. PARK16 SNPs were associated with decreased risk. A pooled analysis with 2 Japanese replication cohorts showed robust association at all 3 loci and also at BST1.

Hamza et al. (2010) performed a GWAS in 2,000 PD patients and 1,986 controls from the NeuroGenetics Research Consortium. They confirmed associations with SNCA and MAPT, replicated association with GAK, and detected a new association in the HLA region. The peak was at rs3129882, a noncoding variant in intron 1 of HLA-DRA (P = 2.9 x 10(-8)), and the association replicated in 2 datasets (meta-analysis P = 1.9 x 10(-10)). It was strong in sporadic and late-onset disease. The authors did not replicate PARK16.

In 1,445 PD patients and 1,161 controls from northern Spain, Mata et al. (2011) replicated associations with MAPT (rs1800547) and SNCA (rs356219). They found no association with markers in PARK16, PARK17, or PARK18.

### Genotype/Phenotype Correlations

Gan-Or et al. (2008) screened 420 Ashkenazi PD patients, 333 elderly controls, and 3,805 young controls for 8 GBA mutations, classified by whether they are associated with mild (N370S, R496H) or severe Gaucher disease. GBA carrier frequency was 17.9% in patients vs 4.2% in elderly controls. Severe and mild mutations increased PD risk 13.6-fold and 2.2-fold, respectively. Mean age at onset was 55.7 years with severe mutations, 57.9 years with mild mutations, and 60.7 years in patients without known GBA or LRRK2 mutations.

In British GBA mutation carriers, Neumann et al. (2009) found early onset, hallucinations in 45% (14 of 31), and cognitive decline or dementia in 48% (15 of 31). All 17 carriers examined pathologically had widespread alpha-synuclein pathology (Braak stage 5-6).

In the multicenter study of Sidransky et al. (2009), GBA mutation carriers had an earlier mean age at onset than noncarriers (54.9 vs 58.8 years). Carriers more often had an affected relative (24.0% vs 17.8%) and more often had cognitive changes, but less often had asymmetric onset, bradykinesia, resting tremor, and rigidity.

In 600 Ashkenazi PD patients, Gan-Or et al. (2010) found that LRRK2 G2019S carriers more often reported rigidity and balance disturbances as initial symptoms, whereas GBA mutation carriers more often reported bradykinesia.

In early-onset PD, Alcalay et al. (2010) found that the GBA L444P mutation was associated with a higher mean UPDRS III score after adjustment for covariates.

### Pathogenesis

Calne and Langston (1983) suggested that in most cases of PD the cause may be an environmental, possibly toxic, factor superimposed on slow, sustained neuronal loss due to aging.

Langston et al. (1983) reported 4 persons who developed marked parkinsonism after intravenous use of an illicit drug. The substance injected by 2 of them was mainly MPTP, and the authors proposed that this chemical selectively damages cells in the substantia nigra.

Di Monte (1991) reviewed evidence for mitochondrial impairment in PD. The toxicity of MPP+, the metabolite of MPTP, likely results from blockade of electron flow at complex I, and complex I activity is deficient in brain and other tissues of patients. Because 7 complex I subunit genes are encoded by mtDNA, he suggested that mtDNA abnormalities might play a role.

Nussbaum and Polymeropoulos (1997) noted that idiopathic PD has a pathologic correlate in Lewy bodies, eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions distributed throughout the substantia nigra, hypothalamus, hippocampus, autonomic ganglia, and olfactory tracts. They suggested that at least some familial PD with diffuse Lewy bodies results from an abnormal alpha-synuclein protein that interferes with normal protein degradation.

Clarke et al. (2000) found that the kinetics of neuronal death in models of several neurodegenerations, including PD, were exponential. This argued against a cumulative damage hypothesis and favored a 'one-hit' model in which a single random event initiates cell death.

Auluck et al. (2002) showed that directed expression of the chaperone Hsp70 prevented alpha-synuclein-associated loss of dopaminergic neurons in Drosophila, whereas interfering with endogenous chaperone activity accelerated toxicity. Lewy bodies in human postmortem tissue immunostained for molecular chaperones.

Teismann et al. (2003) found that COX-2 is upregulated in brain dopaminergic neurons of PD patients and MPTP-treated mice. COX-2 inhibition protected against MPTP toxicity by preventing formation of dopamine-quinone rather than by mitigating inflammation.

Scherzer et al. (2003) profiled genome-wide expression in transgenic Drosophila expressing human alpha-synuclein and identified 51 signature transcripts, including lipid, energy, and membrane transport mRNAs, associated with alpha-synuclein expression, with changes evident at the presymptomatic stage.

Cooper et al. (2006) found that the earliest defect after alpha-synuclein expression in yeast was a block in ER-to-Golgi vesicular trafficking. Elevated expression of Rab1, the mammalian homolog of the yeast Rab GTPase Ypt1p, protected against alpha-synuclein-induced loss of dopaminergic neurons in animal models.

Outeiro et al. (2007) found that inhibiting SIRT2, either pharmacologically or by siRNA, rescued alpha-synuclein toxicity in a cellular model. The inhibitors also protected against dopaminergic cell death in vitro and in a Drosophila model.

Minones-Moyano et al. (2011) found decreased expression of miR-34b and miR-34c in several brain areas of PD patients, at both motor and premotor stages. Depleting these miRNAs in dopaminergic neuronal cells impaired mitochondrial function and reduced DJ1 and Parkin expression.

Chung et al. (2013) generated cortical neurons from iPS cells of a female member of the Contursi kindred, which carries the A53T SNCA mutation, and from mutation-corrected cells. Genetic modifiers from a yeast model of alpha-synuclein toxicity led to the identification of nitrosative stress, accumulation of ER-associated degradation substrates, and ER stress in patient neurons. These phenotypes were reversed by the small molecule NAB2 and by the ubiquitin ligase Nedd4. Tardiff et al. (2013) had identified NAB as a compound that protected diverse cell types from alpha-synuclein toxicity through Rsp5/Nedd4-dependent endosomal transport.

In an eQTL study of CD4+ T cells and monocytes from 461 healthy individuals, Raj et al. (2014) found that monocyte-specific eQTLs were overrepresented among Alzheimer and Parkinson disease risk variants.

Sulzer et al. (2017) studied 67 PD patients and 36 age-matched healthy controls. They showed that peptides derived from alpha-synuclein, from a region near tyr39 and from a phosphorylated ser129 region, act as antigenic epitopes and drive helper and cytotoxic T-cell responses in PD patients. Epitopes in the Y39 region bound the HLA alleles DRB1*15:01 and DRB5*01:01 with high affinity, and about 40% of patients had immune responses to alpha-synuclein epitopes.

Mittal et al. (2017) identified the beta-2-adrenoreceptor as a regulator of SNCA transcription through histone 3 lys27 acetylation of its promoter and enhancers. In the Norwegian population, the beta-2 agonist salbutamol was associated with reduced risk of PD (rate ratio, 0.66), whereas the antagonist propranolol was associated with increased risk (rate ratio, 2.20). Beta-2 activation lowered SNCA expression in iPSC-derived neuronal cells from a patient with an SNCA triplication.

Burbulla et al. (2017) studied dopaminergic neurons derived from patients with idiopathic and familial PD, including patients homozygous for a DJ-1 loss-of-function mutation. They identified a time-dependent cascade that began with mitochondrial oxidant stress, led to accumulation of oxidized dopamine, and ended in reduced glucocerebrosidase activity, lysosomal dysfunction, and alpha-synuclein accumulation. The cascade was seen in human but not mouse neurons.

### Diagnosis

Hughes et al. (1992) examined the brains of 100 patients diagnosed prospectively with idiopathic PD. Seventy-six had nigral Lewy bodies, and in all of these Lewy bodies were also found in the cerebral cortex. The 24 without Lewy bodies had diagnoses that included progressive supranuclear palsy, multiple system atrophy, Alzheimer disease, and basal ganglia vascular disease. Retrospective application of recommended diagnostic criteria improved diagnostic accuracy to 82%.

Using an alpha-synuclein protein misfolding cyclic amplification (PMCA) assay, Shahnawaz et al. (2020) analyzed cerebrospinal fluid from 94 patients with PD, 75 with multiple system atrophy, and 56 controls with other neurologic diseases. The assay distinguished PD from multiple system atrophy with an overall sensitivity of 95.4%, which the authors attributed to different conformational strains of alpha-synuclein aggregates.

### Clinical Management

Lees et al. (2009) noted that dopamine replacement therapy considerably reduces motor handicap and that treating associated depression, pain, constipation, and nocturnal difficulties can improve quality of life.

In a phase 1 safety trial, Gill et al. (2003) delivered GDNF directly into the putamen of 5 PD patients. After 1 year there were no serious clinical side effects. The off-medication UPDRS motor subscore improved by 39%, the activities of daily living subscore improved by 61%, and medication-induced dyskinesias were reduced by 64%. PET showed a 28% increase in putamen dopamine storage after 18 months.

In a randomized double-blind crossover trial in 33 PD patients homozygous for the COMT Val158Met alleles, Corvol et al. (2011) found that entacapone produced a greater gain in best ON time in Val/Val (high-activity) patients than in Met/Met (low-activity) patients (39 vs 9 minutes).

### Population Genetics

In a hospital-based survey of the age-specific prevalence of PD in 551 patients from Helsinki and Vancouver, Teravainen et al. (1986) concluded that the disorder may be starting earlier than previously.

In a door-to-door survey of 982 inhabitants older than 65 years in 2 Bavarian villages, Trenkwalder et al. (1995) found a PD prevalence of 0.71%, with a male predominance (5 men, 2 women).

Polymeropoulos et al. (1997) cited a lifetime incidence of PD of approximately 2%.

Racette et al. (2002) calculated a minimum PD prevalence of 552 per 100,000 among individuals aged 40 years and older in an Old Order Amish community.

In a community-based survey of individuals aged 50 years and older in Singapore, Tan et al. (2004) found an age-adjusted PD prevalence of 0.30%. Rates were the same among Chinese (0.33%), Malays (0.29%), and Indians (0.28%).

### Animal Model

Kordower et al. (2000) delivered GDNF with a lentiviral vector into the striatum and substantia nigra of rhesus monkeys. In aged monkeys, lenti-GDNF augmented dopaminergic function. In MPTP-treated monkeys, it reversed functional deficits and completely prevented nigrostriatal degeneration.

Luo et al. (2002) used AAV-mediated gene transfer to express glutamic acid decarboxylase in glutamatergic neurons of the rat subthalamic nucleus. This strongly protected nigral dopamine neurons and rescued the parkinsonian behavioral phenotype.

Landau et al. (2005) found that Fas-deficient lymphoproliferative mice treated with MPTP developed extensive nigrostriatal degeneration with tremor, hypokinesia, and loss of motor coordination, at a dose that did not affect wildtype mice.

Sotnikova et al. (2006) developed an acute model of severe dopamine deficiency by pharmacologically inhibiting tyrosine hydroxylase in dopamine transporter-knockout mice. The resulting dopamine-deficient (DDD) mice showed severe akinesia, rigidity, tremor, and ptosis.

Kreitzer and Malenka (2007) showed that endocannabinoid-mediated long-term depression at indirect-pathway striatal synapses was absent in models of PD. It was rescued by a D2 receptor agonist or by inhibitors of endocannabinoid degradation, and giving these drugs together in vivo reduced parkinsonian motor deficits.

Chan et al. (2007) found that adult substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons rely on L-type Cav1.3 calcium channels for pacemaking. Blocking these channels induced a reversion to juvenile pacemaking ('rejuvenation') and protected the neurons in in vitro and in vivo models of PD.

Using optogenetic control of striatal medium spiny neurons in mice, Kravitz et al. (2010) found that bilateral excitation of indirect-pathway neurons elicited a parkinsonian state, whereas activation of direct-pathway neurons completely rescued freezing, bradykinesia, and locomotor-initiation deficits in a mouse model of PD.

Berman et al. (2011) found that EAAC1-null mice, whose neurons have impaired cysteine uptake and reduced glutathione, lost more than 40% of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons by age 12 months. The loss was accompanied by nitrosylated alpha-synuclein and microglial activation. These changes were substantially reduced by N-acetylcysteine.

### ALLELIC VARIANTS

**.0001 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA, LEU444PRO [L444P]

Tayebi et al. (2001) identified the L444P and D409H GBA mutations in a woman with mild Gaucher disease who developed tremor at age 42 years, followed by rapid deterioration of gait. Her maternal allele also carried a duplication that included the glucocerebrosidase pseudogene and the metaxin gene.

In the 16-center study of Sidransky et al. (2009), the combined Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio for PD associated with L444P was 6.73. Twenty-two of 43 subjects with L444P carried other pseudogene sequence and therefore had recombinant alleles.

In patients with early-onset PD, Alcalay et al. (2010) found that L444P was associated with a higher mean UPDRS III score.

**.0002 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET**  
SNCAIP, ARG621CYS [c.1861C>T; p.Arg621Cys; R621C]

In 2 apparently sporadic German PD patients, Marx et al. (2003) identified a C-to-T transition at coding position 1861 of the synphilin-1 gene, resulting in an arg621-to-cys (R621C) substitution. The change was absent from 702 control chromosomes. In SH-SY5Y cells under proteasomal inhibition, the C621 protein formed significantly fewer cytoplasmic inclusions than wildtype protein, and cells expressing it were more susceptible to staurosporine-induced cell death.

**.0003 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA, ASN370SER [N370S]

Varkonyi et al. (2003) reported 4 adults with Gaucher disease and early-onset, rapidly progressive parkinsonism. Each had a different Gaucher genotype, and 4 of their alleles carried N370S.

Among 99 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with idiopathic PD, Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 23 heterozygous and 3 homozygous for N370S. Among 1,543 Ashkenazi controls, 92 were heterozygous for N370S.

Sidransky et al. (2009) found that N370S was particularly prevalent among Ashkenazi Jews and was not seen in any of the Asian patients or controls. The combined Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio for PD associated with N370S was 3.96. Gan-Or et al. (2008) classified N370S as a mild Gaucher mutation; mild mutations increased PD risk 2.2-fold.

**.0004 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, SUSCEPTIBILITY TO**  
GBA, 84GG [84GG]

Aharon-Peretz et al. (2004) found 4 of 99 Ashkenazi Jewish patients with idiopathic PD to be heterozygous for the GBA 84GG mutation, compared with 3 of 1,543 healthy Ashkenazi controls.

Gan-Or et al. (2008) classified 84GG among the GBA mutations associated with severe Gaucher disease. Severe mutations increased PD risk 13.6-fold in Ashkenazi Jews and were associated with a mean age at onset of 55.7 years.

**.0005 PARKINSON DISEASE, LATE-ONSET, MODIFIER OF AGE AT ONSET**  
GLUD2, SER445ALA [T1492G; Ser445Ala]

Plaitakis et al. (2010) reported a rare T1492G variant in the X-linked GLUD2 gene, resulting in a ser445-to-ala substitution in the regulatory domain of glutamate dehydrogenase hGDH2. In 2 independent Greek PD groups and 1 North American cohort, hemizygous carriers developed PD 6 to 13 years earlier than subjects with other genotypes. No such effect was seen in heterozygous women. The variant enzyme had enhanced basal activity that resisted GTP inhibition but was markedly sensitive to modification by estrogens.
